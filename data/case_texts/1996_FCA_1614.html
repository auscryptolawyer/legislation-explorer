MIM Holdings Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1996] FCA 1614 (12 July 1996)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

M.I.M. HOLDINGS LIMITED v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. QG 34, QG 35 and QG 36 of 1994

FED No. 609/96

Number of pages - 18

Taxation

COURT

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

DRUMMOND J

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - income - characterisation of payments made to taxpayer, based on estimate of the capital costs to its wholly owned subsidiary of establishing and expanding the latter's electricity generating plant received under an agreement between the taxpayer and public authority in return for a promise by the taxpayer to ensure that its subsidiary would keep a defined part of its generating capacity available to meet a third party's needs.

Taxation - "full and true disclosure" - s 170(2)(b) the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) - whether failure by taxpayer to disclose the contractual framework, as opposed to the economic or financial effect, of an agreement under which it received payments is non- disclosure of a fact material to the characterisation of the payments for taxation purposes.

Electricity Act 1976 (Qld) - s 67

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) - ss 25(1), 170, 170(2)(b), 170(3)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) - s 14ZZ

DECISION

DRUMMOND J This is an appeal under s 14ZZ the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) against decisions made by the respondent Commissioner on 28 January 1994: the Commissioner disallowed the applicant's objections against amended assessments dated 26 April 1991, 21 April 1992 and 21 April 1992, which included, for the first time, certain payments received by the applicant in its assessable income for the 1984, 1985 and 1986 years of income respectively. The original assessments required payment of the amounts assessed by 30 days after service of the assessment, in respect of the 1984 year, issued on 27 March 1985 and, in respect of the 1985 and 1986 years, on 26 May 1986 and on 30 April 1987 respectively. In relation to each of those three years, the questions for determination now are:

1. Whether for each of these three years of income,

there was a full and true disclosure by the applicant

of all the material facts necessary for the assessment

of the relevant income within the meaning of s 170 the

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), as then in

force, prior to the making of the original assessment.

If not, the Commissioner was entitled to issue the

amended assessments.

2. Whether the payments received by the applicant

pursuant to a contract called the "Reservation

Agreement" in each of the 1984, 1985 and 1986 years

and included in the relevant amended assessment, but

not in the original assessment, formed part of the

assessable income of the applicant.

THE SUPPLY BY ISA OF POWER TO NORQEB

2. For many years Mount Isa Mines Limited ("Isa") has conducted a mining operation at Mt Isa. In 1970, following a reconstruction, the applicant, MIM Holdings Ltd ("Holdings") was formed and Isa became a wholly owned subsidiary of Holdings. Well before that, Isa established and operated at the Mt Isa mine site a power station called the Mines Power Station, which supplied electrical power to the mine, smelter and associated installations. The Mt Isa township, close to the mine site, in which many of Isa and Holdings' employees live with their families, has never had any public source of electrical power supply. Power was initially provided by Isa from the Mines Power Station through the Mt Isa City Council, to the town's residents. Because of increased demands for electricity from both the mine and the township, a second power station was established by Isa at Mica Creek in the early 1960s. The Mica Creek Power Station was connected with the Mines Power Station.

3. In June 1972, Isa and the Mt Isa City Council entered into a Bulk Electricity Supply Agreement, pursuant to which Isa agreed to supply electrical power from its power stations to the Council for reticulation within the Mt Isa township and also within the Shire of Cloncurry ("the 1972 Agreement"). In 1976, the North Queensland Electricity Board ("NORQEB") was constituted; it replaced the Mt Isa City Council as a party to the 1972 Agreement. That Agreement was expressed to be subject to review in June 1982. In about February 1982, officers of Isa met with officers of NORQEB to negotiate a new Bulk Electricity Supply Agreement for the Mt Isa region. Officers of Holdings were not then involved. The demand for power, both by the mine and the township, had continued to increase and, by late 1981, Holdings and Isa considered that an additional 30 to 33 megawatts of capacity would be required by 1985/1986, to meet the growing requirements of both the Mt Isa Mine and NORQEB.

4. The State Electricity Commission ("SEC") became involved in the negotiations between NORQEB and Isa. Initially, its interest was to ascertain whether the increasing power requirements of north-west Queensland could best be served by a power line from the coast linked with the State electricity grid or by increased local generation or by a combination of both. Moreover, NORQEB was required by s 67 the Electricity Act 1976 (Qld) to obtain the approval of the SEC before entering into any new Bulk Electricity Supply Agreement and the SEC had a duty under the Electricity Act to ensure that electric power was supplied to consumers throughout Queensland as economically as possible. It turned out that the most economical option was to increase local power generation from the Mica Creek Power Station, although this involved the construction of an additional generating unit there.

5. The representatives of Isa and NORQEB were able to reach agreement on most aspects of the proposed new Bulk Supply Agreement. But they could not agree on the proposed electricity tariffs, in particular, on the capital components of the tariffs (by which Isa planned to recoup, among other things, part of the cost of constructing the additional generating unit) or on the formula for escalation of those capital components. The tariffs Isa proposed that NORQEB should pay for supply under the new agreement would form the basis for the tariffs NORQEB, in turn, would charge the consumers it supplied. If NORQEB agreed to Isa's proposal, that would have resulted in NORQEB having to increase substantially its own tariffs to consumers over its current charges.

6. By about 3 June 1982 negotiations between Isa and NORQEB had reached an impasse. This breakdown was not acceptable to the SEC. On or about 5 August 1982 Mr Galwey, the Commissioner of the SEC, wrote to Sir Bruce Watson, the Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer of Holdings and Chairman of Isa, expressing the SEC's concern about the magnitude of the tariff increase attributable to the capital components proposed by Isa; Mr Galwey asked for Sir Bruce Watson's assistance in arranging discussions that would resolve the differences that existed between Isa and NORQEB. On 27 August 1982, Mr Galwey met Mr Middlin, a director of Holdings. Mr Galwey suggested that the SEC might make what he called "a non-refundable capital contribution" towards the cost of the capital works for the additional generating unit at Mica Creek Power Station, as a solution to the dispute between Isa and NORQEB. When the SEC provided a new bulk electricity supply to a large industrial or mining project in Queensland, it commonly required, as a condition of that supply, the payment by the project owner to the SEC of a non-refundable contribution to the capital cost to the SEC of providing the supply. Mr Galwey considered that since NORQEB was in a position analogous to a bulk consumer of another's supply, it was appropriate to offer the same sort of contribution to that other's capital costs of supply as the SEC required in similar circumstances. If Mr Galwey's proposal were acceptable to Isa, it would enable a tariff to be agreed between NORQEB and Isa which would be within limits acceptable to the SEC, having regard to its statutory duty to ensure economical supply to consumers, because it would cover only Isa's operating costs of supply, with no component for recovery of Isa's capital costs of supply. From that point, senior employees of Holdings participated in the negotiations.

7. The board of directors of Holdings ultimately resolved to accept the SEC's offer of capital contributions. The negotiations culminated in two written agreements, viz:

1. An agreement between the Minister for Mines and

Energy (on behalf of the Queensland Government) and

Holdings dated 11 August 1983 ("the Reservation

Agreement"). This dealt with the contributions to be

made by the SEC to Holdings in respect of certain of

the capital costs incurred and to be incurred by Isa

in relation to its existing and its expanded plant.

The Reservation Agreement was entered into by the

that the SEC had power to enter into an agreement of

that kind.

2. An Agreement for the bulk supply of electricity

at Mount Isa ("the Bulk Supply Agreement") between Isa

periodic payments to be made to Isa for supplying

electricity to NORQEB: the two tariffs provided for

by this Agreement covered only Isa's operating costs

of power supply and did not include any component in

respect of capital costs which had been incurred by

Isa up to 1983 in establishing, and which were

thereafter to be incurred by Isa in expanding, its

power stations.

9. Clause 8 was of special significance to Holdings. Sir Bruce Watson said:

"One of the fundamental principles of our negotiations

with the SEC and NORQEB was that there was to be no

suggestion that NORQEB or the SEC could have ownership

or operational say in the Mica Creek Power Station.

There was no sense in the SEC building a power station

alongside our power station and we did not want the

introduction of different operating conditions that

would apply to State employees and not our workers.

We have an industrial agreement, the Mount Isa Mines

Ltd Award, that was separate from all the others in

the State. Isa was not normally involved in any of

the one day stoppages that were taking place in the

State electricity grid in the early 1980s."

10. Holdings' concern that neither the SEC nor NORQEB should have any ownership interest or operating control in respect of Isa's power stations at Mount Isa was apparent during negotiations to the SEC officers involved. Mr Viertel, a Deputy Commissioner of the SEC who was closely involved in the negotiations, said that it was Holdings' concern about industrial relations considerations and its desire to have Isa, the operating company, removed as far as possible from the SEC and from any possibility of the SEC either having a claim to ownership of or control in the power station that led to the capital contribution being paid to Holdings under an agreement separate from that under which NORQEB periodically paid the operating company, Isa, for electricity supply. The importance to Holdings and Isa of Isa retaining full ownership and control of the generating plant necessary to supply Isa's power needs for all of its own mining and industrial operations at Mt Isa, so clear from the evidence, appears to have been dictated by concern that any reliance on power externally supplied might expose Isa's own operations to interruption: industrial unrest was then prevalent in the State controlled power generation industry. It appears that the opinion of senior Holdings management was that continuity of the supply of power on which Isa relied for its operations was better assured if all power came from Isa's own plant, operated by its own employees who all worked under the one enterprise-specific industrial award.

11. The Reservation Agreement formed but one component of the whole arrangement negotiated between the SEC and Holdings: the Bulk Supply Agreement between NORQEB and Isa, agreed in the course of those same negotiations, although executed a little later, was an integral part of the arrangement. The Reservation Agreement cannot be said to have ignored the fact that Holdings and Isa were separate entities, given the evidence explaining Holdings' insistence on the capital contributions being paid to it rather than to Isa, as the legal owner of the plant, for Holdings' own commercial reasons. Since Isa was wholly owned and controlled by Holdings, the reference in the Reservation Agreement to "(Holdings') generating system at Mount Isa" was not a loose expression; the assumption by Holdings in that Agreement of an obligation to reserve for NORQEB part of that system's output was apt to achieve the mutual objectives of Holdings and the SEC, supplemented as it was by the Bulk Supply Agreement between NORQEB and Isa. It was important to Holdings for its own business purposes, to which I have referred, to ensure that the capital contribution payments came to it as an entity legally distinct from the operating company, Isa.

12. It appears that the payments received by Isa under the 1972 Agreement and then under the 1983 Reservation Agreement and the 1984 Bulk Supply Agreement provided only for recoupment of capital and operating costs, ie, that there was no profit component in them. The price initially payable (subject to escalation) by the Mt Isa City Council, and later NORQEB, under the 1972 Agreement for electricity supplied by Isa, was 2.1 cents per kilowatt hour. This figure was made up of a component of 1.027 cents for fuel and a component of 0.38 cents for labour and maintenance. The residual 0.7 cents was a component to cover part of Isa's acquisition and ongoing ownership costs of the generating equipment and all other capital charges. As the table in Part 3 of Schedule B to the 1984 Bulk Supply Agreement shows, the two tariffs provided for in that Agreement are made up of allowances for salaries and wages, fuel and other operating and maintenance costs: there is no provision for either a capital component or a profit component. I do not regard Mr Charlton's memorandum to Mr Middlin as suggesting the contrary. The evidence shows that the payments to be made to Holdings under the 1983 Reservation Agreement did not include any profit component: they comprise a contribution towards the original capital costs of the Mine and the Mica Creek Power Stations from 1972 to 1982, and towards capital costs of the fifth 33MW generating unit which Isa, with Holdings' approval, decided in 1982 to install at Mica Creek Power Station.

13. The one-off contribution of $5.265M to be paid by the SEC, or rather by the State Government, with respect to the existing plant pursuant to clause 1(i) of the Reservation Agreement was based on the ratio of NORQEB's total demand (20MW) to the total installed capacity of Isa's existing plant in 1982 (139MW), ie, very approximately 14%, applied to the total of Isa's costs of establishing its power plants to 1972 ($33.3M), plus 14% of its capital expenditure on plant from then to end of the 1982/83 financial year, less the capital component of the tariff charges paid to Isa by Mt Isa Council and then by NORQEB for each year from 1972 to 1982, when the new Bulk Supply Agreement came into effect, all converted to a constant value in 1982 dollars. But, as Mr Galwey said, the $5.265M was a negotiating figure, ie, a broad estimate only, that Holdings ultimately accepted: "it has no mathematical support". There were extensive negotiations before agreement was reached on how the SEC's contribution to Isa's capital costs of installing the extra generating capacity at the Mica Creek Station should be determined. Although it is difficult to identify all the figures used in the final calculation, it is apparent that the parties arrived at the provisional contribution figure of $10.605M in clause (1)(ii) of the Reservation Agreement on the basis of the ratio of NORQEB's agreed maximum demand to June 1994 to the total installed capacity of the plant, including the planned extensions (ie 19.76%), applied to the estimated cost of constructing the extensions. This figure was provisional in that the Agreement provided for it to be adjusted when the final costs of these extensions were known. In addition, a similar method of calculating the capital contribution to be paid by the State Government was agreed in relation to certain other capital works projects "on its power generation system at Mt Isa" that Holdings might undertake.

14. A number of reasons emerge from the evidence for Holdings and Isa adopting a policy of supplying power to NORQEB on a costs recoupment basis only. Sir Bruce Watson explained that, in the 1970s, the company was prepared to subsidise electricity supply to the township to support the local community (then mostly Isa employees and their families) and because it was concerned that full cost recovery might cause industrial trouble in the Mount Isa operations. By the early 1980's Mount Isa had a larger and more diverse population. But the industrial relations advantages to Holdings and Isa from continuing to supply subsidised electricity to the town explain, in part, the willingness of Holdings and Isa to continue to supply power to the township on a cost recovery basis under the new Bulk Supply Agreement of 1984. Isa's willingness to supply power at cost to the public was also, I think, seen by Holdings and Isa's management as assisting in ensuring that Isa would retain control over power generation in the Mt Isa region, something of central importance to Holdings and Isa, as is evidenced by clause 8 of the Reservation Agreement: there would be no pressure on the public authorities to provide power to the Mt Isa public if Isa were prepared to supply public needs at attractive prices.

15. It is plain that the arrangements with respect to the supply of electricity to the Mount Isa Region from 1982 were arrived at as a result of arm's length negotiations between officers of Holdings and Isa, on the one side, and officers of the SEC and NORQEB, on the other. The Reservation Agreement and the Bulk Supply Agreement, in my opinion, accurately reflect the objectives Holdings intended to achieve. The form of the two Agreements and the relevant surrounding circumstances, to the more important of which I have referred, show that it was to serve Holdings' own business interests that the arrangements for supply of electricity to the Mount Isa township and surrounding areas that came into effect on the expiry of the 1972 agreement took the form they did of the Reservation Agreement between Holdings and the State Government and the Bulk Supply Agreement between Isa and NORQEB. Holdings insisted, for its own business purposes, that the capital contribution in respect of Isa's existing and new plant was paid to it rather than to Isa, the legal owner and operator of that plant. It insisted on these arrangements taking the legal form of the two separate contracts because, in large part, that was in the best interests of Holdings (and Isa) from a number of industrial relations aspects. It was, I think, of fundamental importance to Holdings to prevent Isa's workforce from being dispersed among different trade unions, as a means of minimising the risk of industrial disruption and thus disruption to Isa's operations. The State power industry had a tradition of industrial volatility. Holdings considered it vital for Isa to retain control of the generation of all its power needs, even if this meant supplying the local residents with subsidised power. This also produced other industrial relations benefits for Holdings and Isa in their relations with their own workforce residence in Mt Isa.

16. The form in which the transaction was structured, at Holdings insistence, was thus of very substantial significance to Holdings for its own commercial purposes. It is a form apt to achieve Holdings' objectives of recouping from the public purse what it regarded as a satisfactory contribution towards the capital costs of its subsidiary supplying electricity for public consumption. There is no suggestion in the evidence that, prior to formulating the statement in Holdings' 1984 tax return purporting to describe the nature of the payments made under the Reservation Agreement in the 1984 financial year, anyone within the Isa organisation considered the payments under the Reservation Agreement as compensation for the sterilisation or fettering of capital assets of Isa or of capital assets of Holdings, viz, its shares in Isa. Rather was it a demand which Isa and Holdings were keen to meet to advance their own business purposes.

FULL AND TRUE DISCLOSURE

19. The applicant challenges the entitlement of the Commissioner to issue the amended assessments in respect of the 1984, 1985 and 1986 income years, amended to include these receipts. There is no suggestion in this case that the avoidance of tax, which the Commissioner contends resulted from the applicant's failure to make a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for assessment when it lodged its returns for each of these years, was due to fraud or evasion. It follows from the provisions of s 170(2)(b) and (3) in force at the relevant times that the Commissioner was only entitled to issue the amended assessments provided the applicant did, in fact, fail to make a full and true disclosure that resulted in an avoidance of tax, and then only if he acted to amend the assessments within six years from the respective dates upon which the tax became due and payable under each of those assessments.

20. There is a distinction between the amended assessments issued in respect of the 1984 and 1985 years on the one hand, and that for the 1986 year, on the other. The Commissioner issued the original 1984 and 1985 assessments in reliance solely on the brief information about the receipts in question that was provided by the applicant in its returns. In both of these returns, the applicant disclosed, in relation to the payments it received from the State Government under the Reservation Agreement, only the following (see Schedule 11A to the 1984 return dated 29 November 1984 and to the 1985 return dated 29 November 1985):

"Compensation from the Queensland State Government for

permanent reduction in value of Company investment in its

subsidiary Mount Isa Mines Limited resulting from the State

Government requirement that Mount Isa Mines Limited reserve

on commercially restrictive terms, portion of power

generating capacity of its Mount Isa power station, for

future allocation to the North Queensland Electricity Board.

(Non-assessable)".

21. In its 1986 return dated 28 November 1986, the only information the applicant included with respect to the relevant payment received in that financial year under the Reservation Agreement was a statement in identical terms. However, on 1 July 1986, many months before the Commissioner issued his assessment for the 1986 year, the applicant had provided the respondent with a copy of the Reservation Agreement. Yet the Commissioner proceeded to assess the applicant in respect of the payments received in the 1986 year under the Reservation Agreement on exactly the same basis as he assessed the applicant in respect of those same payments in the two preceding years, ie., he excluded those payments from the applicant's assessable income. He did not alter this opinion until he issued his amended assessments. Notwithstanding this, the Commissioner argued that the failure of Holdings to disclose the Reservation Agreement was a material non-disclosure in respect of its 1984 and 1985 income.

22. It is apparent that, until it came time for Holdings to lodge its 1984 income tax return, no one within Holdings saw the capital contribution proposal that was ultimately reflected in the Reservation Agreement as an arrangement of the kind described by Holdings in what it had to say about these payments in each of its three tax returns. The 1984 return, the first return to include any of the capital payments under the Reservation Agreement, was lodged on 29 November 1984. The evidence indicates concern on the part of certain Holdings officials in August 1984 at the taxation implications for Holdings, if the company's accounting department were to proceed with its plan to treat the payments as normal operating receipts by Holdings: the concern was that the Taxation Office might well view the payments as assessable income because they were made to Holdings in consideration for services rendered. The evidence indicates that it was probably only on the eve of lodgment of the 1984 return that a Holdings in-house lawyer and another Holdings official settled on the verbal formula describing the payments in question which appeared in the 1984 return.

23. I will assume for the moment that the description the taxpayer included in its three returns of the various payments received by it under the Reservation Agreement is one way of accurately describing the economic effect or the financial consequences of the implementation by the taxpayer of the Reservation Agreement under which it received the payments in question. But that was the only information the taxpayer disclosed to the Commissioner in respect of the payments received in the 1984 and 1985 years. It is the legal form of a transaction and the factual matrix in which it takes place that govern its taxation consequences: "It is not legitimate ... to decide liability to tax upon the basis of the substantial economic or business character of what was done" (Federal Coke Co Pty Ltd v FCT [1977] FCA 3; (1977) 34 FLR 375 at 387). The form in which a transaction is structured under which a taxpayer receives payments can affect, in a material way, the proper characterisation of those receipts for taxation purposes. In Federal Coke Co Pty Ltd v FCT, supra, Brennan J said, at 401-402:

"When a recipient of moneys provides consideration

for the payment, the consideration will ordinarily

supply the touchstone for ascertaining whether the

receipt is on revenue account or not. The

character of an asset which is sold for a price,

or the character of a cause of action discharged

by a payment will ordinarily determine, unless it

be a sham transaction, the character of the

receipt of the price or payment. The

consideration establishes the matter in respect of

which the moneys are received. The character of

the receipt may then be determined by the

character, in the recipient's hands, of the matter

in respect of which the moneys are received."

24. This passage has been applied repeatedly by courts, including the Full Court of this Court (see Allied Mills Industries v FCT [1988] FCA 357; (1989) 20 FCR 288 at 309; J B Chandler Investment Co Ltd v FCT [1993] FCA 641; (1993) 47 FCR 588 at 599 per Hill J). It is well-established that, in characterising, for taxation purposes, payments made under an agreement, the official or tribunal charged with that exercise cannot confine itself to the terms of the agreement, but must have regard to the whole of the circumstances surrounding both the execution and the operation of the agreement and the receipt of the moneys in question. But as Hill J, with whom von Doussa J agreed, observed in Chandler, supra, at 598:

"But to accept that the circumstances in which a

payment is made will be relevant to a

determination of the character of that payment in

the hands of a recipient is not to say that

surrounding circumstances can be used to

contradict the words of an agreement reached

between parties bargaining at arm's length as to

what the consideration for a particular payment is

to be, except in a case (and the present is not

such a case) where it is claimed that the

agreement is a sham and does not represent the

true intention of the parties to it."

25. There is no suggestion that the Reservation Agreement is a sham. The importance that the terms of an agreement under which payment is made has, when the question is the proper character of the payment for taxation purposes is clear. When the agreement is, as is the case here, the product of bargaining at arm's length between the parties and when, as here, it truly reflects the intention of both parties at the time the agreement was made, it is difficult to conceive of a case in which a taxpayer who does not give to the Commissioner either a true copy of the agreement or an accurate statement of the substance and legal effect of the agreement, as opposed to its economic or financial effect, will be able to avoid a charge of having failed to make a full and true disclosure with regard to the payment. It may in a particular case be necessary, in order to make full and true disclosure, for the taxpayer to disclose information to the Commissioner additional to the agreement. But provision of the contract to the Commissioner will generally be the minimum the taxpayer must do, before he can say he has made the necessary disclosure.

26. The statement in each of the three returns suggests that Holdings received the payments in question because Isa met a Government requirement to reserve part of its generating capacity for supply to NORQEB. It thus fails to disclose that Holdings bargained for what that statement calls "compensation" in return for Holdings' undertaking or promise that its subsidiary, Isa, would reserve a fixed part of its existing and planned additional generating capacity for the benefit of NORQEB. It does not therefore disclose that the payments were made to Holdings in return for Holdings promising to do something of importance to the Government and to NORQEB that was well within Holdings' power to do. To fail to disclose that the payments in question were received by Holdings as the contractual consideration for a promise by it to ensure that its subsidiary would reserve the part of its generating capacity for NORQEB is, in my opinion, a failure to disclose a fact material to the proper characterisation of the payment for taxation purposes. Further, the statement that Isa's reservation of power for NORQEB was "on commercially restrictive terms" is true only in that those terms did not make any provision for Isa to make anything by way of profit on the arrangement: it is not a true disclosure in so far as it fails to mention that the reservation of power on those terms was nevertheless considered by Holdings to be in Holdings' and Isa's commercial interests. That Isa was to supply power to NORQEB at cost was, in the circumstances, no more truly on "commercially restrictive terms" than the sale of goods below cost by a trader in order to drive out competition could be truly said to be on commercially restrictive terms. Further, the statement included in each of Holdings' returns asserts that the receipts disclosed in the return were compensation for the reduction in the value of its investment in Isa. This statement also implies that there was a relationship between the quantum of that compensation so disclosed in the return and the quantum of that reduction in the value of its investment. Holdings, however, never made any attempt to calculate what might be said to be the reduction in the value of that investment. That would not have been a straightforward task, since I think any such valuation exercise would have to make proper allowance for the benefits Holdings got from causing Isa to supply power at cost to NORQEB. No valuation of Holdings' shareholding in Isa was ever conducted, nor was any attempt made to ascertain the effect on the value of Holdings' investment in Isa of the Reservation Agreement. But it would have been pointless to do that because the payments made to Holdings were not quantified by reference to any alleged reduction in the value of Holdings' investment in Isa; the evidence I have referred to shows that they were calculated by reference to two specific factors, viz, the proportion between NORQEB's supply demand and Isa's supply capacity and the total costs to Isa of constructing its plant to 1982 and of expanding that plant. It was also untrue to make that particular assertion.

27. Holdings failed to disclose the contractual framework under which it acquired the entitlement to the payments in question here until after it had lodged its 1984 and 1985 returns. This omission, for the reasons given, involved a failure to disclose a matter vital to the proper characterisation for tax purposes of the payments received under the Reservation Agreement in those two years. It also described in all three returns the character of the payments in question in the misleading ways I have mentioned.

28. It was submitted, in reliance on W Thomas and Co Pty Ltd v The Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58 that a mis-description in a significant respect of the true nature of a transaction will not necessarily mean that the taxpayer has failed to make a true and full disclosure of facts material to the assessability of the transaction. But what saved the taxpayer there from a finding that he had failed to truly disclose the relevant information by describing a disbursement as "repairs" was his having provided, along with that description of the disbursement, information which showed that the work the taxpayer described as "repairs" was done in the same year in which the building was acquired and that it was in an amount equal to one-third of the entire cost of acquisition of the building. Windeyer J held that this additional information was such as to "inevitably" lead to the conclusion that prima facie the expenditure was of a non-deductible capital nature. It is, I think, clear that, if the taxpayer had not provided this additional information, his mis-description of the work as "repairs" would have involved a failure to make a full and true disclosure.

29. In my opinion, there was a failure by the applicant to make a full and true disclosure of a material fact necessary for the assessment of its income to tax in each of the 1984 and 1985 years constituted by its failure to disclose the Reservation Agreement. Even if there will only be a failure to make a full and true disclosure if the omission is of information that is truly material to the assessment question at issue, a proposition for which Stapleton v FCT (1989) 88 ALR 606 at 618-619 is authority, there was such an omission, at least in respect of the taxpayer's 1984 and 1985 returns here, since the Reservation Agreement is critical to the proper characterisation for tax purposes of the moneys received by Holdings. The Commissioner issued his amended assessments for those years on 26 April 1991 and 21 April 1992 respectively. He was within the six year limit provided by s 170(2)(b).

30. The failure of the Commissioner to amend his assessments for those two years promptly upon his being given the Reservation Agreement by the taxpayer in mid-1986 is, I think, nothing more than evidence that, for a time, the Commissioner was of the opinion that the belated disclosure of the Reservation Agreement did not involve disclosure of a fact material to the proper assessment of the taxpayer to tax for the 1984 and 1985 and also for the 1986 years. For the reasons later appearing, I consider that the Commissioner held, for a time, an erroneous opinion as to the true character of the receipts. But it was not suggested that the Commissioner could not lawfully change his mind later and amend all the assessments to reflect his new opinion.

31. As to the amended assessment for the 1986 year, the Commissioner issued his original assessment on 16 March 1987. The tax so assessed became payable on 30 April 1987. In terms of s 170(2)(b), in the form in which it stood as governing the Commissioner's right to amend that assessment, the Commissioner was entitled to issue an amended assessment within six years from 30 April 1987, ie, until 29 April 1993 at the latest, but only if it be shown that the taxpayer had not made full and true disclosure. The Commissioner issued his amended assessment, which included the payment of $324,096 received in the 1986 year by the taxpayer under the Reservation Agreement in the taxpayer's taxable income, on 21 April 1992.

32. The Commissioner submitted that, although well prior to issuing his assessment in respect of Holdings' 1986 return he had a copy of the Reservation Agreement, Holdings had still not made a full and true disclosure of material circumstances concerning the payments under the Reservation Agreement received in the 1986 year. On 16 March 1987, the day the 1986 assessment was issued, the Commissioner wrote to Holdings about the claim in the 1984, 1985 and also the 1986 returns as to the non-assessability of the payments received by it under the Reservation Agreement and sought a range of further information, including a copy of the bulk supply agreement between Isa and NORQEB which the Commissioner described as "the restrictive contract between the State Government and Mount Isa Mines Ltd". But counsel for the Commissioner was unable to offer any convincing explanation why any of the information sought was material to the proper characterisation of any of the payments made to Holdings under the Reservation Agreement.

33. In my opinion, although the provision of a copy of the Bulk Supply Agreement and the other information sought by the Commissioner on 16 March 1987 would have enabled the Commissioner to obtain a more precise understanding of the arrangements between Holdings and the State Government reflected in the Reservation Agreement, I think the information the Commissioner had which comprised the brief description of the payments contained in the returns for each of these three years and the copy of the Reservation Agreement, which the Commissioner got in July 1986 from Holdings, was sufficient to ensure that Holdings had made a full and true disclosure of all information material to the proper assessment of the payments under the Reservation Agreement received in the 1986 year. Cf Stapleton, supra, at 618-619. Delivery of a copy of the Reservation Agreement was a sufficient disclosure that the payments made under it by the Government to Holdings were in respect of certain of the capital costs incurred by Isa in relation to its generating plant and not for the suggested reduction in the value of Holdings' involvement in Isa. Although Holdings never disclosed that the cost-recoupment tariffs chargeable by Isa to NORQEB for supply of power to NORQEB could not properly be said to have involved "commercially disadvantageous terms", so far as either Holdings or Isa were concerned, I do not think knowledge of the true position in this regard has any material relevance to the proper characterisation of the payments received by Holdings under the Reservation Agreement. In those circumstances, s 170(3), as it stood at the relevant time, applies, restricting the Commissioner's power to amend to three years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable. Accordingly, the Commissioner was not entitled to amend the 1986 assessment as late as he did on 21 April 1992.

WERE THE PAYMENTS UNDER THE RESERVATION AGREEMENT INCOME?

34. Having concluded that the Commissioner was entitled to amend the 1984 and 1985 assessments, it remains to determine whether the payments received by Holdings in those two years were properly characterised by the Commissioner as income within ordinary concepts.

35. The applicant contends that the payments were in relation to and in consideration of, a dealing by it with a capital asset (namely its wholly owned subsidiary, Isa) in a way which adversely affected the value of that capital asset, and which furthermore concerned the capital assets of Isa (namely its electricity generating facilities). The Commissioner maintains that the payments are properly characterised as income and advances a number of alternative bases for that characterisation.

36. In my opinion, having regard to the Reservation Agreement in the setting of the circumstances surrounding the making of that Agreement to which I have referred, I consider that the payments received in each of the three years in question by Holdings under that Agreement were income within the ordinary concept of income, ie. income within s 25(1) of the Act. Holdings chose, for its own commercial purposes, to structure its arrangements with the SEC and NORQEB in the form of two contracts. The Reservation Agreement between it and the SEC (or rather the State Government) shows that Holdings received the moneys in question in return for an ongoing promise to ensure that Isa would do something: the Agreement gave Holdings the right to payment of the moneys in question in return for promising that its wholly owned subsidiary, which was bound to do its bidding, would keep a defined part of its electricity generating capacity available to meet NORQEB's demands throughout the whole of a period of indefinite duration, until the obligation so assumed by Holdings was brought to an end by mutual agreement or by the expiry of the seven year period of the notice that each party was entitled to give by clause (7) of the Agreement of its intention to terminate it. The payments were therefore income in Holdings' hands, within the ordinary concept of income: see J B Chandler Investment Co Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation, supra, at 599. They were payments made as consideration for Holdings promising, in a legally enforceable way, to ensure that Isa would, continuously over a period of at least seven years, meet NORQEB's supply demands up to the agreed maximum quantum of power. It was a promise that Holdings was well able to keep and so was a promise of real value to the State Government, well worth the payments the Government made in return for it. It therefore does not matter that, apart from approving Isa's expansion of its generating capacity and apart from approving entry by Isa into the Bulk Supply Agreement, Holdings may not have had to take any action to ensure that its promise contained in the Reservation Agreement was kept: cf J B Chandler Investment Co Ltd v FCT (1993) 93 ATC 4810 at 4819-4820. The service provided by Holdings was also provided as an incident of and in the course of carrying on its business: the consideration paid for that service is, for that reason also, income within the ordinary concept.

37. Senior counsel for the applicant presented a detailed argument in oral submissions supplemented by written submissions that ranged over many issues and involved reference to many authorities. In my opinion, however, this is a case in which the contract under which the moneys in question were paid to the applicant is decisive of the characterisation of those moneys in the hands of the applicant for taxation purposes. There is nothing in the surrounding circumstances which it is relevant to take into account for the purpose of this characterisation exercise that contradicts the conclusion flowing from the contract that the receipts were income in the applicant's hands and there is much in those surrounding circumstances which confirms this conclusion. The Reservation Agreement under which the moneys in question were paid to Holdings was a contract which:

(1) accurately reflected the arrangements made

between Holdings, the SEC and NORQEB arrived at

after protracted arm's length negotiations;

(2) was a legally effective means of ensuring

that NORQEB would get the power it needed from Isa

on terms that would enable NORQEB to charge a

tariff to consumers acceptable to NORQEB, the SEC

(and the Government) and which were also terms

acceptable to Holdings and Isa;

(3) was in the form which Holdings insisted upon;

(4) was apt to achieve all Holdings' business

objectives, as they were perceived by Holdings at

the time the contract was negotiated.

38. I do not think it is factually correct to say, as senior counsel for the applicant did, that the moneys were paid to induce Holdings to cause Isa to set aside, for the benefit of NORQEB, part of Isa's generating capacity, ie, to induce Holdings to place a fetter or restriction on the productive capacity of Isa's plant:

(1) It was an essential part of the arrangement that Holdings would not only ensure that Isa kept part of its existing generating capacity available for NORQEB's benefit, but that Isa would undertake an expansion of that existing capacity which was in part necessary to meet NORQEB's needs, but which expansion was not necessary to meet Isa's own increasing needs for power in the future: a smaller expansion than 30 to 33 MW of additional capacity would have been enough to meet Isa's own future needs. Holdings, however, regarded it as in its own commercial interests for Isa to expand its plant beyond Isa's own needs so that Isa could supply NORQEB's demands at cost.

(2) The moneys paid to Holdings under the Reservation Agreement were not calculated by reference to the diminution in value of Holdings' investment in Isa or by reference to the diminution in the worth to Isa of its power generating capacity that might be said to have resulted from the Agreement. In its negotiations with the SEC, Holdings was not, as a matter of fact, concerned at the prospect of the arrangements resulting in a diminution of the value of its investment in Isa or as fettering Isa's capacities. Instead, the parties quite explicitly calculated the $10.605M payment to Holdings provided for by the Reservation Agreement by reference to what they agreed was a fair contribution to their estimate of the costs to Isa of constructing the additional capacity necessary to meet both Isa and NORQEB's total needs to the end of the 1993/1994 year; the other major component of the moneys payable to Holdings under the Reservation Agreement, $5.265M, was intended by the SEC to represent, and was accepted by Holdings as representing, a fair estimate of the benefit the public power authorities would continue to get from the capital expenditure MIM had made in developing its power generating capacity that existed in 1982, prior to the planned expansion.

39. It is difficult to accept that the way the arrangement was described by Holdings in its three tax returns and which I have said could, at most, amount to a description of the economic or financial effect, as opposed to the legal effect, of the arrangement is, in any event, accurate. Holdings relied on the evidence of Mr Curtis, an investment banker, to make good its claim that the payments it received under the Reservation Agreement were on capital account. Mr Curtis' theory was that, by entering into an agreement to reserve part of the output of Isa's power plant at the SEC's direction on commercially disadvantageous terms, Isa, as the owner of the plant, suffered a loss in value of the plant as a going concern and, since Isa did not receive any compensatory payment for that reservation, there was a corresponding diminution in the value of Isa and thus in the value of the shares of Isa held by Holdings. It seems to me Mr Curtis' theory is erroneous in so far as it ignores the fact, established by the evidence, that the decision by Holdings to reserve part of Isa's generating capacity for the SEC on apparently commercially disadvantageous terms, ie, on terms providing for cost recoupment only, was deliberately taken by Holdings because that was considered by Holdings to be in Holdings' long term commercial interests. Mr Curtis also ignores the fact, which I think serves to emphasise that Holdings saw the arrangement as commercially advantageous to it, rather than commercially disadvantageous, that Holdings was prepared to increase Isa's generating capacity beyond Isa's own needs to put Isa in the position of being able to deliver power at cost to NORQEB. I do not attach any weight to Mr Curtis' views in the absence of a satisfactory explanation why these considerations, suggesting that the arrangement was commercially advantageous to Holdings (and Isa), should be discounted.

40. Nothing turns on the fact that the payments are described in the Reservation Agreement as "a non refundable capital contribution". The expression originated with Mr Galwey, who identified the possibility of the SEC paying to the MIM Group the sort of payment it itself generally required when the SEC provided a bulk power supply to a new project. It accurately describes the character of the payment, from the SEC's point of view. It also accurately describes the payments as having been paid to Holdings as a part contribution to the total capital costs Isa had already incurred in establishing that portion of its existing plant and the capital costs Isa would incur in expanding that portion of its plant capacity which Isa needed to be able to supply power surplus to its own needs to NORQEB. But that does not affect the income character of those payments in Holdings' hands.

41. It is unnecessary to consider the other bases on which the Commissioner relied to show that the payments in question were assessable in Holdings' hands as income.

42. The Commissioner was entitled to issue the amended assessments for the 1984 and the 1985 years. The payments made to Holdings under the Reservation Agreement in each of those years were properly included in Holdings' assessable income. Appeals No QG 35 and QG 36 of 1994 will therefore be dismissed with costs. It was too late for the Commissioner to issue his amended assessment for the 1986 year to include the payment Holdings then received under that Agreement. Appeal No QG 34 of 1994 will therefore be allowed with costs.
