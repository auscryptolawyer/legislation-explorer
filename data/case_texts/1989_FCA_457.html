ORDER

The appeal be dismissed with costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The appellant taxpayer, Robert J. Bunting, appeals from a decision of a single judge of this Court allowing an appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the tribunal") in the following circumstances.

The background facts

2. The background facts, as found by the trial judge, are as follows: The appellant, born in the United Kingdom in 1949, was employed in that country for about 10 years as a computer programmer and a computer systems analyst; he migrated to Australia on 27 March 1980; upon arrival in Australia he sought employment in the computer industry. In early April 1980, he had discussions with representatives of Computations Pty Limited; they said that their company was looking for a consultant to take over a project to develop a specific programme for it; it was suggested that, if the appellant were to be involved, he may need to perform the task through the agency of a company with limited liability. (It appears that it is common in the computer industry for the services of computer specialists to be required only for the duration of particular projects and for those services to be provided through companies which may be controlled by the computer specialists.) Computations required a specific programme to be developed for one of its clients, a finance company. (The project was described as the "Fin-Co" project.) Computations suggested that the appellant speak to a solicitor to discuss the formation of an appropriate corporate structure. The appellant accepted his solicitor's advice to adopt the structure described below.

(1) The incorporation of a company, Manting Pty Limited, ("Manting") was effected on 9 April 1980; its objects included the establishment and support of trusts for the benefit of dependants of employees or directors of the company; the two subscribers to Manting's memorandum of Association were the appellant's solicitor and a friend of the appellant, Mr Brown; Manting issued two shares in its capital, one of which was held by the appellant's wife and the other by Mr Brown; Mr Brown's share was held upon trust for the appellant's wife.

(2) A deed of trust dated 14 April 1980 was executed between a Mr O'Reilly as Settlor and Manting as trustee; as a discretionary trust, "eligible beneficiaries" were the appellant's wife, the appellant's children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren, spouses of the appellant's children and any future wife or wives of the appellant and such other persons as Manting might appoint; the class of eligible beneficiaries excluded the appellant himself; the power of removal and appointment of trustees was vested in the appellant's wife.

(3) The only activities carried on by Manting were as trustee of the Bunting family trust; Manting contracted with Computations to make the appellant's services available as a computer consultant to work on the Fin-co project as from 14 April 1980; from that date, the appellant became an employee of Manting, although the terms of his employment were not formally documented; at a meeting of the directors of Manting held on 14 April 1980, it was resolved that Manting "establish itself in the computer industry by the employment of Mr R.J. Bunting, an expert in the field of computer systems design and implementation. Mr Bunting having consented to be so employed, it was agreed that he be paid a salary of $15,000 per annum until otherwise resolved by the company." The minutes of that meeting also record that the appellant advised that Computations had expressed to him an interest in engaging the company as a consultant to its operations; and that it was resolved that the appellant write to Computations "setting out the services that the company was capable of providing and the applicable rates of charge." It was further resolved that "the Company having employed Mr Bunting all attempts should be made to procure such further computer consultancy business as may be available" and that "consideration be given to the employment of such additional persons as may be required to satisfy any additional assignments that become available to the company."

The appellant completed the Fin-Co project; the contract between Computations and Manting then came to an end; the appellant continued in the employment of Manting, since it was able to arrange for his services to be used in another computer project; Manting arranged with a company, Parity People Pty Limited, to make the appellant's services available, and, in turn, Parity People made his services available to a government authority; this arrangement continued until the project was finalised; Manting was remunerated, at first by Computations and, later, by Parity People, on the basis of an hourly rate for hours worked; the rates increased from time to time; Manting, in turn, paid a salary to the appellant.

(4) On 29 May 1981 Manting established a superannuation scheme for the benefit of its only employee, the appellant.

3. The profit and loss statement of the Manting Trust for the year ended 30 June 1981 disclosed total income of $28,324 in the form of consultancy fees of $28,259 and interest of $65; expenditure incurred by the Trust consisted of accountancy fees, bank fees, electricity, entertainment, insurance, legal costs, certain motor vehicle expenses and licences and lodgment fees, stationery, printing, postage, salaries and wages, superannuation, technical books and papers, telephone and depreciation; total expenditure was $21,080, made up mainly of the salary paid to the appellant ($15,194) and superannuation ($3,525) paid on his account; profit and loss statements for the other years in question in the proceeding (years ended 30 June 1980, 1982 and 1983) revealed similar results.

The assessment

5. The Commissioner assessed the appellant pursuant to s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 treating him as the recipient of the income which had been returned by the Trust, but allowing the appellant, as deductions, the deductions claimed by the Trust itself, excluding salaries and superannuation.

The decision of the Tribunal

6. The Tribunal held that there was no scope on the facts of the case for the application of s.260. In reaching that conclusion the Tribunal distinguished the reasoning of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1986) 160 CLR 55 and of the Full Court of this Court in Tupicoff v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 4 FCR 505. The Tribunal held that there was no transction or situation which preceded the derivation of income by the Trust upon which s.260 could operate. The Tribunal held that, although s.260 may operate when an existing stream of income is "rechannelled", it says nothing about how a new stream of income should flow and that in this case there was no existing stream of income which could be or was "rechannelled".

7. The Tribunal said:

"But whatever may have been said in the cases of Gulland

et al and Tupicoff, the same cannot be said in this case.

Prior to the commencement of this arrangement, there was

no 'stream of income'. Like the draftsman in Case T4

(86 ATC 123), before the arrangement was set in place,

the (appellant) was unemployed. By accepting employment

with (Manting) the (appellant) was seeking to increase

his income, accepting with it a liability which he had

not previously had: an obligation to pay income tax.

Unlike the doctors (in Gulland) and Mr Tupicoff he was

not seeking to reduce his personal tax contributions

to the Revenue. It is true that by establishing the

company and electing to work for it rather than working

on his own account he was choosing to have some of the

profit from his labour remain in the hands of the company

with the consequence that his own liability to pay tax

would be less than it might have been. But that was a

course which he was entitled to follow: accepting a

lower income for himself and with it a lesser liability

in tax than might have been."

8. The Tribunal varied the assessments accordingly.

The decision of the primary judge

9. The primary judge reasoned as follows:

(1) Subject to the "choice" principle, s.260 will

operate on arrangements which are implemented in a

particular way for the purpose or effect of avoiding tax.

(2) For s.260 to apply, the purpose or effect of the

arrangement must be seen from its terms or the acts by

which it was implemented. (3) The avoidance of tax

need not be the sole purpose of the arrangement, it is

enough if tax avoidance is one of the main purposes of

the arrangement in the sense that it is not inessential

or merely incidental. (4) There could be no business

or family purpose served by the arrangement in question

other than to provide the appellant with superannuation

benefits and to adopt a corporate vehicle for provision

of his services, as appears to have been a common

practice in the computer industry. However, the

structure which was created was more than was necessary

to provide those benefits. No business or family

purpose can be found for the creation of the family

trust. The trust is a discretionary trust from which

the appellant is expressly excluded as a beneficiary.

This provides a strong indication that the primary

purpose of the transaction was to "split" the appellant's

income between himself and members of his family.

(5) Looking at the arrangement as a whole, its purpose

and effect was to alter the incidence of income tax and

relieve the appellant from a liability to pay the tax

which would otherwise have been imposed upon him.

(6) The "choice" principle does not apply to a case

like the present where the so-called "choice" is as to

the manner in which the appellant conducted his income

earning activity, the choice being to work as an employee

of the trustee company (Manting) rather than for the

computer agency company. (7) The critical question

here is whether there was an "antecedent transaction or

situation", because s.260 does not apply where there was

no "antecedent transaction or situation" whereby income

was derived. This principle is based on the ground that

the operation of s.260 has been said to be dependent upon

an alteration in the incidence of tax or in some existing

liability to pay tax. The cases do not draw precise

distinctions between antecedent "transactions" on the one

hand and "situations" on the other. Yet, there is nothing

in s.260 that supports the view that that section can

apply only when there has been an antecedent transaction

between parties. An arrangement will, for example, be

within the section if it alters the incidence of income

tax in a case in which the only relevant antecedent

circumstance is that the taxpayer is in receipt of income.

(8) Although the appellant derived no income in Australia

until after he migrated here, he commenced working in this

country less than three weeks after his arrival. He must

have taken steps to put the income splitting structure in

place almost as soon as he arrived. In the United Kingdom

he had been employed to do the same kind of work that he

did here. There was no antecedent "transaction", but

there was an antecedent "situation" consisting of the

appellant's practice of his work in the computer industry

as or akin to a computer consultant and as an employee.

He was in receipt of income; and the fact that it was

in the United Kingdom, not Australia, though relevant, is

not a circumstance which deprives the facts of their

character as an antecedent "situation" for the purposes

of s.260. The arrangement changed that "situation" and

did so for the purpose of alterting the incidence of

income tax. The change did not give rise to a new source

of income. It was the appellant's services before and

after the arrangement that produced the income albeit

through the intervention of a corporate structure under

the arrangement. (9) Section 260 annihilates Manting

and the Trust. What is left exposed is the fact that the

appellant by his exertions earned the gross income

attributed to the Trust giving him the net income on which

the Commissioner assessed, i.e. after allowing the

deductions claimed by the Trust excluding salaries and

superannuation payments.

10. The judge allowed the appeal and set aside the decision of the Tribunal.

The appellant's challenge to the judge's reasoning

11. On behalf of the appellant, it is submitted that his Honour erred in holding that the earnings of foreign source income by a non-resident could be regarded as a relevant antecedent situation; and that if the appellant had never before been liable for Australian income tax, it is difficult to conclude that there has been an avoidance of tax. It is further contended, for the appellant, that because s.260 can only annihilate rather that reconstruct, the provision can operate, if at all, to destroy the contract, agreement or arrangement in question, so that what must be left exposed is an antecedent "transaction" or "situation" which is chargeable to tax; but, if no such "transction" or "situation" is found to exist, then it is not open to the Commissioner, or the Court, to construct a fictional arrangement and charge it with tax. Thus, it is said, it was not open to substitute the appellant for Manting as a party to a fictional contract for services, when, in fact, no such contract had existed between the appellant and any other person.

The authorities dealing with antecedent "situation"

12. It is clear from the authorities that s.260 can apply if there is a relevant antecedent "situation". However, what is meant by this phrase has not yet been the subject of any authoritative decision.

13. In Gulland's Case. Gibbs C.J. said (at p 73):

"...there is nothing in s.260 that supports the view

that that section can apply only when there has been an

antecedent transaction between parties. An arrangement

will, for example, be within the section if it alters

the incidence of income tax in a case in which the only

relevant antecedent circumstance is that the taxpayer

is in receipt of income...

Clearly, in the case of Dr Gulland, when the impugned

arrangement is annihilated, what is left is the

situation in which the taxpayer is in receipt of the

income from his practice. In the case of Mr Watson,

Peate v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation...provides

the necessary guidance as to the effect of the

annihilation. Whether one takes the approach of the

Judicial Committee or that of the High Court (in

Peate)...the result is that the income received by the

unit trust should be treated as the income of the five

doctors."

14. Wilson J. agreed with Gibbs C.J. and also with Dawson J. (at p 77).

15. Brennan J. agreed with Dawson J. (at p 77), and, in the course of making some additional observations, said (at p 81):

"The situation that is left after s.260 has done its

work is necessarily a hypothetical one having no

existence in law or fact: it is the situation that

would have existed had the arrangement not produced

the specified effect. The difference between the

situation which does exist in law and in fact and the

hypothetical situation is the 'effect' which attracts

the operation of the section. Section 260 does not

expressly prescribe hat hypothesis is to be adopted

for the purposes of the Act once an arrangement is

avoided. One of the difficulties in the application of

s.260 is ascertaining the hypothetical facts which, as

between the taxpayer and the Commissioner must be taken

to be the facts on which the determination of the

taxpayer's taxable income depends."

16. Dawson J. said (at p 111):

"...the authorities draw a distinction between an

arrangement which modifies an antecedent transaction or

situation and one which merely orders a taxpayer's

affairs in relation to income from a new source or in

relation to a deduction from which the previous

transaction or situation did not preclude him. In the

former case, but not the latter, s.260 may have an

application for there may be an alteration of the

incidence of income tax, etc.: see Mullens' Case...per

Barwick C.J. and Cridland's Case...per Mason J. But

the distinction can be of no assistance in this case

where, if there was no antecedent transaction, there

was an antecedent situation consisting of the

taxpayer's practice of his profession in a particular

manner. The arrangement changed that situation and did

so in order to alter the incidence of income tax. The

change, however, gave rise to no new source of income

nor did it give rise to any deduction from which the

very nature of the previous situation did not preclude

the taxpayer: cf. Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v. Inland

Revenue Commissioner..."

17. Later, dealing with another doctor, Dawson J. said (at p 125):

"The new arrangement did not produce a new source of

income in the sense in which the cases use that term.

Dr Pincus's source of income whilst he was a sole

practitioner, whilst he was practising in partnership,

and now under the substituted arrangement, has been,

and is, the same, namely the practice of his profession

as a doctor. The form of arrangement under which he

has practised from time to time has not altered the

source of his income in any relevant sense. The source

of his income tax has been his professional activities

as a medical practitioner whether he has been a sole

practitioner, a partner or an employee. It is from

those activities that his income has derived and his

liability to tax has arisen."

18. In Daniels v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4830, in circumstances similar to the present case, Sweeney J., following the decision of the primary judge in this matter, held that there was a relevant antecedent "transaction" or "situation" with the consequence that s.260 applied (cf. Baker v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 89 ATC 4660).

Was there an antecedent "situation" here?

19. In my opinion, it was open to the primary judge to hold that a relevant antecedent "situation" did exist in the present case.

20. The term "situation" does not, of course, appear in the legislation. It is not a term of art. Since the phrase has no technical meaning, it was essentially a question of fact for the judge to determine whether the taxpayer's position before entry into the arrangement relied on by the Commissioner should be described as an antecedent "situation" for present purposes. Before entry into the arrangement, the taxpayer was capable of earning, and had in fact earned, income from the application of his personal skills. In my view, the judge was entitled to treat these circumstances as a relevant antecedent "situation". I agree with the judge that nothing here turns on the fact that the taxpayer's previous personal income had been earned overseas.

21. In my opinion, the present case is indistinguishable, in principle, from Tupicoff's Case.

If s.260 is applicable here, what is its operation?

22. The appellant's submission that the Commissioner here embarked upon an illegitimate exercise of a reconstruction by constructing a fictional contract for services must, I think, be rejected. As Brennan J. pointed out in Gulland's Case (at p 81), in the passage cited above, the exercise is necessarily a hypothetical one. I agree with the primary judge that, once s.260 annihilated Manting and the Trust, what was left exposed was the fact that the appellant, by his exertions, earned the gross income attributed to the Trust, giving him the net income assessed, that is, after allowing the deductions claimed by the Trust, excluding salaried and superannuation payments.

Orders proposed

23. I would propose that the appeal be dismissed, with costs.

The appellant ("the taxpayer") appeals against a decision of a Judge of this Court (Lockhart J.) in which his Honour set aside four decisions in favour of the respondent ("the Commissioner") given by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (Taxation Appeals Division) ("the Tribunal").

2. The Tribunal had upheld the objections of the taxpayer to decisions of the Commissioner upon objections by the taxpayer to assessments issued pursuant to the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") for the years of income ended 30 June 1980, 30 June 1981, 30 June 1982 and 30 June 1983. The Tribunal had reduced the taxable income of the taxpayer in respect of each of these years and on this appeal the taxpayer, in effect, seeks to regain the position lost before Lockhart J.

3. The taxpayer submits that Lockhart J. erred in the application of s. 260 of the Act in respect of each of the four years of income. His Honour found that an arrangement had been made or entered into with the purpose and effect of altering the incidence of income tax and avoiding a liability to pay the tax which otherwise would have been imposed on the taxpayer, within the meaning of paras. (a) and (c) of sub-s. 260 (1). Although his Honour did not in terms refer to paras. (a) and (c), that plainly is the meaning of the words he used.

4. I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment of Beaumont J. The primary facts are set out in Beaumont J.'s judgment under the heading "The Background Facts" and I do not repeat what is there said. Like his Honour, I have come to the conclusion that the appeal should be dismissed. I endeavour to express shortly my reasons for reaching that conclusion.

5. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55 at 67-68, Gibbs C.J. reviewed the authorities and affirmed his view that if tax avoidance is one of the main purposes of the arrangement in a sense that it is not inessential or merely incidental, that is enough; see also Dawson J. supra at 105. Looking at the present case, the question is, to adapt the words of Kitto J. in Peate v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1964) 111 CLR 443 at 469, whether the overt acts that were done in utilisation of the corporate and trust arrangements established by the taxpayer are fairly explicable without an inference being drawn that tax avoidance was a purpose of those arrangements as a whole.

6. The trustee was paid first by Computation Pty. Limited and later by Parity People Pty. Limited at an hourly rate for the hours worked by the taxpayer. The trustee paid a "salary" to the taxpayer and made contributions to a superannuation fund for the benefit of the taxpayer. The Commissioner assessed the taxpayer on the footing that he received the gross income returned by the trustee but allowing him the deductions claimed by the trustee other than the "salary" paid the taxpayer by the trustee and the superannuation fund contribution. The shares in the trustee were themselves held in trust for the taxpayer and his wife. The trustee was trustee of a discretionary trust. The taxpayer was excluded from the class of "eligible beneficiaries". Those comprised in that class included the taxpayer's wife and their children.

7. One advantage which the taxpayer had believed would accrue from the taking of these steps was the limitation upon his personal liability; another, he said in evidence, was the payment of less income tax. Whilst the "subjective" purpose of the taxpayer is not essential, if it is disclosed in this way, it cannot be irrelevant to a decision as to whether there was an arrangement within the meaning of sub-s. 260 (1).

8. I agree that what was done is not fairly explicable without an inference being drawn that a purpose, if not the primary purpose, was to effect a "split" of the income of the taxpayer between himself and the members of his family. In particular, the introduction of the discretionary trust, with the exclusion of the taxpayer from the class of "eligible beneficiaries", provides a strong indication that a purpose underlying entry into the arrangement was that of "splitting" the income derived from the personal exertion of the taxpayer; cf. Tupicoff v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 4 FCR 505 at 521.

9. The taxpayer, it is true, derived no income from a source in Australia before his migration to this country. On the other hand, in the United Kingdom he had been employed for about a decade in the same kind of occupation as that which he pursued in this country and the taxpayer must have taken steps very soon after his arrival to put in train the making of the corporate and trust arrangements in question.

10. Sub-section 260 (1) of the Act seeks, in paras. (a) and (c), inter alia, arrangements which directly or indirectly alter the incidence of any income tax, or, defeat, evade or avoid "any duty or liability imposed on any person" by the Act. Section 17 relevantly levied income tax upon the taxable income derived by the taxpayer during each of the years of income in question. The taxable income resulted from the application of the provisions of the Act to the assessable income of the taxpayer, being in this case the whole of his income (not exempted by the Act) which he derived during the year of income in question from any source, within or beyond Australia: s. 25. The central significance to the scheme of the legislation of the concept of derivation of income year by year was stressed by Barwick C.J. in Henderson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612 at 647-648. It was said of s. 17 and related provisions by Gibbs J. in Re Mendonca; Ex parte Commissioner of Taxation (1969) 15 FLR 256 at 259 that:

"(T)he liability to income tax is imposed

by the statute itself and . . . assess-

ment is only a method of ascertaining the

extent of the liability, so that the tax

is a debt due and owing, although not

payable, notwithstanding that no

assessment has been made."

11. It has been settled, since the decision of the Privy Council in Newton v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia (1958) AC 450, that for the purposes of sub-para. (c) of sub-s. 260 (1) of the Act, a taxpayer may make or enter into an arrangement which directly or indirectly avoids a liability imposed upon him by the Act if the effect of the arrangement is to preclude the occasion for the liability arising; "avoid" does not mean "displace". In that sense, how can it be to the point to say that the section does not apply to the income derived by the personal exertion of a taxpayer unless he is engaged over a period in some form of activity from which he has derived income and in respect of a further year of income the arrangement would divert the liability that otherwise would fall upon him? Their Lordships approved, (1958) AC at 464, what had been said by Taylor J. in the High Court[1957] HCA 99; , 96 CLR 577 at 665:

"(S)ince it is clear that the real work

of the section is intended to be done in

cases where the disputed item of income

has not in fact or law been derived by a

taxpayer, the section must be taken to

contemplate that, even before income has

been derived, a taxpayer may, by a

legally effective contract, agreement or

arrangement, avoid a liability to income

tax on future income. But, as already

appears, it is a condition precedent to

the liability of a taxpayer that he shall

derive income and it is difficult to

understand how, except in a loose sense,

a person can be said to avoid liability

to tax by putting himself in a position

where he will, neither in fact nor in

law, derive future income. Nevertheless,

in an attempt to give some intelligible

meaning to the section the view has been

taken that there may be, on the part of a

taxpayer, an avoidance of liability to

tax, within the meaning of the section,

in respect of income before that income

has been derived. In Bell's Case (1953)

[1952] HCA 34; 87 CLR 548 at 573-574 it was said:

'Section 260 (c) postulates a duty

or a liability imposed on a person

by the Act, but this refers, not to

a liability to pay a particular

amount of tax (which would be a

liability imposed by a taxing Act),

but to a liability such as s. 17 of

the Act imposed on Bell, to pay tax

in respect of his taxable income

ascertained by including in his

assessable income his proportion of

the Papuan company's profits if and

when he should participate in a

distribution of them.'"

12. Once this is appreciated, it becomes all the more difficult to find a basis in the Australian legislation for the suggested "doctrine" that, as it was put by Lord Diplock, speaking for the majority of their Lordships:

(T)he description of the contracts,

agreements and arrangements which are

liable to avoidance presupposes the

continued receipt by the taxpayer of

income from an existing source in respect

of which his liability to pay tax would

be altered or relieved if legal effect

were given to the contract, agreement or

arrangement sought to be avoided as

against the commissioner. The section

does not strike at new sources of income

or restrict the right of the taxpayer to

arrange his affairs in relation to income

from a new source in such a way as to

attract the least possible liability to

tax. Nor does it prevent the taxpayer

from parting with a source of income."

See Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd. v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (NZ) (1976) 1 NZLR 546 at 556. The concept of "source" is one which is of great importance to the operation of s. 25 of the Act, but it is there used as a geographical discrimen. For myself, I find it difficult to see how what was said by Lord Diplock (when dealing with the New Zealand legislation) derives support from the terms of s. 260. That provision, on its face, applies to each year of income (there are four years involved in this case) and asks in respect of each year whether there has been a contract, agreement or arrangement, made or entered into (one should note) at any time, which has the purpose or effect described. There is, on the face of the section, no necessity for there to be any derivation of income at all before the arrangement is made or entered into. The question will be whether in respect of the given year of income the arrangement has the purpose or effect which attracts the "annihilating" operation of the section.

13. In any event, like Beaumont J., I agree with the learned primary judge that there was involved in the present case more than the ordering of the taxpayer's affairs in relation to income from a new source, to use the words of Dawson J. in Gulland's Case (supra) at 111.

14. It would, I think, be going beyond the present authorities to treat any discussion therein of "new source" of income as intended to have any special operation where, in a case such as the present, what is involved is income derived from the personal exertion of the taxpayer by the exercise of his skills in a particular field. The taxpayer's exertions in the past had taken place outside Australia and the first exercise of his skills in Australia occurred in circumstances where the income derived therefrom, but for the operation of s. 260 of the Act, would have been diverted successfully from the taxpayer's hands. What was said by the majority of their Lordships in the Europa Case and in the later Australian decisions in which the subject is discussed, was not directed to a situation where, whilst there was a geographic dislocation, there was otherwise a continuity in the personal exertion of the particular taxpayer. I agree that nothing relevantly turns on the circumstance that the taxpayer previously had exercised his skills outside Australia.

15. Reliance also was placed by counsel for the taxpayer upon what was described as the "choice" principle, as a means of fending off what otherwise would be the applicability of s. 260 of the Act. A similar argument was unsuccessful before this Court in Tupicoff's Case (supra). In the course of his judgment, Fisher J. said (4 FCR 505 at 509):

"The taxpayer also contended that the Act

gave him a choice as to the manner in

which he conducted his business

operations and that his decision to work

as an employee of the trustee company was

an exercise of that choice. In so doing

he said he was choosing between the

alternative arrangements open to him

under Div 5 (Partnerships) Div 6 (Trust

Income) and Div 7 (Private Companies) of

the Act. In my opinion the principles

enunciated by Mason J in Cridland's case

((1977) [1977] HCA 61; 140 CLR 330 at 339) have no

application to such choices. As Bowen CJ

said when discussing a like contention in

Gulland's case, 'the taxpayer did not

merely "create a situation by entering

into a transaction" to attract particular

tax consequences'. The Chief Judge went

on to make comments which can well be

applied in this matter and which I adopt.

He said ((1984) [1984] FCA 216; 3 FCR 354 at 357-358):

'The arrangement here goes beyond

mere entry into a transaction such

as a university student buying units

in a trust. Here, with a purpose of

altering the incidence of taxation

in which his income from his

practice was taxable in his hands,

the taxpayer set about creating an

entirely different situation whereby

by means of a number of agreements

entered into by a trust which he set

up for this purpose the income could

eventually end up in the hands of a

family trust rather than his own.'

In my opinion the trial judge correctly

found that the choice principle did not

assist the taxpayer to avoid the

application of s 260."

I should add that the judgment of Bowen C.J. in Gulland's Case was a dissenting judgment, but that the appeal to the High Court was allowed. In the High Court, Gibbs C.J., after discussing the decisions dealing with the "choice" principle and with the argument that the taxpayers in Gulland's Case had done nothing more than adopt the course available under the Act of creating trusts, the income of which would be taxed in accordance with the provisions of Div. 6 of Part 3, said (160 CLR at 70):

"That argument (which could not be

accepted consistently with Peate v

Federal Commissioner of Taxation) fails

because it is simply not right to say

that the Act allows a taxpayer the

opportunity to have his own income from

personal exertion taxed as though it were

income derived by a trust and held for

the benefit of a number of beneficiaries."

Wilson J. (at 77) said that he agreed in substance with the reasons advanced, inter alia, by the Chief Justice. Brennan J. said (at 79):

"The choice principle gives expression to

the scope of s. 260 ascertained by

reading down the words of the section in

order to allow other and specific

provisions of the Act to have their

intended operation. The choice principle

is inapplicable when the arrangement does

not attract a tax benefit conferred by a

specific provision but is an arrangement

which, as in the present cases, seeks to

divert income from one taxpayer to

another. Having ascertained the scope of

s. 260 it is necessary to consider what

is the condition of its operation on

cases which do not depend on a specific

provision of the Act."

His Honour concluded that consideration by saying (at 81):

"The choice principle should not be

applied when there is no occasion to

invoke the rule generalia specialibus non

derogant on which it is founded. If the

choice principle were applied to deny the

operation of s. 260 on arrangements which

do not depend on a specific provision of

the Act, the choice principle would

annihilate s. 260 itself. The true

reconciliation between the choice

principle and the Newton test of purpose

is to limit the former to cases depending

upon a specific provision of the Act. It

is not necessary now to consider the

boundary between the two classes, but in

principle only those arrangements or

parts of arrangements which depend upon

specific provisions fall outside the

scope of s. 260."

16. In my view, whatever be the true scope of any "choice" principle, it has no application in the present case.

17. It remains to consider the proposition that even if s. 260 were applied to the arrangement in question, there would be left no footing upon which to support the assessments made by the Commissioner against the taxpayer for the years of income in question. In John v Commissioner of Taxation [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 63 ALJR 166 at 171, Mason C.J., Wilson, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ, in the course of explaining why, in the instant case, the Commissioner's reliance upon s. 260 involved a hypothetical reconstruction of certain transactions, said:

"(Section) 260 effects a fictitious

annihilation of contracts, agreements and

arrangements. It does not proceed to

substitute an alternative basis on which

tax should be calculated. Of course, in

some cases the annihilation of a legal

form will itself reveal a basis for the

calculation of tax. Federal Commissioner

of Taxation v Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55

was such a case. There the annihilation

of the arrangements in question revealed

the source of income as the personal

exertions of the taxpayer respondents in

the same form as had existed prior to the

arrangements which were held to offend s.260."

18. Nevertheless, there may well be grounds, as Hill J. pointed out in Davis v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 89 ATC 4377 at 4402, for treating Peate v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1966] UKPCHCA 1; (1964) 111 CLR 443; (1966) 116 CLR 38, and perhaps Gulland's Case, as decisions where in some sense there were elements of "reconstruction"; something not done may have been treated as if it had been done.

19. In Newton's Case itself (1958) AC 450 at 468, their Lordships said:

"Now the Commissioner can trace the sum

of 1,661,722 pounds in cash actually into the

hands of the original shareholders. He

is entitled, therefore, to treat it as

income derived by them. He cannot trace

the balance of 102,414 pounds actually into

their hands. It remained in the pocket

of Pactolus Limited. It was ostensibly

the profit of Pactolus on buying the

shares. But when the transfer is

ignored, that profit is seen to be

nothing more nor less than remuneration

which the original shareholders allowed

Pactolus to retain for services rendered.

The position is the same as if the

shareholders had received it as part of

the special dividend and then returned it

to Pactolus as remuneration. The

commissioner can therefore treat this

102,414 pounds also as income derived by the

shareholders."

(Emphasis supplied.)

20. In the present case, the application of s. 260 reveals the derivation of income from the personal exertions of the taxpayer. And, as was pointed out by Kitto J. in Peate's Case (1964) 111 CLR at 471, s. 19 of the Act, which provides, inter alia, that income shall be deemed to have been derived by a person although not actually paid over to him if it is dealt with on his behalf or as he directs, has a role to play in this situation; see also Evans v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 88 ATC 4771 at 4790 per Fisher J.

21. A consequence which follows from the annihilation effected by s. 260 of the arrangements which vested in a person or persons other than the taxpayer the right to receive and deal with the income in question, is that the Commissioner is entitled in the manner described earlier in these reasons to treat the taxpayer as having derived the income which was the return from his own activities. If it is necessary to do so, one finds the taxpayer having derived income from the exercise of his skills as an expert computer consultant, as he had done for a number of years before he became an Australian resident and continued to pursue his calling; that was the "pre-existing situation". The authorities are discussed by Brennan J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kareena Private Hospital Pty. Ltd. (1979) 41 FLR 307 at 313-316; see also Tupicoff v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 4 FCR 505 at 523 per Beaumont J.

22. For these reasons I would dismiss the appeal.

The appellant, Mr Robert Bunting, appeals from the decision of a judge of this Court (Lockhart J) allowing an appeal from the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal") constituted by a Senior Member (Mr Roach). That decision related to the taxable income and tax payable by the appellant in the four years of income 1980 to 1983 and concerned the application of the provisions of s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

THE FACTS

2. The facts are of short compass and were not, it would seem, greatly disputed before the Tribunal. The respondent was of English birth and migrated to Australia when about 30. He had worked for about 10 years in England being employed by various companies initially as a computer programmer and later as a computer systems analyst. Upon arrival in Australia he was looking for work and approached several agencies seeking work. He had several interviews and settled on a company called Computations Pty Limited dealing with a Mr Haliday. That company had a requirement for a person like the appellant for a specific project and needed a "consultant" type person to take over the project and get it started. They suggested that he would as a consultant need a company with limited liability to operate with and recommended him to a firm of solicitors. The practice of consultant computer experts being employed by contracts was widespread in the computer industry and the appellant had encountered it in the United Kingdom. The appellant was aware that one of the advantages of so doing was to pay less income tax, although an important advantage was the limitation of liability having regard to the significant potential liability of a consultant computer expert.

3. The structure which was implemented upon the solicitor's advice involved the incorporation of a company, Manting Pty Limited the shares of which were held by a friend of the appellant in trust for the appellant and his wife. The company became trustee of a family discretionary trust on 14 April 1980 pursuant to a settlement made on that date by a Mr O'Reilly as settlor. The potential beneficiaries of the trust were Mrs Bunting, the appellant's children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren and their respective spouses, any future wife or wives of the appellant and such other persons as the trustee should appoint. The appellant was expressly excluded from the class of eligible beneficiaries. Mrs Bunting could appoint and remove beneficiaries under the trust.

4. On 14 April 1980 the trustee ("Manting") contracted with Computations Pty Limited to make the appellant's services available to that company for work on the project which had been discussed with Mr Haliday and the appellant became an employee of Manting at a salary of $15,000 per annum.

5. Although the initial project was not necessarily a one off project and the appellant saw the relationship with Computations Pty Limited as an ongoing relationship, when the project came to an end a contract was entered into between Manting and another company, Parity People Pty Limited to make the services of Mr Bunting available to that company and in turn Parity People Pty Limited made the applicant's services available pursuant to a contract to a State Government authority. That arrangement continued until the second project too came to an end outside the period the subject of the applications to the Tribunal. Throughout the relevant period Manting was remunerated, first by Computations Pty Limited, and later by Parity People Pty Limited on an hourly rate for the hours worked by the appellant. The rate paid increased from time to time. Manting paid a salary to the appellant and made contributions to a superannuation fund established on 29 May 1981 for the appellant's benefit.

6. Thus in the 1981 year Manting received consultancy fees of $28,259 and incurred expenses for insurance, motor vehicle and the like the most significant of which were salary of $15,194 paid to Mr Bunting and superannuation of $3,525.

THE DECISION OF THE ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

9. The Tribunal upheld the appellant's objection and reduced the taxable income in each year to that returned by the appellant. In so doing the Tribunal confined the operation of s.260 in cases such as the present to circumstances where the taxpayer had an existing source of income within Australia and rearranged his affairs in such a way in relation to that source of income so as to attract less tax. The Tribunal held that the section had no application where prior to the commencement of the arrangement there was no "stream of income" which attracted Australian income tax. According to the Tribunal prior to entering into the arrangement here in question the appellant had never derived Australian income - he had worked and potentially was taxed in the United Kingdom. The arrangement therefore increased his income in the sense that as a result of the arrangement he derived income in Australia for the first time and for the first time came under an obligation to pay Australian income tax. As the Tribunal said in its reasons:

"It is true that by establishing the company and

electing to work for it rather than working on his

own account he was choosing to have some of the

profit from his labour remain in the hands of the

company with the consequence that his own liability

to pay tax would be less than it might have been."

10. That however was not in the opinion of the Tribunal a situation to which s.260 applied.

THE DECISION UNDER APPEAL

11. On appeal, the learned judge below set aside the decision of the Tribunal with the consequence that the decision of the Commissioner was reinstated. In his Honour's view there was, in the language of the cases "an antecedent situation" namely Mr Bunting's employment in the United Kingdom from which he was in receipt of income, albeit not assessable income and the arrangement in question changed that situation and did so for the purpose of altering the incidence of income tax. Hence there was not a new source of income but rather the avoidance of tax operated upon an existing source of income, namely the appellant's services. In his Honour's words:

"The notion of an antecedent situation is broad

enough to encompass Mr Bunting's position. In the

circumstances of the case, including the very short

time lag between Mr Bunting's cessation of work in

England and commencement of work in Australia, the

fact that the previous employment was outside the

jurisdiction does not materially affect the

principle involved. Indeed, it would seem somewhat

artificial to allow this fact to determine the

application of s.260. This is a case where the

arrangements in question were designed to alter the

incidence of income taxation by splitting Mr

Bunting's income amongst his family. Section 260

applies to invalidate the arrangement."

12. Further his Honour was of the view that when s.260 was applied it operated to annihilate Manting and the trust leaving exposed earnings of income by the appellant of the gross income "attributed to the trust".

13. At the heart of the difference between the views of the Tribunal and the views of Lockhart J is the suggestion enunciated by Lord Diplock in Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v. Inland Revenue Commissioner (1976) 1 All ER 503, 511-2 and repeated by Barwick CJ in Mullens v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1975-6) 135 CLR 290, 302 that the operation of s.260 is confined to cases which involve no "new source of income" and that for the operation of the section there must exist an antecedent situation or transaction which when the transaction under attack is struck down leaves exposed assessable income.

14. Although these propositions may generally be said to have been confirmed not only by the Full High Court in Cridland v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 61; (1977) 140 CLR 330 but also by the majority of the members of the High Court in the recent trilogy of cases Gulland, Watson and Pincus reported as Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55 no case has ever sought to explore what was meant by the concept a "new source of income" or indeed what was meant by the concept "antecedent situation". In particular, is it a requirement of an antecedent situation that it involve a taxpayer already in receipt of assessable income? To evaluate the content of these concepts it is necessary to survey briefly the circumstances in which s.260 has been held to apply and the limitations which have been placed upon its operation. Before doing so it is convenient to refer to a number of propositions applicable to s.260 which can now be said to be well established. The section provides as follows:

"Every contract, agreement, or arrangement made or

entered into, orally or in writing, whether before

or after the commencement of this Act, shall so far

as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect

of in any way, directly or indirectly -

(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;

(b) relieving any person from liability to pay any

income tax or make any return;

(c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or

liability imposed on any person by this Act;

or

(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any

respect,

be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, or

in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but

without prejudice to such validity as it may have

in any other respect or for any other purpose."

ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLES OF CONSTRUCTION OF THE SECTION

15. First, the section applies to contracts, agreements and arrangements. Each of these words carries with it the notion that there will be at least two parties to any transaction to which the section will apply. It is necessary to identify with some precision what the contract, agreement or arrangement sought to be impugned is and for this purpose a court will, where necessary, require the Commissioner to particularize the arrangement he seeks to impugn. Cf Bailey & Ors v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 11; (1977) 136 CLR 214. Neither before the Tribunal nor before Lockhart J was the Commissioner required to give particulars of the arrangement. Before us he identified the arrangement as being the establishment of the discretionary trust, the entering into by Manting of the employment agreement with the appellant and with less certainty the entering into of the agreement between Manting and the third parties, Computations Pty Limited and Parity People Pty Limited. As will be explained later there are difficulties with either formulation.

16. It would seem that it is necessary for the taxpayer to be a party to the impugned arrangements: cf Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gregrhon Investments Pty Ltd & Ors (1987) 87 ATC 4988 and per Lord Diplock in Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v. Inland Revenue Commissioners (1976) 1 All ER 503 at 513 where that matter was left open. It may here be inferred that the appellant was a party to each of the three steps referred to above, although he was not the settlor of the trust nor the trustee nor indeed a director or shareholder of the trustee nor was he directly a party to any agreement between Manting and Computations Pty Limited or Manting and Parity People Pty Limited. I note that neither the Tribunal nor his Honour made any finding of facts in this regard. However the appellant's argument proceeded on the assumption that the appellant was a party so I am content to draw the inference of participation if that be a necessary precondition to the operation of the section.

17. Next, it is clear, that tax avoidance (and by that I mean one or other of the purposes set out in paragraphs (a) to (d) of the section) need not be the sole or indeed even the principal purpose of the arrangement. It is sufficient if tax avoidance was a purpose of the arrangement, so long as it was not merely an incidental or unessential purpose: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55, 57 per Gibbs C.J. and at 105 per Dawson J. To the extent that the judgment of Bray C.J. in Jones v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 15 SASR 462 upon which the appellant strongly relied is to the contrary, it must to that extent be seen to have been wrongly decided.

18. It is further clear that the section refers to the purpose (or effect) of the contract, agreement or arrangement (henceforth I will refer to "arrangement" to refer compendiously to all three) rather than to the subjective purpose of a person. In one sense, it may be said, that an arrangement cannot of itself have a purpose. What is meant however is that the arrangement must be looked at objectively to see whether from all the circumstances it would be inferred that the taxpayer had the necessary purpose. That, it seems to me is what was meant by Lord Denning in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1 at 8, (1958) AC 450, at 466 when his Lordship enunciated what Gibbs CJ referred to in Gulland at 66 as "a practical test for deciding whether an arrangement i.e. one to which the section applies" viz.

"In order to bring the arrangement within the

section you must be able to predicate - by looking

at the overt acts by which it was implemented -

that it was implemented in that particular way so

as to avoid tax. If you cannot so predicate, but

have to acknowledge that the transactions are

capable of explanation by reference to ordinary

business or family dealing, without necessarily

being labelled as a means to avoid tax, then the

arrangement does not come within the section."

19. Put in another way, the question to be asked is whether the arrangement bears on its face the stamp of tax avoidance. Cf per Kitto J in Peate v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1964) 111 CLR 443 at 469.

20. Finally it has never been doubted that s.260 operates as an annihilating provision only. Where the section applies to an arrangement that arrangement is, but only as between the Commissioner and the taxpayer and for the purpose of the assessment of the tax payable by the taxpayer, directed by the section to be treated as void to the extent that the arrangement operates in one of the ways described in the section. The corollary is that the operation of the section must leave exposed facts from which the liability of the taxpayer to tax can be ascertained. The section neither directs nor authorises the Commissioner to rearrange the facts to permit the imposition of tax, cf s.177F of the Act which now permits the Commissioner where the provisions of s.177D are satisfied to cancel the tax benefit obtained by the scheme.

21. This principle was expressed with great clarity in Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 34; (1951-3) 87 CLR 548 at 572-3 in the joint judgment of Dixon CJ, Williams, Webb, Fullagar and Kitto JJ as follows:

"The section is, of course, an annihilating

provision only. It has no further or other

operation than to eliminate from consideration for

tax purposes such contracts, agreements and

arrangements as fall within the descriptions it

contains. It assists the commissioner, in a case

like the present, only if, when all contracts,

agreements and arrangements having such a purpose

or effect as the section mentions are obliterated,

the facts which remain justify the commissioner's

assessment."

22. Although the principle is firmly entrenched few cases have depended upon it to reach the conclusion that s.260 had no application. Two, however, stand out. In War Assets Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1954] HCA 81; (1952-4) 91 CLR 53 the taxpayer had an option to purchase surplus war materials which option, after advice as to the tax consequences if it were exercised and the surplus materials resold at a profit, made over the goods upon exercise of the option to a Papuan company at cost. The shares in the Papuan company were beneficially owned as to two thirds by the taxpayer. It was held that s.260 did not permit the Commissioner to assess the taxpayer to tax on the profit in fact derived by the Papuan company. The Court, which comprised Dixon CJ, Williams and Kitto JJ said in answer to a submission that annihilation left the profits to be held in trust as to two thirds for the appellant:

"But the difficulty in the path of Mr Holmes is

that there never was any such antecedent agreement

or arrangement. If you avoid the latter agreement

or arrangement (i.e. that the profit be made by the

Papuan company) you are left with nothing." (p 97)

23. The case emphasises the need for there to be some antecedent situation ultimately left exposed which produces assessable income. An antecedent situation which produced exempt income would not suffice.

24. More recently, the full Court of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Kareena Hospital Pty Limited (1979) 79 ATC 4667 held the section inapplicable to an arrangement whereby the taxpayer discontinued its business, caused its staff to be transferred to a loss company which took a lease from a related company of the site where the taxpayer had conducted a private hospital and thereafter carried on the business formerly carried on by the taxpayer. Brennan J, on the assumption that the arrangement was otherwise of the kind to which the section applied said at 4673:

"Section 260 does not create fictional activity on

the part of Hospital and it does not deem the

income International earned to be a return from a

fictional activity of Hospital. Section 260 does

not substitute fiction for fact, as Lord Donovan

observed in his dissent in Peate's case (supra)

(116 CLR at p 55; and see Bayly v. FC. of T. 77

ATC 4045 at p 4057). The income which

International received was not the income of any

business owned or carried on by Hospital, nor was

it a return from any activity in which Hospital

engaged. Section 260 cannot transform the income

earned by International in carrying on its business

into the assessable income of Hospital. The sums

paid by International to Hospital were paid with

funds received by International as income earned by

its own activity, and sec.260 does not deny the

funds that character."

25. Similar views were expressed by the Chief Justice at 4669 and by Lockhart J at 4681-4686.

26. As I observed, however in Davis it may be suggested that in some cases the Courts have paid but mere lip service to this principle. I shall refer later in these reasons to the problems of reconstruction, in the context of attempts to alter the incidence of personal services income and the tax payable thereon. ALTERATION OF INCIDENCE AND AVOIDANCE OF LIABILITY

27. The difficulty which Lord Denning's test disguises lies in the substitution of the words "tax avoidance" for the lettered paragraphs of the section, for it is in the meaning of those paragraphs that that the first of the complexities of the present case lies.

28. Here the Commissioner relies on both paragraphs (a) and (c) of s.260 and submits that the arrangement entered into in the present case involves an alteration of the incidence of tax and also involves avoiding a liability imposed on the appellant by the Act. The cases afford little assistance in analysing what, if any paragraph is applicable in an instant case, probably because there is much overlap between the two paragraphs.

29. In general terms it may be seen that the cases in which s.260 has been applied fall into three categories: cases where the arrangement converts income to capital, cases where the arrangement gives rise to a deduction and finally, cases where the arrangement diverts income.

Conversion of Income to Capital

30. The first class of case is that where an impending receipt of an income character is, by force of the arrangement, given the character of capital. One of the early examples of this class of case was Clarke v. Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 46; (1932) 48 CLR 56. In that case a company was inserted between a lessor and a lessee to receive a premium upon the grant of a lease which, had the transaction been structured as initially agreed would have resulted in the lessor deriving assessable income. Instead the lessor granted a lease to the company which then assigned the lease at a premium to the lessee. The company was then liquidated, the shareholders receiving capital. The case was seen as one in which the arrangement had the purpose or effect of avoiding the lessor's liability to income tax.

31. In this category of case is to be found as well the dividend stripping cases where the purpose and effect of the arrangement was to produce a capital receipt in place of a dividend: cf. Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 34; (1951-3) 87 CLR 548; F R Mayfield v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No 1) [1961] HCA 57; (1961) 108 CLR 303; F R Mayfield v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No 2) [1961] HCA 58; (1961) 108 CLR 323; Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258; Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1, (1958) AC 450; Commissioner of Taxation v. Ellers Motor Sales Pty Ltd & Ors [1972] HCA 17; (1972) 128 CLR 602; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gregrhon (1987) 87 ATC 4988.

32. In this first category of case, it was early emphasised that when paragraph (c) refers to a duty or a liability imposed on a person, those words refer "not to a liability to pay a particular amount of tax (which would be a liability imposed by a taxing Act), but to a liability such as s.17 imposed...". Thus in Bell's case, where the taxpayer and others had bought military equipment for resale and then, to avoid tax, incorporated a New Guinea company which purchased the equipment at cost and on sold it at a profit, it originally being contemplated that dividends would be distributed over a period of years, the taxpayer thereafter sold the shares in the New Guinea company for a capital sum. The joint judgment of Dixon CJ, Williams, Webb, Fullagar and Kitto JJ described the s.17 liability of the taxpayer as being to include in his assessable income his proportion of the Papuan company's profits if and when he should participate in a distribution of them.

33. It can be seen that this first category of case clearly involved an antecedent situation or transaction which, when s.260 operated left the taxpayer exposed to tax.

Arrangements to produce a deduction

34. The second category of case involves an arrangement having the purpose or effect of producing a deduction to which otherwise there would have been no entitlement. An early example was Jaques v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1923] HCA 70; (1923-4) 34 CLR 328 where, in place of an arrangement already entered into, whereby a company was to transfer its business to a new company in exchange for shares in the new company, whereupon the old company was to be liquidated and the shares in the new company distributed in specie, there was substituted a sale of assets to the new company for cash, a subscription by the shareholders for shares (which subscription carried a tax deduction) and a liquidation of the old company. Again paragraph (c) appeared in such a case the most appropriate paragraph.

35. Apart from the arrangement the taxpayer's taxable income would have been calculated without reference to the deduction: the taxpayer would, apart from the "contract, agreement or arrangement bear a certain liability ... to pay tax in respect of certain income". The effect of the application of the section is that the taxpayer's liability remains just as if there were no such "contract, agreement or arrangement". Other examples include - Franklins Selfserve Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 33; (1970) 125 CLR 52; Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 23; (1970) 123 CLR 71 and perhaps Glenfield Estates Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) ATC 4548.

36. Two difficulties have stood in the way of the application of s.260 to deductions. The first, is the difficulty of applying the section to disallow a deduction when the Act provides specifically and by way of concession for the allowance of a deduction. Thus where the Act gave a deduction for calls on certain mining shares (s.77A) as in Mullens v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1975-6) 135 CLR 290 there was a difficulty in characterising as coming within any of the paragraphs of s.260, an arrangement whereby a taxpayer brought himself within the terms of the section granting the deduction. The second and perhaps related difficulty was the difficulty in applying s.260 to disallow or reduce deductions otherwise properly allowable under s.51(1) of the Act: Cecil Bros Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 82; (1962-64) 111 CLR 430 and John v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 89 ATC 4101 at 4109.

37. In Mullens the High Court did not seek to propound a principle of general application that s.260 could never apply to disallow a deduction, for so to do would have involved overruling Jaques' case. In delivering his judgment Barwick CJ instanced Jaques' case as an example of a case where s.260 could apply contrasting that case with the facts in Mullens where there was no evidence before the Supreme Court upon which it could be concluded that there was some transaction agreed upon by the parties for which the transaction before the Court was substituted in order to gain some advantage in taxation which the former transaction would not have given. At 302 his Honour said:

"It is said in Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v. Inland

Revenue Commissioner (No 2) (1976) 1 WLR at 475 of

the New Zealand equivalent of s.260: 'The section

does not strike at new sources of income or

restrict the right of the taxpayer to arrange his

affairs in relation to income from a new source in

such a way as to attract the least possible

liability to tax.' The present appeal is concerned

not with new sources of income but with a new basis

for deduction. If the only relevant transaction is

that under attack, it seems to me that by parity of

reasoning, s.260 may be said not to be concerned

with the right to a deduction which the only

relevant transaction between the parties would

produce in the future.

Again, it is otherwise if, by reason of an

antecedent transaction or situation, the taxpayer

was already subject to tax in respect of an income

which that antecedent transaction or situation

produced or would produce, or was not or would not

be entitled to any deduction in the situation

created by that antecedent transaction. In such a

situation the avoidance of the impugned

transaction, because it represented an endeavour to

cast what had already been agreed into a form which

avoided or lessened the amount of tax otherwise

payable, would expose the antecedent situation

which did not carry any right to a deduction. Just

as there must be income, not derived from the

impugned transaction but derived from the

antecedent transaction between the parties which,

when that transaction is struck down, is exposed as

producing assessable income, so, in my opinion, in

relation to a deduction, the avoidance of the impugned

transaction must disclose a transaction or situation

which did not entitle the taxpayer to a deduction."

38. The alternative ground of decision in Mullens was that the taxpayer was given by the Act a choice to obtain a deduction provided the terms of the relevant section (s.77A) were satisfied and the satisfaction of those terms did not involve the application of s.260.

39. Stephen J approached the matter in a way similar to the Chief Justice. At 317 his Honour pointed out that the taxpayer in Mullens "was never in the position of having some transaction in mind or already embarked to which they gave a different form so that they might clothe it with tax advantages." In any event, in his Honour's view s.260 could not be applied to negate the Act's specific provision.

40. For present purposes, what is of significance is the emphasis which the then Chief Justice and Stephen J both placed upon the need for there to be a pre-existing transaction or situation involving a liability or potential liability for tax which liability is displaced by the transaction to which s.260 applies. That requirement was expressed by Lord Diplock delivering the majority opinion in the Privy Council in Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v. Inland Revenue Commissioner (No 2) (1976) 1 All ER 502 at 511 in two ways. First his Lordship said of the New Zealand s.108 in relevantly identical terms:

"Any liability of the taxpayer to pay income tax

must be found elsewhere in the Act. There must be

some identifiable income of the taxpayer which

would have been liable to be taxed if none of the

contracts, agreements or arrangements avoided by

the section had been made.

Secondly, the description of the contracts,

agreements and arrangements which are liable to

avoidance presupposes the continued receipt by the

taxpayer of income from an existing source in

respect of which his liability to pay tax would be

altered or relieved if legal effect were given to

the contract, agreement or arrangement sought to be

avoided as against the commissioner. The section

does not strike at new sources of income or

restrict the right of the taxpayer to arrange his

affairs in relation to income from a new source in

such a way as to attract the least possible

liability to tax." (emphasis added)

41. The first requirement of Lord Diplock stemmed from the principle earlier discussed that the section was not itself a taxing section; it merely treated as void for the purpose of the assessment of taxation so much of the arrangement entered into by the taxpayer as had the relevant purpose or effect. The consequence for taxation purposes was to be found elsewhere in the facts left exposed after the section had performed its task of annihilation. The second principle was derived, it would seem from the words of paragraphs (a) to (d) of the section and particularly paragraph (c).

42. While it is not clear what Lord Diplock meant by an existing or new source of income, it certainly seems clear that the existing source of income which his Lordship had in mind was one which subjected the taxpayer to a liability, in that case to New Zealand tax and here to Australian tax, for it is that liability that must be altered with the consequence that less Australian tax results before s.260 can apply.

43. In Europa, Lord Diplock referred to the 1956 Organisation Contract pursuant to which Pan Eastern was incorporated and pursuant to which Europa was entitled to receive dividends on the shares of its wholly owned subsidiary paid ultimately from Pan Eastern's profits as a new source of income, and found that the arrangements entered into by the taxpayer were accordingly not within s.260. But there is no reason why the concept of source of income (a concept usually associated with matters of territorial reach of taxation legislation) should be limited to income from property.

44. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. United Aircraft Corporation Ltd [1943] HCA 50; (1943) 68 CLR 525 and 536 Latham CJ noting that the distinction between income from property and income from personal exertion was but a matter of terms having regard to the definition (in s.6(1) of the Act) of income derived from property as being such income as did not fall within the definition of income from personal exertion, expressed himself unable to think of any sources of income other than property and acts done. If that be the sense in which Lord Diplock was using the expression in Europa then in the context of income from personal exertion it could be said that the expression "source of income" would refer to acts done (i.e. in a case such as the present, employment) but nevertheless employment such that the income derived thereon was subject to Australian tax.

Arrangements for the diversion of income

45. The third category of case to which s.260 has been held applicable has involved income formerly derived by a taxpayer being directed to another entity. Primarily, such a case will be seen as involving the alteration of the incidence of tax but the cases have also applied paragraph (c) of the section. Of the many cases in this category perhaps, at least until the decision of the Full High Court in Gulland, Watson & Pincus, the most significant was that of the Privy Council in Peate v. The Commissioner of Taxation [1966] UKPCHCA 1; (1966) 116 CLR 38.

46. It will be recalled that Dr Peate had been a partner in a medical partnership. A company was incorporated, of which the appellant was chairman, which employed each of the partners. The stockholders of that company were each family companies associated with the doctors and the doctors continued to perform medical services for patients as they had done before the arrangement. The Privy Council held that the arrangement had the purpose or effect of avoiding a liability imposed on each doctor i.e. the case fell within paragraph (c) of the section.

47. At p 43 Viscount Dilhorne described the arrangements as divesting the taxpayer of "income which he would have received if they had not been made" for that reason, but subject to the question of whether the annihilation worked by the section exposed Dr Peate to assessment within the section. That formulation (the emphasis is mine) requires it would seem some prior antecedent situation involving, but for the arrangement, the taxpayer deriving assessable income. For it is only then that it can be said that there is income which the taxpayer would have received if the arrangement were not entered into.

48. The difficulty in Peate's case lay in determining the consequences for tax if s.260 operated. Viscount Dilhorne was of the opinion, having regard to s.17 and s.19, that the Commissioner "was entitled to assess the appellant on the income he would have received in each of the three years if the arrangements coming within s.260 had not been made." Thus the Commissioner was entitled to treat the partnership as continuing until, if in fact it had been in existence, it would have been dissolved by law. After one of the partner doctors withdrew there was, in his Lordship's view, an association of persons (the remaining seven doctors) in receipt of income jointly. As his Lordship said at 46:

"The sums received by Westbank from ... patients

were, as were the fees earned by the doctors

employed by Westbank at a salary, part of the

income of the doctors who were, if the existence of

Westbank is disregarded, in receipt of income jointly."

49. Lord Donovan in a strong dissent, pointed out that the majority view involved reconstruction.

50. What, however, for present purposes is relevant is that the majority of the Privy Council was aided in reaching its conclusion by the existence of the partnership of doctors (sharing income in common) prior to the commencement of the arrangement. It is not at all clear how their Lordships could have reached their conclusion but for the existence of the antecedent partnership arrangement.

51. The difficulties of reconstruction pointed out in Peate's case were not present in Hollyock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4202 nor in Millard [1962] HCA 28; (1962) 108 CLR 336 both cases in which the High Court applied s.260, in the former to assess a pharmacist who declared that he would hold his business in trust for himself and his wife equally while continuing to run it as before and in the latter to assess a bookmaker who arranged for his business to be taken over by a company but who continued to run the business as agent for the company and to run it as before. The section however was held not to be applicable in three cases decided in Supreme Courts around the same time, Peacock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1976) 76 ATC 4375, Jones v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4058 and Bayly v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4045. In each of these cases there were factual findings that the transactions entered into were, in accordance with the Newton test, capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, notwithstanding that a consequence of the arrangement was that the taxpayer could split income with his wife and family.

52. The taxpayer in Peacock was a surveyor, in Jones and Bayly a pharmacist. In each of these cases there was no illegality in the non-professional being concerned with the practice; in Peacock the surveyor and his wife became partners, in Bayly and in Jones the taxpayer's wife was the owner of the business which was managed by the pharmacist. There may be thought to be a difficulty in reconciling the decisions in Jones and Bayly on the one hand with Hollyock on the other but as Gibbs CJ pointed out in the last mentioned case an important feature of it was that the business which the appellant declared that he held in trust for his wife was one that could lawfully be carried on only by a pharmaceutical chemist. Millard's case came under the same category in that the business of a bookmaker could only be carried on by a person who was registered. There Gibbs CJ found that the tax avoidance purpose (income splitting) was a purpose and not merely an unessential or incidental feature of the arrangement with the consequence that s.260 was held applicable.

53. In the present case given the finding that it was common for computer analysts to be employed by their own companies and the obvious advantages of limited liability it may have been possible, notwithstanding the interpolation into the arrangement of a discretionary trust, to have argued that the arrangement in the present case was also capable of explanation as an ordinary family or business dealing but counsel for the applicant specifically conceded that the purpose of the arrangement was income splitting and therefore, but subject to the proper construction of paragraphs (a) and (c) of s.260, constituted tax avoidance.

54. In Bayly, however, Bray CJ of the Supreme Court of South Australia found that even if the transaction were otherwise within s.260 as constituting "tax avoidance" in the required sense, once the arrangement was struck down, the facts did not leave a liability to tax exposed, because the business which was owned by the wife was never the business of the pharmacist, whereas in Hollyock s.260 was capable of operation because prior to the arrangement the business had been owned by the pharmacist. This distinction Bray CJ referred to as a "capricious effect". It is, however, a distinction which displays the significance of the requirement of the concept of "pre-existing situation" to the functioning of s.260.

55. The most recent High Court authorities on income splitting as tax avoidance were the three cases of Gulland, Watson and Pincus. In each, the taxpayer had prior to the arrangement in question practised in Australia as a doctor. Dr Gulland had a one man practice which he sold to the trustees of a unit trust (Dr Gulland himself and another medical practitioner) who employed him thereafter to practice as an employed doctor. Dr Watson was a member of a five man partnership. The partners sold the practice to the trustee of a unit trust which employed the doctors. The facts of Dr Pincus' case were more complex. Dr Pincus had been a partner in a partnership which practised medicine at two surgeries. The partnership was dissolved. The assets pertaining to each surgery were sold to the trustees of two separate unit trusts (each trustee was one of the partners) and one trustee employed at the one surgery three of the doctors and the other trustee employed at the other surgery two of the doctors.

56. In each of the three cases before the High Court units in the unit trusts were held by trustees of various family discretionary trusts and the wife and children of the taxpayers were beneficiaries of these trusts.

57. The majority of the Court was of the opinion that s.260 applied in each case.

58. In the course of his judgment the Chief Justice in considering an argument that the section did not apply because the taxpayer was merely exercising a choice which was available under the Act of creating trusts the income of which would be subject to Division 6 said at p 70:

"That argument (which could not be accepted

consistently with Peate v. Federal Commissioner of

Taxation) fails because it is simply not right to

say that the Act allows a taxpayer the opportunity

to have his own income from personal exertion taxed

as though it were income derived by a trust and

held for the benefit of a number of beneficiaries."

59. This passage was seized upon by counsel for the respondent as support for a broader submission that no person could alienate personal service income. However the passage does not support such a broad proposition and should be seen as what it was, namely a ground for rejecting the version of the choice principle for which the taxpayer contended. It does not follow that s.260 must apply to all cases of income splitting involving personal exertion income. Such a proposition, as has already been demonstrated, could not be supported by the authorities.

60. Both Gibbs CJ and Dawson J who delivered the principal judgments referred, without disapproval, to the passages from Mullens' case in which Barwick CJ referred to the necessity for an antecedent situation. Gibbs CJ at p 73 pointed out that Barwick CJ had referred to both antecedent transaction and antecedent situation and said:

"...there is nothing in s.260 that supports the

view that that section can apply only when there

has been an antecedent transaction between parties.

An arrangement will, for example, be within the section

if it alters the incidence of income tax in a case in

which the only relevant antecedent circumstance is

that the taxpayer is in receipt of income."

In my view, the receipt of income referred to was a receipt of income giving rise to a liability to pay tax.

61. Dawson J after setting out what was said by Barwick CJ in Mullens' case continued in his Honour's discussion of the facts in Dr Pincus' case at 125-6:

"Even if it be assumed that the partnership would

have been dissolved in any event and forms no part

of the arrangement under which Dr Pincus now

carries on his professional activities, that fact

does not, in my view, lead to the conclusion for

which he contends. The new arrangement did not

produce a new source of income in the sense in

which the cases used that term. Dr Pincus's (sic)

source of income whilst he was a sole practitioner,

whilst he was practising in partnership, and now

under the substituted arrangement, has been, and

is, the same, namely the practice of his profession

as a doctor. The form of arrangement under which

he has practised from time to time has not altered

the source of his income in any relevant sense.

The source of his income tax has been his

professional activities as a medical practitioner

whether he has been a sole practitioner, a partner

or an employee. It is from those activities that his

income has derived and his liability to tax has arisen.

When speaking of the source of a taxpayer's income,

the Privy Council, in Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v. Inland

Revenue Commissioner ((1976) 1 WLR at 475; (1976) 1

All ER at 511), was, in the context, speaking of

the real, or ultimate, source of income and not of

the vehicle by which it was conveyed to the

taxpayer. The legal structure within which an income

may be derived by a taxpayer may vary but the source

of that income may remain the same. So it was that

in Gulland's case the taxpayer did not derive his

income from a new source when he ceased

to be a medical practitioner in practice on his own

account and commenced to practise his profession as

an employee. And in Mangin v. Inland Revenue

Commissioner ((1971) AC 739), a farmer who leased

his land to trustees who were to employ him to work

it in the same way as he had done previously and to

pay the income to beneficiaries, did not rid

himself of the source of income constituted by his

farming activities, however much the arrangement

may have reduced the income derived by him from

that source if the provisions of the arrangement

were to be given effect."

62. It would seem clear from the above comments of his Honour (albeit not directed to a case such as the present) that in the discussion of the concept of "new source of income" his Honour refers merely to "the activities" of the taxpayer as being the source of income and the passage cited is equivocal as to whether his Honour would have regarded it as sufficient for the operation of s.260 that the relevant antecedent activities constituting the pre-existing source of income were outside Australia.

63. Both Gibbs CJ and Dawson J were of the view on the facts of the three cases before the Court that s.260 operated to leave exposed all three taxpayers to tax. Gibbs CJ relied on Peate's case to produce this result in Watson's case and in Pincus' case. So far as the facts of the latter case were concerned his Honour commented at 76:

"The application of s.260 revealed that Drs Pincus

and Richardson were practising in association and

that Dr Seet was working part time in conjunction

with them, although his remuneration for doing so

came from the practice at Stafford Heights. It

revealed that Dr Pincus in fact received, from the

practice of his profession, not only the amount

paid to him as 'salary' but also the amount of

profits distributed by Dr Backstrom as the trustee

of the unit trust to Dr Pincus as trustee of the

family trust."

64. Dawson J at 126 said:

"The argument, however, ignores the reality of the

situation in that both before and after the

dissolution of the partnership and the application

of s.260, Dr Pincus was without interruption

carrying on the practice of his profession in a

manner which was essentially the same, however much

the income which he derived thereby might flow

through different channels. The source of income

was the same and the effect of s.260 was to

preclude Dr Pincus from the choices which he might

have had were he to have been setting up practice

afresh or for the first time.

Accepting the dissolution of the partnership and

disregarding the arrangement which took its place,

Dr Pincus carried on practice at Stafford Road in

association with Dr Richardson. As a result they

were in receipt of income jointly and so were in

partnership within the meaning of the definition of

partnership contained in s.6(1) of the Income Tax

Assessment Act. The expenses of the practice were

paid by Dr Backstrom on behalf of Dr Pincus and Dr

Richardson but were nevertheless, by reason of s.19

of the Act, met out of the income derived by them.

Dr Pincus and Dr Richardson were in receipt of the

net profits of the practice as partners and that

portion of those profits which, under the

arrangement, was received by Dr Pincus as trustee

for the Pincus Children Trust is to be treated as

income derived by him." (emphasis added)

65. The operation of s.260 in Gulland, Watson and Pincus was explained by the full High Court (Mason CJ, Wilson, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ) in John in a not insignificant passage having regard to the present problem:

"... sec.260 effects a fictitious annihilation of

contracts, agreements and arrangements. It does

not proceed to substitute an alternative basis on

which tax should be calculated. Of course, in some

cases the annihilation of a legal form will itself

reveal a basis for the calculation of tax. F.C. of

T. v. Gulland 85 ATC 4765; [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55 was

such a case. There the annihilation of the

arrangements in question revealed the source of

income as the personal exertions of the taxpayer

respondents in the same form as had existed prior

to the arrangements which were held to offend

sec.260." (emphasis added)

CONCLUSION

66. The above discussion of the cases reveals that the requirements that there be a pre-existing situation and an existing source of income before s.260 applies seem to have stemmed both from the requirements of paragraphs (a) and (c) of the section and from the realisation that s.260 permits of no reconstruction but requires for its operation that there be left exposed after the section has done its work, a situation where the taxpayer has a liability to tax. While it would seem clear that by "existing source of income", in a case such as the present, is meant no more than activities of a taxpayer and therefore that no particular requirement as to the place where these activities took place is inherent in the section, it does seem to me that there is implicit in the concept of pre-existing situation the concept of a situation which gives rise to a liability to Australian tax.

67. If one starts with paragraph (c) of the section it is inherent in the concept of the arrangement, being one to avoid a liability imposed by the Act, that there be a pre-existing situation which would, but for the arrangement, give rise to the liability which the arrangement seeks to avoid. Avoidance as the Privy Council said in Newton at p 7 is used "in the sense in which a person is said to avoid something which is about to happen to him." "To 'avoid a liability imposed' on you means to take steps to get out of the reach of a liability which is about to fall on you."

68. If paragraph (c) is to apply in the present case it must be seen therefore that the appellant was in a situation where there was a liability about to fall on him or as was said in Peate that there was income which he would have received if the arrangement were not made. No direct finding of fact was made by the Tribunal which permits me to say one way or the other whether the appellant on the balance of probabilities intended to work as an employee of a third party in Australia prior to the advice received from his solicitor and acted upon by the appellant to establish the discretionary trust. The evidence however may support the view that the appellant never contemplated working as an employee of a third party in Australia. He was advised not to and for good commercial reasons. The facts are open to the interpretation that the appellant was prepared to accept advice to work as an employee of a company to minimise his potential liability as a consultant. If those be the facts can it be said that the plan devised for him by his solicitor and into which he entered was to get out of reach of a liability about to fall on him?

69. The true pre-existing situation in the present case might well be not that of a person working as an employee of a stranger or an individual consultant to a stranger but a person who had accepted advice not to so work. In my view, paragraph (c) could have no operation in such a case. It does not, however, follow that s.260 can have no operation to a student seeking a job for the first time or indeed to a person who takes steps to split income and be employed by a trust after a period of unemployment. In my opinion there will be in each case a question of fact to be decided, viz. whether in the absence of the impugned arrangement there was as a matter of fact a liability to Australian tax in the sense used by the cases (i.e. under s.17 of the Act) about to fall upon the taxpayer as a result of income that the person would have derived but for the arrangement.

70. Paragraph (a) of the section carries with it the same concept. For that paragraph to operate there must be an alteration of the incidence of income tax. Although as Gulland makes clear the fact that the arrangement does not reduce tax in an early year of the operation of the plan but was intended to operate in future years, does not take the arrangement outside the operation of the section (at p 72 per Gibbs CJ), the concept of "alteration" in the context of the section must clearly require that the tax payable be altered from some situation which on the balance of probabilities would have existed if the arrangement had not been entered into. Generally when an employee (or for that matter a school leaver seeking employment for the first time) chooses to enter into an arrangement involving his employment by a trust there will be no difficulty in finding as a fact that if the arrangement had not been entered into, an employment arrangement would have followed so that the incidence of tax will have been altered by the arrangement.

71. But in the present case the findings of fact made by the Tribunal are inadequate to permit me to conclude whether there has been in the relevant sense an alteration of the incidence of income tax. It follows, in my view, that the matter should be remitted to the Tribunal to find whether the facts were such that there was a liability about to fall upon the appellant which was avoided by the arrangement.

72. If either of paragraphs (a) or (c) are of any application to the facts of the present case it would then be necessary to turn to the question whether s.260, when applied, left exposed, without reconstruction, income of the taxpayer which entitled the respondent to assess him to tax. I find this a difficult question to answer. In all the income splitting cases to which s.260 has been applied emphasis has been placed on the fact that s.260 left exposed the taxpayer performing work as before, albeit that in Peate and in Gulland a pre-existing partnership had terminated. That factor is absent here. The pre-existing situation has, in the cases decided to date, permitted the section to apply without reconstruction.

73. On the facts of the present case it cannot be said that the appellant continued to practise his profession "without interruption" to use the words of Dawson J in Gulland. When the arrangement comprising the formation of the trust and the employment by the appellant (if that be the relevant arrangement) is set aside there is left exposed an arrangement between the trustee and the company, to which the appellant is consultant, for the employment of the appellant, the appellant performing work and the payment of the trustee. That does not, in my view, without more, leave exposed the appellant to a liability to tax. If the arrangement includes the agreement between the trustee and the third party (to which the trustee is not a party) there is then left exposed the trustee in receipt of moneys, not being consideration for anything. It seems to me that it is only if it is found or can be inferred (as it clearly could be in Peate, Tupicoff (1984) 4 FCR 505, Gulland, Watson and Pincus) that the payments to the trustee were made to the trustee at the direction of the appellant that s.260 can be applied.

74. There is however no factual finding to that effect in the present case and the evidence does not seem to me to be so clear that it would be unnecessary to remit the matter to the Tribunal. In these circumstances I am of the view that the matter should be remitted to the Tribunal to find as well the facts relevant to the question of direction.

75. Accordingly I would allow the appeal.
