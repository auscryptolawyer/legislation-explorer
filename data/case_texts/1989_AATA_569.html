Last Updated: 17 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Allowable Deductions - "Repairs" - Repaving surface of industrial storages area - Whether "initial repairs" - Whether "improvements".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 S.53, S.223

W. Thomas & Co. Pty Limited v F.C.T. [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58

Collector of Inland Revenue, Cook Islands v A.B. Donald Limited (1965) 9 AITR 501

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/1443 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

AAT Decision No 5379

Tribunal : Mr Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 15 September 1989

Place : Sydney

Decision : The decision of the Tribunal is that the determination of the Commissioner under review shall be varied and that taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 shall be reduced by $126,117.

(Sqn) PETER M. ROACH Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/1443

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

15 September 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. When containerisation was approaching its position of dominance in the world of international freight in the late 1960's, three companies formerly associated in the transport industry came together and promoted the incorporation of the present Applicant - Three-Co. Three-Co then commenced carrying on business in relation to the export trade. - It received materials for export; rearranged them; and then placed them in large containers ready for export. To do so it needed appropriate premises. It secured such premises (inter alia) on a site capable of being serviced by both road and rail. A rail line ran close to the point of the road entrance so that road vehicles entering and leaving the site had to cross the rail line. The site occupied 6/10 of a hectare (some one and a half acres). Road access was available from public streets and rail access was to a siding within the property which was linked to the public rail system. Within the property there was a large storage shed. Other structures provided office facilities and amenities. However, most of the site was undeveloped save that all the open areas relevant to the present issues were paved with asphaltic concrete - more commonly known as bitumen - over a fill of road base and silt/clay fill or sand.

  1. The use of the premises by Three-Co involved delivery of export materials to the site. Such deliveries were principally effected by road. Some deliveries were effected by rail. Materials so delivered were collected by fork-lift trucks and transferred to the store for special packaging. Once repackaged the goods were transferred by fork-lift to shipping containers stored at the site. When such shipping containers were positioned to be loaded they were set at ground level, thereby providing access to the fork-lifts. When awaiting loading, and when loaded pending transfer out, they were stored on site in stacks as high as three containers. Loaded containers weighed up to 20 tonnes and could only be moved by a special heavy duty fork-lift which itself weighed 30 tonnes (40.7 tonne according to one report).
  2. Three-Co did not own the site. It held possession by arrangement with others. Initially it held portion of the whole site it later occupied as sub-lessee of one public authority which itself had held possession as tenant of another public authority. Later Three-Co came to ' old its entire interest directly from the freeholder. It held possession of the original site from late 1969/early 1970. The latter interest as tenant of the freeholder was held for a term of 14 years from 1 September 1974. That interest came to be held pursuant to a lease executed only in 1980 - tax litigation has no monopoly on delay. The lease provided inter alia:

"5. MAINTENANCE, REPAIR, ALTERATIONS, ETC.:

The Lessee FURTHER COVENANTS with the Lessor as follows:

(a) The Lessee will during the said term and otherwise so long as the Lessee may remain in possession or occupation when where and so often as need shall he maintain replace repair and keep the whole of the demised premises ... in good and substantial repair order and condition damage by fire, flood, lightning, storm, tempest, and reasonable wear and tear, Act of God, and war damage, only excepted;

(b) The Lessee will at the expiration or sooner determination of the term peaceably surrender and yield up to the Lessor the demised premises and every part thereof in good and substantial repair order and condition and in a tidy clean sanitary and wholesome state in all respects damage by fire, flood, lightning, storm, tempest, and reasonable wear and tear, Act of God, and war damage only excepted.

(c) ..."

4. I find that before Three-co entered into possession of the site the bitumen surface in the uncovered areas was in reasonable but not perfect condition. I find that it was seemingly sufficient to serve, and was actually sufficient to serve, the purposes of Three-Co for some years. I also find that it was actually in a condition suitable to serve well for a prolonged period the interests of many businesses which might have used such storage facilities. But the usage by Three-Co was not to be ordinary. It was to involve exceptionally heavy-duty usige with movement throughout the site of laden and unladen fork-lifts and trucks. Two extremes- of usage were quite commonplace. One involved the movement of the 30 tonne heavy-duty fork-lift while carrying laden containers: a further 20 tonnes. The other involved stacking up to three 20-tonne containers. It was therefore not surprising that, just as bitumen road surfaces on highways, roads and streets break up, particularly after rain, so too the paved storage areas broke up when subjected to such pressures as the weight of laden fork-lifts with all up loads of 50 tonnes and stacked containers with all up weights of 60 tonnes.

5. The' signs of deterioration of the pavement appeared within a few months of the commencement of operations. Thereafter a programme of patching was to become a regular feature of the company's operations. It was a sufficiently regular feature to be provided for by its own allocation in the budget. Over the years tax deductions were claimed and allowed. It is not suggested that the action of the Commissioner in allowing any of those claims can have any bearing on the outcome of the present reference. But some indication of the nature of the problem can be had on considering the reply given by Three-Co to a query raised by the Commissioner as to claims to deductibility in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1972. In its reply by letter of 21 November 1972, the Applicant advised:

"The container loading area was originally constructed of consolidated basement materials sealed with bitumen. This broke up under working conditions viz: fork lifts handling ... containers, after one year and was repaired by re-consolidation, adding additional road base materials and re-sealing with bitumen. The operating conditions are severe and the area will need further repairs at various intervals."

6. By 1985, despite frequent patching of the site, it was generally in poor condition. Rutting was extensive. As the surface gave way in some areas, displaced materials were forced up nearby. The problem was so extensive as to render working operations somewhat dangerous. Something had to be done to overcome the problem in order to provide safe working conditions; in order to continue operations; and in order to avoid being held liable for "breach of the covenants to repair. Consultants were engaged to advise.

7. The consultant, a geotechnical engineer, examined and tested the site. He drilled test holes at 7 points and discovered that throughout the site the residual soils were silty clays of high plasticity. They had been overlaid by clay and sandstone fill ranging from 20 to 60 centimetres covered by asphaltic concrete ranging in depth from 5 to 30 centimetres. According to his report:

"It is our conclusion that the existing pavement is severely fatigued and is unsuitable for rehabilitation. If the current situation where repairs are made as each area, usually small in size, becomes inoperable is to be avoided, then we recommend complete reconstruction."

His first recommendation was that the problem be overcome by providing a substantial concrete surface. In that way the problems of maintenance could be minimised and economies achieved over thirty years. His alternatives were several, all involving an asphaltic concrete surface. In presenting his alternative recommendations the Consultant said:

"On the other hand, asphalt pavements are available for use immediately after compaction. But in view of the depths requiredr larger areas would be needed for the construction stages to be carried out economically. In addition, we are of the opinion that a special asphalt mix design should be adopted to optimise the performance of the pavement. This will be economical only if large volumes are produced at a time, again leading to the need for larger stages in the programme."

8. Three-Co, particularly bearing it in mind that its tenure was to expire during 1988, rejected the first proposal as far too expensive. The costs were then said to be of the order of $1.8 million. Instead of adopting any of the proposals as such, the Company opted to continue with "repairs", substantially in the past. As a result, in a period of some six months concluding early in 1986, a contractor repaved a substantial area; and remade the rail crossing. The latter undertaking necessitated the construction of a temporary rail crossing nearby. All costs incurred were claimed as tax deductions.

9. In its return of income for the year ended 30 June 1986 the Applicant had claimed a tax deduction in the sum of $150,000 for "repairs and maintenance - paving". The claim was originally allowed but later the Commissioner issued an amended assessment. By his adjustment sheet accompanying the Notice of Amended Assessment the Commissioner advised that he had now disallowed (inter alia):

Paving $118,664

Rail siding $ 12,779

Rail Crossing Approaches $ 8,653

Temporary Ramp $ 5,053

By Notice of further Amended Assessment which issued on 27 January 1988 the Commissioner allowed in full the repairs to the rail siding ($12,779) and further amounts of $1,200 and $691, leaving as the amounts in dispute:

Pavement $118,664

Further Paving Repairs at Premises $ 7,453 Repairs to Rail Siding Approaches $ 4,362

$130,479

10. Of the $8,653 originally identified in the Commissioner's adjustment sheet as "rail crossing approaches", $1,200 was identified as "repairs to the rail siding" by the Commissioner and allowed; with the balance being attributed to "further paving repairs of premises". I am satisfied that the work which remains in dispute is "paving" which is not to be distinguished from the principal item of "paving". Of the $5,053 originally identified by the Commissioner as "temporary ramp", $691 described by the Commissioner as "construction of a temporary crossing" was allowed and the balance of $4,362 was described by the Commissioner as "repairs to rail siding approaches". I am satisfied that the work which remains in dispute relates to work done on the approaches to the rail crossing and is distinct from the work in the storage yard area and is associated more closely with the access over the railway line.

Repaying the Storage Area

11. I find that a single contractor was engaged to repave a large area: approximately one-third of the uncovered area of the site. The principal difference between that particular undertaking and the earlier works of repair was in the circumstance that the surface area to be repaved was substantially greater. I find that there was no formal specification prepared to define the works to be done, and that the method used was to remove the existing bitumen; to repack he sub-surface using the previously existing fill and some small amount of newly delivered material (c.20M3); to compact to some degree the sub-surface materials; and to resurface with asphaltic concrete. I find that the surface was laid with a sufficient slope to enable water which had previously tended to lie on the surface to run off - to that extent it overcame a pre-existing problem. I am not able to make findings identifying precisely differences between the thickness of bitumen, or of sub-surface packing or of the bitumen specification. Neither side chose to call the contractor or any other person who might have spoken precisely of such matters.

12. The Applicant might have called its former manager who had been on site when the works were done and who had supervised them. The Applicant might have called the contractor or his workers. It did not do either. Instead it relied on the evidence of its chief executive who, during the relevant period, had visited the site on a weekly basis and who was aware in general terms and by casual observation of the work being done and who was responsible for authorising it.

13. Nor did the Commissioner seek to call any witness on these matters. Instead, at the close of the case for the Applicant the Commissioner by his Counsel indicated his willingness, if the Tribunal requested it, to call an appropriate representative of the contractor to the stand. That was put on the basis that it would be done if it would assist the Tribunal. The invitation reflected the view that proceedings before this Tribunal are not adversarial in character. Despite the circumstance that such a view is expressed from time to time I took the view that, at least when the parties are represented - as here - by Counsel, it is for the parties to determine how to present their respective cases and that this was not a situation in which the Tribunal should take upon itself the nomination of witnesses to be called by the Commissioner. Even so, the Commissioner's invitation having been extended, Counsel for the Applicant was invited to express his views on the matter. As he indicated that his client was prepared to rest its case upon the evidence it had presented I chose not to avail myself of the invitation from the Commissioner.

  1. One result is that it is not possible to be as precise in findings about the comparative dimensions of the original pavement and the substituted pavement as one might have been had such witnesses been called. So it is that, if it was necessary for the Applicant to have success in these matters for the Applicant to establish that the result achieved with the new pavement was a pavement which was not different in its dimensions to the pavement originally installed, it would fail. So too in relation to the bitumen specification. However, I am positively satisfied that, although the new result might have been different from the old, it was not so by reason of any substantial difference in dimensions or specification. I am satisfied on the probabilities that the new pavement, assuming that it was "better", was only so by reason of the more effective laying and consolidation of the sub-surface materials and the improved laying of a slightly thicker and/or better bitumen on the surface.
  2. I am confirmed in that conclusion by the evidence of subsequent events. Three-Co continued in possession under the lease until October 1988 and held on thereafter with the consent of its landlord until March 1989. The site was inspected by the Applicant's witnesses in April and July 1989. Both gave evidence of further breaking up which had occurred but they described what they saw in terms which persuaded me that the rate of breaking up in the later four years had not been greater than that in the -fifteen or so years prior to repaving.

16. When the Commissioner forwarded a letter attending the Notice of Partial Disallowance and thereby sought to explain the adjustments which he had made, he said of the items which remained in dispute:

"These repairs were undertaken to remedy defects in the sub-soil beneath the paving which were in existence at the time of commencement of operations of the taxpayer company at the site. In addition, these repairs are considered to be improvements to the premises and are therefore of a capital nature and not allowable as a deduction in terms of Section 53 of the Act."

At the hearing he defended the assessments on those grounds.

"INITIAL REPAIRS"

17. There can be no doubt that:

"when a thing is bought for use as a capital asset in the taxpayer's business (and) it is not in good order and suitable for use in the way intended, the cost of putting it in order suitable for use is part of the cost of its acquisition, not a cost of its maintenance "

(Per Windeyer J. in.W. Thomas & Co. Pty Limited v F.C.T. [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58 at 72). On the other hand, as his Honour recognised,

"Expenditure upon repairs is properly attributed to revenue account when the repairs are for the maintenance of an income-producing capital asset. Maintenance involves the periodic repair of defects that are the result of normal wear and tear in operation. It is an expense of a revenue nature when it is to repair defects arising from the operations of the person who incurs it". (ibit page 72).

His Honour thought that

"the principle (that what are commonly referred to as 'initial repairs' should be an expense of capital) is obvious without the need for any supporting authority" (ibit page 73).

18. For my part I would have thought it also obvious that repairs carrying out some 15 years after acquisition and after some 15 years of intense use were hardly appropriately to be described by the term "initial repairs". The defects rectified by the outlay here in question were not such as, to use the words of Woodhouse J. in Collector of Inland Revenue, Cook Islands v A.B. Donald Limited [(1965) 9 AITR 501 at 506]:

"have matured by the time of purchase by the new owner".

19. In my view the position here was simply that when the Applicant commenced operations on the site, the site was in a condition ready for use for the purposes intended. Nothing needed to be done in order to make it useable or suitable for use. Further, nothing needed to be done so soon after acoulisition that it should be considered as something which needed to be carried out at point of acquisition. Rather the situation was that the property was in a condition fit to be used for the purposes for which it was used throughout the next 15 years. It was not in perfect condition. It had deteriorated somewhat from the condition in which it had been established initially. That it was not of such strong construction as to have a potential life equal to pavements of stronger construction - pavements such as the concrete proposed by the engineers - is no answer. Some things are more suitable for use than others. Some things are more suited to economic use than others. But the pavement in question was fit for use, and was used, and moreover was extensively used over a prolonged period. That alone provides ample evidence that it was fit for use in the sense spoken of by the Courts in considering such questions.

20. The Commissioner's argument was that there were deficiencies in existence at the commencement of occupation which ultimately made necessary the repairs. I reject that argument for that is to say - incorrectly - that repairs occasioned by an ultimate failure brought on by poor design, or poor materials, or poor worksmanship are not to be allowable as revenue expenses of "repair"; and that repairs occasioned by misuse or excessive use of well designed equipment, or well constructed equipment, fabricated from good materials is not to be so allowable. I know of no such authority as supports any such proposition. In my view the repairs carried out some 15 years after the commencement of operations on the site were not in any sense "initial repairs".

IMPROVEMENTS

21. Of greater force is the argument for the Commissioner about the expenditure constituting such an "improvement" as to give to the relevant outlay the character of capital rather than revenue. But the authorities are clear. As Windeyer, J said:-

"Repair involves a restoration of a thing to a condition it formerly had without changing its character. But in the case of a thing considered from the point of view of its use as distinct from it appearance, it is restoration of efficiency and function rather than exact repetition of formal material that is significant. Whether or not work done upon a thing is aptly described as a repair of that things is thus a question of fact and degree (ibid page 72)." (Emphasis added)

  1. The claimed repair may effect such a change in form, or material, or function as to make what is repaired so improved as to change its character - to make it something new. But the character of the thing repaired is not changed by the mere circumstance that, after the repair, it is in better condition than it was previously. If a timber tread in a stair fails, that may be the result of poor design, or bad workmanship, or defective timber. To more skillfully affix the next tread, or to use better quality timber for the next tread, is only to repair the stairway - not to bring into existence an improvement or something new. Nor will a better design automatically result in a change in the character of what has been improved by the better design. The improved design may call for timber of a different kind or specification in which case the judgment of fact and degree may confirm that it is only a repair. On the other hand the change may be so complete that the result is considered to be so improved and to have effected such a change in character as to result in a new stair rather than a repaired stair.
  2. When one comes to consider the application of those principles to the facts established, one cannot be satisfied that the bitumen was not of superior composition; was not laid in greater thickness; was not better laid; and was not laid over a more stable foundation. Nor could one be persuaded that the foundations upon which the bitumen were not similarly of superior quality. But for all that, the pavement remains of the same character as that which preceded it. It was a bitumen surface. Nor did the circumstance that the surface was now slightly sloped enough to enable a water run-off rather than the pooling and collection of water, sufficient to change the character of the surface.

24. In my view there was no such alteration to the character of that which had become deficient through use as to warrant describing the works of rectification as anything other than a repair and restoration of a pre-existing facility.

THE RAIL CROSSING

25. The evidence about the condition of the rail crossing before and after its remaking was quite imprecise. That was hardly surprising in that the only witness to give evidence in relation to it was the chief executive whose experience of it, both before and after the remaking, was merely to travel across it by car on his weekly visits to the site. All that one can say with certainty is that the road sloped up to the nearer rail and fell away from the far rail; that the section between he rails was filled; and that the surfaces of the three sections were bitumenised; and that after the remaking that condition superficially remained. Beyond that it is not possible to say. It was suggested in cross examination that iron rails may have been substituted for timber beams in the remaking. Perhaps they were. As the evidence does not enable me to make such a finding positively it must suffice to say that, if success for the Applicant depends upon persuading me that there was no such substitution, then the Applicant must fail.

26. In face of those findings the conclusion I have reached in relation to the rail crossing is that the evidence is insufficient to persuade me that there has not been an "improvement", such as to make the cost of the works capital in character. To that extent the decision of the Commissioner upon the assessment under review will be upheld.

THE TEMPORARY CROSSING

27. The evidence as to the temporary crossing was quite imprecise. It did not go beyond establishing that in the period of reconstruction of the principal rail crossing alternative provision had to be made for ingress and egress to the inner site over the rail at a point nearby. I find that it was done and I find that the works carried out had no other purpose than to provide a means of access to the site for a short period during which the access ordinarily used was not available. If any portion of the cost remained disallowed, I would allow it. As, on the findings I have made, the only cost ($691) has been wholly allowed, it is not necessary to go further.

ADDITIONAL TAX

28. When the first amended assessment issued in May 1987, the Applicant became liable to pay additional tax pursuant to the provisions of Section 223 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The amount for which the company was liable, assuming that the assessment was not excessive, was $149,285.40: double the tax avoided ($509,858.02 less $415,215.32 = $74,642.70). The penalty so provided for by the Parliament is so harsh as" to rarely be appropriate. In recognition of that, the Parliament has provided that the Commissioner may exercise a discretion to remit the penalties so determined in whole or in part. In my view, the Commissioner has an obligation to consider the exercise of that discretion and in my experience he invariably does so. In the present instance he did so and reduced the additional tax assessed for incorrect return to $16,022.

29. But what the Commissioner does not often do and what he ought to do, as a simple matter of fairness and of clear communication, is to advise taxpayers at the point of assessment just how it is that he has exercised his discretion to remit. Instead, despite the circumstance that the figure for which he levies additional tax is ordinarily the subject of calculation, and of a calculation made in accordance with principles known to the Commissioner, those calculations are hardly ever made known to taxpayers. All that a taxpayer, such as the present Applicant, can discern upon a perusal of such a notice of assessment as it received is that a sum (in this instance $16,022) has been levied; and that it is "additional tax for incorrect return" - although in this instance advice was also provided that:

"penalty calculated to 14 April 1987."

30. The imposition of additional tax was challenged. The challenge was in part successful and when the second amended assessment issued the Applicant was notified that additional tax had been reduced to $11,149; "as a result of the allowance of the - expenditures incurred in relation to the rail siding and temporary rail crossing". The letter went on to advise:

"Note also that additional tax has not been imposed in relation to repairs to the paving of the premises."

  1. As a result of questions raised at the hearing it now appears that what had occurred was as follows. The evidence established that the net increase in taxable income effected by the first amended assessment was $205,745. But, after excluding $118,664 in relation to the "pavement", additional tax was calculated on a residue of $87,081 giving rise to tax avoided at $40,057.26. A culpability factor of 40% was applied resulting in an assessment of $16,022. Despite the earlier reference to penalty being calculated "to 14 April 1987", there was no period in relation to which a compensatory factor was assessed.
  2. When the second amended assessment issued, taxable income was reduced by a further $14,670. But instead of only reducing additional tax by an amount appropriate to that reduction, the figure for penalizable tax avoided was reduced from $87,081 by $26,485 - the amount of the other disputed deductions.

33. As a result it can now be seen that:

(a) No additional tax for incorrect return has been imposed in relation to the issues which have been canvassed in the earlier parts of these reasons for decision; but that

(b) the additional tax imposed wholly relates to items not in dispute.

As there is nothing in relation to the disputed items to be remitted and as there is nothing before me on the objection or upon the evidence to challenge the imposition of additional tax in relation to undisputed items, that imposition shall be confirmed.

CONCLUSION

34. As a result of the foregoing the order of the Tribunal will be that the assessment of taxable income of the company for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 shall be reduced by $126,117.
