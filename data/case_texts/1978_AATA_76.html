English and Commissioner for Superannuation [1978] AATA 76 (27 November 1978)

Last Updated: 14 March 2007

Superannuation - Restoration of widow's pension -
Relevance of delay to date of restoration -
Administrative delay a "special" circumstance" -

Notification of change in legislation -

Superannuation Act 1922 S.48AA, Superannuation Act 1976 s.154,

Superannuation Amendment Act 1976 s.28, Administrative

Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.29

Ivy Edith English

The Commissioner for Superannuation

No. 78/116

AAT Decision No 116

R.K. Todd (Senior Member), V.J. Skermer, W.R. Tattersall (Members)

2 7 November 1978

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

No. 78/116

Re: IVY EDITH ENGLISH

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The decision of the Tribunal is that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, acting pursuant to s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to vary the decision taken on his behalf on 17 June 1977 directing that the entitlement of Mrs. Ivy Edith English to a widow's pension be restored pursuant to S.48AA of the Superannuation Act 1922 with effect from 16 June 1977 by specifying a date of effect of 16 April 1977, be set aside, and that in lieu thereof there be specified a date of effect of 1 July 1976.

DATED this 27th day of November 1978.

REASONS FOR DECISION

This application concerns the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation (hereafter called "the Commissioner") on re-consideration undertaken pursuant to s154 of the Superannuation Act 1976, to specify 16 April 1977 as the date from which the applicant's entitlement to widow's pension, previously cancelled on re-marriage, should be restored by his direction as provided for by S.48AA of the Superannuation Act 1922.

Concurrently with the legislative changes introduced in 1976 by force of which the "new" Superannuation scheme for Commonwealth employees was introduced with effect from 1 July 1976 (see Superannuation Act 1976), the Superannuation Amendment Act 1976 (which was assented to on 7 May 1976) effected a number of amendments to the Superannuation Act 1922 with effect likewise from 1 July 1976. For present purposes it is enough to refer to the amendment effected by s28, namely the insertion into the Act of 1922 of a new s48AA. It should be stated that prior to this amendment the right of a widow of a pensioner to receive a pension ceased upon her remarriage (see s47 of the Act of 1922). It should also be stated that no such limitation was imposed by the terms of the "new" scheme. S 48AA is as follows:

"48AA.(1) Where -

(a) a widow's or widower's entitlement to pension

under sub-section (1) of section 46, or sab-section

(1) of section 47, of this Act has ceased, before

1 July 1976, upon her or his remarriage; and

(b) the widow or widower makes application to the Commission

for Superannuation for the restoration of the pension

and the Commissioner is satisfied that the widow or

widower is in necessitous circumstances or that the

restoration of the pension is otherwise warranted,

the Commissioner may direct that the widow's or widower's entitlement to pension be restored with effect from such date as the Commissioner specifies and, upon such a direction being given, the widow or widower again becomes entitled to pension from that date at a rate determined by the Commissioner (being a rate that does not exceed the rate at which the pension would have been payable if the entitlement had not ceased).

(2) The date specified by the Commissioner under

sub-section (1) of this section in relation to the

restoring of a widow's or widower's entitlement

to pension shall not, unless the Commissioner is satisfied that special circumstances exist that justify an earlier date being so specified, be a date earlier than the date of the direction, and shall not, in any event, be a date earlier than 1 July 1976.

(3) A direction by the Commissioner under this

section shall be in writing."

In view of the way in which this application has developed it is necessary to set out the chronology of events in some detail.

On 1 March 1970 the first husband of the applicant died leaving two children of the marriage, one having been adopted.

On 4 May 1974 the applicant remarried and by force of the statutory provisions already referred to her widow's pension ceased. Her entitlement to the pension on behalf of the two children continued.

On 1 July 1976 it became open to the Commissioner having regard to the above stated provisions of S.48AA of the Act of 1922 to direct the restoration of pension to a remarried widow of a pensioner pursuant to the abovementioned provisions of s 48AA of the Superannuation Act 1922, should he be satisfied of her circumstances being necessitous or that the restoration of the pension be otherwise warranted, on application in that behalf being made to him by the widow.

While it was not made a matter of proof, the Commissioner's representative very fairly pointed out to us that an advice was sent to persons in receipt of pensions in July 1976 which outlined the new pension increase provisions operating under the 1976 legislation, but no specific mention was made of the matter of restoration in that advice. It did apparently contain a reference to the fact that the legislation for the "new" scheme provided for the continuation of a pension on remarriage. While there was no proof whatsoever that this advice was received by the present applicant, the important point is that an advice was apparently able to be sent to persons in receipt of pension, but that it contained no relevant reference to restoration.

In about early February 1977 a communication was sent by the Commissioner to persons in receipt of pensions, including the applicant. It apparently took the form of a yellow card. The exact terms of that communication could not be established or agreed upon in evidence, but its contents led to the applicant writing to the Commissioner as follows on 2 March 1977:

"To whom it may concern,

Could you please send further information on Benefits for spouses and children as stated on Yellow card received also for additional basis for entitlements."

No answer to this letter having been received the applicant wrote again to a similar effect on 19 April 1977:

"To whom it may concern,

Could you please send me more information regarding additional basis for entitlements also for Benefits for spouses and children re yellow slip you sent.

I have written earlier - no reply."

On 5 May 1977 the Commissioner wrote to the applicant as follows:

"Dear Mrs. English,

I refer to your letters of 2 March and 19 April 1977 and wish to advise that under the provisions of new legislation that became effective from 1 July 1976 it is possible that your pension may be restored. However, before restoring the pension the Commissioner for Superannuation must be satisfied that necessitous circumstances exist or that the restoration of pension is otherwise warranted.

2. In order that your financial situation may be assessed, you would need to show in a Statutory Declaration details of total household gross income and expenditure, the amount should also include the gross income of your present husband. These details should also include the amount and nature of any Social Security benefits received.

3.You will appreciate that I am seeking this information for the sole purpose of establishing any entitlement to benefit under the Superannuation Act and you may be assured that any information provided will be treated in the strictest confidence.
4.I have enclosed an application form and a Statutory Declaration form. I should be grateful if you would complete the application form and return it together with any other supporting documents as soon as possible.

5. If you do not wish to proceed with your application would you please let me know as confirmation that you received this letter. In any correspondence with this office please quote the reference number 111890."

It is plain that the Commissioner sent a communication of some kind to the applicant, informing her of the new situation in regard to the possibility of the restoration of her pension, and that he thought that it was his function to send such a communication. The applicant was in receipt of a pension, though not on her own behalf, from the fund which it was a responsibility of the Commissioner to administer. She had previously been in receipt of a widow's pension from that fund in her own right, and now a change in the law had occurred which created the potentiality of her being again a recipient of a pension of the self-same kind as that which had lapsed upon her remarriage. That change in the law, while not effective until 1 July 1976, was contained in legislation which, as previously stated, had been Assented to as early as 7 May 1976.

In response to the letter of 5 May the applicant on 17 May completed the application form which she had received and made a Statutory Declaration as requested in relation to her financial position. Her declaration in this respect was as follows:

"1. I am in receipt of a veterans affairs pension only, because my husband aged sixty in November 11th this year is a T.P.I.

2.Our joint bank a/c is $2350.00 0/D.
3.Bank card $470.00 as at present in arrears.
4.A new car was necessary because of my husbands disability and is on time payment = (outstanding $3420.00 at $118.00 per month.
5.I am in receipt of $80.00 per fortnight for Susan my daughter and Raymond my adopted son who is now not living with us. My daughter Susan lives with me at all times."

On 17 June a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation made a direction in the following form:

"I direct that under section 48AA of the Superannuation Act 1922, being satisfied that Mrs Ivy Edith English is in necessitous circumstances, that her entitlement to pension of $5382.04 per annum be restored with effect from 16 June 1977. This pension is in addition to any pension payable in respect of children.

COMMENT

Had Mr. Ward survived the family would have enjoyed an income of $15,537 per annum. Mrs. English's present husband is a TPI pensioner and the total household income is substantially below this figure."

On 5 July the direction that the applicant's pension be restored was conveyed to her by letter. By this time however the appropriate figure had, by application of the formula for automatic annual adjustment for cost of living increases, increased to $6114 per annum. On 29 July the applicant wrote to the Commissioner in the following terms:

"Dear Sir,

I am in receipt of your letter of July 25, 1977, advising that my pension has been restored at the rate of $6114 per annum with effect from June 16, 1977.

In view of the fact that the new legislation became effective from July 1, 1976, I should be obliged if you could advise as to why my entitlement should not be a retrospective payment from that date as any delay in applying has been no fault of my own and further delays have been caused by the long delay in your dealing with my application; again no fault of mine.

I believe I am entitled to the pension from July 1, 1976, and again I ask why you are granting same from a date nearly one year later.

Yours faithfully,

(signed) I.E. English"

There was delay in answering this letter and a reply was not sent until 17 November. To a degree the effect of the delay was reduced by the Commissioner accepting the letter of 29 July "as a formal request for reconsideration of your case under s154 of the Superannuation Act 1976". The letter went on: "This means that all aspects of your case are being re-examined including the basis on which the previous decision was made. The decision in respect of the reconsideration is expected shortly and you will be advised immediately."

By 6 February 1978 the applicant had not received any further communication and wrote as follows to the Commissioner:

"Dear Sir,

I am in receipt of your letter dated 17.11.77 regarding my request for re-consideration under section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976.

I am upset at the delay and inconsiderate manner that the Dept. has taken.

My advised legal request is positive in that the Act was passed in 1976 and having been accepted there could not possibly be undue delay in settling my claim."

On 17 May 1978 the Commissioner advised the applicant by letter of the decision made on reconsideration:

"Dear Mrs English,

I refer to your request for reconsideration of the decision to grant you a widow's pension from 16 June 1977, and not an earlier date.

2.On 12 May 1978 the Commissioner for Superannuation reconsidered your case and varied the previous decision by directing that your widow's pension be restored from 16 April 1977.
3.The Commissioner gave the following reasons for the decision:-

'(a) Sub-section 48AA(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 provides, inter alia, that I may direct that a widow's entitlement to pension be restored from such date as I specify. Subsection 48AA(2) requires that the date specified by me under sub-section 48AA(1) shall not, unless I am satisfied that special circumstances exist that justify an earlier date being so specified, be a date earlier than the date of the direction and shall not, in any event, be a date earlier than 1 July 1976.

(b) I am satisfied that special circumstances exist that warrant the commencement of pension two months earlier than the date specified in the direction of 17 June 1977.'

4. The Commissioner also stated:-'By reason of pressure of work there was a delay, beyond normal processing time, of approximately six weeks before the office responded to Mrs English's first enquiry of 2 March 1977 and a further delay of approximately two weeks above normal processing time in the consideration of her application before the direction of 17 June 1977 was made.*

5. Arrangements are being made to adjust your pension as quickly as possible and you will be further advised in this regard.

6. I am enclosing a copy of Section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 for your information."

It is appropriate at this stage to refer to s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 which reads as follows:

"154.(1) In this section-

"decision" has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975;

"decision of the Commissioner" means a decision of the Commissioner, or a delegate of the Commissioner, under this Act or under the superseded Act.

(2) A person who is affected by a decision of the Commissioner and who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by notice in writing given to the Commissioner within a period of 30 days after the date on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision.

(3) There shall be set out in the request the ground on which the request is made.

(4) Upon receipt of the request, the Commissioner shall reconsider the decision, and may either confirm the decision or vary the decision in such manner as he thinks fit.

(5) The Commissioner shall, by notice in writing to the person who made the request, inform the person of the result of his reconsideration of the decision.

(6) Application may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of decisions of the Commissioner (being decisions,that have been confirmed or varied under sub-section (4)) and, for the purposes of such a review, one of the non-presidential members constituting the Tribunal shall be an eligible employee or a pensioner."

What the applicant now did was to write again to the Commissioner, on 24 and 26 July 1978. The first of these letters is of some importance and should be set out in full:

"Dear Sir,

I refer to your correspondence in this matter particularly to your decision to restore my pension as from 16 April, 1977 in lieu of your earlier decision to restore it from the 16 June, 1977 and I refer particularly to the reasons for your decision.

I am not satisfied that you have justly exercised your discretion under the Act and I see no reason why the pension should not have been restored from 1 July, 1976 which is the earliest date under the Act which it could have been restored. I was unable to make application earlier than I did for restoration of the pension due to my ill health and the ill health of my husband. I cannot see any justification for not restoring the pension as from 1 July, 1976.

As you could appreciate from what I have said so far, I am certainly an aggrieved person as defined in the Act and I wish to lodge an appeal with the Administrative Appeal Tribunal but I note that the time prescribed for making that appeal has expired. I have been unable to seek legal advice in this matter until the present time because of my considerable ill health and I would be grateful if you would consent to an appeal being lodged outside the time prescribed by the Administrative Appeals Act.

Would you please advise as soon as possible so that an appeal can in fact be lodged.

I also wish to elect under the Act for commutation of the pension to a lump sum payment. At this stage I am unaware of exactly what Section of the Act my pension was granted under and I am therefore unable to direct explicitly my election to a certain Section of the Act. However, you may treat this letter as an election for commutation of the pension to a lump sum payment and I ask that you exercise your discretion under Section 157(1) and (2) of the Act to treat the election as recognised notwithstanding that it is outside the prescribed time limit of one month. My considerable physical ill health has made it impossible for me to fully appreciate all of my rights under the Act and it has just been impossible for me to do anything about this at any earlier time.

At no time have I been made aware of the level of contributions that my late husband made to the fund and as to whether pension which was paid to me included any additional contribution pension.

Incidentally, although I understand that ignorance of the law is no excuse, I was not aware of the 1976 Amendment to the Superannuation Act until shortly before I made application for restoration of the pension under the Act and I do feel that it would have been proper for the Commissioner to notify widows and eligible employees entitled to pension of those amendments so that application could be made properly.

I would appreciate your reply without delay.

Yours faithfully,

(Mrs. I.E. English)"
The letter of 26 July enclosed a medical certificate and a somewhat cryptic note relating to some aspects of the applicant's financial circumstances. The medical certificate, dated 18 July 1978, read as follows:

"Mrs. Ivy English suffers from Ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia and a stenosed carotid artery.

She is advised to have further assessment of the blocked artery and possible surgery to correct that in the near future."

It is fair to say that until the receipt of these letters the applicant had not placed before the Commissioner any facts said to constitute the "special circumstances" the existence of which the Commissioner had to be satisfied if he were to specify a restoration date earlier than that which he ultimately adopted. She had of course supplied some facts in relation to her then financial situation as it stood on 17 May 1977, but as we understand the position these facts were taken into consideration simply for the purpose of establishing that the applicant was in necessitous circumstances so as to enable the making of a direction that the pension be restored. It has been seen that the Commissioner regarded the means of the applicant as placing her in such a situation as to lead to his being satisfied that she was in necessitous circumstances. But when the Commissioner came later to consider the matter of "special circumstances", the one matter which he took into account was the delay which had occurred in his office in the course of considering the application for restoration.

Having regard to the terms of s.29 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, an application under s l54(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 must be lodged with the Tribunal within 28 days after the decision is furnished to the applicant. The applicant, however, did not apply for review within the required 28 days. This was later corrected by a determination of a Deputy President of the Tribunal that the time for lodging the application should be extended to 16 August 1978, being the date upon which a further letter was received from the applicant. This letter made or re-iterated a number of complaints and comments, and included new matter, in that it set out a claim that the pension should be restored from 10 May 1974. It also repeated the claim for commutation of the pension. We think that we should refer to these two claims now. In the first place we have to find that the Tribunal is unable to deal with them, s154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 not having been complied with in that no request for re-consideration had been made to the Commissioner pursuant to ss 2 thereof. If such a request were made the Commissioner would be bound by s154(4) to reconsider his decision. The applicant's rights in relation to these two matters, we emphasise, therefore remain open. But we should not be taken as encouraging her to pursue them. The provisions of s 48AA of the Superannuation Act 1922 make it as clear as the English language can possibly make it that restoration of the pension could not be made effective from any date earlier than 1 July 1976. As far as commutation is concerned the matter seems almost as clear, although the demonstration thereof is in this case negative in character. We know of no provision in the Superannuation Act 1922 which would permit commutation, and the pension which was restored to the applicant was one under that Act. The Act of 1976 has no application in any event, but it may be noted that that Act provides for commutation in limited areas only, namely in the cases of Additional Age and Early Retirement pensions.

When the application came on for hearing, the applicant was not present. Her husband announced his appearance for her, and said that her health precluded her attendance. He referred to the medical certificate and said that he would be able to give evidence of the relevant facts in relation to her health and her means from his own knowledge. He indicated that the applicant's case for backdating the restoration of her pension rested on her means, on her state of health, and on her failure to be notified of her right to apply.

In evidence, Mr. English satisfied us that the applicant has been in a precarious state of health, certainly from 1975 and probably from 1974. She had an operation for double hernia in that year. Since the onset of high blood pressure she has, he said, been under constant sedation. We are prepared to accept, without going into further detail that her health is, and has been since 1 July 1976, very poor.

Turning to the applicant's means, the evidence given by Mr. English was not at all clear. What he said in substance was that a block of land was purchased at Rutherford, New South Wales, and a house built on it as a War Service home by the applicant and himself. A jointly owned home unit at Collaroy was sold. Taking into account various expenses associated with the home, the debts owing by the applicant and her husband at the date of the hearing were $30,900. Of this sum $5000 was represented by a loan from Mr. English's sister, which has since been "written off", by which we understand that she has forgiven repayment. Mr. English insisted that this situation should be seen as the end result of a course of action that had been embarked upon in 1975. As far as income was concerned, the only income was his TPI Pension.

More details were given, both in answer to questions put by the Tribunal and in cross-examination but we found them quite confusing. Mr. English said that he could not remember the figures but would submit them in written form. The representative of the Commissioner was content that further information should be submitted in this form, and in order to avoid the expense and inconvenience to the applicant and her husband of another hearing, it was agreed that such be submitted and that the Commissioner should be entitled to lodge a written submission, with a right of reply thereto granted to the applicant.

The applicant accordingly lodged further material as to her means, but we found it unilluminating. Indeed, in one respect it revived an attitude adopted by Mr. English at one stage of the hearing, namely that he was reluctant to expose all of the details of the applicant's and his means, for reasons that need not be gone into, but which to him appeared to be sufficient. So be it, for this attitude is one which the applicant is, through her representative, perfectly entitled to adopt. But to the extent that the case is sought to be made out partly upon the basis of the applicant's financial position, such reluctance does not help. On the whole we feel that we must conclude that the applicant's situation in this respect does no more than justify the conclusion that her circumstances were "necessitous" at the point of time in June 1977 when the Commissioner decided to direct restoration of the pension.

As far as her health is concerned, this factor, however regrettable for her, and however unfortunate a circumstance for her, is not a "special circumstance" isolated from her general financial position. It might have been relevant had she known of her rights in July 1976, but been prevented from applying for health reasons, or it might have been made relevant to her financial circumstances, but it was not.

The case thus turns, in our opinion, upon the relevance of the delay which occurred in informing the applicant of her right to apply for restoration. The Tribunal enquired, in the light of the fact that it was thought appropriate in February 1977 to advise someone in receipt of pension on behalf of children of the new arrangements, why it had not been thought appropriate to issue that advice earlier. For it seems clear enough that if the applicant had known of her rights on 1 July 1976 she would have applied immediately for restoration and the same would have been granted. The question thus is: If it was appropriate to let her know in February 1977, why did the office not let her know, in appropriate terms, in July 1976? The answer given by the Commissioner's representative was that it was probably because of all the other work that was involved at the time of changeover, that is to say it was not feasible-It was said that there is a very genuine endeavour to inform people as well as can be, that this was not an obligation but a service, and that it was just not possible to inform everybody of the change in the legislation. Yet the Tribunal notes that some changes were promptly pointed out. Of course, the restoration of pension, as was pointed out, could have applied in theory to persons who had remarried as far back as 1922. The administration was faced with the most difficult task of trying to cope with the workload that resulted from the major changes that occurred in 1976 in relation to contributors' pensions and to other areas of entitlement benefits. We were told that the Commissioner's Office has examined records for the past 15 years to endeavour to establish those cases that could possibly come within the ambit of s 48AA, but changes of address and so on have made this a very difficult task. In these circumstances it was contended that the legislation provides for the individual to make an application and that the situation was like that in relation to social security benefits whereunder benefits are paid from the date of application and not from the date when an entitlement had arisen.

The difficulties facing the Commissioner's Office are appreciated, but we think that the situation is not to be equated with one in which administration is dealing with the public at large, or with that more limited section of the public constituted by former recipients of widow's pension who have remarried, but whose whereabouts are unknown. This was rather a case in which the applicant was in receipt of pension, albeit not on her own behalf, and a potential entitlement arose in her case, on 1 July 1976, to restoration of the widow's pension. We think that she should, as someone currently associated with the payment of pension, have been alerted, and that if it was not administratively possible to do this until February 1977 then that is a "special circumstance". We do not think it is possible for administrative delay in processing the applicant's original application to be treated (as it in fact was) as a "special circumstance", but for administrative delay in advising those presently in contact with the office through receipt of pension for a child not to be so treated.

It was submitted that had Parliament intended that restoration should be backdated to 1 July 1976, the legislation would have said so, whereas the provision that was made was that for retrospective restoration there must be special circumstances and that there must first be an application for restoration. The provisions require that the Commissioner has then to be satisfied that there are necessitous circumstances, or that restoration is otherwise warranted, and even where there are necessitous circumstances it is provided that for restoration to be retrospective there must be special circumstances. For this reason it was submitted that special circumstances must be something rare and unique. Yet we must observe that administrative delay in handling this application was treated as such.

We think that the answer is that everything said in this submission was correct insofar as it related to those potential applicants for restoration who were not in contact with the Office. But as far as those in contact with the Office are concerned, the circumstances were in our opinion different and, in the relevant sense, special. The very use of the word special connotes a discretionary element, and we are much impressed in our consideration of the question whether the failure to notify was, in the present applicant's case a "special circumstance", by the following considerations:

(i) Had the applicant received an appropriate advice in July 1976, it is clear from her subsequent conduct that the applicant would have immediately responded.

(ii) It was conceded, by the sending of the communication in February 1977 that notification of the change in the legislation was appropriate;

(iii) It was conceded that the sending of such notification was the subject of administrative delay, however explicable that delay may have been;

(iv) It was conceded, in and by force of the decision made upon reconsideration, that administrative delay is capable of constituting a "special circumstance".

It will be obvious from what we have said that we have not found the matter as easy to determine as the case put for the applicant would have us find it, a case which of course could not be expected to know or understand the full extent of the difficulties facing the Office at the time of the changeover. But we are impressed by the view that in considering what is "special" we should not overlook the fact that, large as is the fund which is being administered by the Office, we are here dealing with a person who was a former recipient of a widow's pension, who was as from 1 July 1976 potentially a person who could again become a recipient, and who had since 1970 been a recipient of pension on behalf of children. We think that different considerations should apply to her from those apposite in the case of those potential applicants not in contact with the Office in relation to the fund, or in the case of the general public under social security legislation.

Once it is accepted that this applicant would have been entitled to restoration forthwith upon application, and once it is accepted, as we believe is quite plain from her conduct, that she would have responded immediately she became aware of the new provisions, then the only matters to stand in the way of an immediate restoration were her ignorance of the change (which should in our opinion have been brought to her attention) and administrative delay. Thus if she had known, and had applied, restoration would have been almost immediate. Perhaps there is a case for saying that there would inevitably have been some delay in any event, but in all the circumstances we think that we should direct restoration from 1 July 1976. We think that this is the only proper and fair decision having regard to the way the administrative process operated upon this applicant.

In her position then we are of opinion that special circumstances exist accordingly and that our decision should be that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, acting pursuant to s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to vary the decision taken on his behalf on 17 June 1977 directing that the entitlement of Mrs. Ivy Edith English to a widow's pension be restored pursuant to s 48AA of the Superannuation Act 1922 with effect from 16 June 1977 by specifying a date of effect of 16 April 1977, be set aside, and that in lieu thereof there be specified a date of effect of 1 July 1976.
