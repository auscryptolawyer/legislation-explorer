Re Taxation Appeals (No 138) and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 104 (23 March 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS (No. 138)

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. ST86/565

AAT No. 3389

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

R.A. Layton (Deputy President)

D.J. Trowse (Member)

Dr D.B. Williams (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - home study - Principal Education Officer in music - office in Education Department unavailable after 6.00 pm and at weekends - no sound-proofing facilities or musical equipment available - office space "open plan" - room in home unit dedicated to purposes of employment - deductions for rent.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.51

Thomas v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4094

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Faichney 72 ATC 4245

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth 81 ATC 4157

Lunnie v. Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia (1957-58) 100 CLR 478

Handley v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4,185

Swinford v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4,803

HEARING

ADELAIDE

23:3:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and in lieu thereof, the Tribunal decides that the taxpayer's claim of $795 should be allowed pursuant to sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

DECISION

At the conclusion of the hearing of this application for review, the Tribunal stated orally the terms of the decision intended to be made and the reasons therefor. Those reasons are set out below.

2. This is an application for review of a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation who disallowed the taxpayer's claim for the sum of $795 paid in respect of rent in the income year ended 30 June 1984, to be a deduction pursuant to the provisions of sub-s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

3. The facts giving rise to the decision are that the taxpayer, during the relevant income year, was an Acting Principal Education Officer employed by the Education Department. It was a senior position and there were no formal job or duty specifications for her work. The taxpayer worked in the Education Department building and was supplied with a desk, chair, cupboard, filing cabinet and telephone in a large open- space area. Within three to ten feet of her desk, there were three other employees also working at desks. The taxpayer's typing work was done through a typing pool. She had two subordinates who worked in a teachers' college which was situated in another building quite removed from the taxpayer's office.

4. The taxpayer gave detailed evidence of her employment duties and we unhesitatingly accept her evidence. Those duties included development of music curriculum for secondary school students, supplying information re sources for Senior and Principal Education Officers in music, organising conferences for professional development of music teachers and assessment of the performance of music teachers and their pupils in the secondary school system.

5. In order for her to carry out those duties, it was necessary for the applicant to arrange, attend or, in some instances, chair conferences, seminars, committees and sub- committee meetings. It was also necessary for her to attend at secondary schools and teachers' colleges in the city and the country. The taxpayer was also required to meet with Regional and Senior Education Officers. The taxpayer estimated that approximately 50% of her work was performed outside of the Education Department office.

6. Further, it was necessary for her to work out-of-hours in order to complete her duties and her office in the Education Department shut at 6.00 pm on weekdays. She could not use that office after that time, or on weekends.

7. The taxpayer also gave evidence, which we accept, that part of her work included listening to music tapes, records and playing sheet music on either the piano or the violin. This work was sent to her by music publishers. The taxpayer said that whilst it was not specifically written down that this was part of her employment, she was required to listen to and play such music in order to be an information source for music teachers and Education Officers, as well as to make recommendations for use of such material for curriculum purposes. The taxpayer said that it was necessary for her to play the sheet music on a musical instrument, in her case either a piano or a violin, in order to evaluate the music.

8. No piano was made available to the applicant within the Education Department and no sound-proof room was provided in the Department in which she could play the violin. Whilst it may have been possible for her to make a specific arrangement with a music centre at a school, such centres are in high usage by students; a convenient time would have been difficult to arrange and, further, the taxpayer would have had transport difficulties.

9. The taxpayer also gave evidence that it was necessary for her to assess and evaluate student compositions and arrange ments, particularly those for the violin, and she could only do this by performing the works on a musical instrument. Again, neither an instrument nor a room were available to her at the Education Department.

10. Because of extensive country travel, it was necessary for the taxpayer to perform some work, which we understood to be paperwork, after office hours.

11. The taxpayer also gave evidence that in order for her to do her work with some degree of quiet, enabling her to attain a better level of concentration than was available to her at the Education Department office, she worked in a study specifically set up for her needs in her leased home unit. Not only was this work at home permitted by her employers, it was approved and encouraged by them. There were some 15 to 20 days in the relevant year when she specifically planned to perform her work at home and this was indicated in the diary kept by her as a departmental requirement for planning work locations in advance. The only prerequisite prior to arranging such work at home was a notification to her seniors and the switchboard as to her where abouts so that she could be contacted by telephone if required.

12. The applicant began renting her unit in April 1983, being a time when she was already employed as Acting Principal Education Officer and knew its requirements. In her view, it was essential for her to have a home study in order to perform her employment duties. She therefore rented a two bedroom unit, the plan of which is annexed to her income tax return. She uses the main bedroom as a study and in that 3.25 by 3.6 metre room there are three bookshelves with books, a record player, stereo, desk, chair, piano, computer, filing cabinet, sheet music, tapes and telephone. She uses this room as her work room when doing out- of-hours work which she estimated to be on average two hours each work day after 6.00 pm and also a number of hours on each weekend. This is in addition to the days in which she worked at home during the day. She also used that room occasionally for typing private letters and for some personal reading. She used the piano, stereo and record player in the study for listening to the tapes and records and playing the sheet and other music adverted to earlier in these Reasons.

13. The taxpayer gave evidence, which we accept, that if she had not listened to and evaluated those tapes, records, sheet music and assessed students' work, she would not have been performing the work which she was employed to do. Further, we accept that the only place at which she could perform that work was in her study at the unit.

14. The issue before the Tribunal is whether the rent payable in respect of the applicant's study room has been incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and, even if it has been, whether, pursuant to the exception contained in sub- s.51(1) of the Act, such payment is a loss or outgoing of a capital, private or domestic nature.

15. Sub-section 51(1) of the Act has been the subject of considerable legal interpretation and, in particular, in the context of rooms in a house which have been set aside as an office or study.

16. The case of Re Thomas v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4094, was concerned with a barrister who used one room of his house as a study and claimed a proportion of the interest paid on a bank loan to make additions to his house including the study, as a deduction pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Act. It was held by the High Court that the extensions, which included the study, could not be regarded as part of business premises. The payment of interest relating to the extensions was an outgoing "of a capital, private or domestic nature". As Walsh, J. said in that case:>

"In my opinion, it did not lose that character merely

because the appellant, like most professional men, did

some of his work at home, or because he used one of

the added rooms for that purpose."

(72 ATC 4094 at 4097)

17. In the case of Faichney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4245, a research scientist who, although not required by his terms of employment to work at home, did so in a bedroom which was set up as a study. Evidence was given that it was necessary and required of him to do out-of-hours work in order for him to perform his work properly. It was inconvenient to do that work at his place of employment. He claimed a proportion of electricity, mortgage interest and depreciation as deductions pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Act. It was held by the High Court that the claim for the mortgage interest and depreciation should be refused. Mason, J. stated:

"To my mind, a study in a taxpayer's home, no matter

how great the extent of its dedication in point of use

to the pursuit of those activities from which the

taxpayer earns his income, is part of that home.

Expenditure incurred in the erection of the study or

in its renovation is as much an outgoing of a capital,

private or domestic nature as expenditure on any part

of the home."

(72 ATC 4245 at 4248-4249)

And, further, Mason, J. stated:

".... a study does not cease to be part of a tax

payer's home because it is used by the taxpayer for

the pursuit of activities from which he earns his

income."

(72 ATC 4245 at 4249)

That decision also discusses the meaning of losses or outgoings of a "private or domestic nature". Mason, J. made the comment that many expenses of a private or domestic character relate to the taxpayer personally, but it does not follow that because the purpose of the expense is to provide suitable working conditions for the taxpayer, it must fall within the exception of sub- s.51(1) of the Act.

18. The interpretation of sub-s.51(1) of the Act in relation to home studies took a different emphasis in the decision in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth 81 ATC 4157. That case concerned a barrister in a city practice who used a room at home as a study. This room was rented from a family trust which owned the home and the balance of the rooms in the home were used by himself and his family for domestic purposes. It was held by a majority of the High Court consisting of Mason, Murphy and Wilson JJ. that the payment of such rental was not an allowable deduction pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Act. In the course of that case, Wilson, J. referred with approval to the case of Lunnie v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 CLR 478 in which the words "in gaining or producing income" were regarded as referring not necessarily to the purpose for which an item of expenditure was incurred, but rather to the essential character of the expenditure itself. This is sometimes referred to as the "essential characteristic" criterion or test.

19. Wilson, J., after referring to the criterion, expressed a preference for that criterion instead of the "use" criterion used by Canadian authorities in interpreting similar Canadian legislation. Wilson, J. further noted that the study used by the taxpayer was an integral part of the house. In addition, His Honour commented that there was no compulsion for the barrister to work at home; instead, "like many professional people, he finds it convenient to (work at home)". There were no clients who attended at his study, which was mostly used for research and reading. His home was not his business premises. It was concluded by the majority that the outgoings for the licence fee were not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. Further, payment of the licence fee was a payment made "of or belonging to the home, house or household" and therefore was an outgoing or loss of a domestic nature within the exception to sub-s.51(1) of the Act.

20. In the case of Handley v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 165, a barrister bought a house because it included a study room which he used 20 hours per week in an 80-100 hour week, for 45 weeks in a year. He claimed deduction for a proportion of mortgatge interest, water rates and insurance premiums. The majority of the High Court rejected his claim. Wilson, J., who formed part of the majority, took the view that the study remained an integral part of the taxpayer's home, and that the expenses should not be apportioned because they would have been losses or outgoings in any event, regardless of its use as a study. Murphy, J., also in the majority, took the view that the claim was rejected because the study was not a place of gaining assessable income in a real sense because it was not a place of business. In addition, he was also of the view that the outgoings claimed were of a domestic nature.

21. The different emphases placed on the interpretation of sub-s.51(1) in all of these cases relating to home offices, was evaluated and rationalised in the case of Swinford v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803 by David Hunt, J. of the New South Wales Supreme Court. That case involved a self-employed radio and television script writer who claimed a portion of rent for a second bedroom of a flat which she rented and which was used for writing. She was employed on a contract basis by two particular contractors, neither of whom provided her with an office. The claimed room in her flat was the only place in which she could write. David Hunt, J. in concluding that the claim for rent should be allowed, made the following comments. Firstly, that the cases of Handley, Forsyth and Lunnie (supra) are not authority for the proposition that taxpayers may not obtain deductions for a home office. Further, that whilst in the case of Forsyth (supra), Wilson, J. considered it preferable to apply the "essential charactistic" test, this did not mean that the "use" test used was irrelevant. On the contrary, the "use" test may be relevant in determining the essential character of expenditure using the essential characteristic test.

22. In granting the claim, David Hunt, J. noted that the taxpayer worked at her home office, not as a matter of con venience, as had the taxpayers in Thomas, Faichney, Forsyth and Handley (supra), but because it was the only place at which she could carry out the writing activities necessary for her production of income. Further, the fact that the taxpayer performed other minor activities in that home office which were not related to assessable income, did not detract from its essential character and therefore a proportion of the rent was an allowable deduction within the meaning of sub-s.51(1) of the Act.

23. Returning now to the facts in this matter. The taxpayer was a salaried employee and it was not argued that she was in a "business". Instead, Mr Rigano, agent for the applicant, submitted that there was a nexus between the loss and outgoings - in this case, rent - and the performance of duties by the taxpayer in her employment, for which she received income.

24. It was claimed by Mr Rigano that the Faichney case (supra) was distinguishable from these facts in a number of respects. In particular, in Faichney's case, the taxpayer had the option to work after hours in his laboratory by arrangement, which option was not available to this taxpayer. Therefore, it was submitted, working in her study at home was, for this taxpayer, not merely a matter of convenience, but a necessity for her work.

25. Mr Rigano also submitted that there were similarities between the taxpayer in Swinford's case (supra) and this case, in that the only place available for this taxpayer to listen to and play music essential to her work, was in her study at home which was specifically set up and planned for that purpose.

26. We agree with Mr Rigano's submissions on these points, and are satisfied that, in the circumstances of this case, the essential characteristic of the payment of rent for her study area in the unit was an outgoing incidental and relevant to the performance of her work as an Acting Principal Education Officer, being the employment from which she received assessable income.

27. The next question is whether such expenditure is excluded by the exception in sub-s.51(1) of the Act, namely, whether the rent was a payment of a private or domestic nature.

28. As was stated by David Hunt, J., the cases of Handley, Forsyth and Lunnie (supra) do not mean that only taxpayers who are self-employed and have physical separation between the claimed study area from the house may claim expenses in relation to a home study as allowable deductions. One must look at the essential characteristic of the expenditure, including the use of the facility to which the expenditure relates.

29. It is true that this taxpayer's study room is a part of the unit for which there is no separate entrance. However, because of its contents and use, it is distinguishable from the other rooms in the house. The taxpayer rented the unit which includes the study room because of her work requirements. The expenditure on rent in relation to that room is not expenditure for her domestic and private living, but for her work. For the reasoning given by David Hunt, J. in Swinford's case (supra), we consider that the expenditure in this case is not of a domestic or private character, and we so find.

30. Sub-section 51(1) of the Act requires this Tribunal to apportion the expenditure according to the extent to which it relates to assessable income. The respondent did not challenge the quantum of expenditure claimed in this case, and such amount appears to us to be appropriate.

31. For these reasons, therefore, we consider that the decision under review should be set aside, and that in lieu thereof, the Tribunal decides that the taxpayer's claim of $795 should be allowed pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Act.
