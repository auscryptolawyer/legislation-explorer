Last Updated: 22 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - partnership loss - whether partnership existed -whether business carried on by partnership or company

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.6

Softwood Pulp and Paper Limited v. F.C. of T. 76 ATC 4439

I.R.C. v. Williamson (1921) 14 T.C. 335

Case Q81 83 ATC 412

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION No. AT86/285

AAT Decision No 4087

Deputy President R.K. Todd

19 January 1988

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. AT86/285 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Deputy President R.K. Todd

Date : 19 January 1988

Place : Canberra

Decision : The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

(sgd.) R.K. Todd

Deputy President

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. AT86/285 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

19 January 1988 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. The objection decision here under review is challenged by the applicant taxpayer on the footing of a claim that the Commissioner should have allowed to the applicant deductions of amounts claimed as the applicant's individual interest ($62,213) in a loss ($166,698) claimed to have been incurred by a partnership. As will appear, the very existence of the partnership is however in dispute.
  2. While the factual circumstances upon which the applicant relies straddle three fiscal years, the only relevant assessment before the Tribunal is, for whatever reason, that for the year of income ended 30 June 1978 ("fiscal 1978").
  3. Broadly, the applicant's case was this: A company which I shall "P Pty Ltd" developed a building technique involving the construction of concrete houses. It was an ingenious technique in which, after laying a concrete floor slab, virtually a whole house was poured in one moulding operation. The project later ran into difficulties of various kinds into which it is unnecessary to go, but one major problem embedded in it was that effectively a whole estate of identical houses had to be built. It would have been quite uneconomic to move the moulding rig from one place to another to build single houses. It was accordingly accepted that the future of the concept lay in its use in third world countries where cheapness and simplicity of housing would be the main aim, and identical appearance of all houses in one grouping would not, it was thought, be found objectionable. That is not to say that the houses were unattractive to look at. They appear, from photographs tendered in evidence, to have been quite attractive with what might be called a Moroccan simplicity and starkness of style. Some houses were built in a South Pacific country, and some progress was made towards advancing the project in other countries.
  4. In this situation, with the background described above, it is obvious that it would have been some little time before the project showed a profit. In that event, had a corporate structure been adopted for the venture, early losses could have been carried forward in the company's accounts and claimed in returns in future years, unless such losses would have to be characterised as the capital cost of setting up a business (see Softwood Pulp and Paper Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 76 ATC 4439), a likely conclusion in a case such as the present. What in fact is however suggested for the applicant in the present case is that a company ("CP Pty Ltd") which had been incorporated in relation to the carrying out of the project was but a "project manager" for a partnership constituted by a number of promoters of CP Pty Ltd, one of such promoters being the present applicant. Another, one R, was said by the applicant to have been reluctant "to get involved in a company" and, it was inferred, preferred a partnership for the purpose of his interest, said to have been held through a family trust. Having regard to the situs of the first venture of the project, it is piquant to have been told that a partnership, with all its exposure to unlimited liability, was to be preferred to a corporate structure, a facility originally created to avoid in the future the effects of the South Sea Bubble.
  5. In opening, the applicant said that P Pty Ltd "and the managing director used their best efforts to pursue the business activities of the partnership and it was subsequently taken over by [CP Pty Ltd]. [The latter] company had been formed earlier to be no more than a project manager for a partnership but as things developed it was obviously getting more complicated and more people were getting interested and involved and it was necessary to convert the thing into a company...". He went on to say that from 1 January 1979 CP Pty Ltd "operated as a company from that date and took over the actual activities of the partnership on a walk-in, walk-out basis and took over the assets and the liabilities and whatever the partnership had and the share structure of the company was altered accordingly ...".

6. In evidence given by the managing director of P Pty Ltd and by the applicant, and from documents tendered in evidence, the applicant sought to establish these propositions. The applicant was however unable to lead evidence of any conversations from which the existence of any partnership could be inferred, and apart from reference to taxation returns lodged on the footing that the business was that of the partnership, the only documentary evidence of the existence of a partnership was as follows:

(i) Minutes of CP Pty Ltd

Minutes dated 30 may 1977 recorded the first meeting of CP Pty Ltd. The usual matters were dealt with. The last two resolutions were however recorded as follows:

"TRUSTS: Consideration was given to conducting the business through a Unit Trust. However, in view of the strong possibility of punitive legislation in this field being introduced in the forthcoming Budget it was Resolved unanimously not to proceed with the trust.

PARTNERSHIP:

Resolved that the company accept the position of Project Manager for and on behalf of the partnership of [list of names]

An option was granted to Mr. ... to join the partnership as from 1 July 1977 if he wished on terms to be arranged between the partners."

There were also other references to the alleged partnership in subsequent minutes, some relating to meetings held after the fiscal year in question, but I find the making of assertions in the proceedings of CP Pty Ltd, an entirely independent legal entity, quite useless for the purpose of coming to the conclusion that a partnership existed.

(ii) The applicant also relied on an account book described on its front cover as "Account Book -Journal". Pasted on the top of the front cover was a piece of paper upon which there was typedt

"(CP.) PTY. LTD.

  1. Partnership - at front
  2. Company - at back".

Despite this notation, the first page of the accounts set out from the front of the book was headed "(CP) P/L Expenditure 1976-77". Those commencing from the back were headed "(CP) Pty Ltd (Company)". Nowhere is there any reference to a partnership, other than on the notation on the outside of the book which is set out above. I can find no evidence in the Account Book of the existence of a partnership.

(iii) A document dated 25 January 1979 purporting to be an agreement by way of deed entered into between CP Pty Ltd and the persons alleged to have formed a partnership was in evidence. The document is at face value in a sense consistent with the applicant's claim. Its precise terms however are quite curious. By clause 1 it "confirms an earlier verbal agreement between the parties hereto that the company was to take over the operations of the Partners' business effective as from 1 January 1979". CP Pty Ltd by clause 3 was to "take over the assets and liabilities of the business of the Partners as at 31 December 1979". Being by way of deed, the lack of consideration was of itself of no moment, but it is for present purposes significant that the language of the document is not that of purchase. It is open to the construction that it was a self-serving document quite consistent with the business having been at all times the business of CP Pty Ltd and drawn up for some other purpose. The "earlier verbal agreement" was not, as previously pointed out, sustained in evidence and in all the circumstances I am not prepared to place any reliance on the document in question.

7. This being the only evidence which the applicant could adduce in a positive way for the purpose of enabling a finding to be made that a partnership existed at common law, the applicant's case seemed to me at the hearing and now, having reviewed the evidence, is confirmed as being, virtually hopeless. On the negative side, the indicia to which Mr. J.H. Greenwell, who appeared for the Commissioner, was able to refer entirely demolish the applicant's case. It is enough to refer to these indicia in summary form:

(a) There were no independent actions of the purported partnership such as would suggest that it was carrying on a business;

(b) There was no written partnership agreement;
  • (c) The only evidence of creation of a partnership was in the books of CP Pty Ltd;
    • (d) There was no business name in existence. The partnership had no name under which it carried on business other than the name of CP Pty Ltd;
    • (e) There were no financial dealings with any entity save CP Pty Ltd;
    • (f) There was no reference anywhere in the evidence to CP Pty Ltd as acting only as an agent, or nominee, save in taxation returns;
    • (g) All bank accounts were in the name of CP Pty Ltd;

(h) All overseas visits were made on behalf of CP Pty Ltd;

(i) There were no partnership books of account;

(j) All payments to P Pty Ltd were made by CP Pty Ltd;

(k) The building moulds were to be licensed by CP Pty Ltd;

(l) Land was bought, and title taken, in the name of CP Pty Ltd. The applicant said that "as had already been previously agreed that the company would act as the nominee for the partners and it did everything in the name of the nominee", but the statement conveys nothing other than the unreality of the supposed partnership situation that was alleged to have come into existence;

(m) The profit and loss account for fiscal 1978 was prepared by CP Pty Ltd.

8. There was no point in interposing a partnership in the circumstances described other than for tax purposes. The individuals here put forward as constituting the partnership did not carry on the business, rather it was carried on by CP Pty Ltd. In my opinion the alleged partnership was no more than an idea in the mind of the applicant and perhaps some others. It is clear that a partnership is not created merely by an attribution of the quality of partnership to certain activities carried on by certain people by means of a mental process or by a simple process of stating that one exists. As the Lord President, Lord Clyde, said in the decision of the Court of Session in IRC v. Williamson (1921) 14 T.C. 335:

"My Lords, you do not constitute or create or prove a partnership by saying that there is one. The only proof that a partnership exists is proof of the relations of agency and of community in losses and profits and of the sharing in one form or another of the capital of the concern; the only proof of a partnership consists in proof of these things. No doubt the proof may be supplied by what in fact the persons alleging themselves to be partners have done during the currency of the alleged partnership. For instance, if they had treated the capital as if it were partnership capital or consistently with its being partnership capital, perhaps I should say, and if they had treated the profits or losses as partners would treat them, and in fact, if there are facts and circumstances to show that their relations were those consistent with partnership, no doubt the thing can be proved; but I do not see for myself how it could be reasonable to accept such evidence as the three persons gave before the Commissioners as evidence of anything more than this, that they were bona fide under the impression - I shall assume that they were bona fide under the impression - that in carrying on the business of the farm they were, to use a cant expression, all in the same boat and with equal ultimate interests in it. That is not proof of partnership at all."

9. In sum, I find that while a business may have been being carried on or, more likely, was proposed to be carried on, during fiscal 1978, it was not being carried on by the alleged or any other partnership but rather by CP Pty Ltd. There was no control of the business by partners, and no recognition by CP Pty Ltd that it was acting only as an agent. All decisions were taken at meetings of CP Pty Ltd. What I was asked by the applicant to accept was that a situation existed whereunder an agent was to conduct the entire business of the purported partnership. For all the reasons given above I reject entirely the applicant's case that there was a partnership in existence according to the common law.

10. There is another point that should be mentioned. The applicant did not, but Mr. Greenwell very properly did, raise the question whether a partnership existed under s.6 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA"). That section provides:

"'partnership' means an association of persons carrying on business as partners or in receipt of income jointly, but does not include a company." [underlining supplied]

11. In Case Q81 83 ATC 412 a taxpayer who claimed never to have been a partner in the venture in question, and never to have received an alleged share of the profits of the venture, contended that he had never intended to enter into a partnership and that in any event there could not have been such a partnership because no written partnership agreement had ever been entered into. The Board of Review confirmed an assessment issued on the basis of a partnership being in existence, holding that the taxpayer was in accordance with s.6 of the ITAA in receipt of income jointly with his co-venturer and that a partnership therefore existed between them for tax purposes. The lack of receipt of profits was held not to preclude their assessability in the taxpayer's hands. The difference in the present case however, as Mr. Greenwell pointed out, was that no income was derived at all, even by CP Pty Ltd, and I accordingly agree with his submission that the statutory concept of partnership created by s.6 of the ITAA is not applicable in the present case. A sum of $27,366 received by CP Pty Ltd from P Pty Ltd was not a receipt by way of income but rather a reimbursement in respect of excess steel bought and used on other jobs.

12. In the circumstances, it is unnecessary to consider the remaining issue, namely as to whether the expenditure in question was of a preliminary or capital nature.

13. It is as clear as it could possibly be that the applicant's case must fail and that the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
