Decision The Tribunal



. sets aside the decision of the respondent dated 28 March, 1996 to disallow objections dated 6 December, 1995 against notices of assessment issued on 4 October, 1995 in respect of the financial years ended 30 June, 1992, 1993 and 1994; and



. remits the matter to the respondent for re-assessment on the basis that the applicant was engaged in the business of hay production during the financial years ended 30 June, 1992, 1993 and 1994.

REASONS FOR DECISION



On 29 May, 1996, the applicant applied for review of a decision made by a delegate of the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation (“the Commissioner”). That decision, which was dated 28 March, 1996, was to disallow objections dated 6 December, 1995 against amended notices of assessment issued on 4 October, 1995 in respect of the years ended 30 June, 1992, 30 June, 1993 and 30 June, 1994.



2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by an accountant, Mr Reed, and the Commissioner by Ms Macdonald of counsel. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”) were admitted in evidence together with a statement by the applicant, a letter by Dr Hopkinson and Dr English dated 4 February, 1997, a statement of Dr Hopkinson, a bundle of documents regarding farming practices and a colour photograph of the applicant’s property.



THE ISSUE



3. The first issue in this case is whether the money which the applicant received on the sale of his hay could be regarded as income within the meaning of sub-section 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936” (“the Act”). If it is to be regarded as income, the second issue is whether certain of the applicant’s expenses and outgoings may claimed as deductions under section 51. Much of the hearing proceeded on the basis that both issues would be resolved in the applicant’s favour if he were carrying on a business of primary production on the property he owned. If he was carrying on business, the receipts from the sale of hay from the property would be income and the deductions claimed by the applicant in obtaining those receipts would be deductible under section 51. If he is not conducting a business, the receipts would not be regarded as income and the expenses and outgoings would not be deductible.



4. The delegate of the Commissioner had already allowed the applicant’s expenses in relation to activities associated with rain forest trees. He did so on the basis that those activities related to his carrying on a business in relation to them.



BACKGROUND



5. On the basis of the applicant’s evidence, I find that the applicant purchased a property in November, 1989. That property was zoned “Rural A” and comprised some 26 acres. At the time of the purchase, it was divided into eight separate paddocks with small holding yards.



6. The property had originally formed part of a larger property comprising land on four titles. They ranged in size from 9 or 10 hectares to 20 hectares. Other properties in the area range from 5 to 20 acres in size. There are others of 50 acres and yet others of 100s of acres.

EVIDENCE



Purchase of the property



9. The applicant said that he paid for the property by borrowing $130,000 from Westpac Bank in November, 1989. He did so on the basis that he would sell the family home. He had earlier, on 12 September, 1989, sought advice from a real estate agent as to the marketing of the family home. A comprehensive advertising campaign over several weeks was planned. The advertisements appeared locally and nationally in March, 1990. As the applicant had built the family home as an owner builder, the applicant sought approval from the Builders’ Registration Board of Queensland to sell the house (part of Exhibit A). That approval was given on 22 March, 1990. An auction of the family home was arranged for April, 1990 (part of Exhibit A). That auction was unsuccessful, the applicant said, and the family home was not sold.



Intentions in purchasing the property



10. The applicant said that, when he acquired the property, he intended to reside on it and to develop it in such a way as to utilise it in the best way possible. He had two reasons for wishing to do so. The first reason arose from the fact the property was in an area of very rich agricultural land. The property provided an opportunity for the applicant to develop it so that he could maximise the returns he received from it while maintaining his other professional activities.



11. The second reason was based on the needs of his family. One of his children wished to become a veterinary surgeon. The school near the property had facilities for her to undertake courses in animal husbandry and agriculture.



12. The applicant’s wife is a very keen gardener and an equestrian. She and one of their daughters had three horses at the property. Her interests were also satisfied by the move to the property.



13. The applicant continued to pursue his professional commitments after he purchased the property. At first, he continued to do so with the firm with whom he worked when he bought the property. That continued for two or three years. When the professional relationship cooled, he left and started to follow his professional pursuits at a location nearer to the property.



The advice and information the applicant obtained in relation to the property



14. The applicant said that he did not have a great deal of knowledge of farming. He spoke to local farmers, read literature produced by the Department of Primary Industry (DPI) and spoke to advisers from local offices of the DPI and Forestry who visited the property to advise him. He was advised that hay baling would be an appropriate use for the property and also that it might be able to handle a certain number of cattle.



15. He also obtained expert advice. That expert advice was from an accountant who confirmed that he would be able to carry out activities in a manner that would be considered carrying out primary industry. He also obtained farming notes produced by the DPI. Those notes included titles of “Going Farming - initial considerations”, “Costs and Returns - Pasture Seed - Far North Queensland”, “Farm Improvements - will they pay?” and “Will the farm pay?” (Exhibit 2).



16. The applicant read these publications and took account of their principles. He did not, however, make the calculations suggested in the notes entitled “Will the farm pay?”. Those calculations were intended to assess whether the farm would pay for itself. He did look at the property’s potential by assessing its income and its basic costs in terms of outgoings. He assessed the hay production, for example, by speaking to local farmers.



17. The applicant disagreed with a suggestion put to him by Ms Macdonald that he did not have to worry whether the farm paid its way as he had other income. He said that he wanted the farm to pay its way as he did not want an outstanding loan over it. It had the very early potential to stand alone.



18. The applicant also sought advice from Dr Hopkinson, who works with DPI. Dr Hopkinson visited the property on 29 November, 1989 and walked over it. He advised the applicant on a number of matters, including the manner in which the property should be developed. Before the applicant had purchased it, the property had been used for various crops, including hay. Dr Hopkinson advised him that there were certain difficulties with the soil because of the property’s previous use. It was deficient in nitrogen and certain trace elements. The soil could benefit from the addition of potash. At the time, Dr Hopkinson did not feel that it was prudent to continue with hay or cropping because the steepness of the land was not suited to ploughing. The options that were discussed were hay and grass cropping as well as smaller, but intense, agricultural activities such as vegetables.



19. After Dr Hopkinson’s initial visit, the applicant sought further advice from the DPI and did so on a number of occasions. About eight or nine months after he had purchased the property, the applicant asked the DPI about the growing cycle of grasses. He understood that the property had been put under grass 11/2 to 2 years before he had purchased it. He thought that grass had a particular life span of some 6 or 7 years. The advice he received was to plough in the grass, poison it and re-plant it.



20. In addition to his seeking advice from agricultural experts, the applicant also had discussions with accountants regarding the property’s potential. Those discussions took place in approximately 1991. They were in relation to hay production as well as in relation to cattle production. He said that it became very clear that a farm of the property’s size was not a viable proposition as far as cattle were concerned. There was very little analysis done in relation to the production of dried flowers as there would be a four to five year lead time before the flowers would be productive.



The applicant’s actions in relation to the property



21. At the beginning, the applicant said that he made a decision to take what hay he could. One paddock was cut twice. The first cutting was baled and the second left on the ground as a means of mulching the soil. That was done because he had understood from DPI that it was an acceptable method of bringing up the soil value. He did not have the means to fertilise the soil with the use of chemicals. In addition, he understood that it was not viable to do so.



22. The applicant acquired machinery including a 56HP tractor, a second hand slasher, a lifting boom, a plough and a 11/2 ton heavy duty trailer.



23. The applicant started clearing the property in approximately mid December, 1989. There were infestations of tobacco plant, sunflowers and a number of other weeds. There was also a great deal of rock to be moved. Old fences had to be removed as previous owners had simply built new fences next to old ones. The applicant had the boundaries surveyed in order to ensure that he built the new boundary fences in the appropriate places. Internal fences were placed so that access to those places which were particularly steep and dangerous was restricted and so that the property was, in broad terms, divided into two areas. One of those areas, some 20 acres in all, was set aside for the production of hay. A fence divided that area from the rest upon which a different grass, suitable for cattle, was grown.



24. The applicant said that, over the following years, he progressively cleaned and upgraded four paddocks totalling an area of approximately 20 acres. That task was completed by mid 1995. He went on to say that those paddocks were capable of producing Rhodes Brachiaria and Guinea Grass, which are commercially recognised grass for hay crops.



25. The applicant built a residence and, later, a shed. Economic constraints imposed by the failure to sell the family home meant that he had to build the majority of the house and shed either by himself or with the help of his wife. The first stage of the residence was completed by mid December, 1990 and the family then moved to it from a house rented in a town near to the property. The shed was completed in early 1992 as were further fencing repairs.



26. The applicant connected town water to the property for both domestic use and for drinking water for stock. He used heavy duty 32mm black PVC piping to take drinking water to each of the paddocks. That piping was not installed for the purpose of crop or paddock irrigation.



27. The applicant also connected electricity to the property by means of underground piping to a sub-board on the house. It was then connected to the house and across to the shed.



28. The majority of the work was carried out either after hours, at weekends or on days taken from his professional work based in another centre. That professional work was progressively wound down and ended in 1993. The time which the applicant devoted to the property in these years amounted to approximately 16-20 hours each week.



29. The applicant said that he relied on a contractor to cut and bale his hay. Together they determined the area to be cut and baled. He relied upon the contractor to cut and bale the hay at the appropriate time. The contractor cut the grass to a certain height and then flattened it to assist with the drying process. The hay is dropped onto the ground in long lines which are evenly spaced across the paddocks. Later, it would be turned over to assist with drying. When the contractor decided the time was right he baled the hay.



30. A certain amount of hay was pre-sold and collected by the purchasers directly from the paddocks. For a couple of years, the hay that was not sold in this way was collected and stored under tarpaulins. It was then stored in the shed which the applicant had built.



31. The shed was capable of storing 700 bales of hay in half of its area. The other half was divided into three spaces. One was used for the storage of the farm equipment, and the remaining two spaces were used for stables for cattle when they were kept on the property. Later, the stables were used for additional storage space for hay.



32. The applicant agreed that, in 1995, he had permitted a neighbour to cut and bale his hay without charge. He did so as he had missed the prime time for cutting the hay due to adverse weather conditions and because the sub-contractor had not arrived in time to cut the grass. The grass had seeded and was well past the stage where it was worth while baling. It was an arrangement that suited both the applicant and the neighbour. The applicant did not want simply to cut the grass and leave it as mulch. He wanted it removed so that he could get on with the next growing season which, by that time, was well under way. He did not want to keep it as he had difficulty storing it.



33. With regard to the marketing of the hay, the applicant said that he felt that, as the property was developed, a small but improving market could also be developed. That would come about by word of mouth and, as his production became more reliable, he could build up a group of regular clients. Production could grow with the market. He did some advertising of his hay in the local newspaper and in a slightly larger regional newspaper.



34. By 1995, the property had been developed and expanded to its maximum hay potential with approximately 20 acres prepared for hay production. Before that time, not all the area had been cleared for production.

36. The applicant said that he focussed on the production of square bales of hay rather than round bales. There are six square bales to each round bale. He did so as the large round bales, which have the advantage of economies of scale, suffer from a disadvantage as they weigh approximately 120 kilogrammes and require special equipment to be lifted and moved. The square bales weigh approximately 20 to 22 kilogrammes and are easier to move. He could move 80 to 100 bales using his 4 tonne truck and 11/2 tonne trailer. With the square bales, people could travel to the property and collect the hay directly from the paddock. That happened quite commonly.



37. The applicant said that he generally paid the sub-contractor between 70 and 75 cents per bale. As the property was developed, there was a distinct possibility, he thought, that the cost would drop to 65 cents per bale. There would later be an additional cost of 10 cents per bale for fertiliser and handling.



38. The applicant said in his oral evidence that, based on the DPI’s figures, his minimum yearly income would be $11,700. This is calculated on the basis of an average yield of 9.75 tonnes per hectare per year. That would lead to 195 square bales per acre which he would sell at $3.00 per bale. With 20 acres he would produce 3,900 bales each year.



39. The applicant said that, when he purchased the property, it had very few trees. The few trees that there were grew along a creek boundary. As the property was very exposed and the area known for its winds of between 10 and 15 knots, the DPI suggested to him that he plant trees as windbreaks. He sought advice from the DPI and local nurseries as to the species of trees that would provide windbreaks, a supply of native flowers at a later time and look decorative. The trees and bushes were planted for a number of reasons. On the advice of the Forestry Department and a nursery, he planted three row deep rip planting beds. The trees and shrubs selected included a substantial number of decorative trees and shrubs as well as species of trees chosen solely for their qualities as windbreaks. Those chosen for decorative purposes were chosen on the basis of advice received that they were nationally recognised and acceptable as commercially viable in the dried flower/decorative flower market. There was a production lead time in establishing the plants before they would produce flowers that could be marketed.



40. Part of the property located on the creek boundary was identified as suitable only for grazing and forestry activities. The Forestry Department assisted him and other property owners to develop certain areas of their land for future commercial tree harvesting. The applicant assisted in the preparation of the land but did not himself plant the trees. He applied to the local council for approval for the property to be used for forestry. Approval was given in January, 1995. The applicant understands that this will allow the property owner to harvest the trees when they reach maturity.



41. The garden beds adjacent to the house fell into five groups. Two beds approximately 14 metres long and 1.2 metres wide were devoted to growing heliconia. Another bed contained 21 different species of roses. A nursery had advised the applicant that those roses were best suited for growing in the area in which the property was located. Another group of beds formed a decorative garden and a fourth group formed a vegetable garden. The fifth group of beds held extensive decorative/windbreak planting.



42. The applicant said that he sold the tractor/slasher as it had served its purpose in clearing the property. Ongoing cutting, conditioning and baling hay could, in the future, be more efficiently handled by specialist subcontractors. He considered the purchase of a heavy duty ride on mower for clearing fence lines and making firebreaks as well as general garden use.



43. Horses were kept on the property from the outset. They were kept in two paddocks with a separate holding yard. Those paddocks were separated from the remainder of the property with electric fencing. Nothing special was planted to feed the horses. They ate guinea grass which had been planted by a previous owner and which grew in their paddocks. The horses would not eat Brachiaria grass which was grown on some other parts of the property. At times, they were given hay from the bales which the applicant had cut. Generally, however, they were fed with food, including lucerne and other grains, purchased from elsewhere.



Agricultural associations



44. The applicant said that he joined the Agricultural Show Society and was also involved with the community re-afforestation project organised by the Forestry Commission.



The sale of the property



45. The applicant said that the property was sold for financial reasons. He had to find money very quickly as he needed to pay an amended taxation assessment. Although the property was sold in 1995, it had first been placed on the market some 18 to 20 months earlier in 1993. At this earlier time, he had still not been able to sell the family home and wanted to get a feel for what the property would fetch on the market. The land agents had been very reluctant to give him an idea of how much he could sell the property for. He withdrew the property from sale when there was no interest shown from potential purchasers.



Dr John Hopkinson



46. Dr John Hopkinson is a Senior Principal Scientist who has been employed by DPI for 27 years. He has lived and worked in the area in which the applicant bought his property for the past 35 years. His tertiary qualifications are in agricultural botany and his principal area of expertise in tropical pasture seed production. His main duties involve research, communicating the results of his research to farmers and building up seed of new varieties of seed to a level where they can be distributed to farmers. His research is focussed upon the improvement of the reliability, quantity and quality of seed yield from grass and legume crops.



47. Dr Hopkinson said that, in the course of carrying out his duties for DPI, he had acquired a working knowledge of local practices in relation to hay production and the costs of production and the returns to be gained from it. That was so because, for farmers, hay is an adjunct to seed production.



48. Dr Hopkinson considered that a farm of normal size in the area of the property would be of the order of several hundred acres. Although the land was originally subdivided as soldier settler blocks of approximately 60 acres, many have been progressively amalgamated over the years. Most working units are now over 200 acres in area with some over 500 acres. He said that it is generally accepted that the lower limit for a secure living from conventional farming (as opposed to horticulture) is about 200 acres. There have, however, been exceptions but only on the basis of a debt free property and a very simple lifestyle.



49. Dr Hopkinson and Mr B.H. English had prepared a paper entitled “Hay” for the purposes of farmers moving from tobacco crops to other crops (Attachment 1 to Exhibit 4). The paper examined the varieties of grass that could be successfully planted in the area. It went on to say that:



“There is not a living in hay on a normal-sized farm, and no justification for investing in the necessary machinery unless you are a contractor. There is, however, supplementary income to be gained from hay as an adjunct to another activity. It is a by-product of seed production, though opportunities for newcomers to seed production are limited by markets and are not at present great. It obviously combines well with a cattle enterprise, providing conserved feed at cost. And it produces income from land under a rotational rest from cropping.” (paragraph 2)



50. The usual system that applies in relation to the production of hay is for the landowner to produce the crop and for a contractor to mow, rake, bale and load the hay. The contractor may be paid in one of two ways. In the first, the contractor may charge approximately $6 per round bale for the service he or she provides. This leaves the landowner with a margin of approximately $360 per hectare per crop for a good cut yielding 60 round bales each weighing 120 kilogrammes. That margin is calculated on the basis that the total costs of establishment, buying and spreading urea, mowing, raking, baling and loading and replacement fertiliser amount to approximately $595. The return on the sale of 60 bales at $16 per bale amounts to $960. The landowner must still market the hay and incur the costs associated with that.



51. On some occasions, the contractor may take the hay and pay the landowner a price that leaves the contractor a clear $10 from the retail sale to cover his or her haymaking, transport costs and profit. This leaves the landowner with a reduced yield of approximately $125 per hectare from a similar crop but with that hay sold.



52. The grasses most commonly used are rhodes grass, signal grass and humidicola. One rain-grown crop is usually possible each year. That crop may be followed by one or two more cuts from irrigated growth. Irrigated hay may or may not be profitable depending on the system and irrigation. In his oral evidence, Dr Hopkinson said that lucerne and sorghum may be irrigated but never grass. He had never known of a hay crop to be irrigated in the area in which the applicant’s property is located. Lucerne and sorghum are not commonly used in that area.



53. Dr Hopkinson said in his statement that his research had not been conducted in the area where the applicant’s property was situated. The area of his research had a lower rainfall and a much longer dry season, had irrigation available, was hotter and sunnier and had less fertile soils. In general, these conditions led to higher costs in the production of heavy hay yields but provided a more reliable climate for making hay.



54. Dr Hopkinson said that he and Mr English were very cautious in expressing opinions as to the hay yield which might be expected from the applicant’s property. Hay yields are variable and there are so many influential factors that actual production may differ widely from the predicted production.



55. Dr Hopkinson and Mr English assessed the expected hay yield which had certain attributes and which had been managed in a certain manner. They assumed that the property has good red soil and is used for the production of perennial grass hay. They assumed the land had been cropped in the previous one or two years. It was then sown with Callide Rhodes grass. They further assumed that the applicant planned to leave the area down to grass and to take hay from it for four or five years. He did not intend to use nitrogenous fertiliser during this period. Whenever there was enough grass to do so, he would cut hay. If he could, he would take more than one crop per year. He will not, however, take hay off the whole area at once. Sometimes he will take from one part and sometimes from another.



56. Dr Hopkinson and Mr English said that they expected that an area of ground managed in this way would produce one heavy hay crop. That would generally be the first crop due to the residual nitrogen in the soil derived from mineralisation since cropping. The removal of the first heavy crop of hay would mean that large quantities of soil nutrients, of which nitrogen would be the most important, would also be removed. Unless the nitrogen is replaced by fertilisation, any subsequent hay crop will be much lighter. The number of hay crops taken in any one year is of little consequence as the annual yield will be much the same whether one or several are taken.



57. Research carried out by Dr Hopkinson and Mr England had been on the basis of nitrogen sufficient soil. That research led them to estimate that an average heavy yield of hay from land, which is similar to that of the property but which is nitrogen sufficient, would be in the order of 8 tonnes per hectare per year. They had no experience with taking hay in nitrogen deficient soil and had not discussed such yields with farmers. They had, therefore, taken an arbitrary figure of 4 tonnes per hectare per year as a reasonable yield expectation. This was based on an expectation that an average heavy yield on soil enriched with nitrogen would be in the order of 8 tonnes per hectare per year.



58. In Dr Hopkinson’s experience, actual yields diverge from hypothetical estimates by 50% of the true average on 9 out of 10 times. That would mean that the actual yields would, in 9 cases out of 10, be in the range of 2.5 to 7.5 tonnes per hectare per year. In exceptional cases, the range of actual yields could be wider.



59. In relation to the applicant’s property, Dr Hopkinson said that it is possible that he could reach average annual yields of 9.75 tonnes per hectare per year but only if he had planned to use high levels of appropriate fertiliser.



60. Dr Hopkinson and Mr England also dealt with the effect of mulching the soil and, in particular, its effect on subsequent hay yields. Mulching by allowing the cut grass to lie on the soil surface and decompose, they said, does not result in any net gain or loss of nutrients to the soil. It merely recycles those which were there already. It does, however, result in a net gain of organic matter in the system. That is an advantage when the land is returned to cropping rather than while it remains under grass. The effect which mulching would have on a subsequent hay crop is unpredictable. They continued:



“It would set in motion a burst of decomposition which at times would lead to the locking up of soil nitrogen (the critical element), and hence poor grass growth, at times a release of nitrogen and hence good grass growth. It is unlikely ever, however, to release enough nitrogen over a short time to produce hay comparable with that from a deliberately fertilised sward in either quantity or quality.” (Exhibit 4, Attachment 2)



61. Dr Hopkinson said in his oral evidence that 1 in every 4 or 5 hay crops might be lost due to rain. If, for some reason, the hay is spoiled, a decision might be made that baling it is not a worthwhile exercise. It will then be used as mulch.



62. Dr Hopkinson said in his statement:



“When considered in the context of full-time farmers making a living from the land, hay production can readily be seen as being an enterprise that is not justifiable on its own, since the gross margin per hectare is insufficient to provide a living; and in fact no one in the Atherton-Tolga-Kairi ... district makes a living purely on his own hay production. Owners of haymaking machinery do outside contract haymaking work, and individual farmers fit haymaking into other enterprises such as cattle fattening, seed production, and annual mixed cropping.” (Exhibit 4, paragraph 15)



63. Although he could not recall the details of his conversation with the applicant in 1989, Dr Hopkinson thought it highly likely that he agreed with him that grass production would be a possibility. It was also likely that he would have told him that it would return little more than pocket money. That is so as it is what he has always warned potential producers. Dr Hopkinson added in evidence that only if the farm were free of debts and the farm large could hay cropping alone be commercially viable. Even then, it would be a very foolish thing to do.



64. It is a common practice in the area to rotate, every few years, annual crops with grass swards from which hay may be taken. Dr Hopkinson regarded this as sound farming practice. It provided the grass swards with the benefits of soil disturbance and the fertiliser inputs of the cropping phase of rotation.



CONSIDERATION



65. Under sub-section 51(1) of the Act,



“All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”



66. There are two limbs to this provision. The first relates to the allowance of losses and outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. This limb may apply in a case in which the second limb of sub-section 51(1) does not apply and may lead to an apportionment of the losses and outgoings. The second limb of sub-section 51(1) is that upon which the parties focussed in this case. That limb raises for consideration the question whether the losses and outgoings were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing income.



67. It is clear that under both limbs, a finding must be made of the purpose for which the losses and outgoings were incurred. If that purpose is to gain or produce assessable income, the losses and outgoings will then be deductible except to the extent that they are losses or outgoings of capital or of a capital, private or domestic nature or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income. As Brennan J said in Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1981) 81 ATC 4,100:



“Section 51 requires that a deductible expenditure be incurred ‘in’ gaining assessable income, that is to say, it must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that income (Ronpibon Tin N.L. & Tongkah Compound N.L. v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 47 at p.56). An outgoing of interest may be incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income where the borrowed money is laid out for the purpose of gaining that income (F.C. of T. v. Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 C.L.R. 153 at pp.170, 171, 197; Texas Co., (Australasia) Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 C.L.R. 382 at p.468). The laying out of the borrowed money for the purpose of gaining assessable income furnishes the required connection between the interest paid upon it by the taxpayer and the income derived by him from its use.” (page 4,104)



68. This aspect was also considered by the High Court in Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 91 ATC 4,950 (Mason CJ, Brennan, Deane, Dawson, Toohey, Gaudron and McHugh JJ). Although there was some suggestion that the appellants might have engaged in a sham, the High Court put that suggestion to one side in considering the principles relating to the application of sub-section 51(1).



69. The High Court began by confirming that it is plain that the provision contemplates apportionment and what is an appropriate apportionment is a question of fact. It went on to say that the reference to “the assessable income” is not a reference only to the assessable income derived in the particular tax year but also to that derived in other years or to assessable income which the relevant outgoing would be expected to produce. Reference was made to the Ronpibon case in support of these principles.



70. Their Honours then went on to consider the first limb of sub-section 51(1). They pointed out that whether or not an outgoing is made for the purposes of gaining or producing assessable income is a question of characterisation. In answering that question they said that the motive of the taxpayer may be an element to be taken into consideration. They continued:



“Nonetheless, it is commonly possible to characterise an outgoing as being wholly of the kind referred to in the first limb of s.51(1) without any need to refer to the taxpayer’s subjective thought processes. That is ordinarily so in a case where the outgoing gives rise to the receipt of a larger amount of assessable income. In such a case, the characterisation of the particular outgoing as wholly of a kind referred to in s.51(1) will ordinarily not be affected by considerations of the taxpayer’s subjective motivation. If, for example, a particular item of assessable income can be earned by making a lesser outgoing in one of two possible ways, one of which is a loss or outgoing of the kind described in s.51(1) and the other of which is not, it will ordinarily be irrelevant that the taxpayer’s choice of the method which was tax deductible was motivated by taxation considerations or that the non-deductible outgoing would have been less than the deductible one. In such a case, the objective relationship between the outgoing actually made and the greater amount of assessable income actually earned suffices, without more, to characterise the whole outgoing as one which was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. If the outgoing can properly be wholly so characterised, it ‘is not for the Court or the commissioner to say how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income, but only how much he has spent’. [See eg, Ronpibon Tin (1949) 8 ATD, at p.437-438; (1949) 78 CLR, at p.60; Cecil Bros. Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. [1964] HCA 82; (1962) 12 ATD 449 at p.451-452; [1964] HCA 82; (1963-164) 111 CLR 430, at p.434]



The position may, however, well be different in a case where no relevant assessable income can be identified or where the relevant assessable income is less than the amount of the outgoing. Even in a case where some assessable income is derived as a result of the outgoing, the disproportion between the detriment of the outgoing and the benefit of the income may give rise to a need to resolve the problem of characterisation of the outgoing for the purposes of the sub-section by a weighing of the various aspects of the whole set of circumstances, including direct and indirect objects and advantages which the taxpayer sought in making the outgoing. [See, e.g. Robert G. Nall Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1936] HCA 79; (1936) 4 ATD 335 at pp.338,340,342-343; [1936] HCA 79; (1936-1937), 57 CLR 695, at pp. 695, at pp.699-700,706,708-709,712-713] Where that is so, it is a ‘commonsense’ or ‘practical’ weighing of all the factors which must provide the ultimate answer. [See, e.g. B.P. Australia Ltd v F.C. of T. (1966) A.C. 224, at p.264; Hallstroms Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 8 ATD 190 at p.195; [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634, at p.648; FC of T. v Foxwood (Tolga) Pty Ltd 81 ATC 4261 at pp.4264, 4268-4269; [1928] HCA 48; (1981) 147 CLR 278, at pp.285,293] If, upon consideration of all those factors, it appears that, notwithstanding the disproportion between outgoing and income, the whole outgoing is properly to be characterised as genuinely and not colourably incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, the entire outgoing will fall within the first limb of s.51(1) unless it is either somehow excluded by the exception of ‘outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature’ or ‘incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income’. If, however, that consideration reveals that the disproportion between outgoing and relevant assessable income is essentially to be explained by reference to the independent pursuit of some other objective and that part only of the outgoing can be characterised by reference to the actual or expected production of assessable income, apportionment of the outgoing between the pursuit of assessable income and the pursuit of that other objective will be necessary.” (pages 4,957-4,958)



71. The second limb of sub-section 51(1) has also been the subject of a number of cases. The general principles are set out in Ferguson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 29; (1979) 79 ATC 4,261 (Bowen CJ, Franki and Fisher JJ). Bowen CJ and Franki J set out the definition of “business” in sub-section 6(1) of the Act. It is that



“`business’ includes any profession, trade, employment, vocation or calling, but does not include occupation as an employee;”



72. They then summarised the effect of the authorities at the time:



“... This does not afford much assistance in the present case. There are many elements to be considered. The nature of the activities, particularly whether they have the purpose of profit-making, may be important. However, an immediate purpose of profit-making in a particular income year does not appear to be essential. Certainly it may be held a person is carrying on business notwithstanding his profit is small or even where he is making a loss. Repetition and regularity of the activities is also important. However, every business has to begin and even isolated activities may in the circumstances be held to be the commencement of carrying on business. Again, organization of activities in a business-like manner, the keeping of books, records and the use of system may all serve to indicate that a business is being carried on. The fact that, concurrently with the activities in question, the taxpayer carries on the practice of a profession or another business, does not preclude a finding that his additional activities constitute the carrying on of a business. The volume of his operations and the amount of capital employed by him may be significant. However, if what he is doing is more properly described as the pursuit of a hobby or recreation or an addiction to a sport, he will not be held to be carrying on a business even though his operations are fairly substantial. See generally, Trautwein v. F.C. of T. (No. 2) [1936] HCA 46; (1936) 56 CLR 196; Tweddle v. F.C. of T. [1942] HCA 40; (1942) 7 ATD 186; Fairway Estates Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. 70 ATC 4061; [1970] HCA 29; (1970) 123 CLR 153; Thomas v. F.C. of T. 72 ATC 4094; (1972) 46

ALJR 397 especially at pp. 4098-4100; 399-401 in all of which cases it was held the taxpayer was carrying on business; and Martin v. F.C. of T. [1953] HCA 100; (1953) 90 CLR 470 in which it was held the taxpayer was not carrying on business.” (pages 4,264-4,265)



73. Fisher J in Ferguson specifically considered whether the fact that Mr Ferguson had intended to carry on a business of cattle raising or of primary production on his own land precluded a finding that he was also carrying on business at an earlier stage when he was building up a herd on leased land. He said:



“... A person may conduct a business, albeit of a limited nature, the activities of which business are preparatory to or in preparation for the conduct of another business on a larger scale. The question is whether the more limited activities at the earlier stage, standing alone, constitute a business.” (page 4,269)



74. It is clear from the authorities that, whether or not the applicant is carrying on a business is a question of fact. No one factor is definitive. So, for example, it is not necessary that a taxpayer make a profit in every year (Thomas, Ferguson and Tweddle). Evidence that the breeding of percherons could never return a profit was considered by Williams J in Tweddle v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1942] HCA 40; (1942) 2 AITR 360. He said that it was not the role of the court to decide upon the probable success or non success of a venture and continued:



“It is not suggested that it is the function of income tax Acts, or of those who administer them, to dictate to taxpayers in what business they shall engage or how to run their business profitably or economically. The Act must operate upon the result of a taxpayer’s activities as it finds them. If a taxpayer is in fact engaged in two businesses, one profitably and the other showing a loss, the Commissioner is not entitled to say he must close down the unprofitable business and cut his losses even if it might be better in his own interests and although it certainly would be better in the interests of the Commissioner if he did so (Tooheys Ltd. v Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) (1922) 22 NSWLR 432. If the appellant succeeds and makes a profit it will plainly be taxable, and it is difficult to see how his activities could at that moment of time be transmogrified from an indulgence in a somewhat unusual form of recreation into the carrying on of a business. I am satisfied that the appellant is seeking to establish himself at Winlaton as a recognised breeder of high-class stud stock, and that while he is prepared to make losses to achieve this ambition he has a genuine belief that he will be able eventually to make the business pay. Indeed, unless he can do so, his experience will hardly be an encouragement to others to emulate his example.” [1942] HCA 40; (2 AITR 360 at page 364)



75. That, however, is not to say that that the issue of whether or not a venture can ever generate a profit is not relevant. As Mr Burke said as the Chairman of the Tax Board of Review in Case H11 (1976) 76 ATC 59:



“In determining whether a business is being carried on it is, in my view, proper to consider, as one of the elements, whether the activities under consideration could ever result in a profit - see my reasons in Case S54, 17 T.B.R.D. 313. The taxpayer’s estimate of returns from sales was $900 after the first thinning at 14 years and thereafter similar returns from thinning every seven years until final felling is carried out after 40 years to yield a total return of the order of $13,000. The forestry officer’s evidence suggests that the taxpayer’s estimate, based on current market prices, may be somewhat optimistic but against that is the evidence of the taxpayer that over the period 1967 to 1972 (as per the Commonwealth Statistician) the price of timber has increased at a rate greater than that of the Consumer Price Index.



9. It is impossible to predict what the returns from harvesting the plantation will be in 1987 and thereafter at seven year intervals. I conclude however that the taxpayer when he entered into the venture had reasonable grounds for believing and did in fact believe that the project would return him a clear excess of income over expenditure.” (page 61)



76. Nor is it necessary that a taxpayer treat the activity as of prime importance either in terms of the time devoted to it or as to the returns expected from it. A person may conduct a business even though, as Walsh J said in Thomas v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1972) 72 ATC 4094], he or she does so in a small way (page 4099). If the taxpayer is to succeed, he or she must not treat the activity as a hobby or for the purposes of recreation. Although there may be a finding that a taxpayer is not engaging in a hobby, there may equally be a finding that he or she is not carrying on a business. In Case U72 [(1987) 87 ATC 446], for example, it was found that the taxpayer was not engaging in a hobby of racing and breeding horses but nor was he engaged in a business undertaking those activities. All that he was doing was seeking to establish a breeding business but had not done so at the relevant time.



77. Consideration as to the efficiency with which a taxpayer conducts his or her business was also considered in Thomas. Walsh J said:



“I have given close consideration to the criticisms that were advanced as to the lack of business efficiency with which the appellant conducted the operations. But in the circumstances I have not found that this should lead to a conclusion that it was not a business venture. On various matters the appellant did take advice. It is not in doubt that he made mistakes. But many persons carry on a business for the competent conduct of which they have not previously acquired much knowledge or experience...” (page 4100)



78. The applicant in this case argues that he is in the business of hay production for the purpose of gaining assessable income and so come within the second limb of sub-section 51(1). I will consider that argument bearing in mind that, under paragraph 14ZZK of the Tax Administration Act 1953, he bears the burden of proof in establishing that the amounts should be deductions under the Act.



79. I will consider first the viability of hay as a business. If I were to accept Dr Hopkinson’s evidence, I would find that the applicant’s property could, at best and provided he used high levels of fertiliser, produce 9.75 tonnes of hay per hectare per year. That equates with 81.25 round bales per hectare per year or some 487 square bales. The return on the sale of the hay at the rate of $16 per round bale amounts to a return of $1,300 per hectare but from that must be deducted the costs of sale and transport to market. If the hay were sold at $3 per square bale directly from the paddock as was the applicant’s practice, the return would be in the order of $1,460 per hectare.



80. I find that not all of the land could have been used to produce hay for some was used for the house and necessary buildings, some for the gardens and some was not suited for hay production. On the applicant’s evidence, the most that he proposed to use was 20 acres (i.e. a little more than 8 hectares). Use of that area of land would lead to a return of $11,680 each year from hay production.



81. The best hay production that actually occurred in a full year occurred in 1995 when 1,450 square bales were produced from 7.2 acres (i.e. approximately 2.9 hectares). That equates with 13.6 round bales per hectare of the land used to produce the hay. The portion of the year to December, 1996 also showed a return equivalent to 13.1 round bales per hectare of land used by the applicant.



82. I find that the applicant did not use the whole of the 20 acres which he had set aside for hay production. That is explained by the fact that the property was also carrying cattle in the years 1993 and 1994. Grass required for the cattle was not cut for hay. None of the 20 acres was used for the horses.



83. Marketing of the hay, I find, was undertaken although in a small way. The applicant advertised in the local newspaper as well as in a regional newspaper. He also relied on word of mouth advertising. He had in mind a specific market focussed upon those who could come to the property and pick up the square bales themselves.



84. On the basis of the hypothetical figures of Dr Hopkinson, I have doubts whether hay production could ever have become a profitable business for the applicant. On the actual figures produced by the applicant it was certainly not a viable enterprise in the years he held the property. That is so even though he was in 1995 and 1996 achieving high yields on the land he used. It is clear from the case of H11, however, that whether or not an enterprise will become profitable is not determinative of whether or not the enterprise is a business.



85. In the case of the applicant, I am satisfied that he believed that his hay making enterprise would ultimately lead him to return an amount that was in excess of his expenditure. He planned that this would be so and I find that would have been achievable had he been able to sell his family home in town. I find that he made genuine attempts to sell that house but without success. The table at paragraph 8 above illustrates that his outgoings would have been less than his income once he was no longer responsible for interest payments in respect of the property. The applicant had, therefore, reasonable grounds for his belief.



86. The applicant, I find, did take steps to ensure that he developed his property appropriately. He obtained information from local farmers as well as from the DPI in the form of brochures and oral advice from its officers. Based on the evidence of Dr Hopkinson, I find that some of the decisions he has made are not soundly based. His decision, for example, not to fertilise the hay production was driven by lack of money rather than sound principles of hay production. The fact that he did not necessarily display consistent good judgement in hay production does not mean that he was not engaged in a business of hay production.



87. The purposes for which the applicant purchased the property were four fold. I find that he purchased it for its income producing capacity, its capacity to keep the horses of his wife and his daughter, its space to enable his wife to pursue her gardening interests and the fact that it was located close to a school that catered for his daughter’s agricultural interests.



88. The applicant, I find, kept appropriate machinery to undertake the task of haymaking. He contracted out the haycutting and hay baling but, on the basis of Dr Hopkinson’s evidence, I find that this was a normal practice adopted by farmers in the area.



89. Looking at the hay production overall, I find that the applicant did not engage in it merely for the purpose of a hobby or for recreation. He set out to do so as a business. In doing so, he did not display the acumen that he might have wished or make certain decisions with the judgement that an experienced farmer might bring to play. In view of the fact that he intended to clear the property of the mortgage, he had reasonable grounds to expect that he would be able to make a profit on his hay making enterprise. Even though he had not cleared the mortgage, he had continued his haymaking activity and I find that it had gone beyond the preparatory stages and was being conducted as a business.



90. I do not find that the venture of growing plants had yet reached the stage of a business venture. The applicant and his wife did choose their plants carefully and with a twofold purpose in mind when they did. One purpose was to act as a windbreak. The other purpose was for their decorative qualities. While I accept that they had chosen the decorative plants after taking advice from various sources as to their commercial viability, I find no evidence of any long term strategy in relation to their production, marketing and ultimate sale. If there were a business relating to decorative flowers, it could be expected that such a plan would exist. It may have been that the flowers and plants would have become a business but, in the years in question, I find that there was no more than a hope that they would, at some time in the future, become the foundation for business yet to be planned and undertaken.



91. For the reasons I have given, I find that the applicant was engaged in the business of hay production during the financial years ended 30 June, 1992, 1993 and 1994. Consequently, I



. set aside the decision of the respondent dated 28 March, 1996 to disallow objections dated 6 December, 1995 against notices of assessment issued on 4 October, 1995 in respect of the financial years ended 30 June, 1992, 1993 and 1994; and



. remit the matter to the respondent for re-assessment on the basis that the applicant was engaged in the business of hay production during the financial years ended 30 June, 1992, 1993 and 1994.
