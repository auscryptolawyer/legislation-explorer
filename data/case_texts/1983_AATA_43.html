Re Dallas Whatson and Tax Agents' Board (Qld) [1983] AATA 43 (15 February 1983)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: DALLAS WHATSON

And: TAX AGENTS' BOARD (QLD)

No. Q82/103

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

The Honourable J.B.K. Williams

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Registration of a company as tax agent - cancellation of registration of nominee of that company on grounds that nominee not a fit and proper person to remain registered - applicant convicted of criminal offences in his professional capacity as an auditor.

HEARING

CANBERRA

15:2:1983

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review of the respondent Board.

DECISION

A company Verbena Pty. Ltd. is registered as a tax agent pursuant to the provisions of section 251J(1) of the Income Assessment Act 1936("the Act"). The Applicant, Mr. Dallas Whatson was registered as the nominee of that company, pursuant to the provisions of 251J(4) of the Act and at material times was a director of the company and a member of the accounting firm, Whatson and Baker.

On 26 March, 1982 the Tax Agents' Board of Queensland wrote to the applicant a letter in the following terms:

"The Agents' Board at its meeting of 23 March 1982 discussed recent convictions of yourself for offences relating to the failed The Australian Permanent Building Society and also the Australian Co-operative Development Society Ltd.

Section 251K(2)(c) of the Income Tax Assessment Act indicates that the Board may cancel the registration of any tax agent upon being satisfied that in the case of a company, a nominee is not a fit and proper person to

remain a nominee, or is not of good fame, integrity and character.

In consideration of the circumstances and nature of the offences the Board determined that you are not a fit and proper person to remain a nominee and as a consequence the registration of the company of Verbena Pty. Limited practising as Whatson and Baker is cancelled. You are required to return Certificate of Registration, number TA 37157 issued to the company in July 1978, within thirty days of the date of this letter.

Your attention is drawn to Section 251K(5) of the abovementioned Act and Regulation 58PA which provides you with an avenue of appeal against the Board's decision."

The appellant seeks a review by the Tribunal of the Board's decision that he

is not a fit and proper person to remain a nominee of the company Verbena Pty. Limited.

The evidence before the Tribunal discloses, and it is not in contest, that on the 10th August 1981, an indictment was presented against the applicant in the District Court in Brisbane, charging him with the following offences:

"1. That on or about the thirty-first day of October 1975 a company auditor of The Australian Permanent Building Society and Bowkett concurred in publishing a written statement namely the 4th Annual Report and Accounts for the Financial year ended 30th June 1975 of the said corporation which was false in a number of material particulars as he well knew with intent thereby to deceive members or creditors of the said corporation, or to induce persons to advance money to the said

corporation.

2. That on or about the twelfth day of December 1975 a company auditor of the Australian Co-operative Development Society Limited concurred in publishing a written statement namely the Annual Report and Accounts for the financial year ended 30th June 1975 of the said Corporation which was false in a number of material particulars as he then well knew with intent thereby to deceive members or creditors of the said corporation or to induce persons to advance money to the said Corporation."

He pleaded not-guilty to each of these charges and, after a lengthy trial,

was on the 2nd October 1981, found guilty by a jury on each count. On the 5th October 1981 it was ordered by the trial Judge that, in relation to each count, he be fined the sum of $1,500 in default imprisonment with hard labour for six months, such sentences to be served cumulatively.

The letter to the applicant on the 26th March 1982 previously referred to followed a meeting of the Tax Agents' Board of Queensland held on the 23rd March 1982. Minutes of that meeting are in evidence before the Tribunal and are set out hereunder.

"The Board noted that Dallas Whatson who is registered as nominee and director of the above company had been convicted on two counts of publishing a false account of the financial situation of two failing

Building Societies.

It was noted that he had been appointed as auditor of both of the Building Societies and so should have acted as a person independent of the affairs of each of the Societies.

Nevertheless, his prosecutions relate to his concurring with the conspiracy to publish accounts which "he knew to be false in a number of material particulars" and "well knew with intent to deceive members or creditors".

The Board considered these factors and judged him to no longer be a fit and proper person to remain registered as a tax agent. The registration of the company was cancelled."

Evidence was given before the Tribunal by Mr. G.W. Robson, Commonwealth

Chief Auditor for Queensland and a member of the Tax Agents' Board. He said that prior to the meeting of the Board on the 23rd March 1982, the Board had received communication from Queensland Branch of the Institute of Chartered Accountants in Australia. The advice was that the applicant had been excluded from the Institute and enclosed a notice to appear in the February issue of "The Chartered Accountant in Australia." The notice stated that the Disciplinary Committee of the Institute had heard a complaint against the applicant that he had been guilty of an act, omission or default discreditable to a member of the Institute or to a public accountant or which involves a failure to observe a proper standard of professional care, skill or competence within the meaning of Article 21(1) (iii) of the Institute's Supplemental Royal Charter in that in his capacity as the Secretary of a company carrying on an advertising agency, he wrote to the Secretary of the Media Council of Australia stating that specific action had been taken to reduce overheads and restructure the company's equity and loan capital and that the company was operating profitably, its working capital was in excess of the Council's requirements and it was on a sound financial footing, when he knew or should have known that such statements did not reflect the true position. The Committee found that the complaint has been established and ordered the applicant be excluded from membership of the Institute.

It appears from the evidence of Mr. Robson that following receipt of this information, the Tax Agents' Board met to consider the applicants position. As a result, further information was sought from the Institute of Chartered Accountants, which the Institute declined to furnish.

No further action appears to have been taken by the Board until it became aware of the convictions recorded against the applicant to which reference has already been made.

The action which has been related was then taken by the Board.

Section 251K(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act makes provision for cancellation of the registration of a Tax Agent. That section, so far as is presently relevant is in the following terms:

(2) A Board may cancel the registration of any tax agent upon being

satisfied -

(a) -------------

(b) -------------

(c) in the case of a partnership or company, a nominee of the tax agent is not a fit and proper person to be such a nominee, or that a person who has become a member of the partnership, or a director, or manager or other administrative officer, of the company is under the age of 21 years or is not of good fame, integrity and character.

It may here be mentioned that whereas the registration of Verbena Pty.

Limited was cancelled by reason of the Board's finding, that the applicant as nominee was not a fit and proper person to be such nominee, that company is again registered as a tax agent with a different nominee and that Verbena Pty. Limited is carrying on business with Mr. Baker, the applicant's partner in the Accounting firm and the applicant's wife as directors.

There is no evidence to suggest nor is it alleged by the respondent Board that the applicant has been other than competent in carrying out his functions as the nominee of a tax agent, nor that he does not possess the requisite skills to perform this function. The sole basis of the Board for its decision as related in the minutes of the Board's meeting on 23 March 1982 was that by reason of the convictions recorded against him, the nature of them and the fact that his offences were committed in his capacity as an auditor, he was not a fit and proper person to remain registered.

There is before the Tribunal little evidence of the detail surrounding the offences upon which the applicant was convicted. So far as the respondent Board was concerned, there was tendered in evidence before the Tribunal a certificate from the appropriate authority recording convictions in the terms which have already been stated. The applicant gave some evidence of the surrounding circumstances. He said that in relation to the Building Society there was the allegation that expenses incurred by the Building Society were in fact transferred to the Co-operative Society when they should have been borne by the Building Society and charged against the profits of that Society. Another allegation was that in the Building Society a lot of expenses were capitalised as establishment expenses which should not have been so treated. There was also a complaint that, included in an amount under the heading "loans to members", was a substantial loan to Australian Co-operative Development Society and a company, Condomine Country Estates which should have been dealt with separately in the balance sheets. Another matter was concerned with the method of dealing with profits on conditional land sales. As to these matters the applicant as I understand his evidence, claimed that they arose on matters of accountancy principles in which opinions could be divided or from his reliance on information supplied by other persons which, by reason of pressure of work, he did not properly verify.

These matters were put forward in mitigation or explanation of the offences committed by him. These were not matters that were before the respondent Board at the time of making its decision. It appears from the evidence of Mr. Robson that the Board did not look beyond the fact that all the convictions as evidenced by the certificate as "it did not feel that it had to make any further enquiries." The view was apparently taken by the Board that the convictions, in themselves, were conclusive evidence of the unfitness of the applicant.

Mr. Robson who, as has been said, is the Commonwealth Chief Auditor for Queensland also gave evidence concerning the role and function of an auditor. I accept his evidence on this point as expert evidence. He said that the role is a special one involving honesty and integrity which require him to give an independent professional opinion concerning a company's financial statements upon which reliance can be put by shareholders and other interested parties.

The statutory power of the respondent Board to cancel the registration of the applicant arose not from the mere fact of a conviction but from the formation of the opinion that he was not a fit and proper person to remain registered. The position of the Board is therefore to be contrasted to the position of the General Medical Council in the case of General Medical Council -v- Spackman (1943) A.C. 627. It appeared in that case the Council was empowered to direct the erasure of the name of a medical practitioner from the register of practitioners if the practitioner were convicted in England or Ireland of any felony or misdemeanour or in Scotland of any crime or offence of after due enquiry he was judged by the Council to have been guilty of any infamous conduct in any professional respect. The distinction thus lay between deregistration of a practitioner as the result of a conviction on the one hand or for infamous conduct on the other hand. The respondent, Spackman, was found by the Divorce Court to have committed adultery with a person with whom he stood in a professional relationship. The Council directed the removal of the practitioner's name from the register for imfamous conduct relying solely upon the finding of the Divorce Court. It declined to hear further evidence which the respondent desired to call. It was decided that the findings of the Divorce Court, whilst strong prime facie evidence which the Council was entitled to entertain, was not conclusive against the appellant who should have been permitted to adduce further evidence before the Council.

Reference might also be made in the case of Zeims -v-Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of New South Wales [1957] HCA 46; (1957) 97 C.L.R. 279. In that case a barrister was convicted of manslaughter arising form a motor vehicle accident. As a result his name was removed from the roll of barristers as not being a fit and proper person to remain on the roll. The Supreme Court of New South Wales which ordered the disbarment of the applicant apparently took the view that the conviction and sentence constituted grounds in themselves for disbarring the applicant and was not concerned to go behind them and review the facts and circumstances surrounding the matter. An appeal to the High Court of Australia was held that the applicant's conviction and sentence were not in themselves necessarily conclusive of the issue of his fitness to remain on the bar roll. It is to be noted that the conviction was with respect to a matter arising in his personal capacity and not in his professional role.

There are indications in some of the judgments in Ziems case of the distinction to be drawn between offences committed in a private as distinct from a professional capacity.

Fullagar J. at page 290 said:

"But the whole approach of the Court to a case of personal misconduct must surely be very different from its approach to a case of professional misconduct. Generally speaking, the latter must have a much more direct bearing on the question of a man's fitness to practice even the former."

Taylor J. at page 301 said:

"It is of course obvious that many offences are of such a character that the proof of the commission of any one of them by a member of the Bar could produce only one result. And, for the simple fact the proof of such an offence would in itself concerned constituting a disqualification for his profession, this would be so whether a conviction resulted in imprisonment or not. But, on the other hand, it cannot be suggested that a barrister should be disbarred upon proof that he has committed any offences whatever its nature or consequences."

In the present case, it is plain that the offences of which the applicant

was convicted related to his professional activities as distinct from his personal life. As has been said, there is little evidence before the Tribunal as to the details surrounding the convictions. However, the minutes of the convictions spell out certain elements of the offences upon which, it must be assumed, that the jury was satisfied beyond reasonable doubt. These are that at all material times the applicant was the auditor of the companies concerned, that he concurred in the making of false statements which he knew were false, and that he had an intention to deceive members or creditors of the companies concerned. These findings point to very serious misconduct on the part of the applicant in his professional capacity.

The applicant is 49 years of age. He was educated in New South Wales to Leaving Certificate standard. In 1952 he commenced work in Sydney with a firm of Chartered Accountants for whom he worked for about eleven years, during which time he qualified as a Chartered Accountant. He then engaged in commercial employment for about four years, following which he re-entered the accountancy profession. He worked with another firm of accountants in Sydney for about four years and then in 1972 moved to Brisbane. In Brisbane he worked first for a firm of accountants and then, in 1973, commenced practice on his own account. He was first registered as a tax agent in 1973. Apart from the conviction already referred to he has a conviction of a drink-driving charge which I do not regard as relevant for present purposes. There are also before the Tribunal statements by other members of the accountancy profession and clients of the applicant which speak highly of his ability and, indeed, of his integrity.

The question which fell for decision by the respondent Board and now falls for decision by the Tribunal is the fitness or otherwise of the applicant to remain registered as a tax agent.

The phrase "fit and proper person" in the context of a registered tax agent was recently considered by the President of the Tribunal (J.D. Davies, J.) in the case of Stephen Tien-Ping Su and the Tax Agents' Board of South Australia. (Unreported decision delivered on the 24th June, 1982)

"A person is a fit and proper person to handle the affairs of a client if he is a person of good reputation, has a proper knowledge of taxation laws, is able to prepare income tax returns competently and is able to deal competently with any queries which may rise by officers of the Taxation Department. He should be a person of such reputation and ability that officers of the Taxation Department may proceed on the footing that the taxation returns lodged by the agent have been prepared by him

honestly and competently.

There are certain convictions which, in themselves, may be inconsistent with the holding of a tax agent's registration. If a lawyer is convicted of fraud, his name may be struck from the roll of practitioners, for fraud is inconsistent with the practice of the law. If a doctor is convicted of a serious offence relating to illegal drugs, his name may be struck from the register because the offence is inconsistent with the task which medical practitioners perform. If a tax agent is convicted of an offence of tax evasion, his name may be taken from the register, for tax evasion is inconsistent with the role which tax agents are called upon to perform.

If offences for which a tax agent is convicted are not of that character, they may nevertheless justify the removal of his name from the register, if, of themselves, or in combination with other factors, they indicate that the tax agent is not a person of such integrity and competence that clients' affairs should be entrusted to him or that he is not of such integrity and competence that officers of the Taxation Department may place reliance upon returns prepared and lodged by him."

With these observations I respectfully agree.

There is no evidence to suggest that the applicant has not the proper

knowledge and skills necessary to prepare income tax returns competently, nor that he is not able to deal with any queries which may be raised by officers of the Taxation Department. But the very serious question arises as to whether the convictions for offences committed by him whilse in a position of trust as an auditor render him unfit to remain registered as a tax agent.

The statements of Taylor J. in the Ziems' case previously quoted that there are offences the proof of which would, in themselves, establish conduct on the part of the person concerned constituting a disqualification for his profession were made on the context of bar practice. They are, however, in my view, apposite to the practice of any profession or calling involving trust and integrity. The offences were such in my opinion to indicate in the words of Davis J. in Tien-Ping Su's case earlier quoted that "the tax agent is not a person of such integrity ---- that client's affairs should be entrusted to him and also that he is not of such integrity ---- that officers of the Taxation Department may place reliance upon returns prepared and lodged by him." In my opinion the conclusion reached by the respondent Board was the correct one.

The explanations furnished by the appellant in his evidence before the Tribunal do not, in my view, dispel the strong prima facie effect of the evidence of the jury's findings. Acceptance of his explanation would involve the proposition that he acted mistakenly in some instances or, in others, without due care, a proposition which appears to be directly opposed to the jury's findings. I do not think that in the light of the jury's findings that I can attach weight to references from colleagues and clients placed by the applicant before the Tribunal.

As has been stated earlier, the applicant was dealt with by a Disciplinary Committee of the Institute of Chartered Accountants and has been excluded from the Institute. On its face this indicates conduct on his part discreditable to him. The conduct concerned was apparently something separate from that with respect to which he was convicted. It appears that the respondent Board, whilst aware of the Institute's action, based its decision upon the fact of the convictions, a decision which I consider to be the correct one. In these circumstances, I do not think it necessary to consider the weight, if any, to be attached to the Institute's action in the resolution of the present case.

The cancellation of registration of a tax agent may, in many cases, involve serious financial hardship upon the person concerned. In the present case, it appears that Verbena Pty. Ltd. is still operating as a tax agent and that the applicant is employed in that firm. In these circumstances any loss to the applicant may not be as great as might otherwise be the case.

For the foregoing reasons I affirm the decision under review.
