DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

No AT96/51

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION



Re AT96/51

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

Decision No 12795



DECISION



Tribunal Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



Date 15 April 1998



Place Canberra



Decision The Tribunal decides that:



(a) the objection decision in relation to the assessment of the profit on sale of the land as assessable income is affirmed;



(b) the objection decision in relation to additional tax as to the profit on the sale of the land is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with a direction that there should be a further remission of additional tax in this regard so as to reduce the culpability component from 25% to 5% of the tax avoided;



(c) the decision is otherwise affirmed; and



(d) these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.



KL Beddoe

Senior Member



2



CATCHWORDS



INCOME TAX - assessable income - profit from sale of land - whether profits arose from business of company - intention of company director - determination of intention from factual matrix - land purchased for company group headquarters and motel - company group conducted land development business - land offered as security for company group finance land sold in two parcels to fast food chains - company business was land development - profits held to be assessable income.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 25(1), 25A(9), 25A(10)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss 14ZZK, 14ZZJ



FCT v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd 82 ATC 4031

Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v FCT (1950) [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188, 9 ATD 135

Official Receiver in Bankruptcy v FCT (Fox’s Case) [1956] HCA 63; (1956) 96 CLR 370; 11 ATD 119

FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd (1987) [1987] HCA 18; 163 CLR 199, 71 ALR 28



REASONS FOR DECISION



1. The applicant company seeks review of an objection decision in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1988. As the result of an audit by the Taxation Office the applicant was assessed for income tax on profits arising from sale of land in a provincial city. The quantum of the profit assessed is $424,41 9.00.



2. Section 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") relevantly provides for inclusion in a resident taxpayer's assessable income the gross income derived from all sources whether in or out of Australia.



3. Section 25A of the Act may apply in respect of the sale of property acquired before 20 September 1985 so that the assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale, or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme. Section 25A(9) provides that so much (if any) of the proceeds of sale as, in the opinion of the Commissioner, is appropriate shall for the purposes of the Act, be deemed to be profit arising from the sale of the property. The circumstances set out in s 25A(l 0) do not apply to the present case.



4. Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 ("the TAA Act”) has the effect of limiting the applicant to the grounds stated in its notice of objection unless the Tribunal orders otherwise. The section also operates so that the applicant has the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive.



5. At the hearing the applicant's case was conducted by Mr Alexander of counsel and Mr Erskine of counsel conducted the respondent's case. The applicant called five witnesses to give evidence and one witness was called for the respondent. A number of documents were tendered and marked as exhibits. The documents lodged in the Tribunal pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were put before the Tribunal subject to an objection on behalf of the applicant as to their evidentiary value. Because of the operation of s 14ZZJ of the TAA Act these reasons for decision have been framed so as not to be likely to enable identification of the applicant company.



EVIDENCE AND FACTS



6. "A" is a director and the controlling mind of the applicant company - his sister ("J")is also a director. The applicant company is associated with other companies in a group controlled by "A". The principal business of the group is land development but the applicant company does not participate in the ordinary land development activities of the group.



7. "A" entered into negotiations in 1984 with a view to acquisition of the subject land beings Lots 2,3,4,5,6,7,8 and part of Lot 10 in the relevant plan. He did so because his attention had been drawn to the availability of the land. He was at the time interested in and seeking to buy land in the area so as to establish an office for the group. He says that he was concerned that the land development activities of the group were being conducted from premises in another city which was some distance from the principal development activity. The premises were inadequate and did not present well to potential customers. Further he thought it would be prudent to locate his headquarters in the same municipality as the development activity so as to identify with the local community. This would, he said, be an advantage when dealing with the local government authorities. He says that he had searched for vacant land in and around the central business district of the city.



8. Exhibit A includes a copy of a street map which identifies the general location of the land and a copy of the relevant part of a plan of subdivision showing the allotments prior to purchase and resubdivision.



9. Having had his attention directed to the subject land "A" then under-took enquiries to establish the highest and best use for the land taking into account that he wanted to establish his office as part of the development of the land.



10. "A"'s evidence about the purchase of the subject land was uncertain as to detail. I have therefore preferred to rely on the contemporaneous documentary evidence for that detail. However, the evidence of "A" as to his intention at the time of entering into negotiations to purchase the subject land is corroborated in particular by "J" whose evidence I found to be precise. This is perhaps explained by the fact that "J" performed an administrative role in the overall business operations of the group of companies prior to and during her appointment as a director/shareholder of the applicant company. She was clear that a contract for purchase of the subject land was entered into in April 1985.



11. Evidence in the s 37 documents containing copies of letters from the solicitors for the-purchasers of the subject land show the purchasers to be initially "A" and his spouse in the period December 1984/January 1985 (T29/F261-2). However correspondence dated 28 May 1985 is addressed to a company in the group where it is stated that the agreement for sale of the subject land is dated 19 April 1985. (T29/F260) The agreement as signed under the applicant company seal has the words "do not date" on it and is in the name of the applicant company as purchaser for a sale price of $150,000.00. Settlement of the subject land was effected on 2 September after some financing delays and service of a notice to complete (T29/242-251).



.12. "A" said the subject land was then put up for sale in December 1985 for the purpose of increasing the valuation of the land to be used as security for further borrowings by "A"'s group - not the applicant company itself. While it was asserted that this was a method accepted by the financier I am not satisfied that merely putting land up for sale at a nominated selling price thereby increases the valuation of the property. The flaw in the reasoning is readily apparent but there is nothing in the evidence to suggest it was apparent to anyone at the time. It may well be a typical example of the dubious capital raisings that occurred in Australia in the mid 1980's.



13. Witness "F" was the manager of "W Corporation" in 1984. "W Corporation" carried on business as land developers and estate agents. "F" approached "A" in 1984 with a view to "A" purchasing the subject land for use as a truck depot. He says that "A" responded to the effect that he was not interested in a truck depot development but he was interested in building corporate offices. He considered the fact that the land was flood prone was a problem to be taken into account in any development.



14. "F" was unable to recall the land being put up for sale in December 1985 but he did not deny that. He was shown a copy of a "Sales Inspection Report and Selling Agency Agreement" which he acknowledged was signed by him on 18 December 1985. (Exhibit B).



15. Witness "V", also an estate agent, became a business partner of "A" in 1984 in relation to the development of the land subdivision. He said he saw the subject land with “A” in 1984 and thought it suitable for use as the group's headquarters. He said it was envisaged that it be used as company headquarters plus associated -buildings. He also said that he became a shareholder in the applicant company which was incorporated for the purpose of acquiring the subject land. He said the land was still vacant land when he ceased to be associated with the company.



16. A consultant town planner was also called to give evidence on behalf of the applicant. In particular he was involved with "A"'s interests in working on identifying the highest and best use of the subject land. He was also aware at the time that "A" wished to move the administrative head office to the city where the land development activities were being conducted. Document T29/258-9 is a report by the town planner dated 7 June 1985 directed to "A" in his capacity as Managing Director of a company I will call "A Holdings Pty Ltd". The report indicates that discussions had taken place with the city council in relation to the subject land. The proposal was described as "Motel and Ancillary Uses". It recommended the further acquisition of adjoining Lot C to provide access to the site. The report goes on to describe permitted uses, Council planning requirements and draws attention to a similar development containing a motel, offices and shops in a Sydney suburb.



17. Lot C appears to have been acquired by the applicant company for the price of $40,000.00 after the service of a notice to complete on the applicant company dated 17 January 1986 which provides for a settlement date of 3 February 1986 (T29/F229).



18. The town planner said in his oral evidence that he was also working for KFC, a fast food operator, who were interested in acquiring a site in the vicinity. He introduced KFC to "A", the result being the eventual sale of part of the land by the applicant company to another party ("S") who then on-sold to KFC. One of the companies in the group controlled by "A" paid the town planner a fee of $3,000.00 for introducing KFC (T281172). The basis for payment of that introduction fee is said to be that the associated company derived commission on the sale by "S" to KFC. However, it was the understanding of the town planner that the sale was effectively by the applicant company to KFC.



19. Evidence was also given by an accountant who had became a shareholder in the applicant company. He was appointed a director and secretary of the applicant company from 1 July 1985. He said that because of the land development activities of the group, which he described as controversial, the decision was taken to establish a headquarters for the group in the same city as the land development activities. The subject property was acquired for the purpose of putting the group's offices in the city. He noted that the ownership of the applicant company differed from the land development group as a whole and he regarded the investment in the applicant company as a long term investment. He however stated that there were no minutes of the directors of the applicant company in which any decision about acquisition and sale of the subject land by the applicant company was recorded.



20. The accountant said that he had arranged to advance an amount of $70,000.00 to the applicant company in the belief that it was to be used to assist in financing the purchase of the subject property. He said that the funds were provided as short term assistance. By letter dated 25 March 1986 (T29/226) his firm purportedly acting for a client, stated that the loan had initially been for a period of two months to 30 October 1985 but had been extended to 30 December 1985. Repayment was required by 27 March 1986 of the advance ($70,000.00) plus interest ($6,652.09). The accountant said he ceased to be involved in the group associated with the applicant company in 1995 because there was not enough room for "A" and himself in the group.



21. He said he was aware that the subject land was put up for sale and remained on the market for a month or two. He said this was done to convince a merchant bank as to the value of the property for security on loans. However the offer by a fast food operator McDonalds, to purchase part of the subject land "came out of left field". At that time the site was not on the market. He said that the offices of another land developer in the city were put on the market at about the same time as the offer from McDonalds. The offices were an attractive proposition for the group because the building was well located and suitable for the group's purposes.



22. As a result in October 1986 the applicant sold approximately half of the subject land (Lots 2,3,4,,5 and part of Lot 10) to McDonalds for $250,000.00 without effecting any development of the land (T13). That sale was effected after the applicant gave instructions to sell to McDonalds in July 1986. Those instructions were preceded by negotiations which had commenced by 15 April 1986 (T291225).



23. The balance of the land was sold to "S" for $420,000.00 in September 1987 who then onsold to KFC for $537,000.00 with pro rata commission. payable to "A" on the second sale.



FINDINGS



24. While the initial purchase instructions were that "A" and his wife were to be the purchasers, the applicant company was subsequently substituted as the purchaser on the agreement for sale (T29/264-5) which was said to be dated 19 April 1985 (T291260). There is nothing in the minutes of meetings of directors before the Tribunal which sheds any light on the acquisition of the subject land (T28).



25. "A" together with "H" became the only shareholders in the applicant company on 27 February 1985. 1 am satisfied therefore that there was no intention on the part of the company, acting through "A" as its controlling mind to purchase the subject land prior to "A" and "H" becoming the shareholders.



26. There is, however, earlier evidence of intention to sell the land. The evidence of "V" was that "A Holdings Pty Ltd" was a company controlled by "A" and "K Pty Ltd" was a company controlled by another person who has not given evidence before the Tribunal. "A" said that "A Holdings Pty Ltd" was a family company outside the development group for long term investments. He said that "K Pty Ltd" became interested in joining in on the development of the subject land with "A" suggesting a joint venture for certain blocks of the subject land. The evidence of "A" was that those proposals did not eventuate - the proposals "fell by the wayside in late 1984 or early 1985” ie. before the applicant company had come under the control of "A".



27. These proposals were however formalised in September 1984 by a letter of understanding between "A Holdings Pty Ltd" and "K Pty Ltd" (T29/266). The essence of that agreement was that portion of the land (Lots 7, 8 and part of 1 0) was to be sold to "K Pty Ltd" at a price determined by a formula set out in the letter of understanding. The attempt by "A" to convince me that the word "self' in the letter of understanding did not have its ordinary meaning was not plausible.

28. When the proposals with "K Pty Ltd" did not proceed, "A" decided', on his evidence, that "A Holdings Pty Ltd" was no longer the appropriate company to hold the subject land. It was not explained to the Tribunal why that was the case.



29. In his evidence "A" said "I thought, as a developer we had a winning site" and finance of the property was not a concern, this was a view inconsistent with the evidence of the accountant.



30. The subject land was used as security for borrowings for the business of the group conducted outside the applicant company. The evidence about the subject property being put on the market in December 1985 to assist this purpose leads me to the view that there was not any then existing intention to develop the subject land as an investment. To encumber the land to raise finance for other purposes suggests that financing a development of the land was not being contemplated in the immediate future. This is reinforced by the decision of the directors to sell the land in December 1995/January 1986 as evidenced by the "Sales Inspection Report and Selling Agency Agreement".



31. I am satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that when the understanding with "K Pty Ltd" did not proceed and it was decided "A Holdings Pty Ltd" was no longer the appropriate vehicle for holding the land purchase by the applicant company was arranged so that the subject land could be resold at a profit as the opportunity presented itself. I am not satisfied that there is any other explanation for "A Holdings Pty Ltd" not going ahead with the purchase in accordance with the letter of understanding.



32. I am also satisfied that the applicant company did not initially purchase the subject land for the purpose of resale to McDonalds and KFC.



33. An essential feature of the applicant's case is the decision to relocate the group's head office to the city in which its land -subdivisions were located. In this it was competing with "W Corporation". Exhibit D establishes that by April 1985 it was public knowledge that there was a possibility of "W Corporation" being wound up. It would be reasonable to infer that "A"'s situation was such that he would have a keen interest in the day to day operations of "W Corporation" because of their adjoining land subdivisions. That apparent interest resulted in "A ... s group taking over "W Corporation's" land subdivision and its head office in the city which was finalised by 1 September 1986 (Exhibit D).



34. On the basis of the evidence of "A" to the extent it is corroborated by "J", "F" and the accountant I am satisfied that the initial intention for purchase of the subject land was for "A Holdings Pty Ltd" to purchase the site and develop a corporate head office together with an investment in conjunction with "K Pty Ltd". For reasons which are not clear the intention as to purchase changed so that "A" and his wife became the purchasers (T291262).



35. Subsequently, in February 1985 "A" gained control of the applicant company and the intention as to purchase changed again because the applicant company became the purchaser. The applicant company was substituted as purchaser by the time contracts were exchanged for purchase of Lot C on 20 June 1985. It is not clear to me on the evidence exactly when the applicant company was first nominated as purchaser for the subject land.



36. It is also not clear to me when sale of the subject land and Lot C (respectively to both McDonalds and "S") became the dominant purpose of the applicant company.



37. This matter is not to be decided on the basis that the applicant was associated with other entities engaged in land development as its principal business. That does not change the fact that "A" who was the controlling mind of the applicant company also appears to have been the controlling mind of the whole group. However, his evidence was that the applicant company was acquired specifically to hold the subject land. It seems that the desire to have other persons also as shareholders of the 'company probably explains why "A Holdings Pty Ltd" was not used to hold the subject land.



38. The business of the applicant was to hold the land and develop the land presumably to its highest and best use. Although the original intention was to hold the land and develop corporate offices and a further development as a motel whereby part of the land would be sold to "K Pty Ltd" that was no longer the case when the applicant company purchased the land.



39. The factors which are inconsistent with long term holding are the initial financing of the purchase with short term finance and then the use of the subject land as security for substantial borrowings by the land development company. The steps taken to It revalue" the land to impress the financier are, in my view, quite inconsistent with an intention of long term holding. In fact the land was resubdivided and half sold to McDonalds which, it is reasonable to infer, was the highest and best use of the land. That the applicant company sold the balance of the land to a purchaser who then sold to KFC with commission paid to "A" by "S" may suggest a failure to realise the best price but does not change the fact.



SUBMISSIONS



40. The applicant submits that there must be a dominant purpose of profit making by sale for a profit on sale to be assessable income. In this case, the applicant company says the dominant purpose was to provide for a corporate headquarters and for commercial development such as development of a motel.



41. It is submitted that this did not involve the company entering into a profit making undertaking or scheme but was an isolated transaction entered into by the company for other purposes.



WHETHER SALE WAS REALISATION OF A CAPITAL ASSET



42. In FCT v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd 82 ATC 4031 the question was whether the development and sale of land held by the company over many years for another purpose was more than a mere realisation of a capital asset. The Federal Court (Deane J dissenting) held that the. company's activities did not go beyond the realisation of the capital asset so that the proceeds of sale were not part of its assessable income (79 ATC 4648). That decision was reversed in the High Court (82 ATC 4031).



43. Gibbs CJ reviewed the authorities in some detail and concluded at page 4038 as follows:



"If the taxpayer does no more than realise an asset, the profits are not taxable. It does not matter that the taxpayer goes about the realization in an enterprising way, so as to secure the best price. As I have said in F.C. of T. v N.F. Williams, at ATC p.4194;

C.L.R. p. 249:



'The situation is not altered by the fact that the landowner seeks and acts upon the advice of an expert as to the best method of subdivision and sale or by the fact that he carries out work such as grading, levelling, road building and the provision of reticulation for water and power to enable the land to be sold to its best advantage.'



Further, the mere magnitude of the realization does not convert it into a business: Commr. of Taxes v. British Australian Wool Realization Association (1931) A. C. 224, at p.252. But if the taxpayer does engage in an operation of business, the proceeds are income and taxable."



His Honour then went on to discuss the decision of Williams J in Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v FCT (1 950) [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188, 9 ATD 135 and the decision of the Court in Official Receiver in Bankruptcy v FCT (Fox's Case) (1 956) [1956] HCA 63; 96 CLR 370; 1 1 ATD 11 9.



44. At page 4039 his Honour sets out his conclusions in relation to the case before the Court as follows:



"In the present case I gravely doubt whether the profits resulting from the development, subdivision and sale of the land would have been taxable if it had not been for the events that occurred on 20th December 1967. Had those events not occurred, the situation of the taxpayer would have been analogous to that of the company in Scottish Australian Mining Co. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. However, on 20th December 1967, the taxpayer was transformed from a company which held land for the domestic purposes of its shareholders to a company whose purpose was to engage in a commercial venture with a view to profit. Counsel for the taxpayer submitted that it was not permissible to blur the distinction between the company and its shareholders. That of course is true, but in deciding whether what was done was an operation of business, it is relevant to consider the purpose with which the taxpayer acted, and, since the taxpayer is a company, the purposes of those who control it are its purposes. In Ruhamah Property Co. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. the majority of the Court regarded as important, if not decisive, the purposes with which the shareholders and directors of the company acted, although Isaacs J who dissented thought it erroneous to consider a company merely as machinery for carrying out individual purposes: see at pp. 160, 162 and 166. However, in my opinion Isaacs J took too rigid a view of the effect of Salomon v. Salomon & Co. (1897) A.C. 22 if he thought that in determining the purpose with which a company acted it was not permissible to have regard to the intentions of the directors who controlled it. In the present case, the three companies which became the shareholders, or the two which became the managers (it matters not which), represented the directing mind and will of the taxpayer and controlled what it did, and their state of mind was the state of mind of the taxpayer. H.L. Bolton (Engineering) Co. Ltd. v. T.J. Graham & Sons Ltd (1957) 1 Q. B. 159, 172; cited in Tesco Supermarkets Ltd. v. Nattrass [1971] UKHL 1; (1972) A. C. 153 at pp. 171 and 187,. and see B. Elsey Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T 69 ATC 4115 at p. 4117[1970] HCA 5; ,, (1969) 121 C. L. R. 1 19, at p' 12 1. The purpose of those controllin the taxpayer was to engage in a business venture with a view to profit. Moreover, although the taxpayer was not formed for the purpose of selling land, after December 1967 it became a company which existed solely for the purpose of carrying out the business operation on which the new shareholders had decided to embark when they acquired their shares. It is in the light of these circumstances that the extensive work of development and subdivision is seen to be more than the mere realization of an existing asset and to be work done in the course of what was truly a business venture. For these reasons, although the case is not without its difficulties, I have concluded that the profits were income within ordinary concepts and taxable accordingly."



45. Mason J also considered that the intention of the company was to be ascertained by reference to the intention of the company's controlling mind. At page 4047 his Honour stated:



To my mind in deciding whether what the respondent did was the mere realization of an asset or the carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme, it is relevant to take into account that the company came under the ownership and control of new shareholders whose purpose was to use the company for the execution of what, judged from their point of view, was a profit-making undertaking or scheme. In deciding whether the company was carrying on the business of land development it is material that the new shareholders would have been carrying on such a business had they purchased the land from the company and carried out the development and sale on their own account."



46. In Westfield Ltd v FCT(1991) 99 ALR 510 the Federal Court decided that a profit on sale will be assessable as income if the purpose of profit making exists in relation to the transaction and a mere possibility of the sale of land is insufficient to establish a profit making scheme. Hill J deliver the judgment of the Full Court (Lockhart and Gummow JJ agreeing). His Honour referred at pages 518-9 to the decision of the High Court in FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd (1 987) [1987] HCA 18; 163 CLR 199, 71 ALR 28 citing the following extract from the High Court judgment (ALR at 32):



"Although it is well settled that a profit or gain made in the ordinary course of carrying on a business constitutes income, it does not follow that a profit or gain made in a transaction entered into otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on the taxpayer's business is not income. Because a business is carried on with a view to profit, a gain made in the ordinary course of carrying on the business is invested with the profit making purpose, thereby stamping the profit with the character of income. But a gain made otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on the business which nevertheless arises from a transaction entered into by the taxpayer with the intention or purpose of making a profit or gain may well constitute income. Whether it does depends very much on the circumstances of the case. Generally speaking, however, it may be said that if the circumstances are such as to give rise to the inference that the taxpayer's intention or purpose in entering into the transaction was to make a profit or gain, the profit or gain will be income, notwithstanding that the transaction was extraordinary judged by reference to the ordinary course of the taxpayer's business. Nor does the fact that a profit or gain is made as the result of an isolated venture or a 'one-off' transaction preclude it from being properly characterised as income (FCT v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 39 ALR 521; 150 CLR 355 at 366-7, 376). The authorities establish that a profit or gain so made will constitute income if the property generating the profit or gain was acquired in a business operation or commercial transaction for the purpose of profit-making by the means giving rise to the profit. "



47. I am satisfied and find that there was a twofold purpose in relation to the initial purchase of the land. An unascertained portion was to be used for construction of a corporate head office and an unascertained portion was to be developed (probably as a motel) as either a short-term or long-term investment. I can only speculate as to which. It seems to be open on the evidence that this developed portion may well have been intended to be sold as was indicated under the original arrangement between "A Holdings Pty Ltd" and "K Pty Ltd".



48. Within months of acquisition of the subject land by the applicant company steps were taken to realise the potential profit for the benefit of the land development business conducted by an associated company. The informal revaluation and provision of the subject land as security to the financier effectively committed the applicant to the land development business although not as a participant in that business.



49. The applicant also took steps to improve the value of the land in two ways: Lot C was purchased to improve access to the subject land previously purchased and the total holding of land was resubdivided to permit the land to be used for its highest and best use. The applicant company accounted for a capital gain in relation to Lot C in its income tax return (Tl 1).



50. I am satisfied that the situation was summed up by "A" in his evidence when he said "as a developer we have a winning site!" The land had been effectively committed by the applicant company to land development and that became the applicant company's business when it was offered as security (or sale) for financing the land development activities of the group in December 1985/January 1986. It appears that the company group at that time was experiencing some financial difficulties and required capital to continue with it development activities.

51. Consideration of the material before me has led to the conclusion that "A" as controlling mind of the company always had an intention to realise at least part of the land either by development and sale or by resubdivision and sale. As it transpired the land was resubdivided and amalgamated and the subject land sold as two parcels. This was an outcome not inconsistent with the original intention of "A".



52. Therefore, I find that the profits from the sale of the land as two parcels to each respective purchaser are from profit making activities under s 25A and are to be included in its assessable income for 1988. The intention of "A" as controlling mind of the applicant was to gain short term profit from the sale or development and then sale of at least part of the land. Opportunities however arose which maximised that use and gave rise to the sale of the whole of the subject land. This is consistent with the initial intention to develop and or sell the land (or part of it) to thus achieve the highest and best use giving rise to a profit on the eventual sale.



ADDITIONAL TAX



53. Little was said to me about the additional tax assessed. Remission of the additional tax was apparently considered taking into account the respondent's ruling IT2517. This Tribunal is, of course not bound to follow that ruling but would do so if it was thought to be the appropriate basis for exercising the discretion in s 227 of the Act. Mr Erskine submitted that I should do so in this case.



54. On any fair assessment of the facts this case must be regarded as contentious. Some but not a full disclosure was made in the income tax return. In relation to the so called "culpability component' relating to the profits on sale I would further remit the amount imposed by the Act to 5% of the tax understated in relation to those profits.



55. In so far as additional tax was imposed and partially remitted for undisclosed income in the form of interest and commission I will not disturb the respondent's decision to effectively impose a culpability component at 40%. Nothing has been said to me that would justify further remission in relation to these items.



CONCLUSION



56. For these reasons the objection decision in relation to the assessment of the profit on sale of the land will be affirmed. The objection decision in relation to additional tax will be set aside and the matter remitted to the respondent with a direction that there should be a further remission of additional tax so as to reduce the culpability component relating to the profit on sale of land from 25% to 5% of the tax avoided; the decision otherwise to be affirmed as relates to interest and commission.



57. I will certify that these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.
