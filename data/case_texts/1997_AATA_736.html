REASONS FOR DECISION

22 April 1997 Mrs Joan Dwyer, Senior Member

Mr D Elsum, Member

Mr W G McLean, Member



  1. This is an application for review of a reviewable objection decision of the Commissioner of Taxation disallowing a claim for refund of sales tax overpaid by the applicant on equipment imported into Australia between 1991 and 1994. The application is made under s 60(2) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992.
  2. At the hearing Mr R Benson, a solicitor, appeared for the applicant. Mr T Murphy of Counsel appeared for the respondent. The Tribunal had before it the documents ("the T documents") lodged pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") and the exhibits tendered during the hearing. Evidence for the applicant was given by the Managing Director of the applicant company, and by three other witnesses.
  3. It is not in dispute that the applicant company imported therapeutic goods into Australia from a date prior to 1991, and paid sales tax on the importation of those goods until it obtained an exemption from payment of sales tax on the basis that the goods were covered by Item 93 of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992. There is no clear statement in the T documents setting out the dates when the exemption was claimed or granted. An interim decision to treat the goods as exempt pending a review was made by the respondent on 29 April 1994 (T4). On 18 March 1994 an application was made for a refund of $143,789.00 sales tax paid. Under s 51(3) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 a claimant is not entitled to a credit "unless the claim for the credit is lodged within three years after the time when the credit arises" (emphasis added). The parties proceded on the understanding that this provision limited the applicant's refund claim to sales tax paid during the three years preceding the claim for refund. There was some question on the material before the Tribunal as to the precise dates. The parties referred to the three year period from February 1991 to February 1994. The application for refund of sales tax was refused on the ground that the Commissioner considered that any sales tax raised in respect of the goods had been passed on by the applicant to its customers.

RELEVANT LEGISLATIVE PROVISIONS

  1. During the period in which sales tax was paid in respect of the exempt goods, the relevant legislation has been amended. In 1992 a new scheme of Sales Tax legislation was introduced which replaced the former 27 Acts with just six Acts. The former Sales Tax Assessment Acts 1930 (Nos 1-11) provided for the assessment and payment of sales tax in different situations by different persons. The Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 covered the sales tax to be "levied and paid upon the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia by a taxpayer and sold by him or treated by him as stock for sale by retail or applied to his own use" (s17(1)). The Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 6) 1930 covered the sales tax to be "levied and paid upon the sale value of goods imported into Australia by a taxpayer and sold by him or applied by him to his own use."(emphasis added)
  2. As the applicant company paid sales tax on goods imported into Australia, the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 6) applied to the goods, prior to the commencement of the new sales tax legislative package on 1 January 1993. Although the delegate of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation referred in the statement provided under s 37 of the AAT Act to s 26(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930, both parties proceeded on the basis that s 11(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 6) Act applied in this case. We consider that to be a correct analysis. Section 26(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 and s 11 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 6) 1930 were in identical terms. Section 11(1) provided:
“11 (1) Subject to sub-section (1A), where the Commissioner finds in any case that tax has been overpaid by a person, the Commissioner shall -

(a) refund the amount of tax overpaid,. or

(b) apply the amount of tax overpaid against any liability of the person to the Commonwealth, being a liability arising under, or by virtue of, an Act of which the Commissioner has the general administration and refund any part of the amount that is not so applied.
  1. (IA) Sub-section (1) does not apply in relation to any tax paid by a person unless the Commissioner is satisfied that the tax has not been passed on by the person to another person, or, if passed on to another person, has been refunded to the other person. "

By s 4(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment (Transitional) Act 1992, the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 6) 1930 ceased to apply in relation to sales tax imposed on any act, transaction or operation that happened on or after 1 January 1993. Section 51 of that Act so far as relevant provides:

"51 (1) Tables 3 and 3A set out the situations in which a claimant is entitled to a credit.

Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 provides that on an application for review of a reviewable objection decision such as this the applicant has the burden of proving that the decision concerned should have been made differently.

THE ISSUE

  1. The sole issue is whether the sales tax paid by the applicant company has been passed on by that company to its customers. The applicant company claims that it bore the sales tax itself rather than pass it on. In support of its appeal against the rejection of the application for a refund of sales tax, the applicant's customs agent in a letter dated 31 May 1994 stated:
"Our appeal is based on the fact that no Sales Tax has been passed on to the consumer for the relevant products.

To support this claim we have available the original sales invoices for the relevant products, which do no [sic] show any amount of Sales Tax there on.

All sales of the products have been made without Sales Tax being charged to the customer. "

The customs agent wrote that a number of competitors of the applicant company, had received sales tax exemptions. The letter concluded:

"a) For your information, all of the companies in this industry compete for a share of the same market, whether this be at organised trade fairs, casual leasing of shopping centres, trade promotions, professional presentations and direct marketing.

From personal industry experience, we believe that each company's' [sic] imports and locally manufactured product is very similar in actual cost to land/produce.


  1. The setting of retail pricing's [sic] is established through competing 'at the coal face' by aggressive industry marketing.
In our clients' case, they are competing against . . . in particular, who were selling identical goods exclusive of Sales Tax, at a selling price which has been met by our client in order to compete in the marketplace. This is a competitive anomaly in the market place, where our client has incurred and absorbed Sales Tax purely in order to compete at the same selling price.

The Sales Tax component was elected to be paid by our client at the time of importation and absorbed by them at this point in time, as it was an insignificant amount when compared to the full retail selling price. This appeared to be the industry trend until the exemption decision filtered through all importers/manufacturers. "
  1. Mr Benson submitted that it could not be the law that sales tax would have been passed on "in every case where the goods had been sold", whether or not there was a conscious decision to do so. He said that if that analysis were adopted there would never be a case where a refund would be paid, unless the sales tax had been refunded to the customers. He said that the evidence would establish that the selling price of these goods was not calculated to include an element in respect of sales tax. Nor were fluctuations in purchase price due to changes in the financial market reflected in the selling price. He said the books to be produced by his client would show that the prices of the goods remained relatively constant in spite of changes in the cost of the goods and in the sales tax payable.
  2. The applicant's Statement Of Facts And Contentions stated:
"FACTS

(1) [P]rices were determined solely by market forces and were not calculated by reference to an "into store cost”.

(2) From time to time, there were significant variations in various elements of the "into store cost" to the Applicant including overseas price, exchange rate variations, sales tax paid at the point of entry into Australia and landing charges. For example, exchange rates payable on overseas purchase prices varied from 72.73 to 107.10 on sample shipments arriving between April, 1991 and March, 1994 and landing charges varied significantly depending on the size of the shipment.

(3) Despite very significant "into store costs' variations, no comparable or related variations were made in the Applicant's selling prices which remained linked to its competitor's prices and the Applicant retained its market driven pricing policy

CONTENTIONS


(1) No component for sales tax has been included in the prices charged by the Applicant to its customers. No referable increases in prices were made during periods when sales tax liability increased due to unfavourable exchange rate and other variations.

(2) In computing the price of its goods, the Applicant took into account market forces which set a price well in excess of the Applicant's into store costs and it was, therefore, unnecessary to include in its prices the amounts that it erroneously paid as sales tax.

(3) The method of setting its sale prices adopted by the Applicant ignored the amounts of sales tax which it had paid.

The Commissioner should have been satisfied that sales tax erroneously paid on the subject goods by the Applicant was not passed on to another person pursuant to Sections 11 and 11 (1A) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 6) 1930"

THE EVIDENCE

  1. Mr Worswick was the customs agent who prepared the application for a refund of sales tax. He gave evidence that in preparing the necessary documentation he had noticed that the sales tax per unit, which of course was related to the landed costing per unit, varied substantially from shipment to shipment over a period of time in accordance with changes in the exchange rate, changes in purchase price and even changes in the rate of sale tax. But he said there was no corresponding change to the purchase price of the units.
  2. The second witness called on behalf of the applicant was Mr Boxer who is a certified practicing accountant. He said that the only matter in which he had ever been engaged to act for the applicant company was the analysis of its accounts in support of this application. He said he had not acted as accountant for the company in any other matter and had no on-going role with the applicant company. He said that Mr Worswick had given him the applicant company's sales journal and sales ledger and other documents to analyse.
  3. Mr Boxer in a statement dated 1 November 1996 (exhibit A4) set out the material he had reviewed as follows:

“1. Extracts of Sales Journals on the following dates - 31st August 1991 to 3rd September 1991; 11th August 1992 to 18th August 1992,. 31st October 1993 to 26th January 1994.

  1. Photocopied Landed Costing Reports for the following dates - 8th April 1991; 8th June 1991; 11th July 1991; 12th July 1991; 1st November 1993, 5th November 1993 prepared by Mr Warren Worswick.

3. Copy Sales Invoices as follows..-

Invoice # 25976 dated 15th November 1991

Invoice # 15770 dated 20th October 1992

Invoice # 27816 dated 16th November 1992

Invoice # 29902 dated 7th August 1993

Invoice # 29379 dated 28th November 1993

In determining whether sales tax has been passed on [to] the applicant company's customers, we have reviewed the selling price of the products over the period 1991 through 1994. We have also reviewed the landed costing reports as described above.'

He concluded:

"it is my opinion that the sales tax paid on the importation of the ... Units ... was absorbed by the applicant company and not passed on to its customers."


  1. Mr Boxer said that he had looked at sales from August 1991 through to January 1995 and had concluded that by and large the sale price of particular units was consistent throughout that period, except for certain reductions which he had been advised were made to compete with competitors selling items at a cheaper price. Mr Boxer said he had found that the landed cost of the unit which comprised the majority of sales by the applicant company, fluctuated from $48.68 up to $83.29 over the period he studied. He said that the sales tax per unit also fluctuated from $7.00 to near $14.00 over that period, but there was no corresponding fluctuation in the sales price of the unit. He said he therefore concluded that the costs were absorbed rather than passed on. He summarised the basis of his view (at transcript p32):
“Well, looking at the costing - the accumulation of the costs of importing the product 1 could see no bearing between the accumulation of the costs and the establishment of the sales price. The sales price was fixed by market forces, as 1 could ascertain, and really ... any additional costs that were borne in getting those units into the country were absorbed within that room that they had to work within ... - from what 1 can see, they haven't taken the approach of.. okay, these are our costs, this is the margin we'd like to make, this is the sales tax that we've had to incur, therefore we’ll charge this price. So we haven't got costs, the internal margin that the customer obviously would not be aware of and then at the bottom, sales tax at X per cent, thank you. There was no evidence of that in anything that I'd reviewed.'
  1. The Managing Director of the applicant company said that he is the owner of the company. He said that he made the decision as to what prices would be applied to each of the products that his company sold. He said he determined those prices according to market competition. He explained that he could not base his selling price on the costs associated with the units because the commodity was sold at a show or shopping centre and his competitors were present at the same shows or shopping centres selling similar products, so he could not afford to be undercut by those competitors. He said he only found out that his competitors were not paying sales tax when he got talking to one of the opposition people at a show and he told him that he was "stupid" to pay sales tax on those goods as they were sales tax exempt.
  2. The Managing Director said that he did not reduce the price of the goods even if he knew they were going to be exported so that sales tax would not be payable. He was cross-examined about whether he would have to pay sales tax if he was selling goods to a person living in New Zealand, or to a distributor in New Zealand. He replied:
“Of course we do. "

He was then asked whether sales to New Zealand residents were subject to Australian sales tax. He replied that he was not interested in that issue. He said (at transcript p54):

"To tell you the truth,, my mind, and still is my mind, is to sell and generate sales, but really the tax and all this - as you can see, over those 20 years 1 never bother actually. 1 knew that 1 have to sell. To be honest with you 1 didn't take even in consideration. If is an overseas guest here and buying the products from Australia, he has to pay the same price as everybody else ... . "

The Managing Director also said that he had never enquired as to whether sales tax was payable on therapeutic goods he had sold to hospitals.

  1. The T documents (at p13) show that there were some instances when sales tax was not paid on entries of the applicant company's goods into Australia. The Managing Director said that if this had happened it must have been because a different customs agent had, without his knowledge, claimed an exemption. He said that the goods in respect of which sales tax was not paid were still sold at the standard price.
  2. The Managing Director said that he was aware that there had been a couple of incidences when sales tax had not been paid, but he did not think he ever raised any question about it with his office manager. He explained (at transcript p60):
“... my mind is in generating sales, looking after the dealers and making sure that we are dressed well and maintained well and work hard.'
  1. Mr Murphy in cross-examination asked the Managing Director why he did not reduce his sales price when selling to sales tax exempt hospitals. He replied (at transcript p61):
“.... To tell you the truth, the reason why is because it's too much bother to actually apply for one unit to ask the Taxation Office to reply. It's cost us actually more to actually ask them to reply than getting back an amount of money. "

The evidence establishes that the applicant company did not have bulk sales to hospitals. The Managing Director said that no more than one percent of his sales were to hospitals. When the parties' representatives looked through the sales ledger they informed the Tribunal that they had identified sales of four units on four separate occasions to retirement homes, but no sales to hospitals.

  1. Mr Murphy questioned the Managing Director at some length about goods sold to New Zealand but the evidence did not establish whether goods were sold to New Zealand at the relevant time. Nor was there any evidence to suggest that any sales to New Zealand reflected the fact that sales tax was not paid in respect of sales to New Zealand.
  2. There was a discrepancy in the evidence as to the gross income earned by the applicant company. The documents prepared by the applicant's customs agent showed total sales (at T p25) for the 1992193 financial year as $732,662 whereas the profit and loss statement for the year ended 30 June 1994 (exhibit A2) showed total sales for the year ended 30 June 1993 as $833,884.00. The Managing Director was unable to suggest any explanation for the discrepancy other than miscalculation by his accountant or customs agent. At one stage he thought it could have represented sales to New Zealand, but he then said that he had ceased selling to New Zealand by then.
  3. The fourth witness called by the applicant was the former competitor who had called the Managing Director "stupid" when he realised he was paying sales tax on the goods he imported. He said he still sells the same goods but in a different market. He explained that he was a licensed customs agent, but had not practiced as such in recent years. He explained that while working as a customs agent for one of the applicant's competitors in the therapeutic goods field, he had discovered that a sales tax exemption ruling should have been obtained in respect of those goods. He said he obtained an exemption ruling for that client and shortly afterwards commenced selling on a part-time basis for the same client. He said he then decided to work full-time in that field and gave up his work as a customs agent and started his own business selling therapeutic goods.
  4. The witness explained that at one show he engaged in conversation with the Managing Director of the applicant company and realised that he was paying sales tax on importation of the subject goods. He said he suggested to the Managing Director that he engage another customs agent. He said he recommended Mr Worswick with whom he had had business dealings for many years.
  5. This witness said that he and the Managing Director had price wars at shows, where they used to compete to undersell each other, but in the end they decided to stop this situation. He said that at trade shows everybody sold at the same retail price because if one dealer was selling at a lower price nobody else would make any sales.
  6. The witness said that to his knowledge people in the industry sold their goods at the price the market place would bear rather than at a price based on marking up the costs. He said that the companies would never be able to ascertain the profit margin on a particular consignment:
"... because there were too many large variables in regard to the exhibition expenses and property overheads. And then you just couldn't work it out. There was sufficient profit margin in built like in the product - in "the retail price' - to sort of cover incidentals I guess. "


  1. The Tribunal asked the witness whether he costed his own products to the point where he knew what was the cost to him. He said that the price at which he sold goods was dictated purely and simply by the market price. He explained that the price at which the product comes into Australia is very reasonable, but the large variables are the commissions and the displays. He said he simply sold at the market price and knew that would include a profit margin.

FINDINGS

  1. The Tribunal found Mr Worswick and Mr Boxer to be reliable witnesses. We accept that they performed their tasks of analysis of the records of the applicant company in a careful, accurate and professional manner. We accept that they found no evidence that the applicant company had calculated the selling price of its goods on a basis which included a component in respect of sales tax.
  2. We accept that Mr Boxer performed an expert analysis of the extract of sales journals, landed costing reports and sales invoices referred to in his statement dated 1 November 1996 and that from that analysis he found at (paragraph 2 exhibit A4):
“The costing sheets reviewed by us indicate that not only did the sales tax rate increase from 20% in 1991 to 21% in 1993, but due to the movement in the exchange rate between the Australian dollar and the Yen and variations in a number of units imported in different shipments, the unit cost has fluctuated from $48.68 per unit imported on the 8th June 1991 to $83.29 per unit for units imported on the 1st November 1993.

Over that same period, the sales tax per unit has fluctuated from $7.34 per unit to $12.48 per unit. The selling price, however, has remained at $399 irrespective of increases in costs.'
  1. Mr Boxer noted that he had been advised that the sales price was set by the market place in line with the price charged by competitors. That evidence was supported not only by the evidence of the Managing Director of the applicant company but also by the evidence of his competitor and also by a statement sworn by the applicant's office manager, who, unfortunately was unable to be available for cross-examination because of ill-health. We accept the evidence given by the former competitor of the applicant company that he and other companies in the field fix their prices by reference to market forces rather than to costs.
  2. There were some points on which the Managing Director was confused when giving his evidence, such as how he fixed the price of goods he sold overseas or the variations in the profit as shown in the company's financial records, but we do not regard those matters as relevant to the issue before the Tribunal. We accept the Managing Director's description of himself as a salesman who concentrates on sales and is not very concerned about the paper work which he leaves to others in his organisation. Nonetheless we find on the basis of his evidence, supported as it was by that of his other witnesses, that he is the person who determines the selling price of the goods the applicant company imports and that he determines that price by what the market will bear, and the prices at which his competitors are selling similar goods, and not by reference to the cost to him of those goods and their associated costs. We find and are satisfied that the price does not reflect or pass on to purchasers the sales tax paid on the goods.
  3. We accept the evidence of the Managing Director which is consistent with the analysis of Mr Boxer and with the company's financial records that sales tax was a minor expense by comparison to purchase price of goods, shipping costs and the major items of hire of space for the sale of goods at shows, commission to staff, staff uniforms and office overheads. We find that sales tax was simply absorbed by the applicant company. The evidence that even after the sales tax exemption was obtained sales prices of the goods in question did not drop to take into account the saving in sales tax supports that analysis.

LEGAL ISSUES

  1. The parties agreed that the two issues before the Tribunal were the meaning of the words "passed on" in sales tax legislation, and whether on the facts in this matter the Tribunal was satisfied that the amount of sales tax paid by the applicant company was absorbed, and not passed on to its purchasers.

(i) the meaning of the words "passed on".

  1. The parties relied on three decisions as to the meaning of the words "passed on" in sales tax legislation. In Otto Australia Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 25 FCR 257 Lockhart J was considering whether sales tax was payable by Otto on the importation of waste carts from Germany and if not, whether it had been passed on so as to preclude payment of a refund of sales tax. His Honour found that Otto was not entitled to any sales tax exemption. Thus there was no question of a refund of sales tax. However Lockhart J did address that issue. His Honour said at pp263-264:
“Counsel for the applicant conceded that the applicant's charge to each council is computed or calculated by reference to its costs which include but are not limited to the landed costs of the Otto carts and that the landed cost itself includes customs duty and sales tax. But counsel argued that sales tax is not charged as a separate item and that in those circumstances it could not be said that there had been a "Passing on' of the sales tax by the applicant to the councils of the sales tax paid by the applicant. (emphasis added)

...

Although it is not strictly necessary for me to deal with this question it was argued by counsel and 1 think it desirable that 1 express my view on it.

"Passed on" in the context of s 11 (1A) must be given a sensible and practical meaning to cover the wide variety of circumstances which may arise in practice in sales tax legislation. It is plain from the facts of the present case that the applicant did in fact "pass on" the sales tax to councils concerned in that, when calculating the contract price for the tender with the councils, the applicant included a component, though not shown separately in the contract documents with the councils, of sales tax paid by it on importation of the Otto carts. In other words the applicant bore the burden of the sales tax and passed it on in the price which it charged the councils for the performance of its contractual obligations. Had the applicant not done so and had the sales tax not been exigible then s 11 (1) would have required the Commissioner to refund the amount of the overpaid tax.” (emphasis added)
  1. The decision of Lockhart J was appealed to the Full Court of the Federal Court in Otto Australia Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 28 FCR 477. The Full Court also accepted that it had been common ground that the appellants charges were calculated by reference to its costs which included the landed costs of the bins, and this took into account both customs duty and sales tax. In those circumstances Sheppard J said at p480:
“Once it is conceded, as it has been, that the charge for each bin was computed by reference to costs which included sales tax, that cost was passed on. The fact that the sales tax was not passed on in an identifiable form is not in my opinion of relevance.”

Burchett J agreed with Sheppard J



  1. That case is distinguishable from the matter before the Tribunal because there was no concession or evidence before us that the price at which the subject goods were sold was calculated by reference to cost. We have found that the price was not calculated by reference to costs. Nor could it be said to be plain from the facts of the present case that the applicant did pass on the sales tax to its purchasers.
  2. Reference was made during the hearing to the Tribunal decision of Case Y46191 of 91 ATC 431. That decision is also distinguishable. There was evidence that an exempt body had been invoiced by the applicant in such a manner as to show that sales tax had been deducted from the price. The Tribunal held that the invoice established that the applicant had passed on the sales tax to those customers who were not exempt.
  3. Mr Benson relied on Case 45195 95 ATC 395 That case is generally referred to as the Cellar Doors Case. It concerned an application for refund of sales tax which it was conceded had been overcharged on cellar door winery sales. The applicant claimed that it was entitled to a refund of sales tax paid because it had not "passed on" that sales tax to the purchasers. The Tribunal found as follows at paragraphs 6 and 7:
"Prices charged at the cellar door were governed by all of the factors mentioned in the previous paragraph, together with the estimated retail prices now in hand, and the strength of the competition coming from the many other proximate wineries offering the same service. Overriding those issues was the need to pitch cellar door prices at a level marginally less than retail so as to keep faith with retailers, but low enough to attract customers to the cellar door It seems that those elements were on occasions incompatible, yet the point which emerges from the evidence is that the amounts being charged during the period under review remained relatively constant. Notwithstanding the variations in vintages and the changing nature of costs associated with production, the prices charged have to a large extent remained the same. The only two increases occurring during this period reflect acknowledgments of the higher standards achieved for the products concerned.

Any suggestion that costs incurred, including sales tax, had played a pall in their determination of price was rejected by the applicants. Such a system may have its uses in other areas of commerce but it finds no favour with these applicants. 1 accept their evidence as to the methodology applied in the ascertainment of price and also that the imposts emanating from the introduction of sales tax on wine in 1984 and any subsequent increases in rate were not matters taken into account by them in the fixing of their selling prices.

...”

The Tribunal then referred to the evidence that an increase in sales tax payable had not resulted in an increase in the cost of the wines or a change in the method of costing. It said at paragraph 9:

“That method, which remained in place for the months December 1989 to July 1992 inclusive, caused an increase in sales tax liability, and yet on the evidence 1 find that this increase did not result in any corresponding lift in the prices of cellar door product. In fact those prices remained remarkably static throughout this period."

Having made those findings of fact the Tribunal stated in paragraph 12:

“The precise task is to ascertain in what sense the expression "passed on' is used in sub-s. 26(1A). In my opinion the expression is used in a non-technical manner and thus its ordinary meaning must prevail. It is my view that the term "passed on' is descriptive of a process whereby the person carrying a liability takes the decision to shift the burden of that liability to some other person and then gives effect to that decision. "
  1. The Tribunal in the Cellar Doors Case considered the reasons of Lockhart J and Sheppard J in Otto and concluded at paragraphs 16 and 17:
”The decisions in Otto and Chippendale make it abundantly clear that sales tax has been passed on where the tax has been brought to account in the calculation of the contract price, and if it has, the fact that the amount of the tax has not been shown separately on the documents of sale is of no consequence. The evidence of the applicants is that the various costs associated with the wine produced for sale at the cellar door, together with the sales tax thereon, were not factors considered by them in the determination of selling prices. My prior acceptance of that evidence would appear to take the matter outside the procedures of calculation and computation which were central to the decisions of Otto and Chippendale. ...

As mentioned earlier, prices at the cellar door were determined by having regard to likely value in the retail market,. less a reduction sufficient to attract custom but not enough to antagonise retailers; and finally, less any discounts required to combat the forces of regional competition. The question raised is whether such a system has the effect of structuring the sales tax overpaid into the prices charged at the cellar door, (emphasis added)
  1. The Tribunal stated that it could not see how the amounts of sales tax in dispute impacted upon the retail price. It concluded at paragraph 20:
"A matter of paramount importance in the resolution of this dispute, and one which stands directly in the path of the submissions proposed by the respondent, is the decision of the applicants to retain their existing pricing structure notwithstanding the increase in sales tax liability. The fact that prices did remain the same belies any suggestion that the amounts in question were factored into selling prices. The preferable view is that the applicants decided to bear the burden themselves rather than seek relief from their customers. It seems to me to be a classic example of the producer absorbing the extra tax himself in lieu of passing it on to others. "
  1. Reference was also made during the hearing to the decision in Case No. NT 94/439 a decision of Deputy President McMahon and Mr Greenwood, Member, delivered 16 December 1996. In that case, again in obiter comments, the Tribunal referred to the Cellar Doors and contrasted it with Case Y46/91. The Tribunal said at paragraphs 37-38:
“The present applicant sought to establish the winemaker's underlying approach to pricing through the evidence of an officer who was marketing services manager during the relevant period. He said that it was the applicant's general policy that prices of its products were set competitively against prices charged by other suppliers for products broadly similar to those marketed by the applicant. These prices were then adjusted depending upon the features of the particular product in question when compared with the features of the broadly similar competing product Where there were no competitive benchmarks for setting the price of a product the applicant, he said, generally turned to overseas pricing of the same product and in that manner established a relative price in Australia. He acknowledged that it was essential to have reliable and current pricing information about competitors' goods. This information was obtained through examination of published tenders and through competitive research and information collected by sales personnel from prospective customers concerning competitive quotes.

There were occasions, he said, when special prices were negotiated for individual sales for strategic reasons. He said that this flexible approach would sometimes lead to the result that a taxable customer and a sales tax exempt customer could purchase the same product for the same price or a taxable customer could purchase the same product for a price lower than that paid by a sales tax exempt customer. No specific example was put before us of such an incident. On the other hand there were many other examples, to which we will come, clearly showing a differentiation in prices charged to customers depending upon their exemption status.”

The Tribunal concluded at paragraph 40:

"We are unable to accept the manager's evidence as reflecting universal practice within the applicant's business. A number of detailed documents were tendered by the respondent consisting of listings of machine sale invoices for certain months during the relevant period, a breakup of sales showing a comparison of taxable, exempt, and wholesale prices of various models, and graphs indicating the relevant levels of taxable and tax exempt status prices charged during this sample period. "


(ii) was the sales tax passed on?

  1. In the light of our findings in paragraphs 5-29 of these reasons this matter is most similar on the facts to the Cellar Doors Case. There are no documents nor is there any other evidence to show that sales tax was a component in the price fixing. Nor is there any evidence to show that those few goods which may in error have come in "sales tax exempt" were sold at a cheaper price. There is no evidence of any attention being given by the applicant company to whether the purchaser of the goods was a sales tax exempt purchaser such as a nursing home or hospital. There is evidence which we have accepted that the selling price of the goods was not fixed by reference to costs in general and that the sales tax paid was not brought into account in the calculation of the selling price. On the evidence we are satisfied that the applicant has discharged its burden of proof. We are satisfied and find that the cost of sales tax overpaid, or paid when it was not legally payable, has not been passed on by the applicant company to its purchasers.
  2. The objection decision under review will be set aside. The matter will be remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the applicant company is entitled to a refund of the sales tax overpaid during the three years prior to 18 March 1994. The Tribunal will reserve to the parties liberty to apply if necessary in regard to the implementation of this decision.
