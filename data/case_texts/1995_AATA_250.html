NT94/8-11 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 250; 95 ATC 426; (1995) 31 ATR 1242 (1 September 1995)

Last Updated: 13 February 2009



TAXATION - failure to lodge income tax returns - income from investments - default assessments issued - work as a barrister - declining practice - illness of applicant - whether items deductible



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 51, 167

Taxation Administration Act 1953 s 14ZU



South Behar Railway Co Limited v Commissioners of Inland Revenue [1925] AC 476

Avondale Motors (Parts) Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4101

AGC (Advances) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175

Placer Pacific Management Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation 95 ATC 4459



AAT No 10395



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

NO s NT94/8-11

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION



Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



Date : 1 September 1995



Place : Sydney



Decision : The Tribunal sets aside the decisions under review and remits the matters to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that amended assessments be issued having regard to the following —



(1) the applicant has ceased to carry on business as a barrister. All business expenses claimed were not incurred in the gaining of assessable income and are domestic or private in nature;



(2) the claims for deduction mentioned in paragraph 22 of the reasons for decision are to be considered by the respondent and if properly verified adjustments are to be made to the assessments accordingly; and



(3) additional tax is to be reduced to a flat sum of 20 per cent of the final amount of tax found to be payable.



BJ McMahon

Signed............................................... BJ McMahon

Deputy President

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

NOs NT94/8-11

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re :

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



1 September 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)

  1. This is an application to review objection decisions in relation to assessments issued on 7 December 1992 in respect of the years ended 30 June 1988 to 1991 inclusive.
  2. Information had come to the attention of the respondent that the applicant had received distributions from a public company. On 21 February 1990 the applicant was advised that the respondent held information which might, on assessment, give rise to a liability to pay tax. The applicant was requested to file income tax returns. For the next two and a half years, the evidence before me indicated that contacts between the applicant and the respondent did not result in the preparation and filing of those returns. Extensions of time were requested by the applicant. Further evidence of alleged ill health was requested by the respondent and enquiries were at one stage made of the applicant’s former tax agents. The extensions of time expired. The medical evidence was not received. Ultimately default assessments for the years in question were issued under s 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act.
  3. An audit conducted into the applicant’s income tax affairs for the 1986 and 1987 tax years did not result in any assessment as the applicant’s taxable income was found to be below the taxable threshold because of unrecouped prior year losses. In 1986 the Commissioner accepted that the applicant had a net carry forward loss of $19,823. These losses were set off against income in the 1987 year leaving a non taxable income of $2,514, in the Commissioner’s estimation, for that year. Although at the outset of the hearing before me there was a dispute between the parties, it was ultimately accepted by the applicant that these figures were accurate. There were therefore no carry forward losses beyond 30 June 1987.
  4. The assessments for the years 1988 to 1991 are based upon receipt by the applicant of distributions as a unit holder from a public investment trust of the following amounts:
  5. $18,129
  6. $20,030
  7. $23,182
  8. $19,160
  9. As no returns had been filed, no deductions were allowed. Assessments were made of tax referable to these income receipts, together with additional tax under s 222, at various rates for each year. Objections were lodged by the applicant on 6 February 1993. They were, however, in such general terms as to give no indication of the basis upon which the applicant would allege that the assessments were excessive. In my view, the objections may well not have complied with the requirements of s 14ZU(c) of the Taxation Administration Act in that they did not state fully and in detail the grounds relied upon. All attempts at obtaining particulars of the objections and the basis of the applicant’s claims were unsuccessful. Preliminary conferences held in this Tribunal did not result in the lodging of returns or in the particularisation of deductions claimed. In very general terms the applicant had suggested that these deductions would result in losses for the relevant years and that they would result in no taxable income. From 6 February 1993 (the date of the objections) until the hearing before me on 16 August 1995, none of these details was made known by the applicant either to the respondent or to this Tribunal. At no time has the applicant denied receiving the gross income in question.
  10. The applicant’s perception of his own health has played a marked role in these delays. To some extent, the nature of the applicant’s physical disabilities would be relevant only in relation to the rate of imposition of additional tax if it should be found that tax was payable in the relevant years. However at the hearing before me, as a result of negotiations, it was agreed that in the event of a decision adverse to the applicant, additional tax would be payable at the rate of 20 per cent on any amount found to be due. It seems to me, however, that the evidence which the applicant put before me in relation to his health is still important because of its bearing in determining whether or not he was carrying on a business at the time he incurred the expenses to which I will later refer.
  11. Most of the evidence concerning the applicant’s health came from the applicant himself. The objective evidence which he tendered in support of his statements was inconclusive. A certificate of 16 December 1993 from his general practitioners indicated that he had been a patient at their surgery for the preceding 5 years and that during that time he had complained on numerous occasions about undue lethargy and tiredness. His practitioner said that he had had various treatments for this, but with no lasting benefit. He went on to say that the applicant then had an appointment with a specialist so that he could be further investigated about the possibility of his having ME (which I understand to be an abbreviation for myalgic encephalomyelitis — one of the many terms used to indicate chronic fatigue syndrome).
  12. On 8 April 1994, the specialist referred to in the previous paragraph noted in his history that the applicant had complained of a severe fatigue illness for the previous 8 years and that he was presently under investigation as to the cause of his disability. Neither the general practitioners nor the specialist confirmed any specific diagnosis which could account for the symptoms of which the applicant complained. On 25 July 1995 a third certificate was given by another specialist who confirmed : “This man has recently been diagnosed as having moderate obstructive sleep apnoea ... [which] ... can lead to day time tiredness and lethargy”. This certificate of course says nothing of the degree of the incapacitating effect of any recognised syndrome and goes no distance in explaining whether the applicant was incapable of attending to such duties as preparing and filing his income tax returns.
  13. Without the benefit of any corroborative evidence, the applicant also spoke at the hearing before me of other incapacities. He said that an incident in 1957 caused a prolapse of a lumbar disc and, as a result, he has suffered from episodes of severe back pain from time to time. He also said that he had been treated for depression from time to time and had taken stimulant drugs on prescription unsuccessfully. He added that he had been told he had ruptured a tendon in his right foot and that his ankle would need to be arthrodosed. Furthermore he said that he suffered from what is commonly referred to as RSI in his forearms, that he has a pain in his back, in the cervical area, and that he suffers from possible fibrositis in his hands and arms. He said that the pains from these conditions made writing sometimes difficult and prevented him at times from driving his motor vehicle.
  14. It is not necessary for the purpose of my decision to bring in a finding of fact as to the applicant’s exact state of health and as to whether he suffers from any of the conditions to which I have referred. I am clearly of the view, however, that he believes himself to be ill. He said that he does not rise until 10 or 11 in the morning. He believes his condition improves in the evenings. He lives alone and does his own house work but he said on some days nothing gets done. He believes his general incapacity to lead an active life is due to the physical ailments to which I have referred. In my view, his perception of his health has played probably a decisive role in his ceasing to carry on practice.
  15. Born in 1929, the applicant was called to the Bar in the mid 1950s. He is still on the roll of barristers but, of course, this fact of itself is no evidence that he has continued to practise his profession. He had chambers in Phillip Street until they were sold at the end of 1985. One of the factors that led to the sale was an episode of back pain that lasted for 4 or 5 weeks. It was also about this time, according to the history referred to in paragraph 8, that the fatigue symptoms began. After he sold his chambers, he said that he became a “floater” on his floor. His name and profession continued to appear in the telephone book for some years after 1985, but he could not remember when the entry terminated.
  16. He last appeared in court on 11 March 1987. This itself was an isolated appearance for which, he said, he had still not been paid. Since then, he has done no legal work except as executor of a deceased estate and as honorary legal officer of a fraternal organisation. From the estate he drew executor’s commission of $500 in the 1986 year. He has received nothing from the fraternal organisation, although he hopes that his services will one day be recognised with an honorarium.
  17. His house has an internal room with a lockable door which he refers to as a study. It is not architecturally separate from the remainder of the house. When he sold his chambers, he said that he customarily saw solicitors and clients at his home. Having regard to other particulars of his professional income to which I will refer, this seems doubtful, whatever use the room may have been put to in earlier days. Claims for home study expenses were customarily made and allowed while he was in active practice. Contrary to the applicant’s submissions, this fact can have no bearing on current claims for similar deductions.
  18. In the early 1970s he established a service company from which it was intended that his wife and son should derive an income. His son has now grown up and no longer needs financial support from his father. The marriage to his wife was terminated by divorce in 1978. He said that the service company now no longer operates at a profit. It has a bank account. Previously it employed a secretary, hired his library and his motor vehicle and provided him with office equipment. For quite some years now, he said that he has been in a habit of using the service company to pay certain outgoings and to top up the service company’s bank accounts with payments of $1,000 by cheque from time to time and by incurring liabilities on credit cards on the company’s behalf from time to time. Although these top up payments were substantiated, it was not possible to establish exactly what the service company did with the money in its bank account. Its books of account have not been kept up to date. Evidence was given that its last income tax return was lodged in respect of the year ended 30 June 1982. Having regard to the amounts paid to the service company and having regard to the other circumstances of the applicant’s working life, I am not satisfied that moneys were paid to the service company as working expenses.
  19. It was only at the hearing before me that the applicant finally produced some returns. The return for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 shows fees received of $500 (being the executor’s commission) together with interest received from various public companies. The expenses are mostly of a business nature. They include a proportion of expenses incurred in relation to the applicant’s home (home study expenses), various subscriptions, some entertainment expenses, depreciation on the applicant’s robes and a sum of $13,461 paid to the service company. In the 1986 year, the applicant also claimed for a subscription to the New South Wales Bar Association. That claim was not made in subsequent years as no doubt the applicant had ceased to be a member of that organisation.
  20. The return for the 1987 year, which was also tendered in evidence, but not previously seen by the respondent, shows fees received of $2,422. This was explained in evidence by the applicant as fees earned by him in that year for collecting debts for a certain municipal council. He estimated that some 15 to 20 hours of his time had been occupied in the 1987 year in this enterprise. No other fees are noted as having been received. The nature of the outgoings claimed, including an amount of $7,874 paid to the service company, is much the same as the type of outgoings included in the 1986 return. All are said to be working expenses incurred in the gaining of the applicant’s professional income. Although an amount of $23,654 of interest received from public companies is shown as income for that year, none of the expenses claimed are referable to gaining that investment income.
  21. The objection decisions before me do not relate to either of the 1986 or 1987 years. The returns of income for those years would therefore be irrelevant to present considerations were it not for the fact that the applicant alleges that the losses shown in those returns should be carried forward into subsequent years. There was an attempt to lodge a return for the 1988 year at the hearing before me. On closer examination of the document, however, it appears to be simply a copy of the schedule for the 1987 year enclosed in a return form printed up for the 1988 year. No doubt, this was an error on the part of the applicant which was not intended. However the fact is that no return has been lodged for any of the years 1988 to 1991 inclusive.
  22. I have concluded that the applicant ceased to carry on practice as a barrister a long time ago. It is not possible to say upon what exact day this event occurred. He made his last appearance in court on 11 March 1987, having been without chambers for more than 2 years prior to that date. This was, however, an isolated incident. The weight of evidence indicates to me that by 31 December 1985, at the latest, the practice had ceased to exist. More accurately, the applicant has not satisfied me that his professional practice continued beyond the date when he became a “floater”. On 25 February 1988 in a return to Australian Bureau of Statistics, he indicated that he was carrying on practice as a barrister at the Phillip Street address. Not only was this incorrect by his own admissions, it would not be of assistance in establishing whether he was in fact carrying on practice as it was an unilateral declaration unsupported by any other evidence. The applicant agrees that his practice, at best, is in a state of suspense. In his view whether the practice has terminated depends on his intention and he said that he intended to carry on as long as his health permitted. As I have already indicated, his perception of his own ill health, in my view, played probably a deciding part in his determination not to carry on his practice for the past 10 years. It has also been largely determinative in ensuring that the cessation of professional activity has become permanent. The applicant pointed to a quotation of Lord Sumner in South Behar Railway Co Limited v Commissioners of Inland Revenue [1925] AC 476 at 488, quoted with approval by Gibbs J in Avondale Motors (Parts) Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4101 at 4105. His Lordship said : “Business is not confined to being busy; in many businesses long intervals of inactivity occur”. The applicant, of course, bears the onus of showing that the expenses said to have been incurred in 1986 and 1987 were working expenses relevant to a then existing (even if low level) practice.
  23. The view I have taken of the facts leads me to the conclusion that what has happened to the applicant’s practice is not a suspension, such as was considered in AGC (Advances) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175, but a termination. Whether a taxpayer has ceased business is a question of fact. The facts are that for many years now, and certainly during the relevant years, the applicant has not carried on a practice as a barrister either in his chambers (which he has not owned for the past 10 years) or in his home. Consequently all expenses incurred, including the home study expenses, and the expenses said to be reimbursements to the service company were not incurred in the gaining of assessable income and must be regarded as private or domestic in nature.
  24. It is not, of course, necessary for expenditure to be deductible that it be incurred in the same year in which income was earned. Nor can it now be said that the mere fact that the business has ceased precludes the deductibility of outgoings provided that they are properly referable to the earlier business. In Placer Pacific Management Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation (95 ATC 4459 at 4464) their Honours affirmed the proposition ... “that provided the occasion of a business outgoing is to be found in the business operations directed towards the gaining or production of assessable income generally, the fact that that outgoing was incurred in a year later than the year in which the income was incurred and the fact that in the meantime business in the ordinary sense may have ceased will not determine the issue of deductibility”.
  25. The outgoing in that case, however, was clearly referable to the conduct of the earlier business. The outgoings sought to be treated as deductions for the 1986 and 1987 years of the applicant, could not have reference to activities of earlier years. In their nature they must be currently referable to activities to the 2 years in question. As I have held that in those 2 years the applicant was not carrying on the practice of a barrister (notwithstanding the minimal 15 to 20 hours work carried out in the 1987 year) the necessary nexus between the outgoings and the gaining of assessable income does not exist. Accordingly, the outgoings listed as business expenses for those 2 years would not be deductible.
  26. There appear to be certain claims for deduction which could be sustained if they were properly verified to the satisfaction of the respondent. In the 1986 year, an amount of $1,500 is shown as a contribution to a non-employer sponsored superannuation fund. I am not aware wof the details of that claim. Its deductibility does not appear to be dependent upon the applicant carrying on practice as a barrister. If upon further investigation the respondent is satisfied of the validity of the claim, then an adjustment can be made to accommodate it. Similarly, donations of $63 do not appear to depend upon s 51 for their deductibility and an adjustment may be made to accommodate those. In the 1987 return, similar observations can be made about the superannuation contribution and donations. Uncontradicted evidence was given (although not verified by substantiation) that on 9 January 1990 a sum of $2,927 was paid for dental treatment to a dentist whose name and address was given at the hearing. Subject to satisfactory verification, an adjustment may be made to the 1990 assessment to accommodate any deduction available as a result of this payment. No other details of any other claims for deductions during the relevant 4 years have been made known by the applicant. Finally, the agreement between the parties reflects that additional tax is to be reduced to a flat sum of 20 per cent of the final amount of tax found to be payable.
  27. The decisions under review are therefore set aside and the matters are remitted to the respondent with the directions that amended assessments be issued having regard to these findings and to the observations made in the previous paragraph.
