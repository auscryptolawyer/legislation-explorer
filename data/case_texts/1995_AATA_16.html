David John Schwarz and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 16 (3 February 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DAVID JOHN SCHWARZ v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A94/123

AAT No. 9981

Number of pages - 6

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

M.D. ALLEN (Senior Member), N.J. ATTWOOD (Member) AND H.G. JULIAN (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Application to make late election. Various factors taken into account including education and experience of Applicant, the fact that he was located at Departmental headquarters in Canberra and had knowledge of the Superannuation system. Balancing of fairness to the Applicant vis-a-vis fairness to the fund.

Superannuation Act 1976 - Subsection 137(1), 157(1)

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 50 FCR 150

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman 50 FCR 236

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 followed

HEARING

CANBERRA, 13-14 December 1994

3:2:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr R Refshauge

Solicitors for the Applicant: Ms Louise Rafferty, Macphillamy

Cummins and Gibson

Solicitors for the Respondent: Mr Simon Adams, Commissioner

for Superannuation

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

M.D. ALLEN, N.J. ATTWOOD AND H.G. JULIAN This is an application to review a decision by a delegate of the Respondent made the 10 June 1994, confirming a decision of 21 July 1991 not to direct that an election made by the Applicant on 19 November 1990, pursuant to Subsection 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by the said Subsection 137(1).

2. Subsection 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 states:

"A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which

paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled

to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with

section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of

which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58 (3), to have

retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an

eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner,

that this Division apply in relation to the person."

3. Subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 states:

"Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election

under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the

period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election,

and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of

the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized, the

Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as it is had

been made within the period allowed and the election shall have

effect accordingly."

4. The Applicant was born on 27 July 1947. On 7 February 1966, following his appointment as a Clerk Third Division in the then Postmaster-General's Department, he became a contributor to the Superannuation Scheme established pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1922. When that scheme was replaced by the scheme established pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1976, he became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of that Act as and from its date of commencement, namely, 1 July 1976.

5. The Applicant resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service on 11 May 1984. At that time he was employed as a Computer Systems Officer within the Department of Defence.

6. At the time of his resignation, the Applicant completed a form headed:

"APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS

LUMP SUM BENEFIT OR

ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS"

commonly referred to as Form S2A. This form bore the following wording:

" apply for a refund of accumulated contributions )

hereby apply for payment of a lump sum benefit )

elect for preservation of superannuation rights )"

under the provisions of the Superannuation Act

The Applicant deleted the latter two options, thus electing to have a sum representing his contributions to the Superannuation Fund plus interest returned to him.

7. The Applicant gave evidence that in 1987, in conversation with a Mr Robey, he learned for the first time of the right to elect to leave his Superannuation contributions in the Superannuation Scheme upon resignation and then take a pension plus a lump sum benefit upon attaining age 55 (or later until age 65).

8. Neither the Applicant nor Mr Robey knew at that time of the provision in the Superannuation Act 1976 to request a late election to preserve rights. Thus, in 1987, the Applicant did nothing but bemoan how foolish he had been. In the late 1980's or early 1990, because of publicity in the Canberra Times Newspaper regarding the provisions of Sections 157 and 137 of the Superannuation Act 1976, the Applicant learned of the right to request a late election and did so by his letter of 19 November 1990 addressed to the Commissioner for Superannuation.

9. In a later letter, dated 7 February 1991, to the Commissioner for Superannuation, following a request for further and better details by the Commissioner, the Applicant wrote:

"Unfortunately, I do not recall the contents nor the exact forms

which I completed when I left the public service. I assume you have

the original Superannuation Refund form I completed; could I please

have a copy of it? I do recall obtaining several forms from the

Administrative Section within Defence's Computing Services Division

and being told 'complete this one to get your refund of

contributions', the only option I understood was available to me.

It was only very much later that it came to my notice that

contributors could preserve their rights."

10. Following a request for information by the Commissioner, the Department of Defence, in a letter dated 12 February 1991, stated inter alia:

"It is not our practice to advise employees exiting from the

Department of the option to elect for the preservation of their

superannuation rights.

Any queries regarding superannuation, resignees are advised to

contact the Retirement Benefits Office. We have available standard

superannuation pamplets (sic) issued by your Office."

11. The Applicant stated to the Tribunal that at the time he resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service he had been employed in the Department of Defence in what was known as the West Block at Russell Offices. This was a six storey building occupied by staff concerned with computer programming and separate from the normal run of Department of Defence staff. Although there was a small Administration Section attached to the Division, the main Civil Personnel Branch of the Department was at the Department's Campbell Offices some 2 kilometres away.

12. At that time the Applicant had become very disillusioned with his work in the Department of Defence and had in fact been looking for a position outside the Commonwealth Public Service for some three years. This dissatisfaction was caused by what he saw as the misuse of computer programmers and programming within the Department of Defence.

13. The Applicant's salary at the time of his resignation was equal to that of a Clerk Class 9, namely, $32671.00 per annum. As such, he was responsible for subordinate staff and could have expected further promotions. To that date his rise had been swift, advancing from a Clerk Clerk Class 1 to a Clerk Class 9 level in eight years. He had also obtained a Bachelor of Arts, majoring in mathematics from the University of Canberra.

14. The Form S2A, upon which the Applicant made his election to receive a refund of contributions is in evidence as Tribunal exhibit No 9 in the bundle of documents prepared for these proceedings pursuant to Section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The Applicant did not deny that he completed this form but stated that at the time he did so, he was not aware that there was any other option but to seek a refund of contributions. The Applicant conceded that he did not make any enquiries as to what were the other options referred to in the form. The Tribunal finds this somewhat surprising as the Applicant had earlier elected to contribute to 12 additional reserve units under the scheme established by the Superannuation Act 1922 and had, in August 1974, applied for a refund of contributions paid for those 12 additional reserve units.

15. When asked, the Applicant was vague as to the purpose for which he sought a refund of contributions on those units but the form which he completed in order to obtain the refund clearly sets out, under a heading "Outline of Conditions for Preservation of Superannuation Rights", a series of definitions and information regarding the preservation of rights.

16. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant's evidence that he did not, at the time of his resignation, know of the right to elect to preserve rights pursuant to the 1976 Act. However, the form completed by him clearly raises the option so to do and notwithstanding his physical distance from the Department of Defence Civil Branch, the Applicant was in Canberra and in telephone contact not only with that Branch but also with the Retirement Benefits Office and the Office.

17. That the Applicant knew how to make contact with the Retirement Benefits Office is evidenced by a minute of a conversation between the Applicant and an officer of the Retirement Benefits Office on 30 May 1984 at 10.30 hours. That minute (document T10), after identifying the caller, his Department and telephone number, reads:

"Needs money urgently so that he doesn't have to borrow money. Mr

Schwartz would like to collect cheque. S2A not in office as yet.

Mr Schwartz asked his personell sec to send S2A to us but they said

that they could not until after resignation date. I told above that

it is possible to prepare refund before D.O.E. he will endevour

(sic) to have S2A sent or bring it himself."

18. At the same time as the Applicant was resigning from the Commonwealth Public Service, he was in the process of selling his current dwelling house and purchasing another. Exhibit C, a letter of approval from the Commonwealth Trading Bank dated 13 June 1984, supports the Applicant's evidence that he did not need the Superannuation refund as the Bank had approved funds, however, what is clear from Exhibit B, is that the Applicant had a use for those funds in that they enabled him to pay his existing mortgage and thus obtain more funds for the purchase of the new dwelling at Home Loan rates rather than have to obtain additional funds at Personal Loan rates.

19. It was submitted by Mr Refshauge of counsel, who appeared for the Applicant, that a factor the Tribunal could and should take into account was that the Applicant's election as contained in Form S2A was not an election in that he did not make the decision in full knowledge of his rights - cf Brennan J in Commonwealth of Australia v Verwayen 170 CLR 394 at 421:

"Election consists in a choice between rights which the person

making the election knows he possesses and which are alternative and

inconsistent rights"

20. If the Applicant had not made any election at all pursuant to Section 137, then the contributions made plus interest are refunded, thus the concepts are inconsistent with the equitable doctrine of election referred to by Brennan J above and in the cases his Honour cited at p.421. Notwithstanding this the Tribunal accepts the submission that ignorance of rights is a matter to be taken into account by the Tribunal.

21. The factors to be taken into account by the Tribunal were discussed by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 50 FCR 150. As Cooper J said at p.158:

"S 157 merely requires that the Commissioner be satisfied that in

all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be

recognised. Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances

existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for

consideration ..."

His Honour went on to refer to circumstances identified by Davies J in that case. At p.156 Davies J had said:

"In the application of s 157, it may be of little consequence that

the employee did not inquire as to his or her right to elect under

s 137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect.

Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case.

Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts

which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to

elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the

discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered

health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of

retirement. One of the objects of s 157 is to alleviate the

consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances

need not be of a medical character. ...

The superannuation scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth

employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights.

But the entitlement to participate in the scheme and to receive the

benefits of the scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They

form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker

exercising the s 157 discretion could think it fair to the employer,

to the employee, and to the scheme that the benefit should be

calculated by reference to the totality of the employee's service

and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which

must be refunded."

22. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman 50 FCR 236 von Doussa J said at p.244:

"... the connotation of 'desirable' in the context of s 157(1) is

that of being fair in order to do justice between the person who

seeks to make the late election, and the requirements of the

administration of the Fund."

His Honour continued:

"Whether a late election is 'desirable' is to be judged objectively

having regard to all the circumstances of the case. The suggested

meaning 'something worthy of achievement' does not imply other than

an objective evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in

the end, whether the recognition of a late election is 'something

worthy of achievement' must depend on what is fair in order to do

justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and

the requirements of the administration of the Fund."

23. As at 11 May 1984 the Applicant had made a conscious decision to resign from his position in the Department of Defence. He had been anxious to obtain a more rewarding position in private enterprise for some three years and when one was offered, he took it. He was, however, as he admitted in evidence, careful not to burn any bridges. There is no evidence other than that his resignation was made as a conscious rational decision.

24. In the course of making that decision he was handed a form which indicated that three options were available to him regarding superannuation. He had a belief that only one option was open to him, yet, to a man of his education and experience the form should have indicated a need for further and better enquiry.

25. The Applicant was not unfamiliar with Superannuation Schemes, having dealt previously with the taking out of, and subsequent redeeming of, additional units under the 1922 Act scheme. He was familiar enough with the Retirement Benefits Office to telephone that office and request prompt payment of his refund of contributions.

26. As was stated by Deputy President Forgie in Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 at p 473:

"However, what the form does do is to alert the reader to the fact

that he or she must choose one of three things: a refund of

accumulated contributions, a lump sum benefit or preservation of

superannuation rights. Certainly, no information can be gleaned

from the form itself about these options but the form does, in my

view, put the reader on notice that he or she should make further

enquiries as to whether these options apply to his or her

circumstances and generally find out more information about them."

27. As stated above the Applicant made no further enquiries, but did seek the prompt payment of his refund and at that time he had a use, as opposed to a need, for those funds. We are satisfied that he was at that time, more than content to pursue that course.

28. Other matters to be taken into account are the fairness to the Superannuation Fund and its other contributors as opposed to fairness to the Applicant in permitting him to now re-enter the fund. Figures were produced which gave estimates of what the Applicant might expect to receive should he be permitted to elect. At the time of his resignation, he received an amount of slightly over half his annual salary as a refund. He has had the use of that money since then and if re-deposited, whilst the value of the refund figure would now be less in real money terms than when paid to the Applicant, he would receive an advantage after attaining age 55. In other words, he would have had the use of his money whilst its value was high and then achieve another gain against the Superannuation Scheme by returning his money to the scheme when its value had been diminished by inflation.

29. These factors might not count for so much in the case of a person who has rejoined the Commonwealth Public Service and the Superannuation Fund after a short period of absence, but the Applicant had now been in private employment for 10 years.

30. Taking all of the above factors into account, the Tribunal finds that it is not satisfied that it is desirable that the Applicant be permitted to make a late election. The decision under review will therefore be affirmed.
