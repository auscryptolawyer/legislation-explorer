R and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 333 (15 November 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

R v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q94/804

AAT No. 10529

Number of pages - 13

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

K.L. BEDDOE(1) (Senior Member), J.D. HORRIGAN(1) (Member) AND A.M. BRENNAN(2) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation: Late election to join 1990 superannuation scheme - whether desirable that election be recognised - misunderstanding by officers advising applicant of rights - incorrect advice cured - no action taken for some time - complex domestic situation - objective assessment

Superannuation Act 1976: Ss.157, 244

Superannuation Act 1990

Safety Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 14 AAR 456

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 33 ALD 569

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 50 FCR 150

HEARING

BRISBANE, 3 October 1995

15:11:1995

The Applicant appeared in person.

Mrs J. McDougall appeared for the Respondent.

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that the decision under review be affirmed.

DECISION

K.L. BEDDOE AND J.D. HORRIGAN By an application dated 11 November 1994 the applicant sought review of a decision of the respondent Commissioner which confirmed, on review, a decision to refuse to direct that the applicant be allowed to make a late election within the terms of subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). The election sought to be made by the applicant is the election provided for in section 244 of the Act, which is an election to join the 1990 superannuation scheme, commonly known as the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme or "PSS".

2. Section 244 of the Act sets out the provisions relating to elections to join the PSS and, in particular, subsection 244(1) provides that an eligible employee who is not precluded by or under the Superannuation Act 1990 from being a member of the 1990 scheme may, in writing addressed to the Commissioner, declare that he or she wishes to become a member of that scheme. In the event of making that declaration the member must also elect to cease to be an eligible employee within the terms of the Act. In other words the eligible employee under the Act ("the CSS") must elect to cease to be such an eligible employee in the process of becoming a member of the PSS. Subsection 244(1) is subject to various other provisions of the section but, in particular, subsection 244(2) provides that an eligible employee may not make an election and declaration under subsection 244(1) during any period when the eligible employee is absent from duty on leave of absence without pay. The section goes on to provide, in effect, that the declaration and the election must be made during the period from 1 July 1990 to 30 June 1991 (sub section 244(3)).

3. Subsection 157(1) of the Act provides that notwithstanding anything contained in the Act where an election under the Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under the Act for the making of the election and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly. There is no dispute that a refusal by the Commissioner to make a direction within the terms of subsection 157(1) is a reviewable decision within the terms of the Act and the decision which is ultimately reviewable in this Tribunal.

4. At the hearing of this matter the applicant conducted her own case and the respondent Commissioner was represented by Mrs J McDougall. The documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were put into evidence before the Tribunal as the T documents and various other documents were marked as exhibits. The applicant gave evidence and oral evidence was also given by an officer of the Department of Social Security. Prior to the hearing the presiding member made an order under section 35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 prohibiting publication of the applicant's name and the evidence. Pursuant to the order the hearing was closed to the public.

5. The material before the Tribunal establishes that in 1990 the Retirement Benefits Office issued a superannuation information kit guide to members of the CSS. One of those kit guides is marked as Exhibit A in these proceedings. The first page of the kit guide contains a statement as follows:

". As a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS),

you are being offered a choice:

- to remain a member of the CSS, or

- to transfer to the new Public Sector Superannuation (PSS)

scheme.

. This special information kit has been prepared to help you

choose the scheme that best suits your personal needs and those

of your family.

. You have until 30 June 1991 to make up your mind."

6. The kit which was forwarded to all members of the CSS at the time and a copy of which was received by the applicant at that time provided a considerable amount of information to members of the CSS for use in making their decision as to whether or not they would elect to transfer to the PSS scheme. In particular, the kit included a statement of information relating to the member's own potential benefits under both schemes. The kit also included summaries of the CSS and the PSS schemes and the further booklet which made a comparison between CSS and PSS schemes. The kit also included a superannuation election form and it is made clear in several parts of the kit that that form must be completed before 1 July 1991. The form was for use by members of the CSS who wished to remain members of the CSS and, more importantly, was for use by members of the CSS who wished to transfer to the PSS. Mrs McDougall quite correctly, in our view, conceded in the hearing, that it was not necessary to use the form if a member of the CSS wished to remain a member of the CSS. Notwithstanding the requirements of section 244 of the Act, the Commissioner had apparently decided to seek certainty in the process by asking all contributors to elect either to remain with the CSS or to become members of the PSS.

7. It is not necessary for the purpose of deciding this matter that we discuss the contents of Exhibit A except to say that, on any reasonable view of what is contained in that kit, it is a very full statement of information that was necessary to be taken into account in deciding whether or not to make an election within the terms of section 244 of the Act. It has not been suggested to us in the course of this hearing that the information contained in the kit was wrong and nor has it been suggested to us that the information contained was inadequate and we can see no basis from our perusal of the exhibit for saying that such might be the case.

8. During the period 1 July 1990 to 30 June 1991 which may conveniently be described as the election period, the applicant was an officer of the Department of Social Security. She was, however, having difficulties with her health at that time and had been for some considerable time prior to the election period. One of the consequences of those difficulties which revolved around tenosynovitis but not confined to that condition, was that liability for compensation under the Safety Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988 was accepted by Comcare.

9. On 29 January 1991 a Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended that the applicant be retired on invalidity grounds due to regional pain syndrome. In a letter dated 1 February 1991 the Department of Social Security, the applicant's employing department, wrote to the applicant advising her of the recommendation of the Commonwealth Medical Officer and also advising her that the Department does not have the delegation to retire an officer on these grounds without first obtaining the approval of both Comcare and the Retirement Benefits Office. We understand the Retirement Benefits Office to be the office of the respondent Commissioner. The Department of Social Security went on to say that they would be providing a copy of the information to Comcare and asking for their consent to proceed with retirement action and further that if Comcare consent was obtained a submission would then be made to the Retirement Benefits Office for their consideration. The letter went on to say that the procedure could take some time, that it was the Department's understanding that the Retirement Benefits Office had a period of two years before they had to make a decision, and went on to say that they would be asking for a decision to be made as soon as possible. As we understand the evidence and although the recommendation of the Commonwealth Medical Officer was made on 29 January 1991, the applicant has still not been retired from the Public Service. It is not necessary that we set out the somewhat convoluted processes that have been undertaken since January 1991 in relation to the applicant's retirement. Undoubtedly those processes have been confused and drawn out by the uncertainty about the applicant's membership of CSS or PSS and it is that uncertainty that explains some of the delay which has occurred. However, we will come to other factors which also have no doubt contributed to the delay in giving effect to the recommendation of the Commonwealth Medical Officer.

10. We are satisfied, and so find, that when the applicant commenced long term compensation leave from her employment on 1 February 1991 she was advised that because she was on long term leave she was not required to make the election within the terms of section 244 of the Act until she returned to work from the leave. The evidence before the Tribunal indicates that the officers of the Department of Social Security responsible for administration of personnel matters were under a misunderstanding as to the effect of subsection 244(2) believing that the subsection prevented an eligible employee making an election and declaration under subsection 244(1) during any period when the eligible employee was absent from duty on leave of absence with or without pay. Document T22 is an acknowledgment by the Department of Social Security that the officers of the Personnel and Industrial Relations Unit were confused at the time about the making of such elections by officers who were on long term leave, including those who were on sick leave. We accept, and so find, that at or about 1 February 1991 the applicant was advised by an officer of the Department of Social Security that she was not eligible to make an election under section 244 of the Act until she returned to duty from leave. We have some difficulty in understanding how that advice could be given because the wording of section 244(2) is, in our view, quite clear and it only refers to leave without pay, but we presume that the officers involved had not paid sufficient attention to the words of the provision and may have, in fact, been relying upon some other instruction or a misunderstanding of that instruction. We are therefore satisfied, and so find, that the applicant went on long term compensation leave in the belief, which was based on departmental advice given to her, that she was not permitted to make the election and declaration within the terms of section 244 until she returned to duty as an officer of the Department.

11. While the applicant was on compensation leave she was living with her mother and her pet dog and apparently had done so for a number of years prior to that time. In particular, it seems that to varying degrees she had the care of her mother, which became paramount from August 1991 when her mother suffered adverse health which eventually led to her death in early 1993. We accept that from August 1991 the applicant was very preoccupied with the care of her mother and that she had difficulty in concentrating on her own personal affairs and matters arising out of her employment in particular. We also accept, and so find, that the applicant suffered further problems during the period in question because of adverse well being of her pet dog, of which she was fond and to which she was particularly attached. The pet dog required considerable veterinary attention during this period (Exhibit E). Added to the obvious problems with the care of her mother and the perhaps less obvious, but no doubt important concerns about the welfare of her dog, was the appllicant's personal health described in a rehabilitation report by Work Directions Australia, an extract of which forms Exhibit C in these proceedings and reads as follows:

"In my opinion the (applicant) presented at rehabilitation

assessment as extremely pain focussed. She described persistent

bilateral arm pain of a moderate to severe level and generalised

pain. She described a history of a restricted lifestyle due to

various illnesses since childhood. Her current lifestyle seems

very much focussed on her own disabilities and the illness of her

mother."

12. In the course of discussions about her pending retirement on grounds of invalidity an officer of the Department of Social Security contacted the applicant on 29 April 1992 and in the course of that discussion raised the question as to whether the applicant was a member of the CSS or the PSS. Although the terms of that discussion are somewhat in doubt as to who instituted the discussion, that discussion resulted in an approach by the Department of Social Security to the Retirement Benefits Office seeking advice as to the applicant's options on validity retirement in both the PSS and the CSS. On 18 May 1992 the respondent Commissioner replied to the enquiry by the Department of Social Security and that reply is set out at Document T8. In the course of his letter the Commissioner stated:

"3. You are reminded that an election to transfer from the CSS to

the PSS scheme is governed by the operation of section 244 of the

Superannuation Act 1976 (copy attached). As (the applicant) did

not elect prior to her having proceeded on SLWOP, despite a

significant part of the election period having expired, it is

considered that she will have to seek to have a late election

under subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 accepted

prior to acceptance of an election to change schemes.

4. This aspect will require early resolution as the pre-assessment

process must be commenced under the provisions of the appropriate

scheme. Should there be a change in schemes after the

pre-assessment process commences or after submission to the Board,

any decision will be invalidated until re-affirmed by the other

Board. Obviously this will incur significant administrative delay.

5. These matters should be brought to (the applicant's) attention

as a matter or urgency."

13. That letter and the attachments were forwarded to the applicant by the Department of Social Security under cover of a letter dated 21 May 1992. The officer of the Department re-emphasised the quoted part of the Commissioner's letter by drawing attention to the need to resolve the situation fairly quickly and to make a decision as to a late election to enter the PSS. Attached to that letter was not only the quite voluminous advices from the respondent Commissioner as to the various calculations etc, but also a booklet a copy of which is Exhibit 2 in these proceedings and which describes in some detail the PSS scheme.

14. The advice by both the Commissioner and the Department that the applicant should resolve the situation as to her membership of a fund quickly, cured the incorrect advice given to the applicant in February 1991 during the election period. However, notwithstanding the advice given to the applicant in May 1992 she seems to have done very little, if anything, in relation to the matter of her membership of the PSS until March 1993 when, while attending to the affairs of her mother's estate, she had a discussion with a finance officer at a bank and in the course of that discussion the subject of superannuation arose and in particular the issue of as to whether or not she should elect to transfer to the PSS. It seems from the evidence before the Tribunal that the bank finance officer gave her advice to the effect that she was no longer entitled to seek to elect to transfer to the PSS. Whether that advice was given in full knowledge of the facts relating to the applicant or, for that matter, in full knowledge of the operation of section 244 of the Act, we do not know. That advice, however, prompted some action by the applicant and she approached the respondent Commissioner by a long written memorandum of 25 March 1993 which is Document T10 in these proceedings. In her letter to the Commissioner the applicant seems to evidence a misapprehension that she was not really required to decide the issue of whether she wanted to elect to join the PSS until after her invalidity process, described as assessment and pre-assessment, had been taken to a further stage. In particular, she describes the assessment process as being static at that particular time. She also said in the course of that letter that she was totally preoccupied with her mother's care and, in particular, she was totally committed to caring for her mother from April 1992. One of the consequences of her preoccupation with the care for her mother and, for that matter, also with her own medical problems, was that the question of "the matter of superannuation slipped from my consciousness, all concentration being spent on my mother and her welfare." The letter also requested the respondent Commissioner to supply information in response to a number of questions raised by the applicant with particular emphasis upon the benefits paid under both schemes in relation to a person who has been retired on grounds of invalidity. In a letter dated 16 June 1993 the respondent Commissioner replied to the applicant's letter of 25 March 1993 but reserved for further advice the enquiry relating to late elections and benefit options.

15. By letter dated 28 June 1993 (Document T13 in these proceedings) the respondent Commissioner replied in relation to the question of making a late election, referred to section 244 of the Act and set out in the letter a summary of the operation of the provisions and the power of the Commissioner to accept the late election but at the same time drawing the applicant's attention to the fact that there was no power to grant an extension of time as such.

16. By a further letter dated 8 July 1993 (Document T14 in these proceedings) the respondent Commissioner sent the applicant details of invalidity benefits in both the CSS and the PSS as they would apply in the circumstances of the applicant with the various options not only described but also calculated out as to amounts.

17. By a further letter dated 25 August 1993 the respondent Commissioner advised the applicant that the approval had been given for her retirement on invalidity grounds and a copy of the invalidity retirement certificate with reasons for the decision was enclosed with the letter. The letter went on to advise the applicant to get in touch with her employer as early as possible regarding retirement on invalidity grounds and claim for superannuation benefits.

18. By letter dated 31 August 1993 the Department of Social Security wrote to the applicant and advised her that the Department proposed to retire her on the grounds of invalidity. The letter went on to give the applicant information about the process of retirement including the necessity for her to provide written consent to the action before there could be the issue of a notice of retirement - the instrument to affect her retirement from the Australian Public Service. The letter went on to discuss the possibility of the applicant's transfer to the PSS and drew attention to the fact that a transfer to the PSS would cause a delay in relation to the retirement date because of the necessity to have the retirement reprocessed as a retirement involving the PSS.

19. By letter dated 5 September 1993 addressed to the Retirement Benefits Office the applicant set out her desire to make a late election for transfer to the PSS and based that request on the fact that she had only then received financial advice that it would be in her long term interest to be retired on invalidity grounds as a member of the PSS rather than as a member of the CSS. She said that she had been unable to obtain that advice until the time of the letter and referred in particular to sections 244 and 157 of the Act. Document T22 is a copy of the applicant's election to cease membership of the CSS and to become a member of the PSS and that document is dated 6 September 1993.

20. We are satisfied, and so find, that the applicant was given incorrect advice by officers of the Department of Social Security at the time that she went on long term compensation leave in January/February 1991 and we are also satisfied, and so find, that the Department of Social Security corrected that incorrect advice in their letter of 21 May 1992 and made it clear there that it was necessary for the applicant to take immediate action to resolve the question of her late election to enter the PSS. We are also satisfied, and so find, that the applicant was provided with a considerable amount of material during the election period, as were all contributors to the CSS at that time and we are also satisfied, and so find, that the applicant was provided with additional material when she received the Department of Social Security letter of 21 May 1992 which included a copy of the PSS booklet which is Exhibit 2 in these proceedings. By that time, in our view, there can be no question that the applicant had not been fully and correctly informed as to her rights to make a late election and also full information about the PSS scheme and comparisons between the PSS scheme and the CSS scheme as applicable to the applicant and also details of where she could get further information if she required it. We are satisfied, and so find, that the applicant in fact did very little to pursue her concerns and to resolve the question. We find this surprising given the applicant's academic achievement in that she has a Bachelor of Arts degree with honours in English Literature but we accept her evidence that during the period from August 1991 to January 1993 she was caring for her mother and for at least part of that time that care kept her fully occupied in combination with her own health problems, and also to some extent, the problems involving her pet. We accept her explanations that she found it difficult to concentrate on matters outside her domestic situation at that time.

21. We have to decide whether the election dated 6 September 1993 should be accepted as having been made within the period allowed and to have effect accordingly. The period allowed is the election period within the terms of section 244 of the Act and that period expired on 30 June 1991. In deciding whether or not the election is to be treated as having been made within that period, that is on or before 30 June 1991, we have to be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be so recognised.

22. There is a long line of authorities in this Tribunal dealing with late elections under the Act and the effect of section 157. We have not been directed to any cases involving elections under section 244 but the authorities do deal with late elections in the context of preservation of benefits in terms of section 137 of the Act. It is not necessary that we review those authorities and we need only refer to one decision of the Tribunal and two decisions of the Federal Court to direct us to the correct application of the discretion in subsection 157(1). In Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 14 AAR 456 at 471 the Tribunal, as then constituted, considered the authorities in relation to the meaning of the word "desirable" and then went on at page 470 as follows:

"These authorities, and I have been unable to find any others

which assist us, suggest strongly that the word 'desirable'

connotes a 'positive aspiration' and that it is 'something worthy

of achievement' rather than 'merely advisable' (see Celik's case,

at 223). That, to us, seems to be the correct connotation to apply

to the word 'desirable' and I adopt it. I must now consider what

is actually desirable in the context of s 157(1) and to do so must

consider the Act as a whole.

The majority of the Act is directed, in general terms, to

regulating the payment of contributions by 'eligible employees'

(defined in s 3), the benefits payable to those eligible

employees, their spouses and children and to the preservation of

the rights of certain eligible employees. The remainder of the Act

deals with the particular circumstances of a person who has ceased

to be an eligible employee to become a candidate at parliamentary

elections, the preservation of rights of certain eligible

employees, payments of pensions and related payments and

provisions relating generally to the administration of the Act."

23. At page 472 after considering the various provisions of the Act, the Tribunal said:

"Returning to the scope of the discretion in s 157(1) and taking

into account the Commissioner's powers and the fact that the

discretion with which I am concerned appears in the Part entitled,

'Miscellaneous', I consider that a late election should be

recognised only if it would benefit the person making it while, at

the same time, not adversely affecting the payment of pensions and

benefits to other beneficiaries under the Act and not impeding the

efficient and proper administration of the superannuation scheme

established by the Act and administered by the Commissioner. It

may be that what would benefit the individual may not be

consistent with the interests of the other beneficiaries and of

good administration. In order to balance what may be competing

interests, it is my view that it can only be desirable to

recognise a late election if there are objectively good reasons

which justify the person's not being required to comply with the

time limits otherwise clearly specified by the Act."

We respectfully agree with the dicta of the Tribunal in Re Liddle and adopt it as part of our reasons for the purpose of deciding this matter.

24. In Commissioner for Superannuation v. Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 33 ALD 569, Von Doussa J referred to the Tribunal's decision in Re Liddle and dealt with the meaning of "desirable" in the following words at page 577:

"In s 157(1) 'desirable' is not referable to a state of

mind - whether of the elector or of the commissioner. Whether

a late election is 'desirable' is to be judged objectively having

regard to all the circumstances of the case. The suggested meaning

'something worthy of achievement' does not imply other than an

objective evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in the

end, whether the recognition of a late election is 'something

worthy of achievement' must depend on what is fair in order to do

justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and

the requirements of the administration of the fund."

25. Boardman was decided after the Full Court decision in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 50 FCR 150. There the Court dismissed an appeal against a decision of this Tribunal (as then constituted) refusing to exercise the discretion in section 157 in relation to a late election made under section 137 of the Act. The main judgment was given by Davies J (with whom Black CJ concurred and Cooper J concurred subject to further comments).

26. At pages 154-5 Davies J said:

"If a relevant provision confers a discretion, as does s 157 of

the Act, the discretion should be exercised by reference to the

words of the statute and the context within which the discretion

is conferred. Section 157 uses simple words and the issues which

arise under it are invariably issues of fact, the ultimate issue

being whether it is desirable that a late election should be

recognised. Reference to authorities such as (Hospital Products

Ltd v United States Surgical Corporation [1984] HCA 64; (1984) 156 CLR 41), which

enunciate principles of law and equity providing relief in

circumstances entirely different from those within the

contemplation of s 157, simply distract attention from the matters

which ought to be considered under s 157. The operation of s 157

is not dependent upon the existence of fault on the part of

officers of the employer or of the scheme, although matters such

as lack of advice may well be relevant."

And later at pages 155-6 his Honour said:

"Many such provisions, including s 157, are not concerned with

rights. If an act is not performed within the prescribed time,

there is no right to have it performed. But there is a discretion

to permit time to be extended should it be appropriate in the

circumstances to do so. In the exercise of this discretion, it may

be more important to consider the consequences of extending or

refusing to extend time than to debate the reasons why the act was

not done in time. Section 157 merely requires that the

Commissioner 'be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the

case it is desirable that the election should be recognised'. 'All

the circumstances' include all relevant circumstances existing at

the time the discretion is to be exercised."

27. Can we be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election made by the applicant on 6 September 1993 should be recognised and treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by section 244 of the Act, ie made within the election period which ended on 30 June 1991?

28. In coming to our decision in this matter we have taken into account the circumstances of the applicant in relation to her membership of the CSS and we have also taken into account the fact that she had considerable personal problems during the period August 1991 to January 1993. We have also taken into account the fact that the applicant was given incorrect advice by an officer of the Department of Social Security in January/February 1991 during the election period when she commenced long term compensation leave. We have also taken into account that there are four separate references in Exhibit A which was received by the applicant during the election period and those references make it quite clear that the exercise of the election was to be completed before 1 July 1991. As we have already explained the Commissioner apparently adopted the view that members of the CSS had to elect to either remain a member of the CSS or declare that they wished to become a member of the PSS and elect to cease to be a member of the CSS. As we have said that is not in accordance with section 244 but that to our view is not relevant in dealing with the issue with which we have to deal in this case.

29. For these reasons we have had to consider whether on an objective basis it is desirable that the Tribunal exercise the discretion in the applicant's favour in accordance with subsection 157(1) of the Act. We are not satisfied that it is so desirable. In particular, we are not satisfied that the circumstances of this case would justify a decision which would, in effect, extend the time for making the election from 30 June 1991 to 6 September 1993. In our view to do so in the circumstances of this case would be to give no weight at all to the clear intention of the Parliament for a certain election period when it enacted section 244 of the Act. The confused state of the applicant's retirement from the Public Service is itself evidence of the desirability of the section 244 election being made during the election period.

30. We have taken into account the wide discretion conferred by subsection 157(1) but we are not satisfied that it is desirable that the discretion should be exercised on the facts of this case to, in effect, set aside the clear intention of the Parliament as expressed in subsection 244(3).

31. For these reasons the decision under review will be affirmed.

A.M. BRENNAN I agree with the issues and findings of fact set out in the majority's reasons but, on the basis of these, have come to a different decision.

2. I find that the period 1991 to mid 1993 (inclusive) was a particularly difficult time for the applicant. The interaction of factors operating in the applicant's life over this period made it difficult for her to deal with any matters extraneous to her own health and domestic responsibilities.

3. The applicant explained in evidence outlined in the majority's reasons that she was not well herself during this period yet it was necessary for her to provide nursing care and other personal services for her mother who was chronically ill. From approximately August 1991 until her mother's death in 1993 the mother's health deteriorated and she moved into the terminal phase of her illness.

4. In her evidence the applicant spoke about her close attachment to her mother and the grief which she felt about her mother's illness and death. In addition the applicant's pet dog died which was a matter of further sadness (see paragraph 11 of the majority's reasons). The applicant is a single woman and was not supported by any family member or close associates over this period. The applicant did not disclose to work associates the heavy domestic responsibilities she had during 1991 and 1992 although an officer of the applicant's department, who gave oral evidence, indicated that she knew the applicant's mother was not well.

5. The applicant did not call any expert witnesses to explain the social emotional and physical demands on a person caring for a person suffering from a chronic terminal illness. However, I accept the applicant's evidence that she did not have the physical or psychological momentum to address matters concerning elections for a superannuation scheme under the relevant legislation.

6. During the early phase of the applicant's compensation leave she understood, on the basis of incorrect advice she had received from her employer, that she could not make an election about her superannuation while she was on leave. By the time the applicant received detailed and correct advice about her superannuation situation in May 1992 (see paragraphs 12-13 inclusive above) she had disengaged from social and vocational interests activities because she was absorbed in providing for her mother's care.

7. The applicant's efforts to care for her mother at home are consistent with the objectives of many public aged care policies and services and the economic interests of the wider community. For instance the Commonwealth Government funds home care and associated services, a domiciliary care benefit, a "carer's" pension and so forth and these policies include the objective of reducing demands on higher cost levels of care eg nursing homes and hostels. In brief the applicant undertook responsibilities which had positive resource outcomes for the community. I recognise this fact because some people on compensation leave may have the opportunity to pursue their own personal interests and rehabilitation and time to focus on their employment/superannuation status. This was not the situation in this case.

8. Because of the configuration of social, psychological and health factors operating in the applicant's life from January 1991 to mid 1993 (inclusive) I am satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the applicant's election should be recognised and I would exercise the discretion in the applicant's favour in accordance with subsection 157(1) of the Act.
