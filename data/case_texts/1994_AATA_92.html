REASONS FOR DECISIONS



Mr B.H. Pascoe, Member

8 April 1994



1. This is an application to review a decision by the Commissioner of Taxation to refuse applications made by the applicant for refunds of sales tax on a product used by the applicant in its business of providing warehouse storage facilities for furniture and the like.



2. The product can be described as large boxes or crates made from timber in which the items to be stored were placed for ease of movement and storage.



3. Prior to commencement of evidence and argument on the basic subject matter of the application, the applicant sought an order from the Tribunal that the applicant not be limited to the grounds stated in the notice of objection. The order was sought pursuant to section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 ("the Act") which states:



"On an application for review of a reviewable decision:



(a) the applicant is, unless the AAT orders otherwise, limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates;"



This section was inserted by Act No. 216 of 1991 and is applicable to objections where the decisions to which the objections relate were notified after 1 March 1992. The decisions to which the objections relate in this matter were notified on 25 March 1991 and 22 May 1991. Consequently, section 14ZZK of the Act does not apply. However, this section effectively replaced section 42E(a) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act No. 1 1930 ("the Assessment Act No. 1") which provided an equivalent discretion to the Tribunal although in slightly different terms. This section provided:



"In proceeding under this Part on a review before the Tribunal or an appeal to a court :



(a) the objector is, unless the Tribunal or court otherwise orders, limited to the grounds stated in the objection;"



4. At the time of making the application for refund, in subsequent discussions with officers of the respondent at the time of making application to this Tribunal and at all times up until August 1993, the applicant had stated and the respondent dealt with the matter on the understanding that the applicant had arranged for construction of the relevant boxes using its own employees and within its own premises. In preparing documents and evidence for this hearing the applicant took the view in August 1993 that the construction had been undertaken by an independent contractor, H Pty Ltd, and not by the applicant. This change of view was advised to the respondent at a meeting held on 6 August 1993 and confirmed by letter dated 13 August 1993. The respondent then sought affidavits as to the matters raised and these were provided by the applicant in September 1993.



5. Prior to August 1993, the applicant's argument was that, although it had constructed the boxes, it had not "manufactured and applied to its own use" the boxes within the meaning of Assessment Act No. 1 and had, therefore, overpaid sales tax. It sought to rely on decisions in FC of T v Nimrod Theatre Co Ltd [1985] FCA 65; (1985) 5 FCR 269, and Rayson Motors Pty Ltd (in liq) v FC of T [1985] HCA 20; (1985) 156 CLR 651. Subsequent to that date, it considered that a different argument was appropriate, namely, that, if the boxes were manufactured, they were manufactured by another company. Whilst submitting that this alternative argument was covered by grounds 5 and 6 of the notice of objection, the applicant now seeks to add to the grounds stated in that notice to clearly include this alternative argument. Grounds 5 and 6 of the objection state:



"5. To the extent that the taxpayer has paid sales tax on the sale value of boxes manufactured and applied to its own use, the taxpayer has overpaid sales tax. Accordingly the taxpayer is entitled to a refund of that amount of sales tax overpaid, pursuant to subsection 26(1) of the Act or some other provision of the Act or the Sales Tax Regulations.



6. The Commissioner ought to have found or should now find that tax has been overpaid by the taxpayer who has not passed on that tax. Pursuant to subsection 26(1) of the Act, the Commissioner should have allowed or should now allow in full the refund claimed by the taxpayer in respect of tax so overpaid."



6. During the course of submissions on this matter, I advised that parties that, in my view, the grounds of the objection were not adequate to draw the respondent's attention to, or even imply the argument, that the applicant was not the person who manufactured the relevant goods. Consequently, it was appropriate to consider an application for an order pursuant to section 42E(a) of Assessment Act No. 1.



7. Initially, at the hearing the applicant submitted that the respondent would not be prejudiced by the order in that some six months notice of the facts which the applicant would seek to put into evidence was given and the respondent had had ample time to prepare argument in response.



8. On behalf of the respondent, Ms Richards, of counsel, made a long detailed oral submission opposing the making of the order sought on the grounds of prejudice to the respondent. It was said, in essence, that the respondent would be prejudiced in two ways. Firstly, if the applicant is successful in the new and alternative argument, the respondent will not be able to recover tax from H Pty Ltd as a manufacturer of the goods as a result of time limitations placed on the recovery of sales tax unpaid. By virtue of section 12B of the Sales Tax Procedure Act, sales tax is not recoverable after three years from the close of the month in which the relevant transaction act or operation was effected except in circumstances of fraud or evasion. Consequently, it is now too late to recover the sales tax from the third party. If H Pty Ltd was the manufacturer then sales tax should have been paid and included in the purchase price paid by the applicant. If the applicant is successful in this matter then the respondent would be out of pocket and the applicant would gain by neither paying sales tax itself nor paying a higher sales tax inclusive price to H Pty ltd. The second way in which, it was said, the respondent would be prejudiced was the result of section 12C of the Sales Tax Procedure Act. Under this section the respondent may only make refunds of overpaid sales tax where a claim in writing is made within three years from the date upon which the overpayment was made. It was submitted that the granting of the order sought would deprive the respondent of a defence pursuant to this section. The claim now proposed to be made by the applicant is not the same claim as made in the original application and considered by the respondent. The new claim is now outside the three year limitation. If successful, the applicant would succeed in overcoming the three year limit on refund applications whilst depriving the respondent of an ability to exercise his right to recover unpaid tax of another company within that same three year period. The position may well have been quite different if the applicant had provided these new facts in the original application, which facts were, or should have been, known to the applicant. The prejudice which will be suffered by the respondent, it was said, is brought about entirely by the conduct, either in terms of delay or in terms of positive assertions made by the applicant.



9. At the hearing Mr Evans, for the applicant, expressed surprise at the strength and depth of the respondent's submission against the application for leave to expand the grounds of objection and sought an adjournment to enable preparation of a written response. Adjournment was granted and a written submission was received from the applicant with a reply from the respondent.



10. In essence the applicant submitted that:



"1. The refusal to make the order requested would deny the applicant the opportunity to put the true facts before the Tribunal and result in an inappropriate review of the question of whether the applicant had paid sales tax incorrectly.



2. H Pty Ltd is not a party before the Tribunal in this matter and the question of whether that company had a liability for sales tax is not a question to be dealt with in this hearing.



3. Any extension of the grounds of objection will not be a new refund application but simply a change of basis upon which the applicant seeks to rely in support of the original refund applications.



4. The applicant will not necessarily benefit from not having paid a higher sales tax inclusive price to H Pty Ltd. The sales tax position of H Pty Ltd is unknown, is not a question to be established in this matter and commercial considerations may well have affected the price paid to H Pty Ltd.



5. The respondent had been given adequate opportunity to ascertain the true facts from an early date, had been provided with details of the proposed amended basis of argument in August 1993 and, at the hearing both parties had indicated that they were ready to proceed regardless of whether the Tribunal exercised the discretion or not.



11. In support of the respondent's submission several cases were cited relating to applications to amend pleadings in civil matters where the statute of limitations would prejudice the other party. The applicant sought to distinguish these cases as being radically different from the role of the Tribunal or Court in taxation matters. It sought to support this view from the decision of Brennan J in FC of T v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614 where his Honour said (at page 621):



"But an objection and a Commissioner's notice of decision on the objection are not pleadings which so confine the issues as to preclude the Commissioner from putting the taxpayer to proof of the true amount of his taxable income."



Further, his Honour stated:



"It would be inappropriate for a Court determining an appeal to make an order altering the tax liability assessed (section 199) unless the Court were satisfied that the amount to which it proposed to alter the assessment represented the true tax liability of the taxpayer. Although the grounds of objection limit the grounds of appeal, the ultimate question for the Court hearing the appeal is not whether the grounds have been made out but whether the amount assessed as taxable is wrong."



Whilst these passages concern the entitlement of the Commissioner to put the taxpayer to proof, they do reflect the view that the role of the Tribunal or Court is to determine the correct tax payable within the confines of the relevant statute.



12. The leading case on the question of the exercise of the discretion to allow amendment of grounds of objection is Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v FC of T 91 ATC 4942 which was a joint judgement of Lockhart, Burchett and Hill JJ on an appeal by the taxpayer from a decision of this Tribunal. The case was concerned with the discretion contained in section 190(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act but this section is identical with section 42E(a) of the Assessment Act No. 1. At page 4945 their Honours said:



"The words of s. 190(a) are on their face wide and unambiguous. They empower the Tribunal, or the Court as the case may be, to relieve a taxpayer from the constraint of that subsection. Unless there is something in the legislative history or context which requires a departure from the literal meaning of the words they should be given effect to."



Further, at page 4949, their Honours stated:



"The amendment to s. 190(a) introduced by Act No. 48 of 1986 was of a remedial kind and thus must be construed in accordance with well established principles relating to ameliorating legislation. It follows that the Tribunal or the Court has power to permit a taxpayer to argue that the taxable income and tax payable are incorrect and 'excessive' for reasons not initially advanced, even if those reasons involve, as in the present case, entirely fresh grounds in substitution for the original grounds, or even if they require consideration of matters not considered by the Commissioner in the original assessment process.



The decision whether to allow an amendment ought to be made on the same considerations of justice upon which such decisions are regularly made in litigation. It was in the past a reproach to the law that the real issues in taxation appeals could be refused a hearing for a defective objection, and Parliament has legislated to remove that reproach; an amendment under s. 190 should not be considered with reluctance, but on its merits."



In a subsequent decision in the case of Gilder v FC of T 22 ATR 872, Davies J refused to exercise the discretion to allow amendment to grounds of objection. However, he was dealing there with a matter not raised before the hearing and claims for expenditure which were somewhat a matter of speculation. His Honour said (at page 884):



"I take into account not only the fact that the proposed grounds raise matters entirely different from those presently before the court but also the fact that they do not stand out as matters which, in the interests of justice, must be considered by the court. The material before the court does not disclose a prima facie case of invalidity of the assessments. As to the additional tax, if that issue had been raised in the original objections, this matter would presumably have been referred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal rather than this court."



The respondent sought to rely on a decision of Senior Member Balmford (as she then was) in a case reported as Case Y58 91 ATC 497. This case concerned an application for extension of time to lodge objections against income tax assessments by two associated companies. The Tribunal accepted the Commissioner's submission that if the taxpayer's objections were ultimately successful, amounts paid as tax would be refundable to them with interest, but, due to the time limits in section 170(3), the deduction allowed to a third associated company could not be disallowed. However, in this case the extension of time sought was in excess of three years, the objections, if successful, would overturn settlement agreed by all of the relevant parties prior to the issue of the assessments in issue and the third company which would be protected by section 170(3) was associated with the applicants. In my view, this case can be readily distinguished from the circumstances of this application.



13. Having given full consideration to the submissions from both parties, I am of the view that the applicant should succeed in the application for an order that it should not be limited to the grounds stated in the Notices of Objection to the extent that it may include the ground that the relevant boxes were not manufactured by the applicant but by another party. Given that this issue is a very relevant factual consideration to the question of the applicant's liability to pay sales tax, it is difficult to restrict the applicant from pursing this argument in a proper hearing of the matter which argument stands out as a matter which, in the interests of justice, must be considered by the Tribunal. I would adopt the views expressed in the Lighthouse Philatelics case (supra) and allow the applicant to remedy an otherwise potentially defective objection. Whilst accepting the possibility of some prejudice to the respondent in his ability to recover sales tax which may not have been paid by a third party, the liability of that third party is not a matter which can be properly before the Tribunal in this hearing.



14. The respondent included in his submission matters relating to section 40 of Assessment Act No. 1. That argument, together with the argument relating to section 12C, are best left to the substantive hearing.



15. The Tribunal orders that, in the hearing of these applications, the applicant is not limited to the grounds stated in the objections.
