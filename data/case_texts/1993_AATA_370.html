Re Taxation Appeals [1993] AATA 370; (1993) 27 ATR 1174 (17 December 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. ST92/11

AAT No. 9185

Number of pages - 7

Income Tax

[1993] AATA 370; (1993) 27 ATR 1174

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.H. BURNS, B.C. LOCK (Member) AND D.J. TROWSE (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - deductability of management fees paid to a related entity - s.51(1) Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 - decision affirmed

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ss.51(1), 223, 227

Ronpibon Tin No Liability v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100

Robert G. Nall Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 79; (1937) 57 CLR 695

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Phillips 78 ATC 4361

Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4957

HEARING

ADELAIDE, 13 May, 7 June and 28 September 1993

17:12:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr H. Zacharoyannis and Mr C. Kourakis

Solicitor for the Applicant: Zacharoyannis, Luppino and Eckermann

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr S. Cole

Solicitors for the Respondent: External Appeals and Review

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

B.H. BURNS, B.C. LOCK AND D.J. TROWSE The issue in this reference is the deductibility of amounts paid by the applicant to a related entity and which were described as management fees. It is the contention of the applicant, a limited company, that the totality of those outgoings was incurred either in the gaining of its assessable income, or in the carrying on its business for the purpose of gaining such income and, for that reason, they qualify for deduction in terms of sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ('the Act'). The respondent submits that there existed a dual purpose for the incurring of the outgoings, one of which was not related to the gaining of assessable income. It is the amounts attributed to that purpose that have been disallowed as deductions and are the subject of this reference. Also at issue is the quantum of additional tax imposed under former sub-section 223(1) of the Act.

2. The Tribunal had before it the T documents (T1-T18) supplied in terms of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, together with six exhibits tendered on behalf of the applicant (Exhibits A1-A6) and one by the respondent (Exhibit R1). A former director and shareholder of the applicant, who was also the secretary of the related entity for the greater part of the period under review, gave evidence under oath. On the first occasion the applicant was represented by Mr H. Zacharoyannis and on the second by Mr C. Kourakis. The respondent was represented by Mr S. Cole.

3. The Tribunal made the following findings of fact. The related entity is an incorporated body ('the Club') formed to promote a particular code of football and in that regard has three teams, one in a national competition, another in a local reserve competition and the other in a junior grade. It has a membership of three to four hundred. The Club has been in existence for many years and, from a financial point of view, has survived because of the generosity of its members. It has never traded at a profit.

4. The Tribunal finds that in 1977 a city property was acquired for the express purpose of establishing both an office and a social environment for players and members of the Club. The structuring of a bar and coffee shop into the surrounds of the building were focussed at that latter purpose. The prior owner of the property was a limited company and the acquisition by the Club was achieved by the purchase of all the company's issued shares, namely two. The consideration of $200,000 was funded by members' contributions of $36,000 and the balance by way of loan from a trading bank, the repayment of which amounted to $6,000 to $7,000 per month. To assist with that repayment programme an additional 74,898 $1 shares were issued some time during the 1982 financial year, the subscribers being the Club and some of its membership. At the end of the day there were one hundred and twenty five shareholders, including the Club which controlled 39.5% of the applicant's issued capital. On the evidence before the Tribunal it seems possible to infer that at that time some, if not all, member shareholders had no expectation of any return on their investment and indeed saw their contributions as being a gratuity. Whether that perception still prevails is not known and yet it is clear that the value of the shares has increased markedly and that friction has developed between members who hold shares and those that do not.

5. In any event the task of meeting the commitment to the bank proved too much and for this reason it was resolved that the property be realised, the resultant proceeds be re-invested in another and less costly property and the applicant company be used again to hold that asset. It seems that some Club members had reservations about the managerial skills of the Club committee, particularly as they related to the buying of players, and for that reason the holding of the real estate in a separate entity was to be preferred. That view predominated notwithstanding that the persons serving on the committee of the Club were also the directors of the applicant.

6. The property was sold in September 1984 for $768,000 and another was purchased one month later for $450,000. At the time of acquisition the replacement property comprised a small two storey office building at the front, vacant land in the centre and two warehouses at the rear. The ground floor of the office building was used by the Club in its administration, whereas the first floor was converted into a coffee shop for members and then leased out at $600 per week. The warehouses were also leased to tenants who paid their rental on a monthly basis. The vacant land was used as a car park for 'permanents' who also accounted for their fees on a monthly arrangement. It is accepted that various improvements and repairs to the office building were effected soon after the taking of possession and that those works were carried out on a voluntary basis by certain Club members and/or shareholders of the applicant. A new building to house the Club administration and members' facilities - including licensed premises - was erected during the 1989 financial year at a cost of approximately $285,000. It appears that at that stage the Club conducted the bar operation in its own right whilst the applicant continued to receive rents from the coffee shop and warehouses. At no time has the Club paid any rent for the area occupied by it in the applicant's premises.

7. The years in question are 1987, 1988 and 1989, and yet a reference to certain events and decisions taken in preceding years is essential in understanding the background of the payments now in dispute.

8. The financial statements of the applicant for the 1985 and 1986 years reveal that the rentals being received exceeded deductible expenditure to the extent of $14,871 and $15,658 respectively and that it would have been taxable were it not for the bringing forward of losses incurred in earlier years. It is also clear that the earning of those profits created a cash surplus which, to some extent, enabled the applicant to benefit the Club. This was achieved by the transfer of funds, $10,000 in 1985 and $40,000 in 1986, which were described in the applicant's books of account as donations. No tax deductions for those donations was sought nor was it allowed.

9. The losses available for future carrying forward had been substantially depleted by the profits derived in the 1985 and 1986 years, to the extent that the chartered accountant engaged to prepare the applicant's final accounts, balance sheets and income tax returns, advised the directors, by letter dated 26 February 1987, that the company was now in a taxable position. It seems that further discussion took place and that the accountant was able to convince the directors that the paying of a management fee by the applicant in lieu of the non-deductible donations was the preferable choice. The date upon which the applicant and Club reached consensus on this arrangement is not known. Notwithstanding that it was some time after the 26 February 1987, it was agreed that the payment of the fee ($400 per week) be made retrospective to the 1 July 1986. It appears that at that time the parties saw no need for documentation of the arrangement and yet that perception changed in November 1988 when the respondent demonstrated an interest in the management fee of $20,800 claimed as a deduction in the applicant's 1987 income tax return. Solicitors were instructed on the 14 November 1988, the agreement was presented for consideration on the 16 November 1988 and it was executed soon after and bears as its date the 30 September 1988. It was accepted that back-dating had occurred but no explanation as to why was provided.

10. The following provisions taken from the agreement are relevant to this consideration -

' NOW IT IS HEREBY AGREED as follows:

1. The Principal agrees to appoint the Manager and the Manager agrees to

accept its appointment as the Manager of the business affairs of the

Principal.

2. The appointment referred to in clause 1 hereof is hereby acknowledged

by the parties to be for a term of five (5) years, such term having

commenced on the first day of July 1986 to expire on the 30th day of June

1991.

3. The Manager shall be responsible:

(i) To generally manage the affairs of the Principal.

(ii) To maintain all properties owned or leased by the Principal.

(iii) To arrange for leases of such property to be entered into where

necessary and to collect rents generated from the lease of such

properties.

(iv) To keep all financial records and accounts of the company.

(v) To carry out all functions which are incidental to all or any of the

duties referred to in the foregoing sub-clauses.

4. In consideration of the Manager carrying out its duties hereunder, the

Principal shall pay to the Manager a weekly management fee which is

currently $400.00 per week provided that such management fee shall be

reviewed as at the 30th day of June 1989 and the 30th day of June 1990

provided that in the event of the parties failing to reach agreement as
to

the basis of such review, the management fee shall be increased by year

per cent (10%).

5. Unless otherwise agreed between the parties, the Principal shall pay

the management fee to the Manager weekly throughout the duration of this

agreement. '

11. The amounts claimed for management fees were $20,800 for each of the 1987 and 1988 years and $40,100 for 1989. It was accepted that the additional $20,000 in the last year related to work involved in the construction of the new building, which in turn was followed by the applicant's concession that that portion was of a capital nature and thus excluded from the positive provisions of sub-section 51(1) of the Act. The Tribunal observes that the payments to the Club were not in accord with clause 5 of the agreement and in fact were effected by the following transfers of funds -

In respect of 1987 year

12 May 1987 $10,000

2 June 1987 $16,000

Total $26,000

The excess over the agreed figure of $20,800, i.e. $5,200, was debited

to the loan account of the Club in books of the applicant.

In respect of 1988 year

6 May 1988 $10,000

3 June 1988 $ 5,000

Total $15,000

On this occasion the shortfall of $5,800 was credited to the Club's

loan account.

In respect of 1989 year

13 July 1988 $5,000

19 July 1988 $5,000

3 February 1989 $5,000

13 February 1989 $5,000

ex Building Project Bank account $20,000

Total $40,000

12. The respondent has always taken the view that the amounts claimed were unrealistic and lacking in commerciality. Initially he adjusted the three returns to disallow that part of the amounts claimed in excess of 7% of gross rentals received. That formula is one advocated by the Real Estate Institute of South Australia in situations where real estate agents manage properties for absentee owners. The effect of those adjustments was to increase the taxable income for the 1987 year and to convert returned losses into taxable incomes for the other two years. Those adjustments were attended by the issue of an amended assessment for the 1987 year and original assessments for 1988 and 1989. Included in those assessments were amounts of additional tax for incorrect returns, all of which had been calculated on the basis of 14.2% for the per annum factor and 25% for that attributed to the degree of culpability.

13. The objection lodged on behalf of the applicant placed substantial reliance upon the fact that the applicant did not have any staff of its own and that the functions pertaining to the management of the property were performed by a full time office administrator employed by the Club. In addition to her duties as administrator of the Club, she was required to undertake the following tasks for the applicant -

. manage car park including collection of fees

. chase up unpaid accounts and arrears of rent

. maintain prime books of account

. preparation of monthly statements

. attendance at meetings of directors and shareholders and typing

minutes

. preparation and distribution of notices of meeting to shareholders

. generally to liaise with shareholders

The objection also referred to the construction of the new building in 1989 and how the preliminary work required to establish that project was carried out by members of the Club. At that stage no mention was made of other alleged services rendered by members of the Club and which apparently included such activities as repairs to the building and the providing of explanations and advise to shareholders regarding a plan for the Club to acquire all of the issued capital of the applicant. Indeed, it seems that the first reference to those inputs occurred at the hearing of this matter.

14. It will be recalled that not all members of the Club were shareholders in the applicant company and it seems that some of those excluded saw this as a divisive issue in the conduct of the Club's affairs. In an effort to restore harmony, a representative of the Club sought legal opinion regarding a possible re-organisation of the affairs of the Club. As a consequence of the advice received, it was decided by the management committee of the Club that the Club make an offer for all of the shares not already in its possession. The Tribunal accepts that members of the committee and the office administrator spent considerable time in the development of this strategy and the explanation thereof to the members of the Club, and that such involvement persisted throughout the period under review.

15. In the determination of the objection, the respondent considered first, whether the transactions in question could be seen as being of a business kind, particularly where the parties were not dealing with each other at arm's length, and, if so, secondly, whether the amounts claimed were commercially realistic. Notwithstanding an acceptance that there existed a sufficiently sound basis for entering into the agreement, the respondent formed the view that the payments made were in excess of commercially reasonable and realistic rates. Furthermore the respondent concluded that a calculation based on a percentage of the office administrator's salary would represent a more commercially acceptable value of the particular services rendered by the Club to the applicant. Acting on a statement made by a representative of the applicant that the office administrator spent 50% of her time on performing the duties of the applicant, the respondent allowed the objection in part to reflect deductions of amounts equivalent to 50% of the salary so paid in all of the years in dispute. That decision was followed by the issue of amended assessments for the 1987 and 1988 years whereby both actual tax and additional tax were reduced, the latter being on a proportional basis. The effect on the 1989 assessment was different inasmuch that the further allowance created a loss of $2,570. For that year the respondent issued an amended assessment which showed both taxable income and tax on taxable income as nil. Also the additional tax previously imposed was remitted in full. It should be noted that the respondent accepts that the applicant is a public company for income tax purposes and thus section 109, which forms part of Division 7 of the Act, has no application.

16. The applicant was dissatisfied with that decision and appealed against it to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. Before the Tribunal, the applicant contended that the purpose of the arrangement was, first, to recognise the contribution being made by the Club in the conduct of the applicant's affairs and, secondly, that the time had come for the applicant to pay its own way. Furthermore, that, having regard to the combination of services being rendered by the Club, the amounts of $20,800 were commercially realistic. As part of that contention, the applicant submitted that the percentage attributed by the respondent to the work performed by the office administrator on behalf of the applicant was too low and that, after allowing for the time spent on the possible acquisition of the shares, 'the biggest part of her time was spent on company affairs'. For those reasons the applicant maintains that the amounts in question, i.e. the differences between $20,800 and the amount allowed by the respondent, were incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income or, alternatively, were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, and accordingly qualify as allowable deductions in terms of sub-section 51(1) which reads as follows:

' 51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are

incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are
necessarily

incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing

such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which

they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or

domestic nature or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production

of exempt income. '

The Tribunal accepts that a company engaged in the type of activities currently under consideration is carrying on a business and thus both positive limbs of the sub-section are available to the applicant. Irrespective of that assent it is clear that the actual working of the second limb adds but little to the operation of the leading words, 'losses or outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' - see Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p 56.

17. In the case of Ronpibon Tin NL (supra) the Full Court of the High Court of Australia provides at pp 56-57 the following explanation of the words 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' in the context of sub-section 51(1) of the Act -

' For the expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing

incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be

incidental and relevant to that end. The words "incurred in gaining or

producing the assessable income" mean in the course of gaining or

producing such income. Their operation has been explained in cases

decided under the provisions of the previous enactments: see
particularly

Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at pp

303-304, 307, 309, 310; and W. Nevill and Co. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. (1937)
56

CLR at pp 300, 301, 305-306, 308.

Notwithstanding the differences in other respects in the present

provision, the expression "incurred in gaining or producing the
assessable

income" has been left unchanged and bears the same meaning. In brief

substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both

sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should

be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none
be

produced, would be expected to produce assessable income. '

Whether an outgoing should properly be seen as being wholly or in part 'incidental and relevant' to the 'end' of gaining or producing the assessable income is a question of characterisation. In the case of Ure v F.C. of T. 81 ATC 4100, the process of characterisation was described at p 4109 by Deane and Sheppard JJ in the following terms -

' In the ordinary case where the income which is expected to flow from

an outgoing offers an obvious commercial explanation for incurring it the

relevant characterization can readily be determined by reference to the

gaining or producing of that income. In the more complex case however,

where there is no such obvious commercial explanation, the solution of
the

problem of characterization must be derived from a weighing of the many

aspects of the whole set of circumstances including direct and indirect

objects and advantages which the taxpayer sought in making the outgoing.

Some of those circumstances may point in one direction, some in the
other.

In such a case, as was said by the Privy Council in B.P. Australia Limited

v. FC. of T. ((1965) [1965] UKPCHCA 2; 112 CLR 386 at p 397) in relation to the question

whether a particular outgoing was of income or capital according to

ordinary concepts, it is "a common sense appreciation of all the guiding

features which must provide the ultimate answer". '

The deductibility or otherwise of expenditure can generally be determined

by answering the question whether 'the circumstances of its expenditure

give it the complexion of money laid out in furtherance of a purpose of

gaining income' - see of Robert G. Nall Limited v F.C. of T. (1937) 57 CLR

695 per Dixon J at p 712.

18. In the event of the outgoing being partially characterised as related to the gaining of assessable income, the sub-section requires apportionment between what should properly be regarded as incurred in earning assessable income and what should not.

19. The Tribunal turns to consider the evidence tendered by the applicant on the question of purpose and in particular the concept that it should pay its own way rather than rely upon the goodwill of the Club. Standing in the path of that submission is the fact that the Club has, since 1977, occupied premises owned by the applicant on a rent free basis. Surely, a re-arrangement for the purpose given would have rectified that position. In the opinion of the Tribunal, the gratuitous provision of significant accommodation over such a lengthy period makes a nonsense of the so-called quest for financial independence. Having rejected that evidence, the picture that remains is of a past history of donations from the applicant to the Club; the adoption of the chartered accountant's tax driven suggestion that the practice of making donations be replaced by the payment of a management fee; the resolution to make the arrangement retrospective to the 1 July 1986; the backdating of the agreement; and the non-compliance with the provision that the charge of $400 per week be paid weekly. When those aspects are taken in conjunction, it seems at least arguable that the circumstances of the expenditure do not give any part of it the complexion of money laid out in the furtherance of a purpose of gaining assessable income. However that argument overlooks the respondent's acceptance that the Club did render some service to the applicant and that, on balance, there existed a sufficiently sound commercial basis for entering into the agreement. The task of the Tribunal is to determine whether any amount in excess of those previously allowed by the respondent qualify for deduction in terms of the sub-section.

20. The kind of duties allegedly rendered to the applicant will be considered under the groupings of those provided by the directors of the applicant, then those by individual members of the Club and finally those by the office administrator. But first let us dispose of the submission that the Club acted for and on behalf of the applicant in connection with the possible acquisition of its shares by the Club and that, accordingly, the time so spent should be reflected in the annual charge of $20,800. It is recalled that the disagreement was between two factions within the membership of the Club and that the action taken by the committee of the Club, as well as the office manager, were directed to the restoration of goodwill between their members. Notwithstanding that the issued shares of the applicant were central to the dispute, it seems to us that the applicant was no more than a bystander to the events that developed. The Tribunal fails to perceive any reasons as to how or why the time spent in resolving this dispute between the members of the Club should be a charge against the applicant. In any event expenditure incurred in the devising of a plan to acquire the remaining shares in a company is a classical example of capital expenditure. Expenditure incurred in the carrying out of that plan, irrespective of success or failure, is similarly regarded. Such outgoings are not only once and for all payments, but they are made with a view of bringing into existence an advantage for the enduring benefit of the Club and its members. The expenditure relates to the structure and organisation of the Club rather than the processes it applies in the derivation of assessable income.

21. It is accepted that the directors of the applicant were active in the conduct of its business and that no charge was made for the services so rendered. However the Tribunal is not able to comprehend how the provision of those services was in any meaningful way related to the Club nor why the Club should be entitled to charge for those services. In performing those duties, the directors were doing no more than what was required of them in their appointed positions. In the calculation of the management fee the work undertaken by the directors on behalf of the applicant has no role to play and should be excluded.

22. The Tribunal acknowledges that certain Club members did from time to time undertake various tasks pertaining to the repair and improvement of the applicant's property and that this was particularly so upon the purchase of the replacement property in 1984. It is also accepted that those works were carried out on a no charge basis to either the applicant or the Club. The Tribunal is left to ponder on what, if any, tasks were performed during the years in question and whether those works related to repair or improvement, i.e. whether any part thereof was of a capital nature. In the absence of any prior agreement, and none was presented, the Tribunal is at a loss to understand how, in all the circumstances, the Club is able to charge for services rendered by volunteers. Once more the Tribunal concludes that those works should be excluded from the management fee calculation.

23. The respondent recognises that the office administrator was an employee of the Club and in that role she rendered service to both the Club and applicant. For that reason he accepts that there existed a commercial reason for the agreement. The question of what is a reasonable charge for the service rendered by her to the applicant is one of judgment. Is it to be an amount based on the formula of 50% of salary as adopted by the respondent or some greater figure? The only evidence before the Tribunal is that, inclusive of the time devoted to the problems of share acquisition, the biggest part of her time was spent on the applicant's affairs. If that additional time is excluded, as it must for reasons enunciated in paragraph 20, the remaining percentage becomes even more obscure. In the view of the Tribunal, the applicant has failed to establish that the amounts already allowed by the respondent fall short of what is commercially realistic and on that basis the decision on the objection will be affirmed.

24. The concept that 'it is not for the court or the Commissioner to say how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income, but only how much he has spent' was relied upon at the hearing and although not much emphasis was put upon it, the Tribunal feels obligated to make some comment. This principle finds its genesis in the case of Ronpibon Tin NL (supra), and is expressed in very general terms. More recent decisions of the Federal Court - see for example Ure (supra), and F.C. of T. v Phillips 78 ATC 4361 and the High Court Fletcher v F.C. of T. 91 ATC 4957, indicate that in some circumstances the Commissioner is not limited in the manner suggested. The Tribunal is satisfied and so finds that the applicant and the Club were not at arm's length and the outgoing in question is not capable of being characterised as being wholly relevant to the production of assessable income. In that situation, we find that both the respondent and this Tribunal are justified in looking to the indirect objects and advantages which the applicant sought in making the outgoings.

25. Section 223, as it then appeared in the Act, provides for the imposition of additional tax of double the amount of the tax avoided where, inter alia, a taxpayer omits from a statement any matter without which the statement is misleading in a material particular. It was not disputed that an omission had occurred and that this section has application. Section 227 bestows upon the Commissioner a discretion to remit all or part of this additional tax. As previously stated the respondent in this matter has assessed the penalty on a combination of 14.2% for the time factor and 25% for culpability which, according to his representative, represents an average rate of 30.1%. The question for determination is whether the remission so made should be further increased. After considering all the circumstances, the Tribunal concludes that the amounts claimed were knowingly excessive and were not the result of either mistake or misunderstanding. The Tribunal sees no reason to increase the rate of remission.

26. For the reasons stated above, the Tribunal affirms the decision on the objections.
