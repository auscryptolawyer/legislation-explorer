Re Jeremy Ross Wood and Commissioner of Superannuation [1989] AATA 3; 4864 Superannuation 9 AAR 528 (10 January 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: JEREMY ROSS WOOD

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A88/69

AAT No. 4864

Superannuation

9 AAR 528

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd(1) (Deputy President), Dr D.B. Travers(1) (Member) and

N.J. Attwood(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - whether employee ceased to be eligible employee - meaning of "immediately"

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.3(3), 17(2), 154

Customs Act 1901, s.273GA

Re Rattray and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 2 ALD 979

Re Rowe and Commissioner for Superannuation Decision No.3870, 30 October 1987

Hobbs v London and South Western Railway Co. (1875) LR 10 QB 111

Kuswardana v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1981) 3 ALN N66

Director-General of Social Services v Hangan [1982] FCA 262; (1982) 5 ALN N4

Director-General of Social Services v Hales [1983] FCA 81; (1983) 47 ALR 281

Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 2 ALD 913

HEARING

CANBERRA

10:1:1989

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision of the respondent that pursuant to s.3(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 the applicant is deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee following his resignation from the Department of Defence on 25 July 1986.

DECISION

This application for review involves the same point as that in issue in Re Rattray and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 2 ALD 979 and in Re Rowe and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No.3870, 30 October 1987).

2. Section 3(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") provides:

"(3) Where a person ceases to be an eligible

employee and, immediately after so ceasing, again

becomes an eligible employee, he shall, for the

purposes of this Act, be deemed not to have so

ceased."

3. In Re Rattray it was held that a break of one day, in all the circumstances, satisfied the requirement of immediacy expressed in s.3(3). In Re Rowe it was held that a break in the period in which the applicant was an eligible employee between 31 December and the succeeding 2 August did not satisfy the requirement of immediacy. Although the unsuccessful contentions in each case had been thought worthy of placing before this Tribunal, looking back on those cases reminds us of what was said by Blackburn J in Hobbs v London and South Western Railway Co. (1875) LR 10 QB 111 at 121: "... it is something like having to draw a line between night and day; there is a great duration of twilight when it is neither night nor day; but ... though you cannot draw the precise line, you can say on which side of the line the case is...".

4. In the present case the issue arises in a slightly more acute form than in the two decisions mentioned. Yet in the result we are of opinion that the facts before us are not in a twilight zone. The applicant, whose skills are in computing, became an eligible employee on 21 February 1975 on his permanent appointment as a clerk with the Department of Defence. He had in fact commenced as a clerk with the Department in 1974. He said that he was made permanent on completion of a trainee computer operator course. The applicant subsequently became dissatisfied over certain matters and contemplated a change of employment. He said that he had considered going out on his own as a consultant in the area of computing. He then however saw an advertisement for a position with the then ACT Electricity Authority ("ACTEA"). The chronology of events then continued, as we find, as follows:

12 June 1986: Applicant applied for a position with ACTEA as

a computer programmer.

8 July 1986: Applicant interviewed by ACTEA for the

position. He was told that ACTEA were

desperate for computer service officers

("CSOS"). He said however that he thought

that he had not done well at the interview.

9 or 10 July

1986: Applicant informed by ACTEA that his selection

as a CSO 2 had been approved. Applicant

sought appointment at a higher level of

increment than had been offered, and indicated

that he intended to resign and claim refund of

superannuation contributions. He was advised

that this might not be acceptable.

11 July 1986: Applicant told that his request for higher

salary had been approved.

14 July 1986: Applicant applied for registration of a

business name.

15 July 1986: Applicant tendered to the Department of Defence

in writing his resignation from the Australian

Public Service, effective from 25 July. In it

he referred to "an attempt at freelance

consulting."

16 July 1986: Applicant completed an application for refund

of his accumulated contributions to the

Superannuation Fund.

Applicant successfully underwent pre-employment

medical examination (necessary because of

applicant's expressed intention to resign).

Applicant was informed by examining

Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") that he

had passed his medical.

Between 16 and Somewhere between these dates 4 August was

25 July 1986: agreed as applicant's starting date with ACTEA,

and applicant expressed no reservations about

starting on that date.

25 July 1986: Applicant's resignation took effect.

26, 27 and Applicant advertised in Canberra Times his

28 July 1986: services as a computer consultant. Neither

his name nor any business name was included in

the advertisement.

30 July 1986: Formal written offer of employment sent to

applicant.

4 August 1986: Applicant commenced employment with ACTEA.

21 August 1986: Processing of repayment of superannuation

contributions completed.

ACTEA commenced regular deductions of

superannuation contributions, with one extra

deduction being made to reflect the date at

which he had commenced.

26 August 1986: Certificate of Business Name "Wood 'n' System"

issued.

26 August 1986: Refund cheque issued by Department of Finance.

5. The decision under review was expressed to be a decision that "pursuant to sub-section 3(3) of the Act, (the applicant) is deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee following his resignation from the Department of Defence on 25 July 1986". The character of this decision has given the Tribunal no little concern as to whether it is indeed a reviewable decision. It has no ultimate consequence unless it be a decision that the applicant either is, or alternatively is not, liable to refund the amount of accumulated contributions which were refunded, namely $14,465.30. If the Tribunal should find against the applicant, and he were not to accept that decision, the Commissioner would be obliged, if the matter were to be pursued, to sue in a court of competent jurisdiction. Such court would not be bound by the Tribunal's decision. On this footing, would not the Tribunal's decision be advisory only?

6. The parties invited the Tribunal to review the decision notwithstanding these difficulties, but it is of course clear that the Tribunal cannot assume jurisdiction by consent: see Kuswardana v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1981) 3 ALN N66, an extremely important decision which, unfortunately, was not fully reported. What then is the jurisdiction of the Tribunal under the Act? Section 154 of the Act provides:

"154.(1) In this section -

'decision' has the same meaning as in the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975;

'reviewable decision' means a decision of the

Commissioner, or a delegate of the

Commissioner, under this Act, under the

superseded Act or under the regulations

made under either of those Acts, and

includes a decision of the Superannuation

Board, or a delegate of the Superannuation

Board (other than a decision under section

141 of the superseded Act);

'Superannuation Board' means the

Superannuation Board established by the

superseded Act.

(2) A person affected by a reviewable decision

who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by

notice in writing given to the Commissioner within

the period of 30 days after the day on which the

decision first comes to the notice of the person,

or within such further period as the Commissioner

allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the

decision.

(3) There shall be set out in the request the

reasons for making the request.

(4) Upon receipt of the request, the

Commissioner shall reconsider the decision and may

confirm or revoke the decision or vary the decision

in such manner as he thinks fit.

(5) Where the Commissioner confirms, revokes

or varies a decision, he shall, by notice in

writing served on the person who made the request,

inform the person of the result of his

reconsideration of the decision and his reasons for

confirming, revoking or varying the decision, as

the case may be.

(6) Applications may be made to the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of

reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or

varied under sub-section (4) and for review of

decisions of the Superannuation Board, or delegates

of the Superannuation Board, made under section 141

of the superseded Act.

(7) ..."

7. The basic review provision under this legislation is thus quite general. It does not, like some other review provisions, refer to very specific decision-making functions: see for instance s.273GA of the Customs Act 1901. It is rather described as "a decision of the Commissioner ... under this Act ...,". The Commissioner, by s.17(2) of the Act, has "... general administration of this Act...". He thus has a multifarious decision-making function. We would not care to estimate how many areas of specific decision-making function are open to him under the Act. One such however must be to decide, in a case such as this, and in other contexts that may be imagined, whether the requirements of s.3(3) of the Act have been satisfied or otherwise. In the present case, no question of recovery could arise unless and until the Commissioner had made a decision about the applicability of s.3(3). If a decision in favour of the applicant should be made, that would be the end of the matter. If it were adverse to the applicant there would then have to be a decision by the Commissioner whether or not to sue for recovery of the contributions formerly refunded. Consideration would then have to be given to other factors, such as whether the rules as to recoverability of monies paid by mistake were likely to be satisfied. A decision on this point would have to be taken quite separately. It will be seen that, so analysed, the case for a conclusion that the Tribunal has jurisdiction, or better "power", to deal with the matter, is a good deal stronger than it was in Director-General of Social Services v Hangan [1982] FCA 262; (1982) 5 ALN N4 and in Director-General of Social Services v Hales [1983] FCA 81; (1983) 47 ALR 281. We therefore proceed on the footing that the Tribunal has power to review the decision taken by the Commissioner in this case.

8. We have already referred to Re Rattray and Re Rowe. We now set out again the passage from the judgment of Lockhart J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 2 ALD 913 at 930 which was cited in Re Rattray and in Re Rowe:

"Mr Phillips QC contended .... that 'immediately'

does not necessarily mean instanter; but rather has

a meaning which may vary from case to case,

sometimes allowing a gap in time of hours, days or

weeks. In sub-s (3) it has the meaning of 'within

a reasonably short space of time' in all the

circumstances. In support of his contention, Mr

Phillips QC referred to the definition of the words

'immediately' and 'forthwith' in Stroud's Judicial

Dictionary and Butterworth's Words and Phrases

Legally Defined; Permanent Trustee Co of New South

Wales Ltd v d'Apice [1968] HCA 29; (1968) ALR 437; 118 CLR 105,

and Dorsman v Nichol (1978) 20 ALR 231.

Mr Phillips QC contended that sub-s (3) is a

deeming provision merely used for more abundant

caution to put beyond doubt a particular

construction that otherwise might be uncertain.

He relied on St Aubyn v Attorney-General (1952) AC

15, per Lord Radcliffe at 53 and Hunter Douglas

Australia Pty Ltd v Perma Blinds [1970] HCA 63; (1969) ALR 676;

[1970] HCA 63; 122 CLR 49, per Windeyer J. at 65-7.

A deeming provision may be included in an

interpretation section in a Statute for the purpose

of extending the meaning of a defined term to

something it would not mean in ordinary parlance;

see St Aubyn v Attorney-General, supra, and Hunter

Douglas Australia Pty Ltd v Perma Blinds, supra, at

the pages mentioned earlier. In my opinion this

is the purpose of sub-s (3). It is intended to

ensure that a person does not lose his status as an

eligible employee merely because he ceases to be

one, and later becomes one again, provided the gap

in time is not unreasonably large. It is designed

to cover all cases where a person has a particular

position or holds a particular office and ceases to

do so, but 'immediately' thereafter takes another

position or office, in each case being a person who

satisfies the statutory denotation of a 'permanent

employee'. It matters not whether the interval of

time is a second, a day, or a week, provided the

person again becomes an eligible employee

'immediately' after ceasing to be one."

9. In our opinion the present applicant, having ceased to be an eligible employee, again became such "immediately" after so ceasing. We have taken into account the following considerations in forming that opinion and in finding as follows:

(a) The break between the old and new employment

occupied no more than one working week;

(b) the applicant never, as we find, genuinely

contemplated going into full-time consultancy work

at that time, for

(i) he had made the barest kind of preparation

therefor,

(ii) his advertising thereof was little more than

vestigial;

(iii) he had a wife and young family (one child and

one shortly to be born) to support, with a

house subject to a mortgage;

(c) while the applicant had conducted one isolated

previous consultancy, the question of the proposed

consultancy was at best an idea with which he was

toying, and was at worst part of a scheme to obtain

a refund of contributions by seeming to have

created a break in employment;

(d) the applicant at all times intended to go straight

to his new employment with ACTEA once he knew that

the appointment was his at the level of salary

increment which he sought. This was on 11 July

1986, before any of the steps in relation to the

"consultancy" were taken, and well before his

resignation from the Defence Department (tendered

on 15 July, effective from 25 July);

(e) the applicant in fact carried out no "consultancy"

work in the week in question;

(f) not only had the steps taken to secure appointment

with ACTEA been commenced before the applicant left

the Department of Defence, they had all been

completed save only for the formal written offer of

employment. In particular his starting date with

ACTEA had been agreed prior to leaving the

Department of Defence.

10. It should be noted that the applicant attained the age of 31 the day before his resignation from the Department of Defence took effect. He was thus able to rejoin the scheme, according to the plan which he had developed, at an age which would offer him a period of prospective service long enough to afford him the full benefits which that scheme grants. We might also add that the applicant has obtained one advantage. We were informed that repayment would involve simply repayment of the amount of the refund without any form of penalty or additional interest, and that the respondent would be prepared to consider a reasonable offer to repay by instalments. In addition, despite what we have said above about prospective service, the applicant's superannuation benefits would be calculated on the basis of his original eligibility date of 21 February 1975.

11. In sum, we affirm that the assessment of "immediacy" in the context proceeds not upon the basis of a technical break of service, and certainly not on some rigid concept of any specified number of days or weeks being enough to prevent the operation of s.3(3). Such would be a "legalistic" concept in the pejorative sense of the word. We have rather proceeded upon a consideration of the quality of the events which occurred, looked at as a whole. Having done so the case does not fall within the twilight zone , and we are completely satisfied that the decision under review should be affirmed.
