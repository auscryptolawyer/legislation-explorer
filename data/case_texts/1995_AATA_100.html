ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review to extend the time allowed for acceptance of patent application number 42052/89 from 14 August, 1993 to 14 March, 1994, both dates inclusive.

DECISION

S A FORGIE, H M PAVLIN AND E K CHRISTIE On 13 January, 1994, a delegate of the Commissioner of Patents ("the Commissioner") decided to grant to Mr Ron Thomas and Mr Allan Garnham an extension of time under section 223(1) of the Patents Act 1990 ("the Act"). Following that decision, the applicant, Jiejing Pty Ltd ("Jiejing") lodged on 10 February, 1994 an application for review of the Commissioner's decision. Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham were joined as parties by order of the Tribunal dated 14 April, 1994.

2. At the hearing, Mr Anderson, an office holder in Jiejing represented it, Mr Hanson, Senior Government Lawyer, represented the Commissioner and Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham represented themselves. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") were admitted in evidence ("the T documents") together with a document headed "Chronology of Relevant Events" and a list of relevant applications that had been made. No oral evidence was given. An order was made by Senior Member Beddoe on 5 July, 1994 directing that, until further order, publication of the T documents be "prohibited to all persons except the Tribunal and the parties".

3. At the hearing, Mr Hanson made a number of oral submissions as did Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham. Mr Hanson also relied on written submissions. Mr Anderson relied upon written submissions lodged on behalf of Jiejing by Messrs Cullen and Co, Patent and Trade Mark Attorneys ("Cullens"), on the day before the hearing. As none of the other parties had been give a copy of Jiejing's submissions before the hearing, we allowed them time to make additional written submissions. Both Mr Hanson and Mr Thomas made written submissions in response and the last of those submissions was received on 23 March, 1995.

DECISION UNDER REVIEW

4. The delegate who made the decision was Mr D. Herald, Assistant Commissioner of Patents. Mr Herald decided, pursuant to sub-section 223(1) of the Act, to extend the time for acceptance of patent application number 42052/89 from 14 August, 1993 to 30 August, 1993 and thereafter to the date of his decision i.e. 13 January, 1994. He also decided that, as a result of the decision of Mr Justice Cooper in the matter of Petty Patent 616154 (divided from the application in these proceedings), Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham were entitled to a further extension of two months from the date of his decision in which to address the matters arising from that decision. He made that decision under either paragraph 223(2)(b) or sub-section 223(1) of the Act. He remitted the application to the examiner to issue forthwith an examination report raising objections that arose pursuant to the decision of Mr Justice Cooper. Mr Herald decided, finally, that there was no need for Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham to pursue their application which sought an extension of time under sub-section 223(2) and which was filed on 4 January, 1994 (T documents, pages 22-23).

LEGISLATION

5. The provisions relating to extensions of time are found in section 223 of the Act. Sub-sections 223(7) to (10) deal with the consequences of an extension's having been made. In this case, therefore, only sub-sections 223(1) to (6) and sub-section (11) are relevant and they provide:

"(1) Where, because of an error or omission by the Commissioner, a

Deputy Commissioner or an employee, a relevant act that is required to

be done within a certain time is not, or cannot be, done within that

time, the Commissioner must extend the time for doing the act.

(2) Where, because of:

(a) an error or omission by the person concerned or by his or her

agent or attorney; or

(b) circumstances beyond the control of the person concerned;

a relevant act that is required to be done within a certain time is

not, or cannot be, done within that time, the Commissioner may, on

application made by the person concerned in accordance with the

regulations, extend the time for doing the act.

(3) The time allowed for doing a relevant act may be extended, whether

before or after that time has expired.

(4) Where an application is made for an extension of time for more

than 3 months, the Commissioner must advertise the application in the

Official Journal.

(5) An application cannot be made under subsection (2) for an

extension of time for more than 3 months in respect of an act or step

required to be done or taken for the purposes of Division 2 of Part 3

of Chapter 6.

(6) A person may, as prescribed, oppose the granting of the

application.

...

(11) In this section:

"relevant act" means an action (other than a prescribed action) in

relation to a patent, a patent application, or any proceedings under

this Act (other than court proceedings), and includes the making of a

Convention application within the time allowed for making such

applications."

6. Sub-regulation 22.11(3) of the Patents Regulations ("the Regulations") provides that:

"For the purposes of the definition of 'relevant act' in

sub-section 223(11) of the Act, each of the following actions is

prescribed:

(a) an action or step prescribed in Chapter 5 ('opposition'), not

being an action or step taken under regulation 5.3 ('filing of

opposition') or paragraph 5.4(1)(a) ('filing of statement');

(b) paying a fee in a period that is extended because of

subregulation 13.3(3) ('prescribed period: continuation fees') or

subregulation 13.6(4) ('time for payment of renewal fee for standard

patent')."

BACKGROUND

7. Many of the matters leading to the current proceedings were not in dispute among the parties. The real area of disagreement among them was the significance which those matters should be given. We will return to that but, in light of the general lack of disagreement and on the basis of the written material we have been given, we will set out the findings of fact which we have made.

8. On 5 September, 1988, Mr Thomas and Mr Helmut Stohr filed an application, number PJ 0247, together with a provisional specification for a standard patent. The provisional specification related to an invention with respect to the storing and retrieval from a computer of Chinese characters to perform word processing. The invention was to be applied to other languages and so the provisional specification was drafted in wide terms.

9. Later, on 10 February, 1989, Mr Thomas and Mr Stohr applied for a petty patent based on the original provisional application. A petty patent number 583008 with a priority date of 10 February, 1989 was issued to them on 13 June, 1989.

10. Mr Thomas and Mr Stohr then filed a Patent Cooperation Treaty application ("PCT application"), number PCT/AU89/00379. It was filed on 5 September, 1989. The PCT application used the provisional specification filed with the original application number PJ 0247 as the basis for the claims defining the invention. As the PCT application had been lodged within 12 months of that original application, the priority date was, if all other criteria were met, the date of filing of that original application i.e. 5 September, 1988. The PCT application was published on 2 April, 1990.

11. Also on 5 September, 1989, Mr Thomas and Mr Stohr lodged a national phase application number 42052/89. That was derived from the PCT application and so claimed a priority date of 5 September, 1988.

12. Jiejing commenced the distribution of its products in December, 1989. This became relevant a little later when CCOM and Mr Thomas brought infringement proceedings in the Federal Court (see paragraphs 18-23 below).

13. Petty patent number 583008 continued in force for a year. That term could be extended provided the procedures in section 68B of the Patents Act 1952 ("the 1952 Act") were followed and provided the petty patent was not invalid. Indeed, provided he was not satisfied that there was any ground on which the petty patent was invalid under sub-section 100(1), the Commissioner was obliged to extend the time in those circumstances. It was open to third parties to give notice that they believed the petty patent was invalid. Such a notice had to be lodged within the prescribed time. Within that time, and on 15 December, 1989, Mr Yates and Dr Chappell informed the Commissioner that they believed that petty patent number 583008 was invalid. They asserted a number of grounds set out in sub-section 100(1) of the 1952 Act.

14. The issue of whether or not petty patent number 583008 should be extended went to a hearing before a delegate of the Commissioner and he refused to extend its term. The delegate decided that the claim had been anticipated by two British specifications.

15. Rather than lodge an appeal in relation to the delegate's decision, Mr Thomas and Mr Stohr decided to proceed with the division of a further petty patent application and to maintain a priority based on the original standard patent application filed on 5 September, 1988.

16. This they did on 19 March, 1991. Various submissions, hearings and amendments to the specification and claim occurred. Before the matter was finalised, an application was made to the Commissioner to transfer Mr Stohr's interest in the application to Mr Garnham. A petty patent number 616154 was then issued to Mr Thomas and to Mr Garnham on 26 August, 1991.

17. As grantees or proprietors of the petty patent number 616154, Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham issued a licence to CCOM Pty Ltd ("CCOM") and gave it the right to exploit the patented invention throughout the patent area to the exclusion of the patentee and all other persons.

18. CCOM and Mr Thomas, who was one of its directors, applied in the Federal Court for relief for alleged infringement of petty patent 616154 for an invention entitled "Symbol Definition Apparatus". The claimed invention was for computer processing apparatus for assembling text in Chinese language characters. The respondents to the application were Jiejing, Paravet Instruments ("Paravet"), Mr Garnham, Mr Yates and Dr Chappell.

19. Mr Garnham was a director and shareholder of CCOM and had provided funds to CCOM and Mr Thomas to advance their litigation. He stood to gain financially if they succeeded. Mr Garnham was also, together with Mr Thomas, one of the patentees. As such, he had to be joined as a defendant in infringement proceedings if he were not a plaintiff (see sub-section 120(1) of the Act). Mr Yates was a director of Jiejing and of Paravet and Dr Chappell was also a director of Jiejing.

20. CCOM and Mr Thomas alleged that the respondents had infringed the petty patent by supplying products styled "Jiejing Documents Processor" and "Jiejing Character Word Processor". These products comprised computer programs with written instructions for the use of the programs in combination with IBM, or IBM compatible, computers.

21. Jiejing, Paravet, Mr Yates and Dr Chappell denied that they were in breach of the petty patent and, in any event, argued that the petty patent was not valid. Dr Chappell cross claimed against CCOM and Mr Thomas on the basis of an alleged infringement of the copyright in certain literary works. Those works were a compilation of Chinese characters together with the stroke categories relevant to each character. He claimed to be their author and the owner of the copyright in them.

22. Mr Justice Cooper found that the petty patent was invalid because it did not claim a manner of manufacture within the meaning of section 6 of the Statute of Monopolies 1623. Even if it had been valid, it would not have been infringed by the conduct complained of for he found that its priority date was 22 July, 1991 rather than 5 September, 1988 as claimed. The conduct complained of occurred in December, 1989 and so at some time before the priority date Cooper J determined.

23. On appeal, the Full Court of the Federal Court found that the petty patent was invalid but on the ground of fair basing rather than those relied upon by Cooper J. It upheld his finding that there had been no infringement. In relation to fair basing, the Federal Court indicated that it may be open to CCOM and Mr Thomas to apply for an amendment of the petty patent to narrow the claim in order to bring it into a form which would be fairly based on the matter disclosed in the provisional specification.

24. CCOM and Mr Thomas lodged an application for special leave to appeal to the High Court. This application was refused on 10 March, 1995.

EVIDENCE

Summary of processes leading to current proceedings

25. As we have said, patent application number 42052/89 was derived from PCT application number PCT/AU89/00379. Both were lodged on 5 September, 1989. The Commissioner examined the patent request and specification relating to application number 42052/89 and issued a report of his first examination on 14 June, 1991. An objection was raised by the Commissioner and he advised the applicants, Mr Thomas and Mr Stohr, that they had 21 months from the date of his report in which to overcome his objection. The final date for acceptance was, therefore, 14 March, 1993.

26. On 27 April, 1993, Mr Thomas applied, on behalf of Mr Garnham and himself, for an extension of time of three months from 14 March, 1993 to 14 June, 1993 in respect of application number 42052/89. Mr Thomas swore a statutory declaration but the essence of his reasons are in the following extract from his application:

"- A BREAKDOWN IN COMMUNICATIONS BETWEEN THE APPLICANTS AND OUR PATENT

ATTORNEY LED TO OUR MISTAKEN BELIEF THAT TIME TO RESPOND TO THE

EXAMINATION HAD NOT LAPSED.

ADDITIONALLY.

- BECAUSE OF FEDERAL COURT ACTION PERTAINING TO
INFRINGEMENT OF OUR

RELATED PETTY PATENT 616154 I WRONGLY ASSUMED THAT THE 'CLOCK HAD

STOPPED' ON ALL OUTSTANDING MATTERS." (T documents, page 246)

Mr Thomas gave as his address for service "P.O. Box 194, Kallangur, QLD 4503".

27. On 18 May, 1993, one of the Commissioner's officers wrote to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham and advised them that the time in which to gain acceptance had been extended to 14 June, 1993. The extension would be advertised in the Official Journal of Patents dated 3 June, 1993. The letter went on to say that:

"... further extensions of time will not be likely to receive

favourable consideration, whether or not the court issues its decision

before that time." (T documents, page 250)

28. Mr Basil John Clift deposed that Mr Thomas lodged the response to the Commissioner's objections on 11 June, 1993. He did so at the Commissioner's Brisbane office by delivering them personally over the counter (T documents, page 391). Mr Robert William James Finzi, an Examiner of Patents responsible for examining the application, reviewed the application on 16 June, 1993. At that time, Mr Finzi said, he became aware that an extension of time had been granted. He then issued a second adverse report specifying that certain matters were still outstanding regarding the application. That report was sent to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham at their Kallangur post office box on 25 June, 1993 and gave them until 16 June, 1993 to respond i.e. they were given until a date nine days earlier than the date of the report. (T documents, pages 253-4 and 393-394)

29. In his report, Mr Finzi noted that he had found that the

"... application is not in order for acceptance because:

4 You have not filed a notice stating the entitlement of the nominated

person to the grant of a patent.

5 You have not provided a Statement of Proposed Amendments

substituting the specification of 9 June 1993 for the specification on

file.

6 Nevertheless, your amendment would not be allowable for a number of

reasons such as:

(i) there is no disclosure in the original specification of the

details claimed in substitute claims 23-27;

(ii) claims 17 and 18 appear to be incorrectly appended;

(iii) claims 9 and 12 appear to claim matter not disclosed in the

original specification.

7 Your application is in a state of lapse." (T documents, page 253)

30. Mr Thomas stated in his statutory declaration of 12 October, 1993 that he telephoned the Commissioner's Canberra office to check the progress of the material he had submitted in response to the examiner's report. He was told that a letter dated 25 June, 1993 had been posted to him at the Post Office at Kallangur. He stated that the box was then checked and no letter had been received. On 2 July, 1993, he telephoned the Kallangur Post Office to see if the letter had arrived. Further checks up until 5 July, 1993 showed that the letter still had not arrived. Mr Thomas said that he was then told by the staff at the Kallangur Post Office on 5 July, 1993 that he should check the Brendale mail exchange to see if the letter had been mis-sorted. As a consequence, Mr Thomas contacted the Brendale mail exchange and gave them details of the missing letter. On 8 July, 1993, Mr Thomas received a telephone call from the Post Office and was told that the letter had been re-directed by the sorter to the Lawnton address. The letter was found at the Lawnton address, opened and sent by facsimile to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham on 8 July, 1993 (T documents, pages 326-329).

31. Mr Finzi stated that Mr Thomas was in "constant communication with him by telephone" about the application during the period 30 June, 1993 to 8 July, 1994 (T documents, page 393). He said that he told Mr Thomas on "numerous occasions" that he should seek professional advice about the application. Mr Thomas told him that he did not have the funds to do so. In response to Mr Thomas's enquiries about an extension of time, Mr Finzi told him that he should send a request accompanied by a statutory declaration setting out the relevant events. Mr Finzi also said to him that it was normal for applications for extension to be made on a month by month basis. Due to what he described as an error on his part, Mr Thomas made an application for an extension until 12 July, 1994 (T documents, page 394).

32. On 9 July, 1993, Mr Finzi received a facsimile transmission from Mr Thomas. In that facsimile, Mr Thomas responded to his second objections report and included a Statement of Proposed Amendments. Mr Finzi had the file delivered to him on that day or shortly thereafter. Upon its being delivered, he noted that no extension of time had been granted. Consequently, he sent the file to the Patents subregistry to advise Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham that the application had lapsed. At that stage, Mr Finzi said, he noted that the Statement of Proposed Amendments did not comply with regulation 10.1(1) of the Regulations (T documents, page 394).

33. On 9 July, 1993, Mr Thomas lodged an application for an extension of time at the Commissioner's Brisbane office. He originally sought an extension from 16 June, 1993 of one month to 16 July, 1993. He later amended his application to two months to conclude on 16 August, 1993 (T documents, page 260). The address for service he gave at the foot of the application was "P.O. Box 463, Ferny Hills, Q. 4055" (T documents, page 257) He gave as his grounds for seeking the extension the following:

"THE CURRENT EXAMINING OFFICER'S REPORT (2ND) HAS ONLY JUST BEEN

RECEIVED BY ME DUE TO SOME PROBLEMS WITH THE GEOGRAPHY OF OUR

SITUATION AND IN FACT THE REPORT WAS RECEIVED MUCH SOONER THAN

EXPECTED. WE UNDERSTAND THAT THE EXAMINING OFFICER DID NOT RECEIVE

OUR SUBMISSION ON HIS DESK UNTIL TWO DAYS AFTER THE APPLICATION HAD

LAPSED. NO FURTHER AMENDMENTS ARE BEING REQUESTED EXCEPT FOR CORRECT

OF A TYPOGRAPHICAL ERROR." (T documents, page 257)

34. The application was accompanied by a letter dated 8 July, 1993. At the head of that letter appear two addresses: the post office box at Kallangur we have already set out and an address of "717B Gympie Road, Qld." (T documents, page 256). The letter and the application for extension of time were also accompanied by a Notice of Entitlement which the second examination report had noted had not been attended to (see T documents, page 255 and also T documents, page 253 and paragraph 29 above).

35. On 13 July, 1993, the original of the facsimiled response to the second objections report was received in the Commissioner's office. It was not sent to Mr Finzi as the application had lapsed. (T documents, page 258)

36. Mr Finzi took the case to Ms Sharyn Moore on 16 July, 1993 and told her that insufficient time had been allowed for him to examine the proposed amendments.

37. Ms Moore tried to contact Mr Thomas but was unable to do so. She left a message on his answering machine advising him that he would require a further two month extension from 14 June, 1993. She then wrote to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham on 21 July, 1993 as follows:

"I wish to advise you the examiner cannot consider your statement of

proposed amendments due to the fact that the final date for acceptance

was 14 June 1993.

I note an application for an extension of time for 1 month to gain

acceptance was filed but no fees accompanied such request. The fees

of $85 for the extension application and $55 for the time to be

extended. (The time sought should be from 14 June 1993 to 14 July

1993)." (T documents, page 258)

38. Mr Thomas telephoned Ms Moore on 22 July, 1993 to query the dates of the extension as he had been told the date was 16 June, 1993 due to the late arrival of the facsimiled proposed amendments. She checked that with Mr Finzi and, upon being told that it was correct, telephoned Mr Thomas and told him so. She advised him of the fees and said that she would confirm by facsimile message. Mr Thomas then telephoned again to say that he would send in the extra money and to ask that the date of the extension be amended to 16 August, 1993. (T documents, page 265)

39. As she had promised, Ms Moore sent a facsimile message to Mr Thomas. That message read:

"I have spoken to the examiner Mr Finzi who advises me that you will

need to seek extension of time for two months from 16 June 1993 to

16 August 1993 to allow the delegate to consider the circumstances

surrounding the failure to be accepted by the due date. Mr Finzi

corrected my impressions in that you were allowed an extra 5 days due

to the late arrival of the amendments in the Examination Branch. If

this extra extension is not required you will receive a refund of the

overpayment.

I apologise for misinforming you of the situation." (T documents,

page 259)

40. Mr Thomas sent a cheque for the fees as he had been requested to do in the letter of 21 July, 1993. That cheque was marked as received on 26 July, 1993. At the same time he asked that their application be amended to read 16 June, 1993 to 16 August, 1993 (T documents, page 260 and see paragraph 33 above).

41. On 5 August, 1993, the Assistant Commissioner of Patents noted the following matters in relation to the application at that stage:

"1. The application lapsed on 14 June 1993.

2. A report was issued on 25 June, after the application had lapsed

and indicated that the application was in a state of lapse.

3. A s223 application was made which was not accompanied by the

requested statutory declaration and was not processed in the normal

way.

4. The applicant was informed that the period sought was insufficient

and that an extension of 2 months should be sought. The basis of this

is not known.

5. Folio 32 is the applicant's most recent communication which seeks

to amend the s223 application.

6. The applicant has not been informed of the requirements of s223.

7. The office has actioned this case when it has lapsed."

(T documents, page 262)

42. Mr Allen Evans, the Supervising Examiner, and Ms Moore both wrote explanations confirming, in effect, what we have set out above. Ms Moore recorded that, she had failed to notice that a statutory declaration had not been filed (T documents, page 265).

43. On 16 August, 1993, the Assistant Commissioner considered the application for an extension of time. He concluded that the time could only be extended under sub-section 223(1) of the Act until 28 June, 1993. Mr Finzi was delegated to advise Mr Thomas of the decision orally and Ms Moore to advise him of it in writing.

44. Ms Moore wrote on 17 August, 1993 in the following terms:

"The delegate of the Commissioner advises as follows:

The time for acceptance - 14 June 1993 (after three months Extension

under Section 223)

The response to the 1st report filed in the Brisbane Sub-Office on

Friday 11 June 1993 reached Examination Branch on 16 June 1993.

The maximum possible delay justifiable under Section 223(1) here is to

14 June 1993 - ie 3 days

The examiner issued a report on 25 June 1993 indicating a response was

due on 16 June 1993.

Allowing for beneficial application of Section 223(1), an extension of

time past 28 June 1993 cannot be justified.

The applicant responded on 9 July 1993 with amendments and a

Section 223 request with reasons as:

1 Problems with geography of situation,

2 Examination Branch did not receive response till 2 days after the

due date.

Item no.2 is addressed as above - an extension past the 28 June 1993

cannot be justified.

Item no.1 - there is no justification provided for an extension. In

particular, the Examiners' report was sent to the address given on the

letter of 8 July 1993, and there is no evidence of any mishandling by

the post.

I am, therefore, of the view that an extension of time under

Section 223(1) and for (2) to any date past 28 June 1993 has not been

justified, and that this application has lapsed." (T documents,

page 269)

45. On the same day, Ms Moore wrote to Messrs Cullen and Co., Patent and Trade Mark Attorneys ("Cullens") to say that patent application 42052/89 in the name of Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham had lapsed (T documents, page 271). That letter was in response to Cullen's letter of 9 August, 1993 querying the status of the patent application (T documents, page 266).

46. Mr Thomas responded by a letter dated 17 August, 1993 in which he objected "in the strongest possible terms" to the decision and pointed to the following matters:

"- An extension of time was originally applied for on 25th April '93

when it was realised that the 'clock had not been stopped' due to the

court action as it had with the Petty Patent renewal. At the time I

was also told by an Officer of your Brisbane office that in any case

the application was 'in force to 5th September 1993.' Apparently I

have misunderstood the meaning of 'in force'.

- A reply from the Office dated the 18th May granted the requested

extension to 14 June '93.

- A response to the examiner's first report was prepared between the

9th and 11th of June '93. The response was lodged at the Brisbane

Office on the 11th June '93. I was under the impression that as long

as the reply was received by the 14th that it would continue to be

processed and still believed that the application was in force until

5th September 1993.

- A second report dated 25 June '93 was received by us on 8th July

'93. This report gave us until 16th June '93 to overcome the

objections. Although addressed correctly to P.O. Box 194 Kallangur

the Post Office delivered it to the Lawnton address which is only

visited every one or two weeks. Post Office staff can confirm the

enquiries and search for the missing letter. When this problem was

mentioned to one of your Officers he said to use the phrase 'problems

with the geography of the situation'.

- A response was immediately prepared and sent to the Office on

8th July '93. I was still under the impression that this was a

process continuing on from our granted extension.

- If Office policy would not allow justification of an extension of

time past 28 June '93 in this instance why was I sent two facsimiles

on the 22nd July '93 advising me that I would need to seek extension

of time for two months to 16th August '93 and requesting fees be paid

before any decision could be made?

- Apparently no decision was made regarding this extension of time

until the last day, which means that if it had been favourable it

would have been too late.

- The decision is all the harder to accept in view of the fact that as

a result of discussions with the examining Officer I believe all of

the claims are now in acceptable form." (T documents, pages 273-274)

47. On 20 August, 1993, Mr Thomas telephoned Mr Finzi and agreed to his re-writing his Statement of Proposed Amendments in an acceptable form (T documents, page 275). Mr Finzi later declared on 15 December, 1993 that it is common practice for minor formal matters to be dealt with in the office in that way. This is a service offered to both applicants and patent attorneys. The amendments on this occasion were such minor formal matters (T documents, page 395).

48. By a letter dated 22 August, 1993, Mr Thomas wrote on behalf of himself and Mr Garnham and offered to put the contents of his letter of 17 August, 1993 into the form of a statutory declaration. He also changed their address for service to his post office box at Ferny Hills.

49. Cullens wrote on 23 August, 1993 and asked to be advised should an application be made to restore the application (T documents, page 280).

CONSIDERATION

65. Several issues were raised by Jiejing in support of their application and the other parties directed their submissions to those issues. We will follow the same general format but will recast the issues in slightly different language.

What is the decision under review?

66. We have already set out the terms of the decision made by Mr Herald (see paragraph 4 above). Mr Herald granted certain extensions of time from and after 14 August, 1993. Jiejing have made submissions in relation to the period before 14 August, 1993. It has said, in summary, that the extension granted to 14 August, 1993 was not granted, should not have been granted or, if granted, was withdrawn. Its reasons for making that submission are:

. the reasons for granting the extension related to events which

occurred after 14 June, 1993 and did not relate to any failure to

gain acceptance by 14 June, 1993 due to an error or omission by any

relevant person. Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham simply left their

response too late to gain acceptance; and

. Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham did not request that extension of time.

67. Mr Hanson submitted that Jiejing did not make any objection to the validity of this extension at the hearing before Mr Herald, there has been no request for reasons in relation to that decision and there has been no application lodged to have that decision reviewed. Furthermore, the decision granting the extension to 14 August, 1993 has not been overturned or withdrawn.

68. What precisely is the decision under review? That question should be easily answered by saying that it is the decision of which Jiejing has sought review i.e. Mr Herald's decision of 13 January, 1994. We need, however, to explore the limits of that decision for it seems from the reasons it gave for its application that Jiejing, at least, has assumed that it deals with extensions of time relating to periods both before and after 14 August, 1993.

69. The whole matter is one of confusion and so we will start with trying to clarify some preliminary matters before we return to the scope of the decision under review. We are satisfied that a decision had been made on 2 September, 1993 by Dr Barker to extend the time within which to gain acceptance to 14 August, 1993. When Mr Murray summarised the history of the file on 10 September, 1993, he missed that extension for some reason. Looking at the original file numbering on the documents as they appear in the T documents, perhaps that reason was that the extension may have been misfiled for it does not appear before the letters of 2 September where we would logically expect it to be found. It is filed in between Mr Murray's letter and his minute of 10 September, 1993.

70. Does the fact that Mr Murray was not aware of the extension, mean that the decision to grant that extension has been revoked in some way? We observe at the outset that we cannot find any provision in the Act or Regulations giving the Commissioner the power to revoke or vary the decision which he makes on an application for extension of time. May we find that he has an implied power? An argument of this type was considered by Mr Justice Brennan in Re Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (New South Wales) (1978) 1 ALD 167 in relation to section 78 of the Customs Act 1901 ("the Customs Act"). Section 78 gave the Minister power to issue warehouse licences. A licence was duly issued by one of his delegates and, at a later time, a decision was made purporting to revoke the licence. No express power to revoke was given by section 78 and the question arose whether there was an implied power to revoke.

71. Brennan J considered sub-section 33(3) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 ("the Acts Interpretation Act") which provides:

"Where an Act confers a power to make, grant or issue any instrument

(including rules, regulations or by-laws) the power shall, unless the

contrary intention appears, be construed as including a power

exercisable in the like manner and subject to the like conditions (if

any) to repeal, rescind, revoke, amend, or vary any such instrument."

72. It was submitted that the licence was an "instrument" within the meaning of the Acts Interpretation Act and that the power to grant or issue an instrument of that kind under section 78 was, therefore, to be construed as including a power to revoke it. Brennan J decided that instruments were not limited to legislative instruments but could include administrative or executive instruments. The question to ask was whether the power given by section 78 was a power to make, grant or issue an instrument or could it be exercised without creating an instrument. Brennan J concluded that the power under section 78 was not required to be exercised in writing and contrasted that with other provisions in the Customs Act where writing was required. Therefore, the power in section 78 was not a power to make, grant or issue an instrument and so sub-section 33(3) of the Acts Interpretation Act could not be relied upon to imply a power to repeal, rescind, revoke, amend, or vary the exercise of the power.

73. When regard is had to section 223, the power is not to issue anything in writing that could be described as an instrument. All that is given is a power to extend, in certain circumstances, the time limits otherwise set for certain actions. While it would no doubt be desirable to record the decisions in writing, the recording is not the focus of the power. The focus of the power is the decision to extend the time. It follows that section 223 of the Act does not confer a power to make an instrument and so there is no power to repeal, rescind, revoke, amend or vary a decision made under it.

74. That is not an end of the matter, for there remains to be considered whether there has been a purported exercise of a power to revoke or vary Dr Barker's decision to extend the time to 14 August, 1993. Provided it has made an application to this Tribunal, Jiejing may seek review of a purported exercise of a power to revoke or vary Dr Barker's decision even though the Commissioner did not have power to make such a decision. This follows from the Lawlor case where Brennan J looked at the powers given by sub-section 43(1) of the AAT Act to the Tribunal in reviewing decisions, the powers given by sub-section 25(4) of the AAT Act to review those decisions "in respect of which application is made to under any enactment" and the relevant provisions conferring jurisdiction to review decisions under the Customs Act. He concluded:

"... it is clear that there is no impediment against giving

recognition and effect to a right of appeal to an administrative

tribunal from an administrative decision which is made beyond power.

A right to seek a review by this Tribunal of a decision made in the

intended exercise of a power specified in the Schedule or in an

enactment to which s25(1) refers is not defeated if that decision is

made beyond power. Where this Tribunal reviews a decision and for

that purpose draws upon those powers (which s43 confers upon it)

corresponding with the powers of the original decision-maker, it is

this Tribunal's decision which is the operative decision." (page 181)

75. Returning to the case with which we are concerned, it follows that the crucial question to ask is whether a decision has been made, or purported to have been made, under the Act even if it is without power. We are satisfied that, up to the date of the hearing before Mr Herald, there was no decision to revoke the decision made on 2 September, 1993. Mr Murray's minute of 10 September, 1993 is, we consider, only a record of the events he could glean from the file. It is apparent that he has not turned his mind to whether there should or should not be any extension of time granted or the variation of any previous extension. This is so despite the fact that Mr Murray has said in his minute that he has "reviewed this file" (T documents, page 287). Taken in the context of the minute and the surrounding events, we find that is all he did. He did not review the decision but only reviewed, in the sense of surveyed, the course of events in the file. The fact that Mr Murray's views were transmitted in writing to Cullens and to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham does not alter our conclusion in this regard. Indeed, they strengthen it for those letters are clearly only purporting to set out "the pertinent details of the application" and to state that the previous advice that the application had been accepted was incorrect (T documents, pages 288-289 and paragraph 54 above).

76. Despite the fact that the decision of 2 September, 1993 had not been revoked, it is understandable that Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham applied for an extension seeking an extension from 12 July, 1993 and so covering the period to 14 August, 1993 already covered by Dr Barker's decision (see paragraph 4 above). That letter was seen by Cullens who then advised that they objected to the "further extension of time requested" (T documents, page 304). It is apparent from their submissions that they objected to the extensions granted prior to 2 September, 1993 for they addressed them at some length. It is also apparent from the summary of Jiejing's submissions in Mr Herald's reasons.

77. Has Mr Herald purported to vary or revoke an earlier decision in addition to making a decision about matters after 14 August, 1993? If he has, then, even though he would not have power to do so, we may review his decision.

78. Mr Herald considered the events which had occurred both before and after 14 August, 1993. He said that he could not review Dr Barker's decision of 30 August, 1993 to grant an extension until 12 July, 1993 and cited The Queen v Smith and Another; Ex parte Mole Engineering Pty Ltd [1981] HCA 25; (1980-1981) 147 CLR 340 (T documents, page 16).

79. He did not make the same statement in relation to Dr Barker's decision of 2 September, 1993 to extend the time up to 14 or 16 August, 1993 from 14 or 16 June, 1993. He noted that no extension had been given for the period 14 June to 16 June, 1993 but that Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham had been advised that it had been granted to 14 August, 1993. On that basis, he "treated the extension of time as having been granted from 14 June to 16 June" although he would also have been prepared to grant an extension under sub-section 223(1) for the period of 14 June to 16 June, 1993. (T documents, page 17)

80. On the face of his decision, Mr Herald has not purported to make a decision to extend the time in relation to the period 14 to 16 June, 1993 but to construe the decision previously made by Dr Barker. We agree with his construction when read in the context of Dr Barker's earlier decision to grant the extension "to" 12 July, 1993, so encompassing the period 14 to 16 June, 1993.

81. It follows that we do not consider that Mr Herald has made a decision in relation to the periods prior to 14 August, 1993 but is dealing solely with the periods on and after that date. That was a period in relation to which no previous decision had been made.

82. As Jiejing has sought review of Mr Herald's decision dated 13 January, 1994, it follows that his is the only decision which we may properly consider and the periods on and after 14 August, 1993 are the only periods we may consider. Had Jiejing wanted the earlier decisions to be reviewed by this Tribunal, they would need to have lodged an application for an extension of time within which to lodge that application for they were outside the 28 day time period allowed by the AAT Act when it lodged its application on 10 February, 1994 in relation to Mr Herald's decision.

Was there a basis under sub-section 223(1) for the extension of time from and after 14 August, 1993 to 13 January, 1994?

83. Jiejing has submitted that Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham should not have been granted an extension of time form 14 to 30 August, 1993 as they did not request that particular extension of time. As Mr Hanson has submitted, an application for an extension of time is not required if the extension is granted under sub-section 223(1). An application is only relevant if an extension is sought under sub-section 223(2).

84. Mr Hanson has submitted that the Commissioner must extend the time beyond 14 to 30 August, 1993 on the basis of sub-section 223(1). The errors or omissions in this case were the incorrect advice which Mr Finzi gave to Mr Thomas as to the date to which the extension would be required, the following delay in processing Mr Thomas's application for an extension of time and Dr Barker's oversight in granting the extension until 14 August, 1993. The errors or omissions are causally linked to the relevant act which, in this case, is the failure of the patent application to be accepted.

85. The effect of Mr Hanson's submission is that an error or omission can encompass incorrect advice given by the Commissioner's officers to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham. He referred to the judgement of Stephen J in L. Shaddock and Associates Proprietary Limited and Another v The Council of the City of Parramatta [1981] HCA 59; (1981-82) 150 CLR 225 (Gibbs CJ, Stephen, Mason, Murphy and Aickin JJ) when he said:

"In the present case the Council had, as Moffitt P puts it, set

itself up as an information centre. It alone possessed the store of

information which was of quite vital concern to those who owned or

contemplated acquiring property in its municipal district. This was

information which the Council must be taken to have known would, as a

matter of course, be sought from it by all those concerned with

property dealings in the municipality. As a matter of course it

supplied that information, in common with other councils, as a regular

and constant activity. It was under no statutory duty to furnish such

information; whatever may be thought to be the effect of s.342AS of

the Local Government Act 1919 (NSW), it does not extend to the

particular information here in question. But it had voluntarily

devised a system and method by which it could readily make the

information available to inquirers.

The information in question was of a kind which was known by all to be

of great importance to those seeking it and it was largely

inaccessible through other channels. Moreover, much of the

information sought would concern the Council's own actions. But it

would be worse than valueless, it might be positively harmful in its

effect, should it prove to be incorrect. In those circumstances it

cannot, I think, be supposed that the Council did not hold itself out

as exercising care in relation to this information which it offered to

impart. The matter can be looked at in this way. Were a council

expressly to qualify its answers, stating that they might be subject

to errors for which it accepted no responsibility, the present

practice would be rendered largely worthless. Conveyancers could no

longer rely upon a council's answers and would instead, in the case of

each transaction, have to engage in extensive searches of council

records, no doubt to the great inconvenience of all concerned. Why

this does not now occur is because councils are rightly regarded as

holding themselves out as exercising care in the answers they give."

(page 242)

86. What is meant by an "error or omission" was considered by Jenkinson J in Kimberly-Clark v Commissioner of Patents and Another [1988] FCA 421; (1988) 84 ALR 685 in relation to the 1952 Act. Section 160 of that equates with section 223 of the 1992. Although the language is slightly different, the differences do not affect the relevance of Jenkinson J's conclusions regarding the meaning of the words "error or omission". He reached those conclusions in relation to sub-section 160(2) which is the equivalent of sub-section 223(2) and which relates to errors and omissions of a person, his agent or attorney. It had been submitted that

"... unless the words 'error or omission' be limited by construction

to that which is unintended or which has been produced by accident or

inadvertence, every failure to do an act or take a step within the

time prescribed by the Act to the causing of which failure some mental

process contributed which the Commissioner considers erroneous will

satisfy the condition specified in s16092)(a). This, they submitted,

reduces the provisions of s160(2)(a) to the status of a general

discretionary power to extend prescribed times, whereas it is plainly

- as it has been described in Lehtovaara v Acting Deputy Commissioner

of Patents (1981) 30 ALR 103 at 112 - a provision for 'extensions

needed in special cases'." (pages 693-694)

87. After considering the history of the legislation, Jenkinson J concluded:

"... It is in my opinion difficult to suppose that only the

inadvertences and accidental steps, and not errors resulting from

faulty reflection, of the former class of persons were intended by the

draftsman to be within s160(1). Further, the word 'error' is not

easily assigned a clear meaning restricted by reference to one or

several particular categories of flawed mental function. The attempt

is likely to lead to the drawing of fine and often unrealistic

distinctions. And some errors of judgment by agents and attorneys may

be as bizarre and as little to be anticipated as lapses of memory and

accidental slips. Although the latter kinds of error may be those

which the draftsman had principally in mind, I do not think the phrase

'error or omission' should be given by construction the restricted

meaning which the delegate assigned ..." (page 694)

88. There is no reason to adopt a different meaning for the words "error or omission" as they appear in section 223. Nor is there any reason in section 223 itself to adopt a different meaning simply because the error or omission is that of the Commissioner or his officers and is dealt with in sub-section 223(1) rather than an error or omission of another and dealt with in sub-section 223(2). Consistency of interpretation requires that the same interpretation be given to the same words in this case.

89. If we find that there is an error or omission, that in itself is not enough to require the grant of an extension of time. There must be a causal link between the error or omission and a relevant act that must be done within a certain time and which either is not done or cannot be done within that time. The causal link is described by the use of the word "because" in sub-section 223(1). The word does not require explanation although we note that it is given the meanings of "for the reason that" and "due to the fact that" in the Macquarie Dictionary. Whether or not that causal link is established will depend on the evidence but we have gained some guidance from the cases which considered the words "by reason of" which appeared in section 160 of the 1952 Act and which were replaced, in effect, by the word "because".

90. The previous wording in sub-section 160(1) of the 1952 Act where the act or step required has not been taken "by reason of" an error or omission. Similar wording was used in section 131 of the Trade Marks Act 1955. That was considered by Beaumont J in Lyons and Another (carrying on business as Mitty's Authorized Newsagency) v Registrar of Trade Marks and Another [1983] FCA 236; (1983) 50 ALR 496 where he said:

"The meaning of the phrase 'by reason of' has been considered in a

number of authorities. In Main Electrical Pty Ltd v Civil and Civic

Pty Ltd (1978) 19 SASR 34, Bray CJ (at 49) said that the phrase

implies a relationship of cause and effect but it is a relationship

which may be indirect (see The Diamond (1906) P282; see also Vickers v

Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs [1982] FCA 72; (1982) 43 ALR 389 at 407).

In R v Justices of the Peave of Yarram; Ex parte Arnold [1964] VicRp 3; (1964) VR 21,

Sholl J held (at 24) that 'by reason of' could extend to consequential

matters. In my opinion, the phrase, where used in s131, should be

given the meaning explained in those cases." (page 509)

91. Although we have taken guidance from those cases, we have asked ourselves whether the substitution of the word "because" for the words "by reason of" indicates that Parliament intended that the causal link between the error or omission and the relevant act not being done within the appropriate time must be a more direct, rather than an indirect link of the type found in, for example, Vickers' case (at pages 407-408). In the circumstances of this case, we have not needed to answer that question.

92. Unless the time limit is extended by the Commissioner, an application for a patent must be accepted within 21 months from the date of its filing. That time limit has, of course, been extended as we have already set out. "Acceptance" is an action or step taken by the Commissioner. It is dealt with in Part 3 of the Act. In relation to an application for a standard patent, sub-section 49(1) provides:

"Subject to section 51, if the Commissioner considers that there is no

lawful ground of objection to a patent request and complete

specification relating to an application for a standard patent, or

that any such ground of objection has been removed, the Commissioner

must accept the request and specification."

If sub-section 49(1) does not apply, the Commissioner may refuse to accept the request and specification. For the purposes of our decision, the remaining provisions of section 49 and those of section 51 are not relevant.

93. The application lodged by Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham lapsed on 14 August, 1993 unless Mr Herald's decision of 13 January, 1993 to extend the time is upheld. Acceptance of the application had not taken place by 14 August, 1993. Acceptance is, as we have said, an action or step required to be taken by the Commissioner if an application comes within sub-section 49(1) and provided section 51 is not applicable. It is also a relevant act within the meaning of sub-section 223(11) for it is an action in relation to a patent application and is not a "prescribed action" within the meaning of sub-regulation 22.11(3).

94. Why could the Commissioner not accept the application within the prescribed time as extended? There are two possible answers to this and each needs to be explored. One is that there were lawful grounds of objection to the application. The other is that the time had not been extended sufficiently to enable the Commissioner's officers to carry out the necessary work for acceptance.

95. Taking the second reason first, we find that the only reason why Mr Thomas sought an extension of time to 16 August, 1993 was that he was advised to do that by Mr Finzi. That the application was granted only to 14 August and not to 16 August, 1993 seems to us to be an error. There was no logical reason to grant the extension to 14 August, 1993. The extension sought had been to 16 August, 1993 and, in any event, an extension to 14 August, 1993 was meaningless. Some time before 2 September, 1993, Mr Finzi was ready to accept the application. We find that he was only stopped by his concern that he could not execute the instrument of acceptance by pre-dating it. Looking at the whole course of the events and the circumstances of the last extension, we are satisfied that Dr Barker had intended to extend the time sufficiently so that the application could be extended. The grounds which were given for that extension (and of which we are satisfied on the evidence) were the incorrect advice given to Mr Thomas regarding the date of 16 August, 1989, the delays in the Commission's office in processing the application and the delays in making the decision itself. All applied equally to a decision to extend the time to 30 August, 1993 or to 2 September, 1993 as to 14 August, 1994 which was the date selected. That he did not extend the time to 2 September, 1993 is an error or omission that caused another of the Commissioner's officers to be in a position where he could not accept the application within the prescribed time (as extended). Therefore, looking only at the steps taken in relation to the extension of time, it appears that the criteria of sub-section 223(1) have been met up to 2 September, 1993 and the discretion must be exercised under that sub-section.

96. Before deciding whether the criteria in sub-section 223(1) have actually been met in relation to all matters, we must look at whether the application would not have been accepted under section 49 for some other reason. That is to say, were the lawful grounds of objection to the patent request and specification that had not been removed? We accept Mr Finzi's statement that he prepared the acceptance document and the only difficulty in executing it was that the final day for acceptance had passed. It seems to us, therefore, that, had the prescribed time been extended, the application would have been accepted at some time between 30 August and 2 September, 1993 by Mr Finzi. Mr Finzi had prepared a First Statement of Proposed Amendments in relation to the application and specification. We accept his evidence that it is the practice of the Office to assist those who use its services with minor, formal matters of the type which appeared to be raised by the application at that time.

97. In support of his submission that acceptance of an application will take place where the matters that are outstanding are relatively trivial, Mr Hanson has referred to Commissioner of Patents v Microcell Limited and Others [1959] HCA 71; (1958-59) 102 CLR 232 (Dixon CJ, McTiernan, Fullagar, Taylor and Windeyer JJ). The Court said:

"The words 'any other lawful ground of objection' are very wide, but

it is well settled that the Commissioner ought not to refuse

acceptance of an application and specification unless it appears

practically certain that letters patent granted on the specification

would be held valid. As Menzies J has pointed out, he will not

normally have before him the material necessary for the formation of a

concluded opinion. Moreover, whereas refusal of acceptance is final,

acceptance is not: the application may be opposed after acceptance on

any grounds mentioned in s.56, and, if a patent is granted, its

validity is open to attack in proceedings for infringement or for

revocation. ...

On the other hand, it is not to be overlooked that the Commissioner

has a duty to the public as well as to the applicant for a patent,

and, if it appears manifest that a valid patent could not be granted,

the Commissioner not merely has power, but is under a duty, to reject

the application under s.46. So, in Sir W G Armstrong Whitworth and Co

(Engineers) Ltd v Commissioner of Patents (1936) 42 ALR 131, Latham CJ

(Starke, Dixon, Evatt and McTiernan JJ concurring) said: 'Generally an

application would not be stopped at this stage, unless it were a clear

case as far as the subject-matter is concerned; but where the Court is

of opinion that, on the face of it, it is clear that no valid patent

could result from the grant, ... I think the Commissioner may properly

be supported in refusing to give the grant. In my opinion, it is much

more proper that the Commissioner should do that than that it should

be left to the change of some member of the public being prepared to

undertake the not inexpensive task of challenging the patent'."

(pages 244-245)

98. In light of that and in view of our being satisfied that the matters left to be attended to in relation to the application were trivial, we find that the Commissioner would have been required to accept the application at some time between 30 August and 2 September, 1993. Our conclusion in that regard is not affected by the subsequent judgement of Mr Justice Cooper in relation to petty patent number 616154. At 2 September, 1993, that decision was not known and we are concerned with what would have been the correct and preferable decision at the time and not at some later time. As soon as the criteria of sub-section 223(1) were satisfied the Commissioner had a duty to extend the time. Consequently, Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham had a corresponding right to have that time extended. That duty and that right could not be affected by a subsequent change in the circumstances.

99. It follows that we are satisfied that the only cause for the Commissioner's not accepting the application was the failure to extend the time and that failure was due to an error or omission or a series of errors or omissions in his office. Therefore, the Commissioner was obliged to extend the time for acceptance. In our view, he was obliged to extend it until at least the date on which Mr Justice Cooper's judgement was handed down i.e. 24 December, 1993. Either on that day or some time thereafter, it became clear that there was a real question whether there was a lawful ground of objection. This followed from the findings made in the Federal Court and from the similarities of the claims made in relation to petty patent 616154 and the claims made in the application 42052/89. Once it was decided that there were prima facie lawful grounds of objection, the Commissioner did not accept the application for two reasons: the errors or omissions which we have found had previously occurred and the fact that, at least on the face of it, there were lawful grounds of objection. On that day, then, failure to accept the application did not occur simply because of the errors or omissions of the Commissioner's officers. The claim of causation between the errors and the lack of acceptance had been broken by the addition of another factor. The date on which that claim of causation was broken was, we have decided, the date of Mr Herald's decision. That was the first date on which it became clear that there were prima facie grounds of objection.

Is there a proper basis for granting an extension of time after 13 January, 1994?

100. We understand from Jiejing's submission that it is contending that Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham must apply for the precise period of time required for the extension. With that in mind, we have looked at what is required under the Act and Regulations. Under sub-section 223(2), the Commissioner may grant the extension in certain circumstances and "on application made by the person concerned in accordance with the regulations, ...". Sub-regulation 22.11(1) provides that, for the purposes of sub-section 223(2), an application for an extension of time must be in the "approved form". It must be accompanied by a declaration setting out the grounds on which the application is made.

101. The words "approved form" are defined in the Act to mean "... a form approved by the Commissioner for the purposes of the provision in which the expression appears;" (section 3 and Schedule 1). We are satisfied that an "approved form" for an extension is of the type shown at page 246 of the T documents and reach that conclusion on the basis of the codes and references to the Act and Regulations appearing at the top of the document. On the evidence, we are not satisfied that this is the only "approved form". Clearly the Commissioner's officers have accepted the handwritten form sent in by Mr Thomas. Acceptance in the circumstances of this case seems to us to equate with approval. In the absence of any evidence or provision that there can be only one approved form, we find that Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham have made an application in an approved form.

102. Declarations are dealt with in regulation 22.13. Sub-regulation 22.13(2) deals with the format in which a declaration "must" be made and the formal information which "must" be included. Sub-regulation 22.13(3) deals with the person before whom the declaration "may" be made. Mr Thomas did not send a declaration with his original application for an extension on 21 September, 1993 but certainly did so on 12 October, 1993. That complied with the substantive provisions of regulation 22.13. Taking all of these matters into account, we have concluded that Mr Thomas lodged an application for an extension of time in accordance with the regulations. There is no requirement that he specify the precise period for which he sought the extension and, indeed, he left it open.

103. It seems to us that it has not been established that Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham have made an error or omission that has led to the application's not being accepted and an extension of time being justified under paragraph 223(2)(a). Whether there are circumstances beyond their control that have led to non acceptance and an extension's being justified is a different matter.

104. What are "circumstances beyond the control of" the people concerned (i.e. of Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham) in this case? Those words have been used in other legislation such as the Student Assistance Regulations and the Trade Marks Act 1955 ("the Trade Marks Act"). The most helpful comments on the meaning of the words come from the judgement of Jenkinson J in Rohrmoser v Registrar of Trade Marks (1986) 70 ALR 613. His Honour considered paragraph 131(1)(a) of the Trade Marks Act and whether the notice of opposition to the registration of a trade mark had not been lodged by reason of "circumstances beyond the control of the person concerned". Jenkinson J considered a submission that the circumstances to which paragraph 131(1)(a) referred were those which stood outside the spheres of action available to the person and his servants and agents. Only circumstances which neither the person nor any person acting on his behalf could control by changing them. Jenkinson J concluded:

"In the context in which it is found, the expression 'circumstances

beyond the control of the person concerned' designates, and designates

only, occurrences which neither the person concerned nor any person

acting on his behalf to do the act or take the step could prevent.

The operations of nature and the activities of strangers may result in

such occurrences. So, too, may the acts and omissions of certain

independent contractors engaged by the person concerned or by his

agent, as for example the carrier of mail or the office cleaner,

either of whom causes the loss or destruction of a document to be

filed. But the acts or omissions of the agent who on behalf of the

person concerned is to do the act or take the step are not occurrences

of the description specified in para.131(1)(a). Nor are the acts or

omissions of that agent's servants." (page 619)

105. In this case, the circumstances beyond the control of Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham were the terms of the judgement of Mr Justice Cooper and the impact that the judgement and the reasons for it would have upon the examiner's view as to whether or not there was a lawful ground of objection to the patent request and specification relating to an application. Had Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham been able to see into the future, they would no doubt have been able to frame their request and specification differently to overcome the difficulties raised by the judgement and its application to their request and specification. That, however, is a circumstance too nebulous to be able to say that it was something over which they had any control. The judgement and the application of its principles are, therefore, circumstances beyond their control.

106. Guidance as to the circumstances in which it is appropriate to exercise the discretion is found in Vangedal-Nielsen and Others v Smith (Commissioner of Patents) and Another [1980] FCA 163; (1980) 33 ALR 144 (Bowen CJ). Sub-section 59(1) of the 1952 Act provided that a person might lodge a notice of opposition to the grant of a patent. He or she had three months from the date of acceptance, or such further period not exceeding three months as the Commissioner allowed, in which to lodge the notice of objection. Bowen CJ said:

"... it will be noted that no express guidance is afforded by s.59(1)

of the basis on which the extension of time is to be allowed or

disallowed. Where this is so, one has to consider the purpose and

scope of the provision in order to determine what are relevant or

irrelevant considerations in dealing with an application for an

extension (Water Conservation and Irrigation Commission (NSW) v

Browning [1947] HCA 21; (1947) 74 CLR 492 at 496 and 504; Administrative Decisions

(Judicial Review) Act 1977 s5).

The right to lodge a notice of opposition within three months is

clearly given mainly in the interests of the person wishing to oppose

a patent on any one or more of the grounds stated in s59(1). No doubt

there is also a public interest involved in ensuring that worthless

patents are not granted because insufficient opportunity has been

afforded of raising those grounds (Kaiser Aluminium and Chemical

Corporation v Reynolds Metals Co [1969] HCA 7; (1969) 120 CLR 136). Three months

has been considered by Parliament to be an appropriate period to allow

for this. But it has been recognized that cases may occur where, for

one reason or another, three months may prove insufficient.

Accordingly, it has been provided that further time may be allowed not

exceeding a further three months. The Commissioner is interposed as

the arbiter whether such an extension should be allowed and how long

it should be. Clearly, the Commissioner will have to consider the

interests of the prospective opponent who, for some good reason, has

not been able to mount his opposition within the initial period of

three months. The Commissioner will further have to have in mind,

where a serious opposition is foreshadowed, the public interest which

has been mentioned, but he will have to require to be satisfied by an

applicant for an extension that a proper case has been made out

justifying an extension. It would be wrong if he granted an extension

simply because no one had raised rather exceptional circumstances why

it should not be granted. Reasons why this is so include the

desirability of operating the system efficiently and without

unreasonable delays and also the interests of the applicant for a

patent which are also clearly involved." (pages 149-150)

107. We have already noted that a decision was made on 14 March, 1994 that there are lawful grounds of objection to the patent request and specification and that those lawful grounds preclude acceptance. On one view, this would seem to be an end of the matter and there is no point in considering whether or not an extension of time should have been granted on 13 January, 1994. That view is not, however, the whole picture for if the extension of time is not granted, the application lapses. If the application lapses any divisional applications based on that application also lapse. Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham have another such divisional application lodged between 24 December, 1993 and 14 March, 1994.

108. In view of the way in which the Act permits the divisional applications to be lodged on the basis of a principal application, it seems to us that we should take into account the facts as they existed at the date of the exercise of Mr Herald's decision i.e. 13 January, 1994. That is the date on which Mr Herald exercised a discretion but it is also the date on which, by exercising that discretion, Mr Herald affected the status of the principal application and any other divisional applications based upon it. The decision under review is, therefore, not an investigation of a present entitlement to the grant of a right or privilege but the investigation of a past entitlement to the grant of a right or privilege. It is, therefore, distinguishable from the general principles usually applied in this Tribunal and discussed in Re Costello and Secretary, Department of Transport (1979) 2 ALD 934 (Mr Hall, then Senior Member, Mr Thompson and Mr Marsh, Members).

109. In the circumstances of this case, we consider that Mr Herald exercised the discretion to extend time appropriately. It would have been unjust if Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham had been denied any opportunity to amend the request and specification in an attempt to overcome the difficulties revealed by Mr Justice Cooper's judgement. They had not had a proper opportunity to do so as at 13 January, 1994. That they had such a proper opportunity is in keeping with the scheme of the Act. The interests of third parties must also be taken into account. Those third parties include those who may wish to give notice of an objection at a later stage. They are entitled to do so under the scheme of the Act and to do so after the application has been accepted but the possibility that they may wish to do so at a later stage does not justify, in this case, Mr Thomas's and Mr Garnham's being denied the opportunity to gain acceptance if they are able. Therefore we agree with Mr Herald's decision to extend the time to 14 March, 1994.

110. We have reached our conclusion on the basis of paragraph 223(2)(b). We note, however, that Mr Herald based his decision either on that paragraph or on sub-section 223(1). He put his decision on sub-section 223(1) on the basis that, arguably, the failure of the examiner to identify the ground of invalidity identified by Cooper J was an error or omission of the Commissioner. We do not wish to express a concluded view on this argument as we have already reached our conclusion on the basis of paragraph 223(2)(b). We do, however, query whether the causal claim between such an error and the failure to accept the request and specification is sufficiently direct. If the ground of invalidity had been identified, amendments would have had to have been made and they would have had to be such that the ground of invalidity was removed. In the circumstances of this case, can it be said that because of the error, the request and specification were not accepted? As we said, we do not propose to answer this question.

111. In their submissions generally but particularly in their submission received on 23 March, 1995, Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham have argued that the Commissioner did not advise them that acceptance had to take place within a 21 month period. They also argued that it was not enough for the Commissioner to tell them that any objections had to be overcome within that period. This correspondence was summarised in paragraph 25 above. Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham have argued that they were led to understand by the Commissioner that they had only to respond and not that the application had to be accepted. That may be so for that view is consistent with the statutory declaration sworn in support of an application for an extension of time from 14 March, 1993 to 14 June, 1993 (see paragraph 26 above). Whether it is so or not is not relevant in these proceedings for any misunderstanding, however it arose, related to the period prior 14 August, 1993. That is not a period with which we are concerned. In any event, the requirement that acceptance was required was clear by 18 May, 1993 when the Commissioner wrote to Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham (paragraph 27).

112. We have reached the same conclusion in relation to the information in the Commissioner's computer which Mr Thomas and Mr Garnham said misled them. If they were misled and if that were an error of the Commissioner, its effect was exhausted before 14 August, 1993.

113. For the reasons we have given, we affirm the decision under review to extend the time allowed for acceptance of patent application number 42052/89 from 14 August, 1993 to 14 March, 1994, both dates inclusive.
