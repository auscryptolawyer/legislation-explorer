Perrett and Commissioner for Superannuation [1988] AATA 625 (24 August 1988)

Perrett and Commissioner for Superannuation [1988] AATA 625 (24 August 1988)

Last Updated: 2 July 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Superannuation - retirement on ground of invalidity before maximum retirement age - pre-existing physical condition specified in benefit classification certificate - psoriasis subsequent and physical condition causing, or substantially contributing to incapacity resulting in early retirement psoriatic arthritis - whether "connected with" pre-existing condition

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.16 (4) and 66(2) Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 s.6

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 411: decided on 30 September 1980) (note only at (1980) 3 ALN N1)

Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237

Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (No ACT G68 of 1986: decided on 14 April 1988)

Re Birtles and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 1309: decided on 22 November 1983)

Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70

Words and phrases: "connected with"

Decision: The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's invalidity retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate that was in force in respect of the applicant immediately before her retirement, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with that condition.

(sgd) Mrs R.A. Balmford

Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No V88/29

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: CHRISTINE D. PERRETT

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

24 August 1988 Mrs R.A. Balmford, Senior Member

Professor R.W. Webster, Member

Mr C.G. Woodard, Member

  1. This is an application under section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") for a review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent made on 15 December 1987, confirming a decision made on 7 March 1986 by another delegate, being a decision "to be of the opinion under paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Perrett's invalidity retirement was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate that was in force in respect of Mrs Perrett immediately before her retirement, or by a condition or conditions connected with that condition".
  2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") and numbered Tl/1 to T28/85, together with other material lodged at the hearing, to some of which it will be necessary to refer. Evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant; by Dr McInnes, Senior Commonwealth Medical Officer; and by Dr Harkness and Dr Webb, both of whom are rheumatologists. The applicant was represented by Mr Tobin of counsel and the respondent by Mr Whithear of the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office.

3. Sub-section 66(2) of the Act reads, so far as relevant;

"(2) ... where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled [to substantially reduced benefits]."

4. The applicant, who was born in 1948, has suffered since the age of about 15 from a skin condition which was diagnosed, some years after its first appearance, as being psoriasis. She joined the then Postmaster-General's Department in 1969 as a telephonist, and became an eligible employee, for the purposes of the Act, on 31 October 1974 when she received a permanent appointment. On 16 January 1975 she underwent a medical examination for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1922. She was accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account on 21 July 1975. In his report of the medical examination, the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer referred to her psoriasis and recommended that she be accepted for the Provident Account. A benefit classification certificate ("BCC") was issued in respect of Mrs Perrett on 17 April 1985 pursuant to sub-section 184(3) of the transitional provisions in the Act specifying the condition "psoriasis". The issue of that BCC has not been challenged by the applicant.

5. On 28 June 1985 Mrs Perrett was examined by Dr McInnes for the purpose of assessing her fitness for continued employment or retirement on invalidity grounds. Dr McInnes ticked the boxes opposite the statement "I consider that the examinee is unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity, but may in due course, partially or completely, recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment". On the portion of the form which he signed specifically as advisor to the Commissioner for Superannuation, he wrote "retirement recommended". Under the heading "Brief history of the condition(s) including origins and the time first noted by contributor" he wrote:

"Sudden onset of pain in hands and feet 3 years ago, followed by swelling. Pain and swelling in other joints occurs intermittently, but hands and feet are always painful. Attends a rheumatologist, treated with injections into joints and anti-inflammatory drugs.

O/E overweight woman with slow awkward gait. Swelling of joints of hands, especially MP. joints, and MP joints of feet. Pain on movement of wrists, elbows, knees. BP 120/84. Heart, lungs, CNS normal."

  1. Mrs Perrett was retired on invalidity grounds with effect from 28 June 1985, this being well before she attained her maximum retiring age in terms of paragraph 66(2)(a). The BCC specifying the condition "psoriasis" was in force in respect of her immediately before her retirement. Thus the requirements of paragraph 66(2)(a) and (b) are met in her case.
  2. In order to ascertain whether paragraph 66(2)(c) applies to Mrs Perrett, the first question here to determine is: what was the incapacity which was the ground for her retirement? It is not in issue that her retirement was based on Dr McInnes' report and recommendation, and we so find.

8. The Tribunal, presided over by Mr Todd, Senior Member, as he then was, said in Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 411: decided on 30 September 1980) (note only at (1980) 3 ALN N1) at paragraph 22:

"The phrase 'incapacity which was the ground for ... retirement' is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. It would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between 'incapacity' and a 'condition' is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions. We understand Dr Kay's difficulty with the phrase 'coronary insufficiency' as having been that he did not regard it as a disease process. In Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th ed, coronary insufficiency is defined as 'Prolonged precordial pain or discomfort without conventional evidence of myocardial infarction; subendocardial ischemia due to a disparity between coronary blood flow and myocardial needs'. It is thus a result, a symptom, which is precisely what Dr Kay meant when he declined to regard coronary insufficiency as a disease. But it is we think appropriate to regard it as having constituted the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. Likewise, coronary sclerosis may on the evidence be aptly regarded as having been the physical condition which caused or substantially contributed to the coronary insufficiency."

9. In the present case the incapacity apparent from Dr McInnes' report was continuous pain in the hands and feet. That was to adopt the words of the Tribunal in Burr "the outward result" that led to Mrs Perrett's "being unable to perform, that is to say, to lack the capacity to perform [her] duties". As we have found, the decision to retire Mrs Perrett was based on Dr McInnes' report. Thus we find that the incapacity which was the ground of Mrs Perrett's retirement, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act, was continuous pain in the hands and feet.

  1. The next question is whether, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c), that incapacity was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition "psoriasis" (being a condition specified in the BCC), or by a physical or mental condition connected with the condition "psoriasis".
  2. It was not suggested that the incapacity in question was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition psoriasis. The submission of the respondent, however, was that the incapacity was caused or was substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition connected with the condition psoriasis.
  3. In his report, under the heading "I consider that the examinee is suffering from the following medical condition(s) (in order of severity) and with estimated percentage each contributes to the incapacity", Dr McInnes wrote "Rheumatoid arthritis 100 per cent". However, after obtaining reports from Dr Webb and Dr Harkness, he formed the opinion that the correct description of the condition was "psoriatic arthritis".
  4. Dr Harkness has been Mrs Perrett's treating specialist since October 1984. He has practised as a specialist rheumatologist since 1974, and is concerned in the training of medical students in this area. Dr Webb has practised as a specialist rheumatologist since 1959. They each referred the Tribunal to authoritative medical publications. They agreed, and the Tribunal finds, that the cause of psoriasis is unknown, and the cause of psoriatic arthritis is unknown; that people who suffer from psoriasis may develop arthritis of a particular kind, which is easily recognisable on clinical examination, and is known as psoriatic arthritis; that some people without a previous history of psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis, and in such people it is described as "psoriatic arthritis sine psoriasis"; and that of these people, some subsequently develop psoriasis, and some do not.
  5. Dr Harkness said that, on his reading of the authorities, the percentage of people with psoriasis who developed psoriatic arthritis was about 4 per cent; only very slightly more than the percentage of people in the general population who develop arthritis of one kind or another. There was a strong association between psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, but nothing was known about the causation of either.
  6. Dr Webb was of the view that the authorities indicated that approximately 10 per cent of people with psoriasis would develop psoriatic arthritis. There might be a causal connection between psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, but there was no evidence on which that could be said. It was a possibility, not a probability. The distinction between psoriatic arthritis and rheumatoid arthritis was at the level of the clinical picture only, not some underlying process which could be distinguished on examination at the cellular level. He agreed that the statistical relationship was not of assistance in the context of ascertainment of a causal relationship except insofar as it raised questions.
  7. The leading case on the meaning of "connected with" in paragraph 66(2)(c) is Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237. Davies J said at pages 238-239:

"The words 'connected with' are not technical words and in ordinary parlance permit of an association which is not causative. In my opinion, however, Parliament did not intend the words to be given a wide interpretation unrelated to the context in which they appear.

Sections 16(4), 16(10) (b) and 66(2)(c) are all, in my opinion, concerned with questions of causation. These provisions look primarily to the circumstance where a medical or physical condition existing at the time the employee first became eligible is likely to lead to early retirement and in fact causes or substantially contributes to early retirement.

In the context, it appears to me that the words 'connected with' were introduced to deal with the circumstance where the early retirement arises not from the medical condition which existed at the time the employee became an eligible employee but as a result of another medical condition that arose out of or developed from that condition. Thus, in the present case, the immediate or proximate cause of the retirement was not the employee's condition of 'hormonal mastopathy presenting as breast lumps', which had ceased to exist, but from the adhesions which had arisen as a result of the operation on the employee's breasts.

In the context, I would read the words 'connected with' in sections 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) as dealing with the circumstance where the immediate or proximate cause of the incapacity leading to the retirement was a medical condition other than the condition existing at the time the employee became an eligible employee, but where, nevertheless, there is a chain of causation such that the medical condition existing at the time when the employee became an eligible employee can be said, through a chain of events, to have caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity causing the retirement.

This reading gives due attention to the causative elements prescribed by sections 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) and also to the context of the Act in which those provisions function. It also gives due weight to the crucial provision (section 16(4) of the Act). That provision requires the Commissioner to prognosticate as to whether, by reason of the medical condition existing at the commencement of eligibility or for a reason connected with that condition, the employee would not be likely to continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retiring age.

The provision requires the Commissioner to be able to postulate a chain of causation between a medical condition existing at the time of the commencement of eligibility and an incapacity leading to early retirement. Sections 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) of the Act look at the matter after retirement to see whether such a chain of causation actually existed."

and Pincus J at page 247:

"The expression 'connected with' is not very precise. It is my view that the sort of connection which is meant is a causal one. For the purposes of section 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976, two conditions are connected with one another if there is a substantial causal link between the two. That may be so because one is a cause of the other, or because both have a common cause. It is my opinion that unless there is a substantial causal link, there is no connection, I appreciate that the word 'substantial' introduces questions of degree, but they are neither questions which need to be resolved for the purposes of this case, nor questions as to which one can usefully give any guidance. The view I have expressed on this point accords, I think, with that set out in the reasons of Davies J.

It is necessarily involved in what I have said that, if there is a substantial causal link between two conditions, they are 'connected with' one another, whether or not the final condition was foreseeable."

Beaumont J did not find it necessary to reach a concluded opinion on the meaning of "connected with".

17. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (No ACT G86 of 1986: decided on 14 April 1988) Neaves J rejected a submission that these passages from Miller were obiter dicta, and a further submission that the view of Pincus J was in fact wider than that of Davies J. At page 18 His Honour said (referring to the expressed view of Davies J):

"In my opinion, it is clear from a reading of the passage as a whole that his Honour's view was that, in a case where the early retirement arises from incapacity which is caused, or substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition other than that specified in the benefit classification certificate, the employee will be deprived of the benefits provided for by sub-section 66(1) of the Act if the decision-maker is satisfied that that physical or mental condition 'arose out of or developed from' that specified in the benefit classification certificate. So understood, his Honour's view and that of Pincus J are in accord and clearly support the view which the Tribunal took."

He went on to say (at page 19):

"Nor do I think that the Tribunal misdirected itself in law by identifying the question for decision as being whether there was a substantial causal link between the two physical conditions. It may be said that to import the word 'substantial' is to put a gloss on the statutory provision and thus depart from the test prescribed by the legislature. In my view, however, to ask whether there is a substantial causal link between the physical or mental condition specified in a benefit classification certificate and the physical or mental condition which caused, or substantially contributed to, the incapacity resulting in early retirement is but to recognise, and indeed emphasise, what is implicit in the provision when read in the light of its obvious purpose and object. What the provision, so considered, clearly requires is a finding that there exists a causal connection between the two conditions which is sufficient to justify depriving the employee of the invalidity benefits which sub-section 66(1) of the Superannuation Act would otherwise require to be paid."

18. There is no evidence at all before the Tribunal on which we could find that the condition psoriatic arthritis, being the cause of the incapacity which led to Mrs Perrett's retirement, "arose out of or developed from" the condition psoriasis specified in the BCC, to use the words of Davies J which were adopted by Heaves J.

19. It is clear that, as Dr Harkness said, there is an association between the two conditions. Looked at in isolation, the expression 'a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions" might be seen as referring to a situation of this kind, where there is an association between the two conditions but no causal relationship is known to exist. However, the majority in Miller, looking at that expression in the context of the Act as a whole, reached a different conclusion.

20, The Tribunal was referred by Mr Whithear to the decision in Re Birtels and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 1309; decided on 22 November 1983). On our reading of that decision, it was based, not specifically on the statistical evidence of correlation between the BCC condition and the cause of the incapacity, but on the firm opinions of a number of medical witness that there was a causal connection between the two. No evidence before us in the present case indicated more than the possibility of a causal connection between psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis.

21. Mr Whithear referred to the amendment of sub-section 16(4), to which Davies J referred in the passage above cited as "the crucial provision", which was made after the date of the decision in Miller. by section 6 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986. That amendment, which came into operation with effect from 1 July 1976, substituted the expression "there is a real risk that the person ... will not continue" for the expression "the person is not likely ... to continue". He submitted that that amendment significantly affected the basis of the reasoning of Davies J. The BCC had been issued, specifying psoriasis, on the basis that there was a "real risk" (for the reason, among other reasons, that she might contract psoriatic arthritis) that Mrs Perrett would not continue to he an eligible employee until her maximum retiring age. If psoriatic arthritis was not found to he a condition "connected with" psoriasis, the BCC was rendered nugatory, and the Act unworkable,

22. We would express some doubt as to whether the amendment affects His Honour's reasoning to the extent suggested by Mr Whithear, or indeed at all. We do not see it as leading to the result that the Tribunal, since the making of the amendment, is no longer bound to follow the decision of the Full Court in Miller. Further, the administration of the provisions of the Act relating to BCCs must strike a balance between the provision of benefits to contributors on the one hand and the reduction of the liability to provide such benefits "in circumstances in which it would be inconsistent with proper stewardship of public funds for it to do so" (Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70 at 73. It may be appropriate to say that the decision of the Full Court in Miller tips the balance some distance in favour of the applicant, rather than that it renders the Act unworkable.

23. Having considered the evidence before us, in the light of the authorities to which we have referred, we find that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Perrett's retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the BCC that was in force in respect of her immediately before her retirement, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with that condition. The decision under review will be set aside and the matter remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with an appropriate direction.
