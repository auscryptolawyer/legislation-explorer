Zivojin Gavrilovich and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 227 (11 August 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ZIVOJIN GAVRILOVICH v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A95/2

AAT No. 10351

Number of pages - 8

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

M.D. ALLEN (Senior Member), N.J. ATTWOOD (Member) and J.A. HOOPER (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Preservation of contributor's rights. Late election - extension of time. Failure to make further and better enquiries at time of resignation. Resignation to undertake commercial activities and out of Public Service for 10 years.

Superannuation Act 1976 - Ss.137(1), Ss.157(1)

Re Chalk and Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal Decision No 8954)

Re Anne Margaret Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal Decision No 9987)

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; 123 ALR 239

HEARING

CANBERRA, 27 July 1995

11:8:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Hugh Selby

Solicitors for the Applicant: Ms Katherine Armytage,

Minter Ellison - Lawyers

Counsel for the Respondent: Ms Tess Munoz

Solicitors for the Respondent: Ms Vicki Guthrie,

Comsuper

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

M.D. ALLEN, N.J. ATTWOOD AO, AND J.A. HOOPER CBE In this matter the Applicant seeks review of a decision by a Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation made 8 December 1994 affirming a prior decision of 3 February 1994 that it was not desirable to direct that the election to preserve benefits made by the Applicant on 29 September 1992, pursuant to Ss.137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, be treated as if it had been made within the time period of 21 days after resignation as required by the said subsection of the Act.

2. Ss.137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 states:

"A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which

paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled

to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with

section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of

which the person is deemed, under subsection 58 (3), to have retired

involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an

eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner,

that this Division apply in relation to the person."

Whereas Ss.157(1) of the said Act states:

"Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election

under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the

period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election,

and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of

the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized, the

Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had

been made within the period allowed and the election shall have

effect accordingly."

3. The Applicant joined the Commonwealth Public Service in 1965 and resigned from that employ as and from 27 August 1981. The Applicant possesses the Degree of Batchelor of Arts and a Diploma in Public Service Management.

4. At the time of his resignation the Applicant was a Clerk Class 9 in the Australian Development Assistance Bureau (ADAB). The Applicant had in the course of his duties developed an expertise in the management of foreign aid programmes in the South Pacific, principally in the countries of Fiji, Western Samoa and Tonga.

5. The Applicant was aware that because of lack of a Managing Agent in Australia, countries in the South Pacific were failing to obtain Foreign Aid from Australia that otherwise would have been available to them. He had raised with the Director of ADAB, Mr Ingram, this fact and the circumstance that the countries were having difficulty in choosing an agent and preferred to act with someone they knew and trusted.

6. As events transpired, the Applicant was approached to advise on the choice of a Managing Agent. He discussed this with the Director of ADAB and indicated that it was his desire that he be granted leave without pay in order to take up the position but once that position had been established he would return to the Public Service. This proved impossible as the Director of ADAB considered that to be on leave without pay from the Bureau whilst acting as a Managing Agent would create a conflict of interests. Another complicating factor was that the Governments of Fiji and Western Samoa required that the contract to act as Agent be with a corporate entity.

7. The Applicant therefore accepted that if he were to act as Agent for developing countries seeking Foreign Aid from Australia he would have to resign from the Commonwealth Public Service.

8. In order to comply with the requirements of Fiji and Western Samoa the Applicant formed a Proprietary Company.

9. The circumstances of the Applicant's resignation from the Commonwealth Public Service were precipitous. The company formed by him had submitted a tender to the Government of Fiji to act as its Managing Agent. That tender was accepted but instead of advising the Applicant (or his company) first, the advice of appointment of a Managing Agent was communicated in the first instance to ADAB.

10. At the time the advice of the appointment of its Managing Agent was transmitted by the Government of Fiji to ADAB the Applicant was absent from work on sick leave. Upon receipt of the advice he was interviewed by medium of a telephone conference call by his Branch Head in the presence of witnesses. He was advised that he should not return to his office and to submit a resignation effective forthwith.

11. The Applicant presented as a careful and truthful witness and the Tribunal accepts his evidence that because of the precipitate nature of his departure from ADAB he was given no exit counselling including a briefing on Superannuation options.

12. We further accept the Applicant's evidence that he does not recollect deleting two of the options on the form S2A and that at the time he did resign he believed that the only option available to him was that of a refund of Superannuation contributions plus interest. He did not need those funds to go into business and had he known of the right to elect to preserve benefits he would have done so.

13. At the time of his resignation from the Commonwealth Public Service and ADAB the Applicant had no knowledge of the period during which his appointment as a Managing Agent would last, depending as it did upon Australian Government Aid Programs. In fact the appointment lasted some 10 years and the Applicant was eventually a Managing Agent for 14 governments not only in the South Pacific but also the Indian Ocean and Africa.

14. The ADAB programs for which the Applicant acted as a Managing Agent were wound up in 1991. The Applicant then applied to rejoin the Commonwealth Public Service and was accepted, being placed in a position in the Australian Capital Territory Government Service.

15. On 29 September 1992 the Applicant wrote to the Retirement Benefits Office in the following terms:

"I was appointed to the ACTGS on 31 May, 1991, and I am currently

contributing five percent of salary to the PSS Scheme.

From 1965 to 1981 I was a member of the CSS Scheme but was not

advised on resignation that I had the option of preserving my

entitlements in the scheme. I was simply repaid my contributions

plus interest.

In the circumstances, I wish to obtain clarification on whether my

previous service can be counted towards the requirement for ten

years service to be eligible for employer contributions greater than

the 0.16 benefit multiple. ..."

This letter was, after a conversation between the Applicant and a member of the Respondent's staff, regarded as an application pursuant to Ss.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to make a late election to preserve benefits pursuant to S.137 of the said Act.

16. The criteria which should guide the Respondent and this Tribunal in the exercise of its discretion pursuant to Ss.157(1) have been canvassed in a series of decisions by the Federal Court and this Tribunal. The principles espoused by Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450 and Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; 123 ALR 239 are now so well known as to not require repeating here and the Tribunal (Deputy President McMahon, Messrs Attwood and Julian) has extensively canvassed the principles to be applied in the matter of Re Anne Margaret Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal Decision No 9987).

17. In Ward's case supra the Tribunal stated at paragraph 18:

"A General considerations setting the context within which the

discretion is to be exercised

(1) The discretion given by s 157 should be exercised by reference

to the words of the statute and the contents within which the

discretion is conferred. Section 157 uses simple words and the

issues which arise under it are invariably issues of fact, the

ultimate issue being whether it is desirable that a late election

should be recognised (Chalk at 455).

(2) On the one hand an election recognised under s 137(1) will

extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits

which fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible

employee. That would be in accordance with the general policy of

the Act. On the other hand the orderly administration of the

superannuation scheme requires that time limits be applied,

generally speaking, so that decisions which impinge on investment

and accounting procedures are made as soon as possible after an

event such as resignation occurs. Matters of this kind set the

context within which the discretion under s 157(1) is conferred and

the context in which the discretion must be exercised (Boardman at

246 and 247).

(3) In s 157(1) 'desirable' is not referable to a state of mind -

whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late

election is 'desirable' is to be judged objectively having regard

to all the circumstances of the case...whether the recognition of a

late election is 'something worthy of achievement' must depend on

what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to

make the late election and the requirements of the administration

of the fund (Boardman at 247).

B Observations on whether extension of time principles apply -

(1) Wilcox J referred to the general principles on which time for

the performance of an act may be extended but His Honour did not

limit the factors which might arise from the context in which a

particular statutory discretion to extend time arises (Chalk at

455).

(2) It is understandable, indeed inevitable, that a

decision-maker considering an application under s 157(1) will have

close regard to the assistance which decisions such as Hunter

Valley Developments and A'hern offer (Boardman at 247 and 248).

(3) There is a public interest in enforcing procedural time

limits which extends beyond the immediate parties (Boardman at

248).

(4) The time limit specified in the statute gives a prima facie

rule as to when the application should have been made. Before an

exercise of discretion in the applicant's favour can occur, it must

be made to appear that it is fair and just in all the circumstances

to extend time. One of the relevant considerations will be whether

there was a reasonable explanation for the delay, although the

absence of a reasonable explanation is not necessarily fatal :

Comcare v A'hern at 88. There is therefore an evidentiary burden on

the applicant to place information before the decision-maker

sufficient to satisfy the decision-maker that in all the

circumstances of the case, it is desirable that the late election

should be recognised (Boardman at 249).

(5) The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that

there will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant

requires that the time limit be relaxed. In such cases it is the

policy of the Act, and the legal right of the applicant, to a

favourable exercise of discretion under s 157 (1). If the proper

application of s 157(1) to individual cases, results in many people

obtaining recognition of late election, so be it, that is the law

(Boardman at 252).

C Application of extension of time principles may not always give

the correct answer -

(i) Most provisions which authorise an extension of time are

instances of beneficial legislation which, accordingly should be

applied beneficially (Chalk at 455).

(2) In the exercise of the discretion under s 157 it may be more

important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to

extend time, than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in

time (Chalk at 456).

(3) Extension of a procedural time limit gives a party an

opportunity of establishing rights but to recognise a belated

election immediately confers rights. However in each case the

question is whether it is fair and just that the party who is out

of time should by the exercise of the discretion be extended the

rights which would have been enjoyed had the act in question been

done within time (Boardman at 248).

D The existence or non existence of fault on the part of the

respondent or its agent will have little bearing on desirability -

(1) The operation of s 157 is not dependent upon the existence of

fault on the part of officers of the employer or of the scheme,

although matters such as lack of advice may well be relevant (Chalk

at 455).

(2) Whether or not there is a fiduciary duty by the Commissioner,

is not relevant. The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant

to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within

time under s 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or

did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct

of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration (per Cooper J

in Chalk at 458).

(3) Section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine

whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the

appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend

time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the

existence or absence of attributable fault...the section merely

requires that the Commissioner 'be satisfied that in all the

circumstances it is desirable that the election should be

recognised' (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(4) It is unnecessary to decide whether the Commissioner was

under a legal or equitable duty to provide to contributors to the

scheme information as to choices available to them under it. The

relevant fact may be that the Commissioner did not provide the

information (per Cooper J in Chalk at 459).

E What obligation is there on an employee to make enquiries?

(1) In the application of s 157 it may be of little consequence

that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect

under s 137 or even that the employee positively decided not to

elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either

case (Chalk at 456).

(2) Whenever it is necessary to have regard to factual

circumstances as they existed at a date in the past, delay in

making the application which initiates that consideration is likely

to add to the difficulties of ascertaining those circumstances.

This difficulty is likely to be a consideration in almost every

application to extend time no matter what the context (Boardman at

249).

(3) Depending on the circumstances, the decision-maker might

proceed on the footing that there is no reasonable explanation for

delay beyond the point when lines of enquiry so suggested would

have revealed to the applicant the right to apply for an extension.

But where the evidence establishes that the suggested lines of

enquiry would have produced no useful information, so as to leave

the applicant in the same state of ignorance or misinformation, the

absence of enquiry becomes irrelevant (Boardman at 251).

F Does delay of the applicant create prejudice to the respondent?

(1) Frequently the discretion is exercised favourably because the

facts which the employee took into account when he or she decided

not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Often times the

discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has

encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the

time of retirement. One of the objects of s 157 is to alleviate the

consequences of such a change in circumstances (Chalk at 456).

(2) It is difficult to see prejudice to the fund in a late

election. A favourable exercise of the discretion would extend to

the respondent the benefit of additional years of service, but this

liability would have arisen in any event had the election been

exercised in time. To grant the respondent the benefit of years of

service actually performed is consistent with the policy and

objects of the Act (Boardman at 252).

(3) In saying that the issue before the Tribunal involved a

'determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of the case,

when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be

granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that

extension is granted' the Tribunal did not apply a wrong test

(Boardman at 247).

G Should the decision-maker have regard to the totality of

service?

(1) A decision-maker exercising the s 157 discretion could think

it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the scheme that

the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of

the employee's service and to the totality of his contributions,

including any sums which must be refunded (Chalk at 456).

H What significance should attach to the form of investment of

the lump sum?

(1) Where the accumulated contributions were immediately invested

in a business enterprise such a use of the money would in itself

often indicate that a deliberate decision had been made to reject

the option of preservation in favour of receiving the immediate

benefit of the accumulated contributions (Boardman at 250)."

18. In particular, but not excluding other matters outlined above, this Tribunal has regard to the following sub paragraphs, namely, A(2), B(4), D and E(3).

19. At paragraph 19 the Tribunal in re Ward supra said:

"By and large the Federal Court has taken a broad brush approach to

the exercise of the s 157(1) discretion. Ultimately the justice of

the case will determine the outcome. The Federal Court has shown a

reluctance to limit the elements to be considered in deciding what

is just. Some of those elements, however, have been established.

They include a consideration against the background that the

sub-section is beneficial legislation. They include the fact that

excusable ignorance of rights is a relevant factor to be taken into

account. They include an assertion of the applicant's evidentiary

burden to explain the delay, although failure to offer an acceptable

explanation is not necessarily fatal. Other elements have been

considered in individual contexts in the many decisions of this

Tribunal. It may well be that in the absence of the adoption of

detailed criteria of universal application by the Federal Court,

other steps should be taken."

20. In this matter the Respondent has conceded that the late election will have little effect upon the Superannuation Fund. At page 13, paragraph 35 of his reasons for decision the Respondent's Delegate states:

"Obviously the cost to the Commonwealth in funding his preservation

benefits will be no greater if he elects to preserve his rights now

than if he had elected to preserve them in 1981."

That is not to say that there will not be a cost. Obviously actuarial calculations will have been made on the basis of the Applicant's non membership of the Fund. At paragraph 36 his reasons the Delegate refers to a potential impact on the taxpayer generally as the extra cost should the election be allowed would be met by consolidated revenue.

20A. Another factor is that the Applicant had made a conscious decision to resign from the Commonwealth Public Service to pursue a commercial career. When his company tendered for the position of Managing Agent for the Fijian Government the Applicant could have been under no illusion, given the prior discussions between him and the Director of ADAB, that if the tender were successful he would have to resign from the Commonwealth Public Service.

21. The actual resignation from the Commonwealth Public Service was precipitous but could not be said to have come as a complete surprise to the Applicant. At that time he was a public servant of long experience and in a senior position. He was stationed in Canberra and had full access to all facilities. We accept that his understanding of the Superannuation Scheme was imperfect in that he believed he was, upon resignation, only entitled to a refund of contributions but he made no further or better enquiries upon realising that he may have to resign, as to what his rights were under the Scheme.

22. As has been pointed out in previous cases, the Applicant was not an ill educated or unsophisticated worker in a department remote from headquarters or diffident in approaching administrative staff.

23. Further, there is no evidence that at the time of his resignation the Applicant had plans to return to the Public Service. He may not have envisaged that the business he undertook would have lasted as long as it did but his resignation, although not his preferred option, was a deliberate step in order to take up what was after all a commercial opportunity.

24. Upon rejoining the Commonwealth Public Service the Applicant made no immediate application to make a late election to preserve Superannuation benefits. His explanation for contributing to the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme at the rate he did is understandable but no explanation was given as to why an enquiry as to prior Superannuation status was not made until 29 September 1992 when he was reappointed to the Commonwealth Public Service on 31 May 1991.

25. The remarks of the Tribunal in re Chalk and Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal Decision No 8954) are equally apposite in this case, namely:

"There is nothing in this case that attracts us to the view that it

is desirable to grant the late election. We must give weight to the

time limit in the Act, that there is no acceptable explanation for

the delay - we do not regard ignorance of the law per se to be an

acceptable explanation and we are satisfied that the applicant had

no real interest in the matter until later in his life when he

realised he had made a mistake (whether wittingly or unwittingly)

with the benefit of hindsight."

26. So far as the argument based upon the Government's belief in the value of Superannuation and the necessity to preserve benefits, we consider that they are of general application and unhelpful in deciding with the specific wording of the Superannuation Act 1976 and a more reliable guide to the manner in which an application for a late election is to be considered is found in the Federal Court decisions of Chalk and Boardman supra.

27. The decision under review will therefore be affirmed.
