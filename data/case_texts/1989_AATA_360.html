ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Allowable Deductions - Member of Church of Scientology - income derived by marketing courses in Scientology - courses in Scientology undertaken to achieve personal development and to improve marketing ability whether courses undertaken in course of deriving assessable income - whether allowable - apportionment.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 51(1).

REASONS FOR DECISION

14 April 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

In 1961 the Applicant was a university student. He was then near to graduating with double degrees, including one with honours. He became interested in a religion and, since then, has pursued that interest as a matter of personal conviction: his aim being to achieve for himself a state of spiritual perfection. In the years following, he married; he reared a family; he advanced in the service of a major company; and he progressed towards his religious goal. In his principal employment his salary rose from $26,550 (to 30 June 1980) to $61,521 (to 30 June 1988). Pursuit of matters relating to his personal and religious development arising out of those convictions and practices generated some assessable income. It also gave rise to claims on the part of the Applicant for allowable deductions. The issues for determination are whether all of the income tax deductions claimed in the years of income ended 30 June 1980 and 1984 are allowable.

  1. To appreciate the claims in their context, it is appropriate to say that the religion in question is that of Church of Scientology - recognized on the evidence then presented as a "religious institution" by the High Court of Australia in The Church of the New Faith v. Commissioner of Payroll Tax (Vict.) [1983] HCA 40; (1983) 154 C.L.R. 120.
  2. The Commissioner does not dispute the Applicant's assertion that Scientology is a religion. That is not inappropriate. For reasons I explained in Case V13 (88 A.T.C. 163), the question to be determined is not whether the amounts claimed as deductions by the Applicant were paid to a religious institution, but rather whether the Applicant is able to satisfy the tests for deductibility prescribed in s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). Nonetheless, to understand the claim, it is necessary to have regard to some aspects of the structures of the Church of Scientology. The spiritual end sought to be attained by the Applicant through his membership of the Church of Scientology was a state of personal development in which he would be "no longer affected by (his) past existence": he would then be in a state of "total freedom". This was to be achieved by passing over "the bridge to total freedom" by following two routes of development of "Training" and of "Processing" planned by the Church, each calling for progression through a series of courses, leading the disciple to ever-more advanced states of personal development.
  3. I accept that, before the period in dispute, the Applicant had come to enjoy the status known within the Church, as a Field-Staff Member (FSM). That advancement in status as recognized by the Church would not have had any financial or fiscal significance but for the circumstance that it made the Applicant eligible to receive remuneration from the Church should he render certain services to it. The practice of the Church was to encourage FSM's to introduce others as fee-paying customers to courses operated by the Church. For their service in effecting such introductions, FSM's would be paid a commission: 15% of the gross fee in relation to Training courses; and 10% of the gross fee in relation to Processing courses. The Applicant went about persuading persons to pay for and to undertake such courses of instruction. He dealt at two levels: at one, introducing strangers to Scientology and its concepts; and the other in persuading initiates to undertake further courses of personal development presented by the Church,
  4. Scientology has its own jargon - something not in itself remarkable. But some of the jargon is remarkable in that it attributes non-dictionary meanings to commonly well understood used words. The evidence of the Applicant was that a student makes a "donation" upon undertaking a course of study. However, the term "donation" is not used in its ordinary sense. The minimum fee payable for each course of study presented by the Church was prescribed and payment of that fee was a pre-requisite to undertaking the course. The Applicant indicated that there was no objection to paying more: a comment that the Commissioner might make about the payment of income tax.
  5. One way for the Applicant of going about gaining those commissions was by using business names not suggestive of any association with the Church of Scientology; by hiring halls and lecturing; and by engaging in advertising, leaflet dropping and other practices designed to attract interest. Another was by persuading persons already with some experience of Scientology courses to undertake further courses.
  6. It is the latter field of activity which most directly gives rise to the present problems. The contention of the Applicant is that it was difficult for an FSM to persuade a prospective student to undertake any course of instruction if the FSM had not himself experienced the course. I accept that. It is surely easier for a salesman to sell a book, or a car, or a bottle of whisky if he has read the book, driven the car or tasted the whisky. So, the Applicant says, to increase his effectiveness as a salesman he undertook further courses of study to advance his understanding of Scientology and to extend his experience of the courses he was seeking to sell. He did so, so the argument runs, just as a pilot-instructor might undertake courses of advanced flying-training, or a restaurateur might extend his experience as a chef. He did so in 1979 by travelling overseas to there undertake some five weeks of study as a residential student in a course described as OT 5 (Operating Thetan 5) -a higher level of study than was available at the time in Australia. As a result, in addition to returning a nett income of $74 from "Consulting and Lecturing" ($1015 less expenses $941), he claimed deductions for

Depreciation on library $75

Communication course - allowed in 1975,

1979 and also allowed in prior year after

successful objection $11,307

Product De Bug Mini Course $286

Airfares $894

Residency (17/11/79 - sic - to 26/11/79) $995

Travel insurance $39

$13,596

I accept the evidence of the Applicant of the actual period of residency was from 17 October 1979. The Commissioner initially allowed the claims for depreciation and the De Bug Mini Course but disallowed the balance of the claims: $13,235. The Applicant objected. By a later amended assessment the Commisioner disallowed the De Bug Mini Course costs and again the Applicant objected.

  1. I find that all the expenses were incurred and that the "Product De Bug Mini course" was an incidental course of instruction directed to the same ends as all courses of study undertaken with the Church.
  2. In the following year further claims were made. Income from "Consultancy and Lecturing" was returned at $1043, but there was no clear line drawn to distinguish between the portion of the claimed expenses of $3,797 attributable to that income as distinct from expenses attributable to other small income earning activities commenced in that year. No problem arises in that regard because the claims so made were wholly allowed. However, there was a further claim made as follows:

"Re: COMMUNICATION COURSE

Advanced communication course (paid 79/80) $11,800

Interest on Loan for Course covers (sic)

(includes Fees) $1,238

Communication course in Sydney 594

$13,632

I accept that the sum of $11,800 related to the course undertaken during the 1980 year of income and that the expenses incurred were expended during the year of income ended 30 June 1980. As there is no request for reference properly bringing any such matter of dispute in that regard before me, and no power to permit a review, I say no more about it.

  1. However other claims are properly before me in relation to the year of income 30 June 1984. During that year of income the Applicant happened to be overseas in the course of his employment. While there he took: advantage of an opportunity to complete the course of study he had commenced late in 1979. As a result he incurred expenses claimed as deductions in the following terms:

"Expenses re advanced educational enterprises "Philadelphia Doctorate Course" $2,626

Special Councelling (sic)/Consulting course 2,909

Both claims were disallowed and, following objection the disallowance was confirmed. That decision is before me for review.

  1. The Commissioner established in evidence the history of the Applicant as disclosed by income tax returns for all of the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1988 inclusive. They disclosed the derivation of income through the Church of Scientology as follows:

$

Year ended 30 June 1980 1,015

1981 1,043

1982 1,940

1983 200

1984 946

1985 445

1987 75

1988 909

  1. The total of expenses as claimed, whether allowed or not allowed, cannot be as precisely identified. But it is helpful to record two generalisations put by the Applicant and not challenged by the Commissioner. The first is that over all the years of his association with the Church he has expended something of the order of $20,000 on courses and derived some $10,000 in income. The second is that it was his expectation in January 1989 that in the year of income 30 June 1989 his assessable income from that source would exceed the sum of deductions to be claimed by him in that year.
  2. The action of the Commissioner in disallowing some of the claims made in the years of income 30 June 1980 and 1984, and only in those years, is the subject of the proceedings before me. In the course of those proceedings the representative of the Commissioner contended that not only were the disallowed expenses not deductible, but also that other expenses which had been allowed were not allowable either. However, there was no suggestion that the Tribunal should increase the taxable income of the Applicant. The essential argument underlying the submission - which was not strongly put - was that the expenses all related to that most private and personal concern of man: his religious beliefs and practices. For that reason, it was argued, that the expenses were necessarily wholly of a "private" nature in the sense of s.51 (1) of the Act and were not allowable. I reject any such view of the matter. I adopt the principles expressed by Mason A.C.J. (as he then was) and Brennan J. in the High Court of Australia in the Church of the New Faith v Commissioner of Payroll Tax (ante) where, in defining the concept of a "religious institution", they affirmed that

"freedom of religion, the paradigm freedom of conscience, is of the essence of a free society"

but later went on to say

"the present case is not concerned with a personal freedom of religion; it is concerned with an exemption of a 'religious institution' from a fiscal burden imposed upon other institutions."

These references of course do not concern claims as to fiscal consequences for any religious institution. But as I said in Case V 13 (ante at 166 para 11)

"I am firmly of the view that the entitlement of the Applicant to the deductions claimed is not to be determined by reference to either the identity or characteristics of the entity presenting the course. Her entitlement would be no more or less if the courses in which she enrolled in had been presented by a university (whether public or, having regard to the fees paid, private); or by a major professional body, such as the Australian Medical Association; or by a prominent professional firm, such as some of the more entrepreneurial firms within the profession of accounting. What lies at the heart of the matter is not the social acceptability of the Church of Scientology but rather questions as to the relationship between the expenditure incurred by this lady and the derivation of her assessable income; and the character of the courses undertaken by her".

Indeed, it would be a strange and unreasonable interpretation of the Act which made income generated by an individual in the course of his religious practices assessable, but did not allow deductions for the expenses incurred in its derivation. An interpretation giving rise to such an unreasonable and unfair result is, for those very reasons, probably unsound. Nor is it a sufficient answer to the claims for the deductions to simply point to the disproportion between the expenditure incurred in a given period and the income derived in consequence of that expenditure, either in that period or later. As the High Court of Australia said in Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. F.C.T. ((1949) 78 C.L.R. 48 p.60):

"It is not for the Court or the Commissioner to say-how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income, but only how much he has spent."

Nonetheless that inter-relationship is a relevant circumstance to be taken into account in assessing the problem.

  1. Nor is it an answer to point to the circumstance that the experience was also directed to the satisfaction of personal wants or needs. The pilot-instructor is not to denied a deduction because he enjoys flying, or the restaurateur because he enjoys food and wine. The fiscal consequences of his income producing activities are the same ' whether the experience generating the income is pleasant or not. On the other hand as I said in Case R113 84 A.T.C. 750 at p. 752: a case dealing with claims of an employed travel agent to deductions for incidental expenses incurred in relation to overseas travel;

"One can well understand the proposition that travel experience is an advantage to a salesman of tour and travel services, just as the experience of drinking whisky may be of advantage to a salesman of whisky. But it does not follow for the whisky vendor that all expenditure he incurs relating to the consumption of whisky lacks any private character. Product sampling in the course of employment cannot be the only explanation for such expenditure".

  1. The Applicant accepts that, but for the derivation of assessable income, there would be no entitlement to any of the disputed deductions. I agree. But the issue arises because, by reason of that assessable income, the Applicant says that the disputed amounts are wholly deductible by reason of being income related. On the other hand the Commissioner says that, notwithstanding the assessable income derived, nothing is deductible because the expenditure is wholly "private". In my view neither contention is necessarily correct An expense may be wholly deductible even though the circumstances giving rise to the expenditure provided pleasure or personal satisfaction. The costs of a travel-writer in sailing the seven seas may be wholly deductible though pleasant. But at the same time, even for such a person, some travel expenses may be private and unrelated to the derivation of. assessable income. In some circumstances there may be a dual element. Sometimes the private element may be so insignificant as to not affect the character, or the characterisation, of the entire journey. In another situation the income-related element may be quite insignificant and without fiscal effect. In yet other circumstances, questions of apportionment may arise in which case the duty of the Tribunal -as stated by the High Court of Australia in Ronpibon Tin N.L. v F.C.T. ((1949) [1949] HCA 15; 78 C.L.R. 47) - is

"to make an apportionment which the facts of the particular case may seem to make just".

As the formal order of the Full Court had it in that case, the learned trial judge was directed to:

"decide as a matter of fact what part or proportion of (the amounts to be apportioned) was fairly and properly attributable to gaining the assessable income".

  1. In some circumstances where, for example, the question was of apportioning expenses attributable in equal parts to the derivation of assessable income and exempt income as an officer in the Citizen Military Forces, the apportionment may be most appropriately effected with mathematical precision: 50/50 (Case T 40, 86 A.T.C. 334). In other instances a judgment must be made without any attempt at mathematical precision (Ronpibon - ante -was such a case). In its most common form the problem arises in the need to apportion expenses wholly directed to income producing activities, but with those activities providing for a mix of assessable income and exempt income. However, it is not always so. The decision in Ure v. F.C.T. (81 A.T.C. 4100) provides an example. There the incurring of a liability to interest was partly directed to the generation of assessable income by way of on-lending borrowed monies at a much lower rate of interest than the borrowing rate; and partly by a desire to advance the interests of others. In that instance the determination of the Full Federal Court was that the problem of apportionment was to be resolved by allowing as a deduction of only so much of the out-going interest charges as equalled the assessable income generated.
  2. It was argued for the Commissioner that the incurring of the expenses and the undertaking of the courses provided the Applicant with a new income earning opportunity, (as in Maddelena v FCT 71 A.T.C. 4161) and therefore took on the character of capital. But I am not persuaded that that was so. The Applicant had already established himself in the position of being able to derive income from the Church of Scientology by negotiating sales. Rather than create a new earning opportunity, the courses contributed to the expansion of the skills and sales-abilities of the Applicant.
  3. In appraising the evidence presented I am satisfied that it is appropriate to regard the income-producing dimension as something incidental to the personal development objectives of the Applicant. The outlays were so large relative to the income derived as to indicate that no person would be likely to undertake the courses only for financial reasons. Further, although the principal motivation for undertaking the courses was personal development, I am not persuaded that the motivation in "selling" was so much the derivation of assessable income as it was establishing a tax-deductible basis for reducing the net cost to the Applicant of his personal, spiritual development. The question which then arises is whether any of, or any part of, the expenses of that personal development can be characterised as income-producing and something other than private. I can accept that there is a relationship between undertaking such courses and the development of an individual, including his effectiveness as an income producing person. But so much could be said of the lady who saw her studies with the Church of Scientology as increasing her proficiency as a seller of life assurance (Case V 13 - ante) and also of a medical practitioner who took drama lessons in order to assist himself in gaining advancement to specialist status within the profession and the increased levels of remuneration to be expected thereby (Case V189:87 A.T.C. 1082). If there were no more to the claim than in those two cases, nothing would be allowable. But, for this Applicant, there was something more: and that something more formed a closer nexus between the expenses incurred and the derivation of his income. He incurred expenses in studying further and by doing so improved his ability to sell "Scientology" and its courses and thereby derive an income. It may be that what occurred was the economic equivalent of the Church providing him with a discount on course fees. But that is not a proper approach to the assessment of income tax (Europa Oil (N.Z.) Ltd No. 2 v. C.I.R. (W.Z.) 1976 A.T.C. 6001). What he earned was assessable income. It was to be taken into account in assessing his taxable income (s.48). So too were his expenses to the extent allowable by s.51 (1) of the Act.
  4. An apportionment is called for. In making it I take into account the prospect of recurrent earnings and the possibility not realised prior to the currrent year of income - of a 'net profit'. I think that justice will be done if I allow a further $1,152 and $453 in the years of income ended 30 June 1980 and 1984 respectively. As the parties have agreed that a further $2,303 shall be allowed in relation to other matters in the 1984 year, I shall order that the decisions of the Commissioner be varied by reducing taxable income by $1,152 and by $2,756 for the years of income ended 30 June 1980 and 1984.
