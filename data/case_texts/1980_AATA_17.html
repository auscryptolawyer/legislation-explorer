Rattray and Commissioner for Superannuation [1980] AATA 17 (21 March 1980)

Last Updated: 13 March 2007



Superannuation - Retirement Benefits - Involuntary retirement -Cessation of status as eligible employee - Commencement of new employment as eligible employee - Whether re-commencement immediate

Superannuation Act 1976 (Comm) ss.3(3),58(3),62,80, Part IX;

Commonwealth Bureau of Roads Act 1964 (Comm) ss.5,14,15,16, 18,19;

Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 (Comm) ss.5,8

ALAN LINDSAY RATTRAY

COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION





AAT Decision No 369

No.12001 of 1979

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Mr W.B. Borthwick (Member)

Mr R.B. Hutchison (Member)

21 March 1980

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No.12001 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: ALAN LINDSAY RATTRAY

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION The Tribunal decides:

(i) To affirm the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made by his delegate and confirmed by the Commissioner upon reconsideration pursuant to S.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") on 21 November 1978 to the extent that the same had the effect that upon the cessation of the employment of the applicant with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads ("the Bureau") he did not become entitled to any lump sum benefit pursuant to the provisions of s.62 of the Act.

(ii) To set aside the decision of the Commissioner to the extent that it had the effect that upon the cessation of the employment of the applicant with the Bureau an amount was payable to him out of the Superannuation Fund pursuant to s.80 of the Act.

(iii) To remit the matter for reconsideration by the Commissioner in accordance with a direction that the application of the Act in the relevant circumstances be considered upon the footing that upon the applicant commencing employment with Telecom Australia on 1 July 1979 and upon his having thereby immediately again become an eligible employee within the meaning of the Act he was by s.3(3) of the Act deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee under the Act notwithstanding the cessation of his employment with the Bureau at the end of 29 June 1977.

(iv) (iv) To grant liberty to apply to the Tribunal should the same be necessary.

DATED the 21st day of March 1980.

(R.K. Todd)

Senior Member

(R.B. Hutchison)

Member



(W.B. Borthwick)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No.12001 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: ALAN LINDSAY RATTRAY

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. This application for review is concerned with the entitlement of the applicant under the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") having regard to the events surrounding the cessation of his employment with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads ("the Bureau") when that body ceased to exist at midnight on 29 June 1977 and with his subsequent commencement of employment with the Australian Telecommunications Commission ("Telecom") on 1 July 1977. In the circumstances the application is concerned with a fact situation closely related to that with which the Tribunal was concerned in Re Bayley and Commissioner for Superannuation (No.78/12031) (which we shall refer to as "Re Bayley") and which came before the Federal Court of Australia on appeal as Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley (as yet unreported) which we shall refer to as "Bayley' s Case". The fact however that the applicant in this instance re-commenced employment in the circumstances to be hereafter set out has the consequence that additional and different problems must now be faced.

2. The applicant commenced employment with the Bureau on 28 January 1975. The Bureau was constituted by the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads Act 1964, but its existence was effectively terminated as the result of the commencement, on 30 June 1977, of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 ("the repealing Act").

3.The Bureau was, by section 5 of the Bureau of Roads Act, established as a body corporate, with consequential provisions. Section 14 of that Act provided that the functions of the Bureau were to investigate and from time to time to report to the Minister on, matters relating to roads or road transport for the purpose of assisting the Government of the Commonwealth in the consideration by the Government of the grant of financial assistance by the Parliament to the States in connection with roads or road transport, and to investigate, and report to the Minister on, any matter referred to the Bureau under section 15.
4.Under section 16 of that Act the Bureau was given power to do all things necessary or convenient to be done for or in connection with the performance of its functions, and in particular to engage persons, or to arrange for persons, to advise and inform the Bureau on any matter being investigated by the Bureau.
5.Sections 18 and 19 of the Bureau of Roads Act should be set out in full:
"18(1) Subject to the next succeeding sub-section, the Bureau may appoint such officers and engage such employees as it thinks necessary for the purposes of this Act.

(2) The Minister may, from time to time, determine the maximum number of officers who may be appointed under the last preceding sub-section and the maximum number of employees who may be engaged under the last preceding sub-section, and the Bureau shall not appoint officers in excess of the number applicable in respect of officers and shall not engage employees in excess of the number applicable in respect of employees.

19. The terms and conditions of service of persons appointed or engaged under the last preceding section are such as are, with the approval of the Public Service Board, determined by the Bureau."

6.By section 3 of the repealing Act the principal Act was repealed; and by section 5 of the repealing Act it was provided that as from the day of commencement of that Act (later proclaimed as 30 June 1977) any rights, property or assets that immediately before that day were vested in the Bureau were vested in the Commonwealth, and the Commonwealth became, by force of the section, liable to pay and discharge any debts, liabilities or obligations of the Bureau that existed immediately before that day. For present purposes it is enough to say that when the -Bureau ceased to exist the position or office which the applicant held with the Bureau also ceased to exist. So much is undoubted having regard to the decision of the Federal Court on appeal in Bayley's Case.
7.After it came into existence, the Bureau, with the necessary approval of the Public Service Board, determined terms and conditions of service applicable to officers and employees engaged by it for the purpose of the Act. The applicant commenced employment with the Bureau on 28 January 1975. He commenced as a contributor to the superannuation fund established under the Superannuation Act 1922 on 20 March 1975. It is to be presumed that an appropriate direction was made having the effect that he was deemed to be an employee within the meaning of section 4 of that Act. When the Act commenced on 1 July 197 6, he became an "eligible employee" within the meaning of section 3 of the Act as being a person who, immediately before the commencement of the Act, was an employee for the purpose of the superseded Act, and had continued to be, or was, such an employee: See clause (a) of the definition of "eligible employee" in section 3 of the Act. He was, it seems, also a "permanent employee" and thus fell within clause (b) of the definition as being, within the definition of "permanent employee" found in section 3, a "person employed....by an approved authority in a permanent capacity".
8.On 29 June 1977, the applicant's position was "Principal Transport Planner (Class 9)". Prior to that date the applicant had been offered the opportunity to elect to accept appointment to an office in the Public Service as a Clerk Class 9 in the Department of Transport. In order however to put this fact into perspective it is necessary to go back in time to December 1976. On 17 December 1976 the applicant responded by letter to an advertisement which had been placed by Telecom offering a position as a Social Scientist. On 3 March 1977 the applicant was offered temporary employment by Telecom as a Principal Planning Officer Class 10. The applicant discussed this offer of employment with Telecom and it was apparently resolved to advertise a permanent position with the consequence that the applicant decided not to accept the temporary position that had been offered pending the outcome of his application for the permanent position. He advised this decision to Telecom by a letter dated 20 March 1977. The permanent position having been duly advertised in the Australian Government Gazette the applicant by letter dated 11 April 1977 duly applied for the position.
9.By letter dated 16 May 1977 the applicant was offered a permanent appointment as "Principal Planning Officer (Class 10)" with Telecom. The offer was stated to be conditional upon completing certain formalities and he was requested to indicate in writing within 14 days whether he intended to accept or decline the offer of appointment. By letter dated 24 May 1977 from the Public Service Board the applicant was offered an appointment as a Clerk Class 9 in the Department of Transport. This letter stated that the government had decided that the Bureau and the then Bureau of Transport Economics were to be amalgamated to form a new Bureau of Transport Economics. The new Bureau was to be staffed under the Public Service Act and it was stated:
"In accordance with the normal practice staff from the Bureau of Roads will be transferred to the Australian Public Service under Division 9(F) of the Public Service Act.

To ensure that staff from the Bureau of Roads and the present Bureau of Transport Economics are on an equal footing when the amalgamation occurs it has been decided that staff from the Bureau of Roads should transfer to the Public Service at their substantive level in the Bureau of Roads."

A good deal more detail could be given about the form and substance of this offer of employment and such detail was so given in the Reasons for Decision of the majority of the members of this Tribunal in Re Bayley. That decision however, along with the decision of the Federal Court on appeal in Bayley's Case and the facts before us in this application make it, in our view, unnecessary to go into such detail or to attempt to make any assessment of the extent to which the offer of employment that was made had indeed constituted an offer of employment within the Public Service upon terms and conditions that might be identified as being materially the same as those to which the applicant had been subject as an employee of the Bureau.

10. By letter dated 26 May 1977 addressed to Telecom the applicant advised that he accepted the offer of permanent appointment as "Principal Planning Officer (Class 10)", with Telecom. He indicated that he would await the advice of Telecom requiring the formalities that needed completion and continued:

"Concerning the date of commencement I expect to be available well before June 30, but in any event no later than July 1st, 1977. I will be discussing the question of a more definite commencing date with you. Please do not hesitate to contact me should these arrangements be unsatisfactory."
11.By letter dated 27 May 1977 to the office of the Public Service Board the applicant thanked the writer for the letter of 24 May 1977 outlining an offer of appointment as a Clerk Class 9 in the Department of Transport, but stated that before responding to that offer written clarification was sought regarding the terms of the offer. Detailed questions in relation thereto were then set out.
12.Notification of the appointment of the applicant as "Principal Planning Officer $18552-19208 (Class 10)., Planning Directorate, National Telecommunications Planning Branch, Melbourne, 31.3.77 (No.7)" was contained in Australian Government Gazette No.PS24, 16 June 1977. At some stage a document headed "Appointment Certificate" was brought into existence which noted that the applicant had been offered the appointment described and which contained the following statement: "Subject to meeting the requirements of Section 39 of the Telecommunications Act the appointment will take effect on and from the date of commencing duty, or on and from the date of this memorandum if the appointee is already employed in the service." There is nothing to indicate when this document was completed. It contains a statement that the date upon which the applicant commenced duty was 1 July 1977 and it may be that the document was completed on the date upon which he in fact commenced duty, but at all events it was agreed at the hearing that the appointment was one which would, as the certificate stated, take effect on and from the date of commencing duty, the applicant not being already employed in the service of Telecom.
13.On 27 June 1977 the applicant wrote to the Chairman of the Bureau a letter in the following terms:

"I understand from Office Circular No. 46/1977 of June 20th, 197 7 that my employment by the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads is to be involuntarily terminated on June 29th, 1977 as a result of the proclamation of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977. Consequently I request you to take the necessary steps for the authorisation of payment to me of:

(a) lump sum benefit in lieu of long service leave pursuant to Section 17 of the Long Service Leave (Commonwealth Employees) Act 1976;

(b) lump sum benefit under Section 62 of the Superannuation Act 1976; and

(c) lump sum payment in lieu of recreation leave and annual leave bonus entitlements.
I enclose completed forms for payment of Long Service and Superannuation benefits as outlined above."
14.As previously stated the Board ceased to exist with effect from the end of the day preceding 30 June 1977, and the applicant's employment with the Board then likewise ceased to exist. The applicant was not, on 30 June 1977, in employment at all. On 1 July 1977 he duly commenced employment with Telecom. It is plain that the choice of 1 July 1977 as his day of commencing duty with Telecom was one made by the applicant. We do not find it necessary in the circumstances to consider whether such choice was deliberately made for the purpose of bringing about a situation in which the eligible employment of the applicant within the meaning of the Superannuation Act 1976 ceased at the end of 29 June 1977. Our conclusion, as will appear, is one based upon the objective facts and not upon the intentions of the applicant. The considerations which have led us to this conclusion and to our putting on one side an argument put on behalf of the Commissioner that the applicant was involved in a "colourable" device to create a "sham" break designed for the purpose of creating a break in the continuity of his eligible employment must be postponed at this point in order to set out the statutory provisions with which we are concerned.
15.Section 58(3) of the Act provides, so far as relevant, as follows:
"(3) Where, before a person who is an eligible employee attains the age of 60 years, he ceases to be an eligible employee by reason that -

(a) ........

(b) ........

(c) ........

(d) his position or office ceases to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise; or

The person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have retired involuntarily."

16. Division 2 of the Act deals with early retirement benefits, and by section 58(1) a person is deemed to have ceased to be an eligible employee by early retirement if the person is deemed by section 58(2). to have retired voluntarily, or by section 58(3) to have retired involuntarily. Sections 59 and 60 provide for a benefit in cases of early retirement. In the case of involuntary retirement, section 62 enables an election to be made for a lump sum benefit amounting to three and a half times the amount of accumulated basic contributions. In Re Bayley reference was made to some circularity that appears to exist in these provisions but it is unnecessary to pursue this. Section 80 provides for payment out of the fund of a lump sum benefit of an amount equal to a person's accumulated contributions where that person ceases to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of death, and that person is not entitled to benefit under various Divisions of the Act including, relevantly for various purposes, Division 2 of Part V relating to early retirement benefit.

17. The Commissioner's decision was that the provisions of section 58(3) of the Act did not apply and that the applicant could not be deemed to have retired involuntarily from the Bureau, with the consequence that the applicant was entitled only to a refund of contributions under section 80. The applicant had claimed to be entitled to the lump sum benefit provided for by section 62. At the hearing it was first contended on behalf of the Commissioner that the position now was that the applicant was entitled to no benefit whatsoever but as explained below this contention could only apply to the situation obtaining while the applicant's employment with Telecom subsisted and while he thus retained the status of eligible employee.

18. It will immediately be seen that the fact situation in this application is quite different from that which obtained in Re Bayley. There, Mr Bayley, having rejected the offer of employment within the Public Service, did not return to eligible employment. The present applicant did so return. In these circumstances Mr Huttner, who appeared for the Commissioner at the hearing, contended that section 3(3) of the Act has application. Section 3 is an interpretation section, and the subsection referred to provides as follows:

"(3) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and, immediately after so ceasing, again becomes an eligible employee, he shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed not to have so ceased."

19. If of course section 3(3) of the Act applies, the consequence is that the applicant's eligible employment never ceased and that he was not entitled even to the refund of contributions pursuant to section 80 which the Commissioner's decision had conceded. This situation raised the possibility that the Commissioner's new contention meant that he was contemplating the making of a decision other than that under review, one which had not been reconsidered pursuant to the provisions of section 154 of the Act and in respect of which the Tribunal might indeed lack the power to review.

20. Certain facts must now be referred to which, while not relevant to the determination of the substance of the issue before us, are relevant to our ability to resolve that issue. These are that the applicant in fact voluntarily resigned from Telecom on 12 June 1979 and he was refunded, pursuant to section 80, the accumulated contributions made by him during his employment by Telecom. The contributions made by him while employed by the Bureau were, and still are, retained in the Superannuation Fund. Had he not so resigned, the position would have been that, given the applicability of section 3(3), he would have been entitled to a refund of contributions neither under section 62 nor under section 80 of the Act. He would, as a continuing eligible employee, have been obliged to leave his contributions in the fund. Since however he has ceased to be an eligible employee, refund of his contributions under section 80 must, it seems, now follow.

21. The case for the applicant was that there had been a clear break in the applicant's eligible employment and that in those circumstances section 3(3) of the Act had no application. Mr Smith, who appeared for the applicant, conceded that it had been held in other contexts that the word "immediately" need not mean "instanter", but he then went on to argue that in the context of the Act, it must take its literal meaning. As we understand it, Mr Smith's submission was that Part IX of the Act headed "Preservation of Rights of Certain Eligible Employees" provides a procedure which allows a person who, prior to becoming an eligible employee under the Act, had been in other employment, to elect to preserve rights he may have held under a superannuation scheme in the course of his previous employment. By virtue of section 128(1) of the Act, such an election can be made before becoming an eligible employee, or within a period up to three months after becoming an eligible employee, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows. It was admitted for the Commissioner that the provisions of Part IX are wide enough to cover the case of a person whose previous employment had been as an eligible employee, and indeed this would appear to be recognized in the legislation by the definition of "superannuation scheme" in section 125(1) of the Act. Mr Smith then contended that as Part IX constituted a scheme for dealing with gaps in the employment of an eligible employee, there would be an ambiguity in the legislation unless the operation of section 3(3) was restricted to a situation where there was no gap. Hence, it was argued, the word "immediately" in section 3(3) must take its literal meaning. Whilst this argument might be superficially attractive, it is not one that ultimately commends itself to the Tribunal. It seems to us that when construing a passage in a statute by reference to the whole of the instrument there is a danger that ambiguities may be identified by paying too little attention to the subject passage and too much attention to what the rest of the Act does. If one concentrates attention on the operation of section 3(3) of the Act the difficulties posed by Mr Smith diminish. Part IX of the Act operates on the premise that there has been a cessation of employment prior to a person becoming an eligible employee. But the provisions of Part IX are subject to the interpretative provisions of the Act which includes section 3(3). Section 3(3) provides that a person who was previously an eligible employee does not cease to be an eligible employee if it can be said that he immediately again became an eligible employee. Thus, if section 3(3) of the Act is found to apply, there has been no gap in status as an eligible employee and it is hard to see how there could be found to have been a "termination" of employment upon which a transfer value became payable to the eligible employee for the purposes of Part IX. The spheres of operation of Part IX and section 3(3) are not identical. Part IX allows an employee to whom section 3(3) does not apply, be he a previous eligible employee who could not be said to have immediately again become an eligible employee or someone whose previous employment was not as an eligible employee, to preserve his earlier superannuation rights. The coexistence of these provisions does not appear to us to create any ambiguity and we cannot see how an examination of Part IX can aid an investigation into the interpretation to be given to the word "immediately" in section 3(3) of the Act.

22. For the Commissioner, it was contended first that section 3(3) of the Act did apply, and that nothing in the judgments of the members of the Federal Court in Bayley's Case went against the notion that the word "immediately" occurring in section 3(3) could accommodate a break in time of the kind that had occurred here. Reference was made in particular to the judgment of Lockhart J. which is referred to below. Secondly, it was contended that nothing can preclude a consideration of all the circumstances surrounding a break in eligible employment and that a colourable or contrived break in employment would not prevent the application of section 3(3). It was said that it was necessary to enquire whether a break was a genuine break and that it would be possible to reject a break as a colourable or sham break designed for the purpose of negating the applicability of section 3(3). Reliance was placed upon certain authorities in relation to section 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Thirdly, it was said that if the Tribunal should conclude that section 3(3) did not apply in the proved circumstances, there was nothing in the judgments of the Federal Court in Bayley's Case that precluded the Tribunal from again giving consideration to the argument that had been advanced to the Tribunal in Re Bayley based upon the words "by reason that" occurring in section 58(3) of the Act, and which in this context would involve the proposition that the only reason for the applicant having ceased to be an eligible employee was that he had organised a brief cessation of employment in order to achieve that end.

23. At the outset we should say that we cannot accept either the second or the third argument put on behalf of the Commissioner. As to the second, we believe that there is nothing in the statutory provisions with which we are concerned which admits consideration of the subjective elements referred to. The argument based upon section 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act would in any event have to take account of recent decisions of the High Court such as Mullens v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290 and Cridland v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4538. As to the third argument, that is to say the argument based upon the words "by reason that", we consider that it is precluded by the decision of the Federal Court in Bayley's Case. It is nevertheless unnecessary to pronounce finally upon these arguments and we do not stay to examine them. Instead we propose to limit our consideration to the first argument.

24. As we have said, this argument is quite objective. It involves no finding as to the subjective intentions of the applicant in the occurrence of an apparent break in his eligible employment and we make none. The argument is dependent upon it being correct to conclude that the requirement of immediacy imposed by section 3(3) of the Act is not such as to demand that there be no break whatsoever, however small, between the previous eligible employment and that which succeeds it. That no such demand is made is, we consider, clear from the reasons of Lockhart J. in Bayley's Case. At p.21 of his judgment His Honour said:

"Mr Phillips QC contended that sub-section (3) has no application to the present case, where the respondent never ceased to be a permanent employee, because of the principle as to a day being an indivisible unit of time. He contended that sub-section (3) applies to cases where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and again becomes one, provided he does so 'immediately' thereafter. He contended that 'immediately' does not necessarily mean instanter; but rather has a meaning which may vary from case to case, sometimes allowing a gap in time of hours, days or weeks. In sub-section (3) it has the meaning of 'within a reasonably short space of time' in all the circumstances. In support of his contention, Mr Phillips QC referred to the definition of the words 'immediately' and 'forthwith' in Stroud's Judicial Dictionary and Butterworth's Words and Phrases Legally Defined; Permanent Trustee Company of New South Wales Limited v. D'Apice & Ors. [1968] HCA 29; (1967-1968) 118 CLR 105; and Dorsman v. Nichol (1978) 20 ALR 231.

Mr Phillips QC contended that sub-section (3) is a deeming provision merely used for more abundant caution to put beyond doubt a particular construction that otherwise might be uncertain. He relied on St. Aubyn v Attorney-General [1951] UKHL 3; 1952 AC 15 per Lord Radcliffe at p 53 and Hunter Douglas Australia Pty Limited v. Perma Blinds [1970] HCA 63; (1969-1970) 122 CLR 49 per Windeyer J. at pp.65-67.

A deeming provision may be included in an interpretation section in a Statute for the purpose of extending the meaning of a defined term to something it would not mean in ordinary parlance: see St. Aubyn y. Attorney-General (supra) and Hunter Douglas Australia Pty Limited v. Perma Blinds (supra) at the pages mentioned earlier. In my opinion this is the purpose of sub-section (3). It is intended to ensure that a person does not lose his status as an eligible employee merely because he ceases to be one and later becomes one again, provided the gap in time is not unreasonably large. It is designed to cover all cases where a person has a particular position or holds a particular office and ceases to do so, but 'immediately' thereafter takes another position or office, in each case being a person who satisfies the statutory denotation of a 'permanent employee'. It matters not whether the interval of time is a second, a day, or a week provided the person again becomes an eligible employee 'immediately' after ceasing to be one. The general rule that a day is an indivisible unit of time has no application to the provisions of the Act relating to this appeal." (underlining supplied)
25.While it may be said that His Honour's remarks were obiter, they were said in a context which was extremely close in point of the facts and was identical in terms of the relevant statutory provisions, to the context of the present case. For this reason at least, the Tribunal must find them to be highly persuasive and we have not been convinced by the applicant that we should not follow them. In any event, the Tribunal feels no reluctance in adopting His Honour's views as, with respect, they appear to us to accord with the authorities: See the analysis given by Forster C.J. in Dorsman v. Nichol 1978 ALR 231 especially at p.237.
26.The question then is, as we have said, an objective one. Further, we are not concerned to say in the abstract how long a break may be before it becomes "unreasonably large" and the requirement of immediacy be lost. We are concerned simply to decide whether, in relation to the proved facts in this application, the applicant, having ceased to be an eligible employee when the Bureau went out of existence, again became an eligible employee immediately after so ceasing. In our opinion he did. Our reasons for so concluding are as follows.

27. The break was very short. At the very most, it must have been about 33 hours. The length of that break must be considered in the light of the following factors: First, the applicant engaged in no other employment in the meantime, but went from one to the other. As far as employment was concerned no "non-eligible" employment intervened between the first eligible employment and that which succeeded it. It is to be noted that in Reseck v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 38; (1974-1975) 133 CLR 45 at 50, Gibbs J. said that "In most cases in which a workman ceased his employment on a Friday and commenced employment again with the same employer on the following Monday it would be impossible to say that his employment had ever been terminated". In fact in that case it was not open to the High Court so to conclude because the case stated to the Court contained a finding that there had been a termination of employment, but His Honour's view is clearly stated and while the situation is not precisely in pari materia with that in the present application, it expresses something of the notion that has commended itself to us. Again, we consider that it is of significance that not only was there a very short gap between the cessation of one eligible employment and the commencement of another, but also that the process of seeking appointment to the second employment had commenced well before the first ceased. The appointment to the second employment was in fact made before the first ceased, and needed only for its consummation the actual commencement of duty. Indeed before the first employment ceased, the applicant had not only accepted employment with Telecom but had nominated 1 July as the latest date by which he would commence such employment. In the result we find that by virtue of section 3(3) of the Act the applicant is to be deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee. We have considered whether it may be said that the provisions of section 3(3) are intended to be invoked only for the benefit of an eligible employee and not to defeat a claim that the employee has identified as benefiting him. We do not consider that such an argument could be supported. Section 3(3) is a general interpretative provision and the whole of the Act is subject to its provisions. It is stated to be "for the purposes of this Act".

28.Reference should finally be made to the tender by Mr Smith of a document headed "Section 10: Transferability and the Preservation of Rights". This appeared to be an extract from a superseded Superannuation Board Instruction Manual relating to the 1922 Act. In relation to a section of that Act similar to the provisions now before us, the Instruction said: "If a permanent officer of the Commonwealth resigns...and immediately (next working day) after the date of termination becomes employed..." (underlining supplied). It may be that in relation to the 1922 Act the Commissioner had been of the opinion that "immediately" means the next working day, but the Tribunal does not consider that an administrative statement, such as this Instruction, which has no legal basis, can be used to found any conclusion as to the correct interpretation to be placed on the word "immediately" in section 3(3) of the 1976 Act.
29.The Commissioner's decision is expressed in various ways in the documents. The kernel of it is that the provisions of section 58(3) and thus more particularly of section 62 of the Act did not apply to the applicant upon the cessation of his employment with the Bureau. We shall affirm the decision to that extent, but to the extent that 'it had the effect that upon the cessation of the employment of the applicant with the Bureau an amount was payable to him out of the Superannuation Fund pursuant to section 80 of the Act it should be set aside, and in accordance with section 43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act we shall remit the matter for reconsideration by the Commissioner with a direction that the application of the Act in the relevant ciircumstances be considered upon the footing that upon the applicant commencing employment with Telecom on 1 July 1979 and upon his having thereby immediately again become an eligible employee within the meaning of the Act, he was by section 3(3) of the Act deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee under the Act, a cessation which would otherwise have occurred by reason of his position with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads having ceased to exist at the end of 29 June 1977.
