INCOME TAX - "Trust stripping" - Sham - Purported distribution held to be void -Application of "default of appointment" clause in trust deed - Whether S.260 applied where beneficiary not aware of his entitlement under the trust - Whether in circumstances penalties should be remitted.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1981, sec 260

This matter was originally heard by the late Mr Justice Hartigan on 31 January and 1 February 1989. Regrettably the President became seriously ill some time after the hearing and died in May 1990 before being able to hand down his decision. Because the applicant, Bill Murphy ("Bill") was unwilling to have the matter decided "on the papers", the matter was re-listed before me for a hearing de novo. Adjournments were sought from time to time on the ground that the taxpayer, who had been represented before Hartigan P by Mr David Russell QC, with him Mr O'Grady, had an application before the Legal Aid Commission to have the same or similar senior representation before me. After several further applications on the basis that Bill was receiving no response to his claim for Legal Aid, I refused the last such application on the ground that there must be a finis litem and set the matter down for hearing on 8 April 1991. In the result, Bill appeared unrepresented.



2. At the hearing de novo, Bill was prepared to adopt the evidence of the earlier proceedings. However, because much turned on his credibility, the Crown now insisted on its right to cross-examine him so that I could form my own view on where the truth lay. In the circumstances, I indicated that I had no option but to read the transcript of the earlier proceedings, if only to flesh out the whole of the background to this vast and artificial scheme which led to these proceedings.



3. The issue in the case involves an alleged distribution to Bill in the 1981 tax year of some $162,978 from a trust (which will be referred to as to as the John Murphy Trust). When one turns to that trust's 1981 return, it is stated that the net income of the trust was dealt with as follows: (i) a distribution to the Murphy Subscribable Unit Trust of $397,250, (ii) a distribution to the Murphy No 2 Subscribable Unit Trust of $535,515, (iii) a distribution of $66,660 to the Conduit Discretionary Trust; a total of $999,425. The Commissioner treated this distribution as void as against him in reliance on sec 260 of the Tax Act ("the Act"), and, by recourse to a "default of appointment" clause in the trust deed, "distributed" the net income of the trust (as recalculated by him according to a formula of which the Tribunal was not apprised) equally to the six primary beneficiaries whose names appear in the deed and who take in the event that the trustee fails to exercise his discretion to pay the income of the trust by 30 June in any one year "in the manner aforesaid". The primary beneficiaries are John Murphy, his wife, the three children of the marriage (including Bill) and one grandchild, each share coming to $162,978. It is common ground that the taxpayer did not actually receive one cent of this amount. Whether or not sec 260 has such wide-ranging effect will, in turn, depend on whether or not the taxpayer knew of the scheme, and if so, the extent of his knowledge and, if he knew of it, whether he participated in it or deliberately distanced himself from it. Again, it may be significant whether, having become aware of the trust/scheme, he renounced all benefit under it at the first opportunity. In the alternative, the Crown submits that the whole arrangement is a sham.



4. It is not disputed that the impugned arrangement involved an highly artificial scheme which had, as its sole purpose, the diversion of income from other John Murphy controlled entities into the John Murphy Trust by a "stripping" operation, a process which involved sending the income from the various entities through an almost impenetrable maze (set out below) during which, it was hoped, the income would somehow shed its tax burden, coming out at the exit duly cleansed, falling happily into the lap of the tax-shy John Murphy via the John Murphy Trust.



5. This whole saga can be said to have begun with a deed of settlement made on 11 February 1981 (see cl. VII of Agreed Facts below), the settlor being one Stanley Thomas English, a partner of Peate Marwick Hungerford. The initial trust fund consisted of $10, the trustee was A & B Management (No 118), the name of the trust was the John Murphy Trust, the primary beneficiaries are set out in para 3 above, the secondary beneficiaries are "any corporation or person nominated by the Principal (John Murphy) whether in his her or its own right or in the capacity of trustee or any other person(s) or corporation(s)". The tertiary beneficiary, albeit one destined to be disappointed, is the Australian Heart Foundation. Clause 3 deals with respect to the income of the trust fund.



3. DISCRETIONS AS TO INCOME OF THE TRUST FUND



3.1 The Trustee shall in each year determine the income of the Trust Fund after allowing for all expenses of the Trust Fund.



3.2 The Trustee may at any time prior to the expiration of any year which ends before or upon the Perpetuity Date determine with respect to all or any parts of the Income of the Trust Fund of such year:



(i) to pay apply or set aside the same or any part thereof for all or one or more of the Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Beneficiaries living or in existence at the time of the determination, and (without limiting the generality of the foregoing) may pay or disburse any outgoings associated with such income or any advance of income to any Beneficiary out of the corpus of the fund and recoup from the Income received thereafter such amounts as are paid out of corpus;



(ii) to accumulate the same or any part thereof;



PROVIDED THAT if the Trustee shall not by the Thirtieth day of June have exercised its discretion to pay apply set aside or accumulate the whole or any part of such income in the manner aforesaid then the Trustee shall hold the income not so paid set aside or accumulated for that year in trust for such of the Primary Beneficiaries as are then living or in existence ... .

8. Bill, having recently obtained and read the transcript of his own evidence before Mr Justice Hartigan, was prepared to adopt it without further emendation. In cross-examination by Mr Hack, of learned counsel for the Crown, the following was elicited.



9. Bill is a son of John Murphy and one of the beneficiaries of the John Murphy Trust. In recovery proceedings before the Federal Court, he deposed - and re-affirmed again before me - that he was unaware of the existence of the trust until he received a notice of assessment for the 1981 tax year on or about 3 May 1984. He further deposed that he had some, albeit limited, business dealings with his father in the late 1970s and early 1980s, "but most of my business was my business, and his business was his business." At the relevant time, both father and son operated from the same premises, albeit John Murphy had his office upstairs whilst Bill's business premises were downstairs.



10. In an affidavit sworn on 9 June 1988, Bill asserts that he "disclaimed any beneficial interest or entitlement in or to the income or corpus of the (John Murphy Trust)". There is no evidence when or how the purported disclaimer was said to have been brought about.



11. Exh 20 in the earlier proceedings is Bill's tax return for the 1986 tax year (Form B). An attachment (p. 11) to that return contains the following:



EXPENSES - KENMORE SALE



The taxpayer guaranteed his father Mr John Murphy on business loans in approximately the sum of $2 million. Mr Murphy, Senior, subsequently defaulted on the loans and the guarantees were called up and as a consequence Mr Bill Murphy was required to make good the guarantees. The guarantees were made by Mr Bill Murphy in anticipation of benefiting both directly and indirectly from the profit making venture of Mr John Murphy.



Mr John Murphy was involved in land development and Mr Bill Murphy was running the Real Estate Agency, Williams & Co at Ipswich at the time. There was an agreement between Williams & Co and John Murphy where Williams & Co was given the exclusive sale rights to sell the property developments for which the money was raised. Mr Bill Murphy also stood to gain being a beneficiary under the John Murphy Family Trust which Trust was involved in the property development. As a result, the taxpayer has had to pay large sums over the last few years including the present tax year where he was obliged to sell his dwelling house to satisfy the guarantee to Natcorp Ltd and had to pay $11,338.09 expenses for the sale of that property which then resulted in the partial discharge of his obligations to Natcorp. We therefore contend that the expenses were spent in gaining or producing assessable income or the nexus was sufficient to enable the taxpayer to obtain a tax deduction for this amount. (my italics)



Bill agrees that he signed the return but does not recall specific details ("It was prepared by the accountants. I signed it off. It's as simple as that. That's what they were paid to do, and they were paid, and I signed it.").



12. Pausing here, at first blush it is not easy to reconcile the statements in the return with Bill's oral evidence and his affidavit. Thus, on the one hand it is stated that Bill first became aware of the existence of the trust and his interest therein in May 1984, when he received his assessment, after which, it seems, he disclaimed any beneficial interest in the income and/or corpus of the trust. Yet in his 1986 tax return appears the clear admission that he stood to gain from the trust "which was involved in the property development". Put crudely, Bill asserts an interest in the trust two years after having disclaimed it.



13. In his evidence before me, Bill claimed to be unaware of the meaning of the words "disclaimed any interest". When I questioned him as to what he understood by these words, he replied:



"Reading it now, Dr Gerber, I would think it says that I didn't get any money from this John Murphy Trust strip. That's what I think it means".



14. Mr Hack, having obtained the concession that Bill had been "basically" aware of the steps involved in his father's trust strip, proceeded to cross-examine him about his involvement with a Mr John Williamson, a farmer and business associate of Bill, for the purpose of establishing that Bill was the settlor of a Williamson trust which was involved in a trust strip similar to the one undertaken by John Murphy. Bill claimed he could not recall anything relating to a Williamson trust, although he was compelled to agree that he had signed the trust deed.



"Mr Williamson was a farmer. I know little about the law. He knew even less. He paid lawyers and he paid accountants for advice which he took. No, he wouldn't discuss the document with me, he would simply sign it where he was told to sign it".



15. Exhibit 13 is a copy of a Deed of Settlement dated 19 February 1981 in which the Principal is Bill. The terms are similar to the Deed entered into by his father eight days earlier, save that the primary beneficiaries are Bill, his father, mother, brother and sister. The secondary beneficiaries are identical to those in his father's deed, as is the charitable intent to benefit the Australian Heart Foundation as tertiary beneficiary.



16. Exhibit 16 is a similar deed in identical terms, again executed on 19 February 1981, involving Mr Williamson, the primary beneficiaries being Mr Williamson and (semble) his wife and daughter. The Australian Heart Foundation is once again earmarked for benevolence (provided there is no distribution and all the Williamsons having gone down with the Titanic, God forbid).



17. The taxpayer was asked whether he recalled being a director of Murphy Corporation Pty Ltd. Bill claimed he had no recollection of such an event.



18. The rest of the cross-examination consisted of questions relating to the borrowing of money by Bill from his father's company prior to 1981. Bill readily conceded that such borrowing had taken place but would not agree that the money came from the trustee of the John Murphy Trust.



"I wouldn't have a clue which company it was, Mr Hack, but the money would have come from my father. I wouldn't know which company it would have come from or which bank account, or any such thing".



19. In the earlier proceedings, Mr English was called who was at the relevant time the accountant for Mr John Murphy and his numerous companies. He deposed that, looking at the tax returns of Murphy Corporation Pty Ltd for 1980 and 1981 prepared by him, he was satisfied that Bill (i) was a director of the company, (ii) had outstanding loans of $36,581 in 1980 and $68,483 in 1981 ("they would be loan entries, for certain"). Unfortunately, most of Bill's records for those years were destroyed in a fire. However, Bill's 1982 tax return, prepared by Mr English, shows under the heading "unsecured loan" that an amount of $10,000 was owed by the John Murphy Trust, as well as showing movements in the loan from Murphy Corporation Pty Ltd to Bill. The witness deposed that the return would have been completed around late October 1983. He also recalled, albeit vaguely, that Bill had set up the Bill Murphy Trust in or around that time (1981), which was a trust similar to the one entered into by his father at or around the same time, and that Bill engaged in a trust strip at or about the same time as the John Murphy Trust was stripped. Bill was recalled after this evidence and denied that the trust was stripped, alleging that it was created on the advice of his (then) accountant, Mr Betar.



20. Mr John Murphy gave evidence before Hartigan J. He was asked in evidence-in-chief how the formation of the John Murphy Trust came about. His answer was crisp and to the point:



The circumstances were that I had a fair amount of profit emerging in that year 1981. At that time, Hungerfords were (my) accountants and auditors. I was informed by another accountant about forming a trust, and understood that money would go from my trust to that trust and back to our company group." (p 63)



21. Having conceded that he knew of his father's trust strip and, given the fact that he himself engaged in a similar exercise some days later, it is difficult to escape the conclusion that Bill was aware that the purpose of a trust strip was to reduce the taxable income of those engaged in the exercise.



22. The one point on which I have not been satisfied is that Bill was aware that he had any entitlement under the John Murphy Trust in the year now under review. In the result, it may be necessary to consider what effect - if any - that ignorance has as against the Commissioner pursuant to sec 260 of the Act, if only because an entitlement under a trust is valid notwithstanding that the beneficiary had no knowledge of it; cf Standing v Bowring (1885) 31 Ch D 282; FC of T v Cornell [1946] HCA 32; (1946) 73 CLR 394.

23. The conclusion of sham becomes almost inescapable when one looks at the particular elements of the various transactions.



24. Whilst it is almost axiomatic that it must be rare for a tax avoidance scheme to be a sham, if only because, to be effective, the documents which are critical to its implementation must create between the parties the very legal rights and obligations they intend to create, in this case I am satisfied that this Emperor has no clothes; that his garment, although no doubt sewn together with the most expensive silk money can buy, nevertheless falls apart on even the most cursory inspection.



25. The following two propositions may be stated which do not in themselves give rise to the inference that the transaction is a sham:

(i) that the transaction involves a round robin of cheques. This is so even where there are no funds to meet the cheques ; cf Re Barnett; Perpetual Trustee Co Ltd v Barnett [1969] 2 NSWR 721.



(ii) that the scheme is highly artificial and/or complex, provided each link that holding the scheme together has the effect that it purports to have and none other.



Nevertheless, where a scheme is both highly artificial and complex, and involves a round robin of cheques, the cumulative effect of these elements warrants a closer inspection, and where, as here, there is a purported creation of debts in circumstances which give rise to the strongest suspicion that the parties had no intention of giving effect to these transactions, a strong inference of sham arises, which at the very least requires some explanation and/or evidence.



26. When the facts of this case are looked at in sequence, they demonstrate yet another circular game in which Bill's daddy and a few hired performers act out a play; nothing happens save that the Houdini daddy appears to escape from the manacles of tax. Templeman LJ, in the Court of Appeal in WT Ramsay v IRC [1979] 3 All ER 213 put it prosaically:



"The game is recognisable by four rules. First, the play is devised and scripted prior to performance. Secondly, real money and real documents are circulated and exchanged. Thirdly, the money is returned by the end of the performance. Fourthly, the financial position of the actors is the same at the end as it was in the beginning save that the taxpayer in the course of the performance is to create the illusion that something has happened, that Hamlet has been killed and that Bottom did don an asses head so that tax advantages can be claimed as if something had happened."



27. One need only to look at steps XXI and XXXIII where Bill's sister Mary, as trustee of the Murphy Final Discretionary Trust purports to lend Murphy Enterprises, in its capacity as trustee of the Murphy Trust some $929,465 in two sums. These "loans" are said to occur on 20 and 27 May 1981. Looking at these transactions in isolation, one is tempted to say "pigs might fly".



28. No one before Hartigan P was able to offer any explanation of these so-called "loans"; indeed, Mr John Murphy expressed surprise at his daughter's involvement in this daisy chain of "loans". The "lender" was not called to give evidence and no explanation was offered for her absence. It is said that neither "lender" nor "borrower" have filed tax returns since 1982. The receipt of the money by the trustee of the family trust not having been disputed, prima facie the payment imports an obligation to repay the loan in the absence of any circumstances tending to show anything in the nature of a presumption of advancement, a doctrine which can hardly be prayed in aid where the efficacy of this arrangement depends on the existence of a loan, made by a trustee who onlends money, received seconds earlier from another trustee, and lent (semble) at no interest and without any arrangement as to when and how the "loan" is to be repaid. Certainly no one came forward to depose that the money has been repaid or was ever intended to be repaid. In the result, I am satisfied that if one card is pulled out from under this artificial construct, the whole house of cards collapses. Indeed, if there is a presumption of regularity in this case, it operates against John Murphy, his trusts, the "loan", his advisers and, indeed, against anyone who had a hand in this game of cards.



29. I am therefore satisfied that these "loans" had no reality and were never intended to take effect, and without these loans, the whole scheme collapses in what might be called a "shambles". That is the end of the case.



30. On this finding, I do not propose to delve any further into Mr Hack's careful argument on sham, in which he sought to attack the scheme from other perspectives, and in particular, on the basis that these eccentric arrangements defy any commercial explanation other than tax avoidance - as he put it: "It really was an exercise of looking oneself in the mirror".



31. In the result, the effect of a finding of sham is that the so-called "distribution" is void, thus activating the "default of appointment" clause in the deed. This, in turn, has the effect that Bill is deemed to have received his share of income in the 1981 tax year, viz $162,978.



32. Much reliance was placed by the applicant on the decision in Stamp v FC of T 88 ATC 4803, a case involving the same promoter and many of the same "funny" companies and trusts. In that case, the Commissioner submitted, albeit only formally, that all steps in the scheme were a sham. However, on the facts as proved in evidence, Pincus J held "that there is nothing to support that (submission), nor any other reason to doubt the effectiveness of the distribution as against the (taxpayer)". Against that background, and having found "sham" in the case before me, the facts as found by the Pincus J will need to be set out in some detail.



33. The essential steps in Stamp were as follows:



1. Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Family Trust (referred to above) resolved to distribute $29,900 to A. & B. Management (No. 83) Pty Ltd as trustee of the Conduit Discretionary Trust, and $370,100 to A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Subscribable Unit Trust.



2. Cheques No 218533 and 218534 drawn on the trust account of Ahern Betar and Dunn (the accountant who promoted the scheme) were paid to make the distributions just mentioned.



3. Josby Pty Ltd as trustee of the Centaur Memorial Fund for Nurses resolved to apply $368,000 (i.e. the $370,100 referred to in step one, less $2,100 which was intended to remain with the Fund) for the benefit of the Fund by purchasing 'B' class units in the McCracken Subscribable Unit Trust in the name of Intercontinental Shelf No. 104 Pty Ltd as trustee of the Centaur Trust (No. 2).



4. A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Subscribable Unit Trust resolved to issue 368,000 one dollar 'B' class units in the trust to Intercontinental Shelf No. 104 Pty Ltd as trustee of the Centaur Trust (No. 2).



5. The units just mentioned were paid for by a cheque. No. 218536 drawn on the same trust account in the sum of $368,000.



6. A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Subscribable Unit Trust resolved to distribute income of $370,100 to the holder of the 'B' class units, that is, to Intercontinental Shelf No. 104 Pty Ltd, as trustee of the Centaur Trust (No. 2).



7. That distribution was effected by cheque No. 218535 drawn on the same trust account.



8. A. & B. Management (No. 83) Pty Ltd as trustee of the Conduit Discretionary Trust resolved to distribute the $29,900 mentioned above to Tarbet Investments Pty Limited as trustee of the Betar and Ahern Trust trading as Rocklea Industries.



9. That distribution was effected by cheque No. 218539 drawn on the same trust account.



10. Intercontinental Shelf No. 104 Pty Ltd as trustee of the Centaur Trust (No. 2) resolved to distribute income of $370,100 to Josby Pty Ltd as trustee of the Centaur Memorial Fund for Nurses.



11. That distribution was effected by cheque No. 218537 drawn on the same trust account.



12. A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Subscribable Unit Trust resolved to declare that that trust vest on 22 May 1981 (i.e. the following day) and resolved pending that vesting to distribute $368,000 being the capital of the Fund to the holder of the 'A' class units, Ronald Graham McCracken as trustee of the McCracken Final Discretionary Trust by way of advance distribution upon the vesting.



13. That advance distribution of $368,000 was made by cheque No. 218540 drawn on the same trust account.



14. Ronald Graham McCracken as trustee of the McCracken Final Discretionary Trust resolved to lend $368,000 to Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Family Trust.



15. That loan was made by cheque No. 218532 drawn on the same trust account.



It should be added that it is not entirely clear as to step No. 12, how it came about that R.G. McCracken was entitled to the capital in question. The relevant trust deed does not appear to create that entitlement; however, it does not seem to be necessary to pursue that point.



The way in which the original $400,000 flowed was therefore:



1. $29,900 went to A. & B. Management (No. 83) Pty Ltd and then to Tarbet Investments Pty Ltd: that was in substance the promoter's fee.



2. $370,100 was distributed by Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd to A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd and then to Intercontinental Shelf No. 104 Pty Ltd and then to Josby Pty Ltd from which point that sum was divided into two streams:



(a) $368,000 was paid by Josby Pty Ltd to A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd for 'B' class units and the same sum was distributed by A. & B. Management (No. 72) Pty Ltd to R.G. McCracken as the holder of 10 'A' class units and then paid by way of loan to Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd (the originator of the flow);



(b) $2,100 remained with Josby Pty Ltd as trustee of the Centaur Memorial Fund for Nurses.



The money went around in a circle from Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd to Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd $29,900 being syphoned off on the way to the promoters and $2,100 to the Centaur Memorial Fund for Nurses.



34. In essence, Pincus J held that Kenmac Investments Pty Ltd, as trustee of the McCracken Family Trust, had in fact exercised his discretion on 21 May 1981 to distribute (i) $29,000 to A & B Management (No 83) Pty Ltd as trustee of the Conduit Discretionary Trust and (ii) $370,100 to A & B Management (No 72) Pty Ltd as trustee of the McCracken Subscribable Unit Trust. On that finding, there was no operation for the "default of distribution" clause.



35. On the issue of "sham", his Honour found "that point was not, however, elaborated upon, nor pursued by putting appropriate suggestions to the deponents who gave evidence about the way the scheme was effected." (p 4807). That is a far cry from the way the case was conducted before Hartigan P, where John Murphy was closely cross-examined on the workings of the scheme, but was unable to give any - let alone any credible - explanation of the "way the scheme was effected". In these circumstances, there is no room for the presumption omnia praesumuntur rite esse acta, particularly in circumstances where I am presented with a round robin of cheques relating to an highly artificial scheme involving a complex series of transactions making no commercial sense other than in terms of the reduction of taxable income.



36. I am satisfied that a finding of "sham" in one case - however analogous the facts may be - does not create a precedent in terms of the doctrine of stare decisis. The evidence given in Stamp's case is very different from the evidence in this case and nothing said by his Honour in Stamp is relevant to my fact finding in this case insofar as it relates to "sham".



37. The case is, however, of critical importance on the issue of sec 260. Thus, Pincus J made a critical observation in Stamp on the effect of sec 260 in circumstances where taxpayer is unaware that he/she is a beneficiary under a trust. Thus, at p 4806 Pincus J observed:



"It was argued for the Commissioner that if there was, in fact, an exercise of discretion by the trustee under the provisions of cl. 3.2 [the same clause as the clause identified as cl 3.2 in this case quoted above], but that exercise was struck down under sec 260, then under the proviso to cl 3.2 the primary beneficiaries would, for tax purposes, be regarded as taking the income. The appellant contended that on its proper construction the proviso does not have that effect; it operates, so it was argued, if the discretion is not exercised and the question whether the exercise is effective for tax purposes has nothing to do with the matter.



It appears to me that the dispute just mentioned reflects a deeper one, namely whether the avoiding effect of sec 260 may be relied on by the Commissioner against people (like the appellant) who have in no sense participated in the impugned scheme, nor in any transaction forming part of it. But if sec 260 had indeed such a universal effect, then it appears to me that the Commissioner's argument on the construction of the proviso would have to be accepted. Putting that point another way, the assumption on which the Commissioner's contention about the proviso is based is that the avoiding effect of sec 260 operates against all taxpayers, whether or not taking part in the scheme; if that is so, then it would appear to follow that, as against the appellant, the trustee must be deemed not to have exercised the discretion referred to in the proviso (although it did so in fact). As appears from what is written above, I do not accept the correctness of the assumption."



38. Mr Hack, relying on sec 260 in the alternative, submitted that a more recent decision of Hill J - Davis & anor v FC of T 89 ATC 4377 - was directly in point and not readily reconcilable with Stamp's case. Whilst any decision of Hill J can hardly be discounted as lacking weight or illumination, nevertheless, and with all due respect, I do not regard Davis as bearing on the case before me, if only because Stamp's case decided that sec 260 cannot be avoided merely by "the three monkey" trick of hearing nothing, seeing nothing and saying nothing, whereas in Davis, Hill J held that the taxpayer's reliance in that case on her husband and his solicitor and deliberately distancing herself from business transactions meant that she was sufficiently a party to the impugned arrangements engineered by the husband and the solicitor for sec 260 to apply in relation to her. Davis is thus a far cry from the facts in Stamp, where the taxpayer was found to be a wholly innocent party, having nothing to do with the arrangements in question.



39. Applying the ratio of Stamp's case to the facts in this case, and having concluded on the evidence that Bill, whilst not a wholly "innocent" party, nevertheless in a critical sense lacked any awareness of his entitlement under the deed, it follows that sec 260 can have no application. It may be that the result in Stamp may have been different if his Honour had been referred to Standing v Bowring (supra).



However, that is not a matter for this Tribunal and the point may have to be decided elsewhere, and in another place.



40. The Commissioner imposed a penalty of (roughly) 52% of the tax avoided. Given the fact that the applicant was, at the time he lodged his return unaware of any deemed distribution from the trust, I am not prepared to impose a penalty of this order of magnitude. Justice will be done if I were to limit his liability to "interest only" at the rate of 14.026% per annum on the tax avoided, calculated from the due date for payment of the assessment. Whilst it is harsh to penalise an "innocent" taxpayer, I am comforted in the knowledge that on the findings I have made, such a taxpayer has rights over against the offending trustee(s) and/or the advisers who lands him/her in this pickle.



41. For the above reasons, subject to the variation in penalty, the Commissioner's decision on the objection is otherwise affirmed.
