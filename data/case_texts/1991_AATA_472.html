ST88/64,ST90/1 and Commissioner of Taxation [1991] AATA 472; (1991) 22 ATR 3157; 91 ATC 250 (30 April 1991)

Last Updated: 27 May 2009



INCOME TAX - applicant obtained loan for purchase of rent producing property - security for loan included charge on term policy of insurance on life of applicant - whether policy premiums qualify for deduction in terms of ss.51 or 67 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 - premiums not deductible - decisions affirmed



Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ss.51, 67, 82KL



Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153

British Insulated & Helsby Cables Limited & Atherton (1926) AC205

The Texas Company (Australasia) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382

Ronpibon Tin No Liability & Tongkah Compound No Liability and Federal Commissioner of Taxation, [1949] HCA 15; (1948-49) 78 CLR 47

Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100

Lau v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4618



AAT Decision No 6917



DECISION AND REASONS



Re:



Applicant



And:



Respondent



Nos: ST88/64 & ST90/1



Tribunal: Deputy President B.H. Burns



Place: Adelaide



Date: 30 April 1991



Decision: The decisions on the objections in relation to the deductibility of the premiums of $10,032 paid to the AMP Society in respect of each of the years ending 30 June 1986 and 30 June 1987 are affirmed.



(Signed)

...............................

Deputy President B.H. Burns



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos. ST88/64 & ST90/1

Taxation Appeals Division )



Re:



Applicant



And:



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



30 April 1991 Deputy President B.H. Burns



1. These references relate to two payments described as premiums and, more specifically, whether those outgoings qualify for deduction in terms of either sections 51 or 67 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ("the Act"). The amounts involved are $10,032 and represent premiums paid to the AMP Society on an endowment policy taken out on the life of the applicant. Payments were affected on 30 June 1986 and 30 June 1987. The reference for the 1987 year included several other issues which were settled in the course of the hearing and accordingly, the Tribunal takes those matters no further.



2. The applicant contends that the policy of life assurance is related to the purchase of a rent yielding property and that as such the premiums paid are outgoings incurred in the production of the rental income. Alternatively, he submits that in the circumstances of the acquisition, particularly the relationship between the policy and the monies borrowed to purchase the property, the premiums should be viewed as an expense of borrowing and thus deductible under s.67 of the Act, Conversely, the Commissioner maintains that these outgoings were not incurred in the production of assessable income and in any event they were outgoings of a private and capital nature. He further contends that the borrowed funds were not used in the production of the applicant's assessable income, nor do the premiums paid answer the description of borrowing expenses and accordingly s.67 has no application. As a final alternative, the Commissioner submits that the payment of the premiums comes within the description of an expenditure recoupment scheme and consequently any deduction otherwise allowable is denied in terms of the provisions of s.82KL of the Act.



3. Prior to an examination of the facts, some comments on the applicant and the structuring of his financial affairs may be of assistance. He is a solicitor and a partner in a legal practice. Companies and trusts under his control, either alone or with others, display not only significant investment in real estate, but also sizable borrowings required to finance those holdings. I not unnaturally infer from the nature of the applicant's occupation and from the nature and extent of these investments, that the applicant is well versed in matters pertaining to the purchase and sale of real estate.



4. The first link in the chain is C Properties Pty Ltd (C), a company which until about 1986 had been involved in property investment. At that time all or some of the real estate was sold for a substantial gain and the resultant cash surplus distributed to shareholders. It seems that the applicant may have been a director of C and that a family trust under his control held shares in its holding company. The total distribution from C to the holding company and then to the shareholders of the holding company amounted to $1,149,483 of which $180,404 represented the share payable to the family trust. It is to be noted that the distribution to the trust was in the form of directed payment to others rather than a cheque made payable to the trust. Some of those payments relate to the purchase price of the property acquired by the applicant, and which in turn is associated with the borrowing and the policy of assurance.



5. The trustee of the family trust is a company of which the applicant and his spouse are the directors. The trust is discretionary by nature and whilst members of the applicant's immediate family come within the range of possible beneficiaries, the applicant does not. Notwithstanding that exclusion, the transactions within the trust suggest that, for practical purposes, the applicant viewed its resources as his own. All kinds of private expenditure, including the purchase price of the property purchased by the applicant, were paid from trust funds and, as will be seen later in these reasons, it is the allocation of those outgoings that causes problems for the applicant.



6. The hearing of these references was aided by the production of a statement of agreed facts. Facts not agreed related, in the main, to the source of funds used to purchase the property in question and more particularly how the payments made on behalf of the applicant by the trustee were recorded in the books of the family trust. In that respect evidence was given by two witnesses who played a part in the writing up of the records of C and the family trust. The applicant did not give evidence before the Tribunal and this perhaps explains why some of the facts remain obscure. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr K. Schurgott of counsel and the Commissioner by Mr F. Carnovale also of counsel.



7. The applicant, on 18 July 1985, entered into a contract to purchase a unit located in the City of Adelaide for a consideration of $95,050. The contract provided for payment of a deposit of $9,505 and the balance, after adjustment for rates and other costs, upon settlement. That balance became a figure of $85,629.52 and settlement took place on 16 June 1986. The unit was purchased "off the plan" and this explains the lengthy interval between the signing of the contract and settlement. There is no dispute that the property produced rental income throughout the 1987 financial year and for the period until December 1988 when it was sold.



8. The total purchase price of $95,134.52 was paid with cheques drawn as follows :- Cheques drawn on

Date Amount bank account of

17.7.85 9,505.00 Family Trust

16.6.86 11,475.23 Family Trust

16.6.86 72,075.00 C Properties Pty Ltd

16.6.86 2,079,29 C Properties Pty Ltd

$95,134.52



Extracts taken from the books of account of C, the holding company and family trust were tendered in evidence, and the Tribunal is satisfied that these payments occurred and that they represented payment of the purchase consideration. Furthermore, the Tribunal accepts that the amounts paid from the bank account of C ultimately became debits to the loan account of the applicant in books of the family trust. That acceptance has been arrived at only after close scrutiny of the general journals of the various entities which reveal a shunting effect of those transactions first from C to the holding company, then from the holding company to the family trust and finally, in the trust to the account of the applicant.



9. The accounting processes applied to the payments from the bank account of the family trust are more problematical. It seems that these and other outgoings were initially posted by the accountant of the trust to a suspense account. There they remained until the accountant became better informed about these transactions so as to determine which member of the family should be charged with these outgoings. There is no doubt that the two payments under consideration were identified as belonging to the applicant and yet it seems as if the balance in the suspense account was transferred to the individual loan accounts of the applicant, his spouse and children on an arbitrary basis. That evidence prevents a finding that the totality of the purchase price is reflected in the account of the applicant in the books of the family trust. The evidence clearly establishes that the acquisition of the property was funded from within and that no external borrowings were applied in this regard. That is contrary to the assertions made in the applicant's objections and yet for the purpose of the hearing it was accepted that the borrowing occurred after settlement.



10. At or about the time of settlement, it seems that the applicant was engaged in business discussions with a person associated with the AMP Society about the benefits of a "Loan Security Programme". An illustration prepared for the applicant and which formed part of the statement of agreed facts was based on a policy of life assurance of $95,000 for a period of 10 years and with an annual premium of $10,594. Assuming the tax deductibility of those premiums, the programme would produce an estimated net profit of $116,733. The plan was that the policy be used as security for monies borrowed and applied towards income producing pursuits, the notion being that such a link would satisfy the criteria of both sections 51 and 67 of the Act.



11. On 26 June 1986, the applicant wrote to the finance company confirming his application for a loan to permit repayments in full of the $95,000 borrowed by him from the family trust to complete the purchase of the unit. He acknowledged the need for proper security and proposed that the loan be made on the security of a mortgage on the unit together with a charge on a ten year term policy of life insurance to be taken out on his life. The Tribunal was not informed as to the reasons why a policy of life assurance yet to be taken up was selected in preference to other assets owned by the applicant, but the following request contained in that correspondence is of interest:-



"...it will be appreciated if your loan approval clearly requires the policy to be secured to you and the premiums during the term of the policy paid so that proper arrangements can be made by me and through the Trustee to provide such security".



The provision of such an instruction by a borrower to a lender is, by normal standards, uncommon and the Tribunal accepts that one possible explanation might be the need to establish, at some time in the future, evidence of the required link between the policy and the borrowing.



12. In any event, the finance company agreed to the terms and conditions as requested and the applicant was advised accordingly on 30 June 1986.



13. It was at that time that the pace of activity increased markedly, with the occurrence of the following transactions and events :-



.applicant signed life assurance proposal with AMP Society and paid from bank account of the family trust an amount of $10,436 which included premium on term policy for $95,000 due to mature on 30 June 1996.



.AMP Society issued receipt bearing particulars "J10 Loan Security Programme".



.(Applicant) Insurance Policy Trust established with the trustee of the family trust appointed as trustee - the trust is discretionary and the range of beneficiaries included the applicant and members of his family.



.cheque for $15,152, described as interest, drawn on family trust bank account and paid to the finance company notwithstanding that loan not received at that point in time.



14. On about 15 July 1986, the applicant executed a transfer of the life policy to the trustee of the Insurance Policy Trust. This was immediately followed by the assignment of the policy to the finance company as security for the pending advance of $95,000. Finally, the mortgage document securing the unit of property was completed and signed and on 18 July 1986 the finance company paid the loan amount of $95,000 into the applicant's personal bank account. The applicant maintains that the purpose of the borrowing was to repay to the family trust the monies utilised to acquire the unit and with that in mind the application of the receipt from the finance company warrants close examination.



15. First, it is noted that the applicant's bank account as at that time was overdrawn to the extent of $12,906 thus leaving a credit balance of $82,094. There then followed a series of transfers, approximately twenty in number, as between the bank accounts of the applicant and the trust, the result being that by the end of August 1986, all the remaining funds had been expended. It seems that the object was to maintain a credit balance of $10.00 in the family trust bank account and that variable amounts were transferred from the applicant's bank account to cover cheques drawn by the trustee. It is not possible to ascertain the kinds of expenditure covered by those payments out of the trust account and yet, having regard to earlier patterns, it seems more probable than not that they included payments made on behalf of the applicant. In other words, the monies repaid by the applicant or at least portion of them, were then applied by the trustee in the creation of new debts as between itself and the applicant. In all the circumstances, the methods adopted by the applicant in the distribution of the $95,000 and the application of those monies by the Trustee, are hardly supportive of the claim that the funds were borrowed to repay the debt created by the trust bearing the costs associated with the acquisition of the unit by the applicant.



16. The provisions of sub-section 51(1) of the Act as they relate to these references, are as follows :-



"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income ...... shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature ..."



17. These provisions were comprehensively considered by the High Court in the case of Ronpibon Tin No Liability and Tongkah Compound No Liability and Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1948-49) 78 CLR 47, and the following passages taken from the joint judgment demonstrate the nature of the relationship which must exist between the derivation of assessable income and the incurring of the outgoing, if such expenditure is to qualify as an allowable deduction. At pages 56-57 the Court expressed the following opinions:



"For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income ' mean in the course of gaining or producing such income. ...

In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income."



and further, at page 58:



"It is therefore necessary to return to the opening words of S51(1) and inquire to what extent the expenditure of the respective companies was incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income."



18. From the foregoing, it is clear that the extent or degree of the relationship between the premiums paid on the policy of assurance and the rental income received by the applicant is of prime importance. Subject to some reservations by the Commissioner on the question of purpose, that need not worry the Tribunal at this stage, it being common ground that that relationship would exist if the applicant could establish that the monies borrowed from the finance company had been used or laid out in connection with the purchase of the unit. The use concept is a product of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 and The Texas Company (Australasia) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 and more recently Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100. Notwithstanding that those cases involved the deductibility of interest paid on borrowed funds, the Tribunal accepts that the use test is appropriate when considering borrowing expenses of a revenue kind.



19. In an effort to establish the use connection, the applicant submits:



. that all the costs of acquiring the unit were paid from the trust bank account on his behalf;



. that as a result of those payments he became indebted to the trust to the extent of $95,134.52;



. and, that the amount of $95,000 borrowed from the finance company was applied towards the discharge of that indebtedness.



In that way, so it was said, the borrowing of $95,000 could be linked directly to the purchase of the unit and on that basis, the premiums on the policy assigned as security should be seen as outgoings incurred in the production of the rental income.



20. Before proceeding to an examination of those submissions, the following observations are apposite:



. numerous cheques of a private nature were drawn on the trust bank account, some being debited to the respective family member and the remainder to a suspense account;



. the balance in the suspense account was distributed arbitrarily between family members;



. from time to time and as the need arose, the applicant transferred funds to the trust and presumably these amounts were posted to the credit of his account in the trust ledger;



. balances of the applicant's loan account in the trust fluctuated wildly, and one is left guessing as to the state of that account when monies were paid for the unit, or conversely when they were advanced to the trust by the applicant from funds provided by the finance company.



In the opinion of the Tribunal, these considerations lead to a conclusion that the applicant perceived little, if any, difference between trust funds and those of his own and that trust funds could be expended for any purpose he chose. On that basis, it is difficult to accept that, as between the trustee of the family trust and the applicant, there was an agreement that the payments of a private nature should be treated as repayable loans.



21. In returning to the applicant's submissions, the Tribunal is satisfied that all payments associated with the purchase of the unit were provided from trust funds. However, the haphazard allocation of those outgoings precludes a finding that the full amount of $95,134.52 was debited to the account by the applicant. In any event, the Tribunal is not satisfied that it was the intention of the parties that the amount so debited should represent a loan repayable by the applicant.



22. In all of the circumstances, the Tribunal rejects the contention that the $95,000 borrowed by the applicant was applied by him in repaying to the family trust, amounts expended towards the purchase of the unit. The advances to the trust lack that kind of identification. The Tribunal is of the view that the purpose of the borrowing was no more than the provision of additional funds, and that the decision to borrow was to some extent precipitated by the need to attach the policy of life assurance to a borrowing in the hope of gaining a tax deduction of the premiums. The funds were borrowed by the applicant, who in turn, advanced the greater portion thereof to the family trust. There is no evidence to suggest that those advances were at interest and thus the Tribunal is unable to perceive a connection as between the premiums and the applicant's assessable income i.e. the outgoing was not incurred in the gaining of assessable income. Although, that conclusion negates the possible application of sub-section 51(1), some comment on the exclusory provisions, especially those relating to capital, are appropriate.



23. Court decisions over the years demonstrate that in a determination of this kind, there is no one test capable of universal application. What does become clear, is that the character of the outgoing must be considered in the context of all the circumstances. There is no doubt that the payment of the premium brought into existence an asset of some significance i.e. the policy of assurance, and that at some time in the future, either upon death or at maturity, the applicant and/or members of his family stood to gain financially. In simple terms, the policy is an investment and the premiums paid represent its cost. That the policy was used as security alters nothing, in fact it supports such a conclusion.



24. In the circumstances of these references, the Tribunal is of the opinion that the enduring benefit test, which takes its origin from the judgment of Viscount CAVE L.C., in British Insulated & Helsby Cables Limited & Atherton (1926) AC205, is relevant. His Lordship's opinion as to the characteristics of capital expenditure was expressed in the following terms:



"But when an expenditure is made not only once and for all, with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefits of a trade, I think that there is very good reason (in the absence of special circumstances leading to an opposite conclusion) for treating such an expenditure as properly attributable not to revenue but to capital".



In the Tribunal's view the paying of premiums on a yearly basis rather than once and for all is, in the circumstances, of no consequence and does not alter the finding that the payment under consideration brought into existence an asset which was bound to come home in due course, subject only to continued payment of premiums during the life of the policy. The conclusion is inescapable that the transaction is an affair of capital.



25. Sub-section (1) of section 67 of the Act provides:



"Subject to this section, so much of the expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in borrowing money used by him for the purpose of producing assessable income as bears to the whole of that expenditure the same proportion as the part of the period for which the money was borrowed that is in the year of income bears to the whole of that period shall be an allowable deduction."



26. As previously found, the monies borrowed from the finance company were not used by the applicant in the production of his assessable income and accordingly, the first requirement of the sub-section is not met. Furthermore, the Tribunal is of the view that it would be wrong to describe the payment of a premium on a policy of life assurance, as an expense incurred in borrowing money. The same view was expressed by the Taxation Board of Review No 2 in case 19 13 CTBR (NS) 124. The facts of that case were that monies had been borrowed to purchase income producing property, and that policies of life assurance had been given as collateral security. The question was raised as to whether the premiums on those policies qualified for deduction in terms of s.67 of the Act. The Board, which comprised Chairman, Mr A.M. Donovan and Members Mr J.D. Davies (as he then was) and Mr G.R. Thompson, had this to say at page 125:



"For several reasons, the expenditure with which we are concerned seems to fall outside the provisions of S67. The outgoing had the character of expenditure on insurance, rather than expenditure on borrowing. The premiums under the policy, of the first of which the deduction claimed was part, are payable as consideration for valuable benefits, namely a continuing insurance on the life of the trustee, annual bonuses and a surrender value in the event of the policy being cashed before maturity. They lack entirely the character of a cost of borrowing and, though they are outgoings, are not expenses in the nature of costs at all. They are outgoings which will, in due course, result in a payment under the policy which may well be far in excess of the amount policy."



In any event it will be recalled that in the 1986 year of income, the premium on the policy was paid prior to the receipt of the loan monies and in those circumstances, it is not possible to find that that premium related to funds used in the production of assessable income.



27. Finally, the Commissioner relies upon the provision of Subdivision D of Division 3 of Part III of the Act, which deals with losses and outgoings incurred under various tax avoidance schemes. In certain circumstances, s.82KL operates to deny a deduction to which a taxpayer might otherwise be entitled. The types of schemes towards which s.82KL is directed, are those providing for expenditure to be incurred as part of a tax avoidance arrangement under which the taxpayer or an associate receives a compensatory benefit which, together with the expected tax saving, effectively recoups the expenditure of the taxpayer so that in the end no real loss or outgoing is suffered. The operation of this section is confined to expenditure described as eligible relevant expenditure.



28. In these references it was accepted by the parties that s.82KL did have potential application if is was found that the premiums were deductible under s.67, i.e. they were incurred in the production of the applicant's assessable income and they answered the description of borrowing expenses. To ensure completeness such a possibility must be considered.



29. Whether the premiums in question are eligible relevant expenditure will be determined in accordance with the criteria laid down in the Act, the first of which relates to the reasons actuating the transaction. If the outgoing is incurred by reason of, or as a result of or as part of a tax avoidance agreement, then the first condition is met (s.82 KH(1F)(a)). The decision of Connolly J. in Lau v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4618, makes it clear that the purpose or reason need not be the main or dominant one, as long as the arrangement was "for the purpose which at least included the reducing of his income tax liability". As previously found, one of the reasons for the policy and the borrowing was the anticipated reduction in tax liability and with that in mind, the Tribunal concludes that the agreements between the applicant and the AMP Society, and the applicant and the finance company should be regarded as tax avoidance agreements.



30. The next issue is whether the applicant obtained, in relation to the premiums, an additional benefit as defined by s.82 KH (1F)(b). If benefits of that kind are received by the applicant or an associate of the applicant, and the sum of them and the expected tax saving exceed the amounts of the premium, no deduction is allowed. It is of interest to observe that the definition of associate includes not only relatives of the taxpayer, but also the trustee of a trust where the taxpayer or an associate are capable of benefiting.



31. The Commissioner is of the view that the additional benefit is the policy itself, together with the contractual right to receive nominated benefits upon the happening of certain events. Given that the parties agree as to the quantum of the expected tax saving, the manner in which the benefit is to be measured has caused substantial disagreement. The Commissioner contends that the value to be ascribed should reflect the collective rights conferred on the Policy Trust and that as the final pay out figure will exceed premiums paid then, at worst, the benefit should be assessed as equal to the amounts of premium paid.



32. The counter argument presented by the applicant is that value should be decided on a year by year basis and, that it was not appropriate to have regard to the level of benefits which might become payable in the future. Furthermore, it was submitted that value should be calculated by reference to surrender values existing at the end of each year and, which were agreed as being NIL for 1986 and $3,423 for 1987. By way of reply, the Commissioner referred the Tribunal to sub-s. 82KL(2), which specifically empowers the Commissioner to have regard to events occurring at a later time in deciding the applicability of sub-s. 82KL(1).



33. The Tribunal is of the opinion that the policy represents an additional benefit, that the Commissioner is not restricted to a year by year analysis and that the final estimated payout is relevant in valuing that benefit. The facts clearly indicate that, in time, the amount to be collected will exceed the cost of the investment and for that reason the suggestion that the benefit be valued at less than cost, is rejected. The Tribunal accepts that surrender values may have their uses in other situations as between life assurance companies and policy owners, but their use in these circumstances is clearly inappropriate. There is no justification to value the benefit at NIL after the payment of one premium and $30,629, being the surrender value existing, after the payment of four premiums. The Tribunal is satisfied that the value of the benefit at least equates with the amount of premiums paid.



34. For the above reasons, the Tribunal is of the opinion that the value of the benefit together with the expected tax saving, is greater than the amounts expended by way of premiums. In these circumstances s.82KL comes into play. If contrary to the earlier finding, the premiums are deductible in terms of s.67 then s.82KL operates to negate that entitlement.



35. In view of the various conclusions expressed in these reasons, the Tribunal affirms the Commissioner's decisions on the respective objections as to the deductibility of the premiums in question.
