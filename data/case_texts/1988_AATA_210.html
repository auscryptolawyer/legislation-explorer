NT88/792 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 210; (1988) 19 ATR 3662; 88 ATC 714 (1 July 1988)

Last Updated: 29 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - employee - travelling expenses incurred in course of employment - expense voluntarily incurred - employee not using facilities provided by employer - whether allowable.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s.51(1).

Burton v. F.C.T. 79 A.T.C. 4319;

Lunney v. F.C.T.; Hayley v. F.C.T. (1958) 100 C.L.R. 478.

Applicant COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent No. NT88/792

AAT Decision No 4475

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1 July 1988

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/792

Respondent

Tribunal : Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 1 July 1988

Place : Sydney

Decision : The Tribunal decides that the decision of the Commissioner under review be varied by reducing taxable income by the sum of $244.

PETER M. ROACH

Senior Member.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/792

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re :

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

1 July 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The issue raised by this application is whether the assessment of the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 in the sum of $25,303 was excessive. There is no suggestion that the Commissioner erred in raising the assessment because that was the taxable income returned by the Applicant. However, having been assessed in accordance with his return - lodged with the Commissioner only in March 1987 after a warning by way of final notice for lodgment of an income tax return - the Applicant objected to the assessment so made. He claimed that the Commissioner had "failed" to allow for the following deductions:-

CCH Service $415.00

Calculator batteries, pens, stationery,

folders, staples etc. 25.00

Motor vehicle expenses - 928 km. at 33 cents

per km. 306.00

Meals away from home attending meetings of the branch Council of the Federated Clerks Union and other meetings "together with traveling expenses" 27.30

Repairs to Commonwealth vehicle 122.27

Why it should have been suggested that there was any "failure" on the part of the Commissioner to allow the deductions when they had not been claimed was not explained.

2. Having considered the objection, the Commissioner allowed all of the amounts claimed by the objection save that he did not wholly allow two items: those claimed at $25 and $306. The adjustment sheet which accompanied the amended assessment which attended the notice of partial disallowance stated:-

"4. Calculator Batteries, Pens Stationery etc - allowed in to (sic) the extent of $10.00 being the amount considered reasonable for Calculator Batteries. Pens Stationery etc disallowed as these are supplied by employer.

5. Motor vehicle expense allowed to the extent of $77.00 as being amount considered reasonable for Union business.

Government motor vehicle expenses disallowed as vehicles are available." Thus, the difference between the Applicant and the Commissioner came to be $244 in taxable income or $114.68 in tax and Medicare Levy. No penalty by way of additional tax for late

return had been imposed.

  1. Although the amount in tax involved was less than $120.00 the Applicant's concern that he had been excessively assessed was such that he raised the $240 which had become necessary as a prerequisite to securing an independent review of the decisions of the Commissioner. In doing so he stated that he would have to forego the right to have his 1985 assessment reviewed because he could not afford a further $240. The fact that he was prepared to undertake such a risk as he did is quite remarkable. Most would have considered that the risk of losing $240 as the price of an unsuccessful attempt to rectify a perceived injustice would have been too high. However, the explanation for the unusual persistence on the part of the Applicant lay in the past.
  2. Over many years the Applicant had been a Commonwealth public servant employed in the service of the Commissioner of Taxation. Circumstances arising out of that service gave rise to earlier litigation before this Tribunal. That touched the assessment of the Applicant to income tax for the years of income ended 30 June 1980, 1981, 1982, 1983 and 1984. He was fortunate that it was not then necessary for him to pay $1,200.00 in fees to secure the right to an independent review. Those matters were resolved by a decision handed down on 27 July 1987: a decision not released for publication because it was thought that publication of the decision as handed down might result in identification of the Applicant to his colleagues in the Australian Tax Office. He has now advised that the matter was so widely known and discussed that he has no objection to publication. As those background circumstances go a long way towards explaining how the difficulties to be addressed here came to arise, I will direct that that decision be released for publication.
  3. In September 1985, long before the issues as to the earlier assessments had been resolved, the Applicant was interviewed by the Executive Office in charge of the Compliance Branch (as it was then known) of the local Tax office with a view to him taking up a position in that section. I accept the evidence of the Executive Officer that, in the course of that interview, the Applicant manifested a "lot of bitterness" over his treatment following an incident involving the use of a Commonwealth vehicle some years before. The endeavours of the Executive Officer to overcome that difficulty seem to have been successful and the Applicant commenced work in the branch.
  4. In the Compliance Branch he was one of a group of officers numbered in the order of 200 to 220 persons, 140 to 150 of whom had occasion to go "on the road" in the course of their work. The local Australian Tax Office had available to it a fleet of vehicles provided by the Department of Administrative Services. Although the number of vehicles presently available appears to be ample for its purposes, during the year ended 30 June 1986 there were occasions upon which fleet vehicles might not have been available in sufficient number to meet demand. Travel by fleet vehicles was not the only means of travel made available to officers to enable them to carry out their duties. If travel interstate was necessary, air transport would be arranged and cabs would be used to transport the officer to the airport. If long-distance country travel was involved, a hire and drive vehicle might be rented as being better suited to the journey than the smaller four-cylinder vehicles which dominated the fleet. If travel by public transport was appropriate, officers would be reimbursed for fares.

7. In addition, it was possible for the Executive Officer to use the travel funds over which he had control to pay travel allowances to officers for the use of their own vehicles. I accept that it was a course resorted to only in exceptional circumstances. From the point of view of the Executive Officer, it was an unwarranted expense to authorise officers to claim on travel funds in relation to use of their own vehicles when fleet vehicles could be used for the same purpose without the cost of their use being a charge upon those same travel funds. I accept the evidence of the Executive Officer that he had no recollection of any request being made for such travel allowances by private car to be paid. I also accept that if, contrary to his recollection, such arrangements had been approved, it is most likely that they would only have been approved in order to enable an officer to provide transport for fellow-officers and himself to the office during periods of transport strikes.

8. I am not persuaded by the evidence that there existed any direction or instruction which purported to deny officers liberty to use their own vehicles in the course of their duties. However, I do accept that, before payment would have been approved to any officer in relation to the use of his vehicle, it would have been necessary for that officer to establish that the vehicle was fully and comprehensively insured and secure the prior approval of his superiors.

9. During the year of income ended 30 June 1986 the Applicant was an officer who was required to travel in the course of his duties in order to attend upon taxpayers, their advisers and financial and other institutions. I find that he chose on such occasions to use his own vehicle and that he did not seek to use a vehicle from the ATO fleet. I find that this practice was not known to the Executive Officer and, not being known to him, was not in any way forbidden. Further, I find that at no time did the Applicant seek payment of any travelling allowances arising out of the use of his vehicle. I further find that his decision to use his own vehicle was influenced to some extent by his belief that he was not entitled to the benefits of comprehensive insurance while in charge of a Commonwealth vehicle: a circumstance of which he believed he had had adverse experience previously (cf. the decision in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1984).

10. It was agreed at the hearing that he had used his vehicle to travel 695 kilometres in the course of the Commissioner's business during ordinary working hours (not including any travel between home and office); and that, should the claim be allowable at all, it should be allowed at the rate of 33 cents per kilometre ($229), notwithstanding the fact that the vehicle was a Holden Statesman sedan some ten years old.

11. I fear that the issue has become confused in that a clear distinction has not been maintained between the obligations owed to the Commissioner by the Applicant as a taxpayer and those owed to the Commissioner by the Applicant as an employee of the Commonwealth. Before me the question to be resolved is whether the requirements of S.5K1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

("the Act") are satisfied. The section provides:-

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

12. The principles to be applied in determining that question are the same for all employees without regard to the identity of their employers. I have found that the expenses in question were incurred by the Applicant in going about the duties he was engaged and required to perform; that they were voluntarily incurred; that the Applicant might have avoided incurring those expenses; that he was not expected, or requested, or required to incur them; and that he could not have been obliged to incur them. I am satisfied that, had he claimed payment of a travelling allowance, the claim would have been refused for the simple reason that it was considered to be more economical for the Commonwealth to have its employees use a fleet f cars owned by the Commonwealth than to pay them travelling allowances for the use of their own vehicles; and further because, in the workings of a bureaucracy the Executive Officer would not have been willing to diminish the travel funds under his control by authorizing travelling allowances when Commonwealth cars could have been used by his subordinates without any charge to his funds.

13. For the Commissioner, much reliance was placed upon the decision of Smith, J. in the Supreme Court of Western Australia in Burton v. F.C.T. 79 A.T.C. 4319. The Applicant in that case was a Stipendiary Magistrate stationed at the Central Magistrate's Court in Perth. He travelled there each day by car from his home 12 kilometres away. From time to time he was required to sit at outlying courts and he also used his car to travel to those courts. Although some of those trips to outlying courts were pre-arranged, others occurred as emergencies. Both on the pre-arranged and emergency attendances, he travelled by his own car. His entitlement to a deduction for the expenses of travelling to outlying courts, whether on pre-arranged occasions or in emergencies, was not in dispute. The Commissioner acknowledged the Applicant's entitlement to the deductions so claimed. Further, there is nothing in the case to suggest that the result could have been otherwise by reason of the availability of alternative travel arrangements which were then in use by the Applicant without expense to himself.

14. What the case was all about was whether the Applicant was entitled to have a deduction for the costs of travel incurred in travelling between his home and central court on a daily basis. As Counsel for the Magistrate put it in argument, in order to carry out his duties in a proper and adequate manner, it was necessary for the Magistrate to have a motor vehicle available to him close to the court to which he was attached throughout the year. Therefore, the argument was whether the circumstances of the Applicant enabled him to have the deduction claimed, notwithstanding the principles approved by the High Court of Australia in Lunney v. F.C.T.; Hayley v. F.C.T. (1958) 100 C.L.R. 478. The answer of the Supreme Court, as it had been of the Board of Review, was in the negative. The Court found that the Applicant had no obligation to have "his car be available for use in the performance of his duties" (at p.4322). Further, it found that no "necessity in the objective sense existed for the appellant to have his car available for use in the performance of his duties at his .... chambers at all times and hence that it was (not) an implied term of the contract of employment that he incur the expense involved in bringing the car to work, each day". Having so found, his Honour then went on to put the essential argument. He said:-

"The additional circumstance which Mr. Temby contended rendered the total expense of getting the appellant's car to work to be deductible was the possible use of the appellant's motor vehicle to fulfil his potential obligation to sit at an out-station at short notice. The existence of this potentiality for the use of the appellant's motor vehicle on any day, coupled with his obligation to visit out-stations on roster, Mr. Temby contended, enabled the appellant to escape the net cast by Lunney's Case in relation to the expense incurred by the appellant in respect of the journey made to his principal place of employment and in returning from it."

His Honour rejected the contention. In the circumstances as found, he was undoubtedly correct in doing so.

15. If that had been the claim of the Applicant in the present case, the same result would have followed - but that is not the claim of the Applicant. His claim is like to that of the Magistrate in relation to his claim to travelling expenses, entitlement to which was not in dispute. Like the Magistrate, the Applicant had no obligation to bring his car to work; like the Magistrate, the Applicant had no obligation to use his own motor vehicle as a means of transport in the course of his duties; like the Magistrate, the Applicant used his motor vehicle to travel in the course of his employment as a matter of personal choice; like the Magistrate, the Applicant may have made use of a Government car or been reimbursed for expenses incurred in utilizing public transport or, in appropriate occasions, taxis. But like the Magistrate, in so far as he used his motor vehicle in the course of his income-earning activities he should have had his claim allowed. Accordingly, I conclude that the claim to deduct motor vehicle expenses is to be allowed.

16. There remains the question as to whether a further deduction of $15 is to be allowed in relation to the item described as "calculator batteries, pens, stationery, folders, staples, etc." At the outset of the hearing, the Commissioner's representative advised that the claim had been conceded. That being so, the claim will also be allowed.

  1. Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal will be that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 will be reduced by $244. As a result his liability in tax will be reduced by $112.24. More significantly in money terms, the Applicant will be able to recover the $240 he had to put at risk in order to have recourse to an independent review to establish whether or not he was excessively assessed.
  2. There are those who would assert that the community cannot afford to provide for such an independent review as this unless at substantial cost, or the risk of substantial cost, to Applicants. I disagree. The present case well illustrates the need for independent review. Even where the amount of the disputed claim is not too small to be claimed by the Commonwealth, it should not be thought to be always too small for the claim to be resisted by the individual from whom the community claims it. If it ever should be appropriate to refer to the "user pays" argument as expressing a principle, it would seem appropriate to apply the "principle" in taxation matters: if the community wants the use of an individual's money, the community should bear the costs of establishing its claim to sue the money.
  3. It should also be borne in mind that "unreasonableness" is not always only on the part of taxpayers. Sometimes, it is the Commissioner who is acting unreasonably in refusing small, just claims. The community has an interest in ensuring that when differences arise as to such matters, the issue is independently judged and fairly resolved.
