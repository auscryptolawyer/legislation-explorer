Re Taxation Appeals (No 110) [1987] AATA 101 (19 March 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS (No. 110)

Nos. NT86/3032, NT86/3033 and NT86/3034

AAT No. 3303

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

C.J. Bannon QC (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - section 23(h) - income exempt from tax - applicant concerned with particular breed of horses in Australia - whether applicant established for purpose of development of agricultural or pastoral resources of Australia - dominant purpose - consideration of applicant's Memorandum of Association - meaning of agricultural pursuit - meaning of pastoral pursuit - whether horse breeding and grazing falls within meaning of "pastoral pursuit".

HEARING

SYDNEY

19:3:1987

ORDER

THAT the decision under review be set aside.

THAT the income of the applicant for years of income ending 30 June 1976, 1982 and 1983 be exempt under section 23(h) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.).

DECISION

The applicant claims its income is exempt from income tax pursuant to section 23(h) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.) ("the Act") which exempts:-

"(h) the income of a society or association not carried on

for the purposes of profit or gain to the individual

members thereof, established for the purpose of

promoting the development of aviation or of the

agricultural, pastoral, horticultural, viticultural,

manufacturing or industrial resources of Australia."

2. The somewhat disparate position of aviation in relation to the remaining collocation of words is historical. In the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 (Cth.), the exempting provision in section 14(1)(j) was as follows:-

"the income of any society or association not carried on for

the purposes of profit or gain to the individual members

thereof, established for the purpose of promoting the

development of the agricultural, pastoral, horticultural,

viticultural, stock-raising, manufacturing or industrial

resources of Australia."

3. The applicant is a company limited by guarantee, incorporated in New South Wales. Its Memorandum of Association (Exhibit B) sets out 38 objects, all of which are expressed to be independent of the others. Its first object is to maintain the purity of a particular breed of horse in Australia and elsewhere. Its second object is to promote the improvement and popularity of that breed of horse in Australia and elsewhere. Its third object is to promote the interests of breeders of that type of horse. Object 4 relates to the maintenance of a stud book and register for the breed (a copy of the Stud Book is Exhibit C) while object 5 is to print and publish information the applicant thinks desirable to promote its objects. Object 6 is to make known the applicant's objects and benefits by means such as advertising, the giving of prizes and the making of donations. (A sample publication became Exhibit 1).

4. Evidence was given by the applicant's honorary solicitor, who is one of the corporators and a former director, that all of the first six objects in the Memorandum of Association are important and that one of the principal objects of the applicant was to promote the interests of breeders. He pointed out that by maintaining a register of the breed of horses, both the interests of breeders and the interests of Australia are fostered in maintaining a pure and valuable breed of horse, thus increasing its value and usefulness in Australia. He also pointed out that the applicant body was founded under the auspices of the Royal Agricultural Society and that its first president holding office for many years was associated with that body. The society has a staff of eleven persons. It maintains a registry, a periodical, and makes donations to the Equine Research Foundation.

5. Assuming for the present that the objects of the applicant society fall within section 23(h) of the Act there is no necessary dichotomy between the interests of the breeders and the promotion of Australian resources in the field of horse breeding. However the Act requires that the applicant body be established for the purpose of promoting Australian resources in the particular field and it has been decided that such purpose must be the dominant purpose. In this regard reference has been made to decisions such as Case No. Q63, 15 TBRD 296, Case No. 47 12 CTBR (N.S.) 265 and to the judgments in Australian Insurance Association v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4569 at 4576, a decision of Sheppard J. and Boating Industries Association of New South Wales v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 85 ATC 4224 at 4228, a decision of Lusher J. in the N.S.W. Supreme Court. It is useful in this connection to pay regard to Walsh J.'s judgment in Smith v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4111, affirmed 73 ATC 4059 and to Fullagar J.'s judgment in Pascoe v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 30 ALJ 402 at 404 in which dominant purposes are discussed in another connection.

6. In ascertaining the establishment purpose, the Tribunal does not consider it necessary or desirable to consider the subjective purpose of each individual corporator, and while acknowledging the problems mentioned by Deane J. when he was a member of the Federal Court, in Tillmanns Butcheries Pty. Ltd. v. Australasian Meat Industry Employees' Union (1979) 27 ALR 367 at 382, 383 and by the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 62 ALR 545 at 553 and 555 which touch on problems which are perhaps different, pertaining to the word "purpose" in section 260 of the Act it appears sufficient to say that an inference as to dominant purpose may be drawn from the circumstances surrounding incorporation of the applicant and the way in which it actually operates.

7. The horse stud book like Debrett's Peerage and the records of genealogical societies may be created and utilised for a number of different purposes. The honorary solicitor acknowledged that he was also a breeder of the particular variety of horse and that the stud book and publicity helped maintain the prices obtainable by breeders. However, I consider that in the light of the Memorandum of Association, the evidence and the surrounding circumstances that the dominant purpose for the applicant society's establishment was to promote the development of the pastoral resources of Australia. See A. & S. Ruffy Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] HCA 18; (1958) 98 CLR 637 at 656 per Fullagar J.

8. It was argued for the respondent that horse breeding does not fall within section 23(h) of the Act, the only relevant headings for the purposes of argument being agricultural and pastoral resources. In my view it does not fall within the former.

9. In the light of section 14(1)(j) of the 1922 Income Tax Assessment Act it was further argued for the respondent that Parliament intended to confine "pastoral" to sheep and cattle grazing and breeding and reference was made to dictionaries which lay emphasis on the former. Speaking of the phrase "let to be used wholly or mainly for pasture" in the Land Law (Ireland) Act 1881, Lord Selborne in Westropp v. Elligott (1884) 9 AC 815 at 819. said:

"I entertain no doubt that 'pasture' in this place means

feeding cattle or other live stock upon land. This is both

the etymological sense of the word and its sense according

to ordinary agricultural use."

10. In Frauenfelder v. Reid [1963] HCA 3; (1963) 109 CLR 42 at 48, Dixon C.J. said that grazing or depasturing sheep or cattle could not be described in Australia as agricultural work, but Windeyer J. at p. 53 was prepared to consider the tending and managing of flocks and herds as falling within agricultural work. In Willeroo and Manbulloo Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 51; (1964) 111 CLR 336 at 341, a case under section 57AB of the Act, Kitto J. discussed "pastoral pursuits" in the context of breeding, rearing and fattening cattle which he regarded as a pastoral pursuit. From Fletcher v. Lord Sondes [1826] EngR 936; (1827) 3 Bing 581, 130 ER 606 at 637, it appears that "cattle" includes "horses". See also McKittrick v. Ryan [1935] VicLawRp 51; (1935) VLR 291 per Macfarlan J. English authority tends to treat animals breeding as falling under the heading "agricultural". See Peterborough Royal Foxhound Show Society v. Commissioner of Inland Revenue (1936) 2 KB 497. In G. Gramp & Sons Ltd. v. The Commissioner of Taxation (Cth.) [1965] HCA 53; (1965) 39 ALJR 244, Windeyer J. held that wine making is not of itself an agricultural pursuit but that was because it was not necessarily incidental to agricultural pursuits.

11. It appears to me that horse breeding and grazing fall within the term "pastoral pursuit". True, horses can be stall fed and breeding may take place in stalls but this is not the normal method of breeding horses in Australia. The normal method is keeping them at stud where they graze and breed on the pastures.

12. In my opinion the main activities of the applicant are the keeping of a stud book and maintaining a registry for the breed. These are not in themselves pastoral pursuits. Nevertheless the predominant purpose for which the applicant is established appears to be to promote the development of a pastoral resource of Australia, namely, a particular breed of a grazing animal, the horse, which like the sheep is a pastoral resource. The founding of the applicant under the auspices of the Royal Agricultural Society tends in my mind to confirm that its predominant purpose is the public interest rather than the private interests of breeders. The words of Rich J. in Royal Australasian College of Surgeons v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1943] HCA 34; (1943) 68 CLR 436 at 447 may be aptly transposed to this matter:-

"Its other objects are not collateral or independent but

merely concomitant and incidental to the main object. And

the fact that some of these subsidiary or ancillary

functions and purposes may indirectly and incidentally be of

benefit to the members of the profession does not destroy

the exemption claimed."

13. The reference in the applicant's objects to Australia "and elsewhere" does not in my view diminish the applicant's position. In practical terms its stud book and register and principal activities are all concerned with Australia and not with elsewhere.

14. The argument based upon the reference to "stock-raising" in section 14(1)(j) of the 1922 Income Tax Assessment Act and its disappearance from section 23(h) of the Act should, in my view, be rejected. The Act is expressed to be a codification. Although I note the later cautionary remarks of Lord Scarman in R. v. Heron [1982] UKHL 9; (1982) 1 WLR 451 following upon the decision of the House of Lords in Farrell v. Alexander [1976] UKHL 5; (1977) AC 59, it appears to me that the clear words "pastoral resources" in the present Act do not need to be restricted by an artificial construction based on previous legislation.

15. In spite of the competent handling of the case on behalf of the respondent I am of the opinion that the applicant's case should succeed. Accordingly I set aside the assessment and declare the applicant's income to be exempt under section 23(h) of the Act.
