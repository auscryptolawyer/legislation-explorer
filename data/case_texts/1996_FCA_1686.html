Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Vera Mary Kavich and Official Trustee In Bankruptcy [1996] FCA 1686 (8 August 1996)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION v. VERA MARY KAVICH AND

OFFICIAL TRUSTEE IN

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The appeal be dismissed.

2. The appellant pay the costs of the respondents of the

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

LOCKHART J The question in this appeal is whether, in the particular circumstances of the case, additional tax under s. 207 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ('the Assessment Act') is a debt provable in the bankruptcy of the first respondent, Mrs Kavich.

2. The appeal is brought by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation from the judgment of a judge of the Court (Hill J), now reported as Re Kavich; Kavich v Official Trustee in Bankruptcy 1 (1995) 58 FCR 82 in which his Honour held that the Deputy Commissioner was not entitled to prove in the bankrupt estate of Mrs Kavich for additional tax of $239,915.58 sought to be imposed by way of late payment penalties for failure to pay income tax the subject of assessments or amended assessments issued on 27 November 1978. His Honour also held that no part of the additional tax under s. 207 which is not admitted as a debt in Mrs Kavich's bankrupt estate could be paid out of surplus in her estate.

3. The facts may be briefly stated. On 28 February 1978 a sequestration order was made against the estate of Mrs Kavich. As a result of her bankruptcy there vested in the Official Trustee, the second respondent, inter alia, a share or shares in a company, Antlers Pty Limited, which is now in liquidation. It appears that the shares in that company are valuable and that, when its winding up is completed, the shareholders will receive a substantial distribution. But for that distribution the only amount realized from the estate has been the sum of $200.

4. On 27 November 1978, after Mrs Kavich became bankrupt, the Commissioner of Taxation issued notices of amended assessments under the Act in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1972, 1973 and 1974 and notices of assessment in respect of the years ended 30 June 1975 and 1976. The total amount of income tax stated to be payable pursuant to the notices of assessments and of amended assessments issued on 27 November 1978 was $102,054.77.

5. On 1 March 1981 Mrs Kavich was discharged from her bankruptcy.

6. On 20 August 1992 the Deputy Commissioner lodged a proof of debt in the estate in the sum of $338,448.98, of which $239,915.58 represented penalty tax under s. 207, described in the proof of debt as 'additional tax for late payment'.

7. The Official Trustee neither admitted nor rejected the proof of debt. In consequence, two applications were made to the Court at first instance: one by Mrs Kavich and one by the Deputy Commissioner. Both applications were made pursuant to s. 178 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 ('the Bankruptcy Act') by way of an appeal to the Court from the alleged failure of the trustee to determine whether to admit or reject the Deputy Commissioner's proof of debt for the amount of tax payable under s. 207. The Official Trustee submitted at first instance to any order which the Court might make except as to costs, and he adopted a similar course on appeal.

8. The essential question is whether the additional tax claimed by the Deputy Commissioner under s. 207 of the Assessment Act in respect of the tax made due and payable by the notices of assessment and of the amended assessment served on Mrs Kavich after the date of bankruptcy, is a debt provable in the bankruptcy of Mrs Kavich. In the alternative, the question is whether, even if it had not been a debt provable in the bankruptcy, it is to be paid out of the surplus in the bankrupt estate which will arise when the proceeds of the distribution on liquidation are ultimately received.

9. Section 82(1) of the Bankruptcy Act is the primary provision concerning the proof of debts in bankruptcy, and it provides:

'Subject to this Division, all debts and liabilities, present or

future, certain or contingent, to which a bankrupt was subject

at the date of the bankruptcy, or to which he may become subject

before his discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before

the date of the bankruptcy, are provable in his bankruptcy'

10. 'Debt' is not defined in s. 52, although it is defined in s. 5(1), the interpretation section, as including a liability.

11. The Commissioner's power to make assessments of income tax arises from ss. 166 to s. 169A of the Assessment Act, and his power to make amended assessments arises under s. 170. Once an assessment has been made the Commissioner is required by s. 174 to serve notice in writing upon the person liable to pay the tax.

12. Section 204 provides that the tax assessed shall:

'... be due and payable by the person liable to pay the tax

on the date specified in the notice as the date upon which tax

is due and payable, not being less than 30 days after the

service of the notice, or, if no date is so specified, on the

thirtieth day after the service of the notice.'

13. The Commissioner is empowered to grant extensions of time for payment of tax or permit payment of tax to be made by instalments (s. 206(1)).

14. Section 207(1) is the section of the Assessment Act with which this appeal is most directly concerned and it provides as follows:

'If any tax remains unpaid after the time when it became due

and payable or would, but for section 206, have become due and

payable, additional tax is due and payable by way of penalty by

the person liable to pay the tax at the rate of ... per annum on

the amount unpaid, computed from that time or, where, under

section 206, the Commissioner has granted an extension of time

for payment of the tax or has permitted payment of the tax to be

made by instalments, from such date as the Commissioner

determines, not being a date prior to the date on which the tax

was originally due and payable.'

15. The Commissioner is empowered to remit the additional tax or part of it by s. 207(1A).

16. The Commissioner is empowered to 'sue for recovery of any tax unpaid immediately after the expiry of the time when it becomes due and payable' (s. 207(2)).

17. When income tax becomes due and payable it 'shall be a debt due to the Commonwealth, and payable to the Commissioner in the manner and at the place prescribed' (s. 208(1)).

18. The arguments of counsel for the Deputy Commissioner and Mrs Kavich were centred on the question whether the additional tax due under s. 207 was a debt or liability, present or future, certain or contingent, to which Mrs Kavich was subject at the date of bankruptcy; and, in the alternative, whether that additional tax was a debt or liability to which Mrs Kavich might become subject before her discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of the bankruptcy.

19. The learned primary Judge perceived the issues between the parties as being reduced to four questions. He stated the first question as being whether, at the date of the bankruptcy, the s. 207 tax the subject of the Commissioner's proof was a debt or future liability, whether certain or contingent, to which Mrs Kavich was subject at the date of bankruptcy. His Honour answered that question in the negative.

20. The second question his Honour posed was whether the s. 207 tax was a debt or liability to which Mrs Kavich became subject before discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of her bankruptcy; and he answered that question in the affirmative, subject to his answer to the third question.

21. The third question discussed by his Honour was whether the s. 207 tax ceased to be payable, either at the date of bankruptcy or on discharge from bankruptcy. With respect to a notice of assessment issued and served before the date of bankruptcy, his Honour held that the liability for additional tax ceased at the date of bankruptcy. In respect of a notice of assessment issued and served after the date of bankruptcy, his Honour held that the debt created by the assessment was not a debt the bankrupt was liable to pay to the Commissioner and, therefore, no liability to additional tax could be imposed by s. 207. Furthermore, liability under s. 207 could not arise on the discharge of the bankrupt from bankruptcy because at that time the debt for the income tax was also discharged. His Honour answered the third question in the negative with respect to both occasions.

22. The fourth question posed by his Honour was whether the s. 207 tax should, even if not provable, be payable out of Mrs Kavich's bankrupt estate by analogy to interest; and his Honour answered that question in the negative.

Findings

23. It is not disputed that the income tax the subject of the assessments issued on 27 November 1978 is a debt provable in the bankruptcy of Mrs Kavich under s. 82 of the Bankruptcy Act.

24. It is well settled that liability to income tax is imposed by the Assessment Act itself. But, as I observed in Re Jon R Tait; Ex parte Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, unreported, 26 April 1996, there is some divergence of judicial opinion over whether income tax becomes due before the assessment of tax has been made or upon assessment and service of the notice of the assessment. In Re Mendonca; Ex parte Commissioner of Taxation (1969) 15 FLR 256 it was held by Gibbs J (at 259-260) that assessment is merely a method of ascertaining the extent of the liability to income tax and that income tax is a debt due and owing, although not payable, notwithstanding that an assessment has not been made. The views expressed by Gibbs J in Mendonca were reiterated by him in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1, where he said (then Gibbs CJ) at 9, after referring to s. 17 of the Assessment Act (which provides that income tax at the rates declared by the Parliament is levied and shall be paid upon the taxable income derived during the year of income by any person), that:

'These provisions suggest that the tax is due, in the sense of

owing, once the taxable income during a year of income has been

derived because there then arises a legal liability to pay it,

notwithstanding that the extent of the liability remains to be

ascertained and that payment is to be made in the future.'

25. Also in Clyne's Case, Mason J, with whose reasons for judgment Aickin and Wilson JJ agreed, said at 16-17:

'However the correct view in my opinion is that income tax

is due when it is assessed and notice is served of that

assessment and that the tax does not become payable before the

date fixed by s. 204. Dixon CJ, McTiernan, Williams, Webb and

Fullagar JJ in George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

((1952) [1952] HCA 21; 86 CLR 183 at 207) said that "tax is only due after it

is 'assessed' (see, for example, s. 204)". I recognise that on

other occasions members of this Court have said that "tax is a

debt due and owing, although not payable, notwithstanding that

no assessment has been made", in the words of Gibbs J in Re

Mendonca; Ex parte Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1969) 15

FLR 256 at 259). This approach can be traced back to the

majority decision of this Court in Commissioner of Stamps (WA) v

West Australian Trustee, Executor and Agency Co Ltd (Mortimer

Kelly's Case) ((1925) [1925] HCA 20; 36 CLR 98 esp. at pp. 105, 116 and 118).

I think that the decision is to be explained on the footing that

it was held that a debt for income tax not assessed until after

the deceased's death was a "debt due by the deceased" for the

purposes of Acts imposing death and probate duties. The

decision was so explained by Taylor J (dissenting) in Deputy

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Brown ((1958) [1958] HCA 2; 100 CLR 32 at

63-64) and this explanation derives support from the judgments

of Higgins and Starke JJ, if not from the judgment of the third

member of the majority, Knox CJ, in Mortimer Kelly's Case.'

26. In Taylor v Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 16 FCR 212 Woodward and Northrop JJ in a joint judgment, after referring to the judgment of Gibbs J in Mendonca, said at 217:

'A consideration of decisions by the High Court shows that

income tax does not become due until it is assessed and notice

of the assessment is served. This is made clear by what was

said in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1981) 150

CLR 1.'

27. Clyne's Case is therefore, in my opinion, authority for the propositions that income tax only becomes a debt due when it is assessed and notice is served of the assessment; and that income tax does not become payable before the date fixed by s. 204, that is, before the date specified in the notice of assessment as the date upon which the tax is due and payable, not being less than 30 days after the service of the notice, or, if no date is so specified, on the 30th day after it was served.

28. In the present case the relevant notices of assessment were not served until on or after 27 November 1978, a date after Mrs Kavich was made bankrupt. Nevertheless, the income tax is a provable debt under s. 82 because it is a debt to which Mrs Kavich could become subject before her discharge from bankruptcy, by reason of an obligation arising under the Assessment Act before the date of her bankruptcy.

29. The case is concerned, however, not with the primary tax but with the additional tax imposed by s. 207.

30. Section 207(1) imposes additional tax where any 'tax' remains unpaid after the time when it becomes due and payable. 'Tax' is defined by s. 6(1) of the Act to mean income tax as assessed under the Assessment Act. Section 207(3) contains its own special definition of tax for the purposes of s. 207, including interest under s. 170AA and additional tax under Part VII of the Act. Nothing turns on those provisions. Income tax that remains unpaid after it becomes due and payable, attracts both penalty additional tax under s. 207 and penalty interest under s. 207A. Section 207A, however, has no application to this case because it was introduced by the Taxation Laws Amendment (Self Assessment) Act 1992 (Act No 101 of 1992), which applies to the late payment of income tax in respect of the 1992/93 year of income and all subsequent years of income, but not previous years.

31. Additional tax payable under s. 207 accrues from the time that the unpaid income tax becomes due and payable, unless the Commissioner has granted an extension of time or permitted payment by instalments under s. 206 and the Commissioner determines that s. 207 is to apply from a date that is later than the date the income tax originally became due and payable.

32. Additional tax imposed for late payment under s. 207 is imposed by force of the Assessment Act itself and not by the Commissioner: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Trautwein [1936] HCA 48; (1936) 56 CLR 211.

33. The validity of s. 207 was challenged before the Supreme Court of Western Australia in Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Ichwan Pty Limited (1987) 87 ATC 5013 and in Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Fontana (1988) 88 ATC 4751. The Supreme Court rejected the argument advanced on behalf of the taxpayers that s. 207 was a law imposing taxation as well as a penal provision and therefore contravened s. 55 of the Constitution. Section 55 provides that laws imposing taxation shall deal only with the imposition of taxation, and any provision therein dealing with any other matter shall be of no effect. The Supreme Court held that s. 207 was a penal provision and not a law imposing taxation.

34. Additional tax imposed by s. 207(1) does not become due and payable until income tax has itself become due and payable and the tax remains unpaid. Otherwise, there is nothing to which s. 207(1) can attach, there is nothing upon which it can bite. The income tax must itself have been assessed and the notice of assessment served on the taxpayer. It then becomes due and payable on the date fixed pursuant to the operation of s. 204. And it is only if that tax remains unpaid thereafter that the additional tax becomes due and payable by way of penalty by operation of s. 207(1). Also, it can not be said, in my opinion, that additional tax under s. 207 in some sense accrues before that time.

35. Section 82 of the Bankruptcy Act should be given a wide interpretation as the learned primary Judge correctly observed, because the categories of provable debts should not be unduly restricted or confined by the evident policy of the bankruptcy legislation to provide a method of administration of the estates of insolvent persons to enable their assets to be brought in, administered and distributed rateably among the unsecured creditors, and to enable bankrupts to be freed from the obligation of repayment of debts upon discharge from bankruptcy. It is trite law that debts or liabilities due to unsecured creditors upon the bankruptcy of the debtor are converted into rights to prove in the bankrupt's estate and to receive a share of the net assets in the estate pro rata with other proved creditors.

36. In my view it cannot possibly be correct that additional tax under s. 207 answers the description of a debt or liability, present or future, certain or contingent, to which a bankrupt was subject at the date of the bankruptcy, when the assessments to the primary tax itself were not made until after the bankrupt was made bankrupt. Nor is it correct to say that such tax is a debt or liability, present or future, certain or contingent, to which the bankrupt may become subject before his discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of the bankruptcy. The primary tax itself in this case was not due until after the date of the bankruptcy of Mrs Kavich. The earliest time at which the additional tax can spring into being must be the date when the primary tax could be said to remain unpaid after it has become due and payable, all of which are events (if they arise in this case at all, a matter to which I shall refer shortly) which occurred after the date of the bankruptcy.

37. The highest point at which the Deputy Commissioner's argument can be put is to say that, because the primary liability to income tax is a debt or liability to which Mrs Kavich became subject before her discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of the bankruptcy (although not due and payable until a date subsequent to the date of her bankruptcy), in some way the additional tax under s. 207 relates to or attaches to that primary tax. I cannot accept this argument because in my view it is plain that additional tax under s. 207(1) in no sense can be said to be an accruing liability or one which is due or payable until the time has arrived when the primary tax answers the description of tax that remains unpaid after it has become due and payable.

38. There is no obligation incurred before the date of Mrs Kavich's bankruptcy that can be said to be an obligation by reason of which the additional tax under s. 207 may be said to be due or payable.

39. Nor can it be said that the additional tax answers the description of a contingent liability for the purposes of s. 82. Certainly it can be said that, before Mrs Kavich became a bankrupt, derivation of assessable income during the relevant years of income could result in liability to income tax, although the tax itself would not be due and payable until notices of assessment were served and the time for payment arrived. If it were not paid, then the Act itself would, by force of s. 207, operate to impose additional tax. But these are all matters of generality. There can be no contingent liability involved in relation to the additional tax under s. 207, until, at the very earliest, the notices of assessment and amended assessment were issued and served (again dates occurring after the date of Mrs Kavich's bankruptcy). In my opinion, there was no contingent liability until the date for payment of the primary tax arose.

40. We were referred in argument to the decision of the High Court in Community Development Pty Limited v Engwirda Construction Co [1969] HCA 47; (1969) 120 CLR 455 where the question was whether a sum payable to a building contractor under a building contract was due to a 'contingent creditor' within the meaning of s. 221 of the Companies Act 1961. The parties were in dispute concerning the question whether work under the building contract had been properly performed. The issue was whether in the circumstances of that case the builder was a contingent creditor entitled to petition for the winding up of the proprietor.

41. The same submission was put to the primary Judge and I agree with what he said about it, namely:

'there is a significant difference between an obligation to

pay s207 tax and the obligation of an owner to pay monies under

a building contract. In the latter case ... the obligation

exists from the date of the contract, notwithstanding that it is

contingent upon work being performed under the contract (and as

in that case the award of an arbitrator in the event of

dispute). However, no obligation at all arises for payment of

s207 tax unless and until there has been a failure to pay income

tax which has become due and payable. In my view, s. 207 tax

was not a debt or liability, whether certain or contingent, to

which Mrs Kavich was subject as at the date of bankruptcy

because the obligation to pay it arose only after the date of

bankruptcy' (Re Kavich v Official Trustee in Bankruptcy (1995)

95 ATC 4417 at 4421).

42. However, his Honour stated that, if he had not held that liability under s. 207 could not arise during the bankruptcy, he would have been inclined to hold that the additional tax under s. 207 is a debt or liability to which Mrs Kavich became subject before her discharge from bankruptcy by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of her bankruptcy. Fundamental to his Honour's reasoning was his acceptance of the principle enunciated in Re Mendonca that an obligation to pay income tax is incurred before assessment to tax. As indicated earlier in the passages cited from the judgments of Gibbs J in Mendonca and Gibbs CJ in Clyne's Case, his Honour was of the view that income tax is due in the sense of owing once the taxable income during the year of income has been derived because there then arises a legal liability to pay it. In my view, as indicated earlier, the reasoning of Mason J (Aickin and Wilson JJ agreeing), that income tax is not due until it is assessed and notice is served of that assessment, shows that before an assessment has been made the obligation imposed on a taxpayer by the Assessment Act is to pay income tax at a future date when the tax has been assessed and a notice of that assessment has been served. There was, in my opinion, no relevant obligation incurred before the date of Mrs Kavich's bankruptcy to which the additional tax under s. 207 can attach to answer the description of a debt or liability to which she became subject before discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of her bankruptcy.

43. Section 207(1) requires that the additional tax once it becomes payable is due and payable 'by the person liable to pay the tax'.

44. Even if there were, in the circumstances of this case, a time at which income tax remained unpaid after it became due and payable, Mrs Kavich does not answer the description of a person 'liable to pay' the income tax to the Commissioner. Upon the issue and service of the notice of assessment to tax, the Commissioner's rights to recover the tax were immediately converted into a right to prove in Mrs Kavich's bankruptcy. The income tax would not thereafter be described as a debt payable by Mrs Kavich to the Commissioner within the meaning of s. 207. Hence, as the income tax is no longer a debt which Mrs Kavich is liable to pay to the Commissioner, there can be no failure by her to pay. Consequently, no liability could arise to pay the additional tax imposed by s. 207.

45. In my opinion, therefore, the additional tax is not capable of being the subject of proof under s. 82(1) of the Bankruptcy Act.

46. It was argued both at first instance and on appeal on behalf of the Deputy Commissioner that, even if the additional tax is not provable in Mrs Kavich's estate, nevertheless it is payable out of her estate by analogy to interest. It is not necessary to consider this question in the light of my earlier reasoning because there is no time in this case at which a liability to payment of additional tax under s. 207 arose.

47. In the result, the Trustee is not entitled to admit the Commissioner's proof of debt for the additional tax under s. 207. Nor is the Trustee entitled to make payment of any amount on account of that tax out of any surplus in the estate of Mrs Kavich.

48. I would dismiss the appeal with costs.

LEE J I have had the opportunity of reading in draft the reasons for judgment of Lockhart J. I agree with the reasons and conclusions of his Honour and add the following comments.

2. In the absence of provisions in the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") which preserve the rights of the Commonwealth in respect of the debt of a bankrupt debtor for tax due under the Act and the liabilities under the Act of a taxpayer who becomes bankrupt, or which impose liabilities under the Act upon a trustee of a bankrupt taxpayer's estate, the appellant's arguments must fail.

3. It may be noted in passing that in the Student and Youth Assistance Act 1973 Parliament has addressed the effect of bankruptcy upon a debt due to the Commonwealth by providing that, in material respects, a debt arising under that Act is not subject to the terms of the Bankruptcy Act 1966.

4. Section 12ZW of the Student and Youth Assistance Act states that a debt owed to the Commonwealth by a student under that Act is not a provable debt in the bankruptcy of the student and the right of the Commonwealth to bring a proceeding against the student in respect of the debt is not affected by that bankruptcy.

TAMBERLIN J This is an appeal from a judgment of Hill J given on 30 June 1995.

2. The appeal calls for consideration of the relationship between s207 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) ("the Tax Act"), which provides for payment of additional tax as a penalty for late payment, and s82 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth) ("the Bankruptcy Act"), which defines debts provable in bankruptcy.

3. The appeal has a complex history but for present purposes it is only necessary to record those events which concern the circumstances giving rise to this appeal.

Background

4. On 28 February 1978 a sequestration order was made against Mrs Kavich.

5. On 30 March 1978 Mrs Kavich swore a Statement of Affairs in which she said that she owned no shares and in particular no shares in a company known as Antlers Pty Limited ("Antlers"). This statement was incorrect.

6. Subsequently Mrs Kavich told the Official Receiver that Antlers did not operate successfully. It went into liquidation on 21 August 1978.

7. Between 1971 and 1976 Mrs Kavich derived taxable income which was omitted from her returns or in respect of which returns were not lodged.

8. On 27 November 1978, amended assessments were issued to her in respect of these years. The day on which additional tax began to accrue was 30 days later on 27 December 1978.

9. A proof of debt for tax due was lodged with the Official Receiver in the amount of $99,620, which included a late payment penalty of $1,086.87. No part of this sum was paid from the bankrupt estate.

10. On 1 March 1981 Mrs Kavich was discharged from bankruptcy under the automatic discharge provisions of the Bankruptcy Act.

11. On 15 December 1987, the Supreme Court of New South Wales ordered that the winding up of the company, Antlers be stayed, subject to a condition as to the holding of a meeting for the election of directors.

12. The assets of Antlers were in fact far more valuable than disclosed either to the liquidator of that company or to the Official Receiver of the estate of Mrs Kavich.

13. The meeting upon which the stay order of the Supreme Court was conditioned was not held and the Full Federal Court, in a decision given on 10 December 1993, decided that the winding up of the company had not been terminated. See McAusland v The Commissioner of Taxation (1993) 47 FCR 369.

14. The Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, on 20 August 1992, filed a proof of debt in the sum of $338,448.98. This proof included an amount of $239,916 claimed under s207 of the Tax Act as additional tax for late payment.

15. This claim for additional tax, which accrued during the period over which the primary tax has remained unpaid, is the subject of this appeal.

16. The Appeal Book includes a report by the liquidator, dated 17 November 1994, which refers to a surplus of assets over liabilities of $1,892,114 on an "optimistic" basis and $1,104,935 on a "pessimistic basis". The Report states:

"These surpluses would be distributed to contributories after

realisation of assets and payment of proven creditors.

On a pessimistic basis, a contingent liability of income tax

assessed on the sale of the balance of the properties needs also

to be considered."

17. The Summary in that Report records that:

"From the above it can be readily seen that when all potential

assets of Antlers are taken into account it is clearly in a

position of balance sheet solvency, with a surplus for its

shareholders."

18. There is provision in the figures set out in the report for a contingent liability for income tax in the sum of $236,846.

The Issues

19. The matter was contested before Hill J on several grounds. These included a submission that the whole of the additional tax was provable in the bankruptcy. That submission is not made to this Court.

20. The submission before this Court is that the Official Receiver should accept the proof of debt lodged by the Commissioner on 20 August 1992 as to income tax in the amount of $98,533 and as to additional tax under s207 of the Tax Act from the due date specified in the Notice of Assessment to the date of discharge of Mrs Kavich from bankruptcy. It is also submitted that so much of the additional tax as is not admitted as a debt in the bankrupt estate of Mrs Kavich, should be paid out of the surplus. In the alternative, it is submitted that the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy should admit the proof of debt lodged by the Deputy Commissioner in the sum of $338,448.98 in the bankrupt estate of Mrs Kavich.

The Decision Below

21. In considering the application, Hill J addressed four questions.

22. The first question was whether under s82 of the Bankruptcy Act, the s207 additional tax was a debt to which Mrs Kavich was subject at the date of bankruptcy.

23. His Honour answered this question: "No". He considered that the additional tax which might be imposed by the Tax Act after bankruptcy did not come within the description of a liability to which Mrs Kavich was subject as at the date of the bankruptcy.

24. His Honour reasoned that no obligation to pay the s207 tax arose until there has been a failure to pay primary income tax which had become due and payable. In his view, the s207 tax did not give rise to an obligation because the obligation to pay only arose after the date of the bankruptcy.

25. His Honour distinguished the decision of the High Court in Community Development Pty Ltd v Engwirda Construction Co [1969] HCA 47; (1969) 120 CLR 455. The issue in that case was whether money payable under a building contract, where the parties were in dispute as to whether the work had been properly performed, was such as to make the builder a contingent creditor entitled to petition for the winding up of the owner. The Court held that the builder was such a creditor. The significant difference which his Honour perceived between that case and the present was that in the Engwirda case, the obligation existed from the date of the contract, notwithstanding that it was contingent upon work being performed under it. In the present case, no obligation arose for payment of s207 tax until there had been a failure to pay income tax which had become due and payable.

26. The second question which his Honour addressed was whether the s207 tax was a debt or a liability to which Mrs Kavich became subject before discharge, by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of bankruptcy.

27. Subject to his response to the third question, his Honour answered this question: "Yes". His Honour considered that the additional tax became payable as soon as the due date for payment of the income tax assessed after the date of the bankruptcy had passed. The liability to pay that tax continued until the date of discharge. He accepted that the term "provable debts" included those arising by statute. Cf Official Trustee in Bankruptcy v CS and GJ Handby Pty Ltd (1989) 7 ACLC 1070. He considered that the broad policy of the bankruptcy legislation called for a wide interpretation to s82 and that the categories of provable debt should not be unduly confined. The fact that a liability arose both because of an obligation incurred before bankruptcy (earning of taxable income) and the happening of an event after bankruptcy (failure to pay from day to day) should not be taken, in his view, to exclude the liability from the reach of s82.

28. The third question was whether s207 tax ceased to be payable on bankruptcy.

29. His Honour answered this question: "Yes".

30. His Honour considered that because the assessment was issued after bankruptcy, the position was complicated. He concluded that the primary income tax assessed was a provable debt. However, upon bankruptcy, the right to be paid the tax, was converted into a right to prove in the bankrupt estate and could thereafter no longer be correctly described as a debt payable by the bankrupt to the Commissioner. Therefore, since income tax assessed was not a debt which after sequestration the bankrupt was liable to pay to the Commissioner, there could never be a failure by the bankrupt to pay such tax. The consequence was that there never arose a failure to pay the tax imposed by s207. Upon discharge from bankruptcy the debt for income tax was discharged with the consequence there was no point of time when liability to s207 tax could arise.

31. The fourth question was whether s207 tax should be treated as analogous to interest.

32. His Honour answered this question: "No".

33. He noted that in view of his decision on the third question it was not necessary to answer the question, but he went on to consider the issue.

34. His Honour pointed out that it was common ground that s207 tax was not interest and therefore s82(3B) of the Bankruptcy Act did not apply.

35. He then referred to the common law rule of bankruptcy that accruing post-sequestration interest is not admitted to proof but is deferred until all other debts are been paid out of the estate and then is payable out of any surplus in the bankrupt estate: Re Hyman and Anor; Ex parte Law (1930) 3 ABC 61. He also referred to the decision of Dixon J in Mackenzie v Rees [1941] HCA 21; (1941) 65 CLR 1 at 8-12. He pointed out that the common law rule had only been applied to interest.

36. He did not consider that, under existing bankruptcy law, a debt which is not provable, could or should be payable out of the surplus in a bankrupt's estate. If s207 tax became payable and was a provable debt it would be payable rateably out of the estate. If it did not become payable, and was therefore never a provable debt, then there was no authority for the trustee to pay it out of the estate, whether before or after the debts of other creditors had been paid.

37. His Honour also considered that s207 tax could not fairly be estimated as at the date of the bankruptcy. He referred to Ellis and Co's Trustee v Dixon-Johnson (1924) 1 Ch 342 at 356-7. He said that where income tax had been assessed after the bankruptcy it would be impossible to estimate the amount of s207 tax. The commencement date for imposition of the tax would be unknown and so would the final date of computation.

38. Accordingly, in the light of his answers to the above questions, his Honour concluded that the trustee was not entitled to admit the Commissioner's proof of debt for the s207 tax and that the trustee was not entitled to make payment of any amount on account of that tax out of the estate.

The Appeal

39. The Commissioner's submission is that the s207 tax is payable, either from the assets remaining in the bankrupt estate, as a provable debt, or from the surplus of the estate which would otherwise accrue to Mrs Kavich as the former bankrupt.

40. The argument for the Commissioner is on the basis that insofar as the additional tax accrues up to the date on which Mrs Kavich was discharged, it is a debt which is properly provable in the bankruptcy, and that insofar as additional tax is not provable in the bankruptcy, it should be paid out of the surplus before any distribution of the surplus is made.

41. The Commissioner does not contend that Mrs Kavich has any present personal liability for the additional tax. The claim is on the basis that the liability is one which falls on the bankrupt estate and should be met out of the surplus in the bankrupt estate before any part of that surplus is paid over to Mrs Kavich.

42. Section 82(1) of the Bankruptcy Act provides:

"Subject to this Division, all debts and liabilities, present or

future, certain or contingent, to which a bankrupt was subject

at the date of the bankruptcy, or to which he may become subject

before his discharge by reason of an obligation incurred before

the date of the bankruptcy, are provable in his bankruptcy."

43. Section 17 of the Tax Act provides:

"Subject to this Act, income tax at the rates declared by the

Parliament is levied, and shall be paid, for the financial year

that commenced on 1 July 1965 and for each succeeding financial

year, upon the taxable income derived during the year of income

by any person, whether a resident or non-resident."

44. Section 204 of the Tax Act provides:

"s204(1) Subject to the provisions of this Part, any income tax

assessed shall be due and payable by the person liable to the

tax on the date specified in the notice as the date upon which

tax is due and payable, not being less than 30 days after the

service of the notice, or, if no date is so specified, on the

thirtieth day after the service of the notice.

(2) In subsection (1), "income tax" includes additional tax

under Part VII."

45. Section 207(1) of the Tax Act reads:

"If any tax remains unpaid after the time when it became due

and payable, or would, but for section 206, have become due and

payable, additional tax is due and payable by way of penalty by

the person liable to pay the tax at the rate of ... per annum on

the amount unpaid, computed from that time or, where, under

section 206, the Commissioner has granted an extension of time

for payment of the tax or has permitted payment of the tax to be

made by instalments, from such date as the Commissioner

determines, not being a date prior to the date on which the tax

was originally due and payable." (Emphasis added)

46. Section 17 imposed an obligation to pay tax before the date of bankruptcy as a result of taxable income having been derived by Mrs Kavich during the preceding years. That obligation is quantified and made payable by the issue of the Notice of Assessment under s204. Additional tax is attracted upon unpaid primary tax as the result of taxable income being derived during the relevant year of income. Accordingly, the primary tax "obligation", within the meaning of s82, arises at the time when the taxable income is derived. The tax does not have to be due and payable in order to give rise to the underlying obligation to pay tax.

47. The primary tax was not quantified (ie assessed) until 27 November 1978 . The assessment crystallised the pre-existing obligation so that interest became payable on and after the due date for payment of primary tax as notified in the Assessment.

48. I now turn to the questions addressed by Hill J.

49. The first question is whether s207 tax is a debt to which Mrs Kavich was subject as at the date of bankruptcy?

50. As at the date of bankruptcy, the Notice of Assessment had not been issued and the time for payment had not been specified. The Notices of Assessment were issued on 27 November 1978.

51. As at the date of bankruptcy, due to the operation of s17 of the Tax Act, there was an obligation to pay income tax for the relevant financial years preceding and up to 30 June 1976, upon the taxable income derived by Mrs Kavich.

52. Accordingly, Mrs Kavich was a person who was subject to an obligation to pay tax as at the date of bankruptcy in respect of the primary tax and as a result of the Notice of Assessment she became liable to pay primary tax by reason of that obligation.

53. However, because as at the date of bankruptcy there had been no Notice of Assessment issued, specifying a date on which the tax was due and payable, additional tax did not begin to accrue until 27 December 1978.

54. In Re Mendonca; Ex parte Commissioner of Taxation (1969) 15 FLR 256 at 259, Gibbs J, after referring to ss17, 204, 208 and 209 of the Tax Act, said:

"It is now settled that the effect of these and similar

provisions is that the liability to income tax is imposed by the

statute itself and that assessment is only a method of

ascertaining the extent of the liability, so that the tax is a

debt due and owing, although not payable, notwithstanding that

no assessment has been made..."

55. However, in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1 at 16-17, Mason CJ with whom Aickin and Wilson JJ agreed said:

"However the correct view in my opinion is that income tax is

due when it is assessed and notice is served of that assessment

and that the tax does not become payable before the date fixed

by s. 204. Dixon CJ, McTiernan, Williams, Webb and Fullagar

JJ in George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1952) 86 CLR

183 at 207) said that "tax is only due after it is 'assessed'

(see, for example, s. 204)". I recognize that on other

occasions members of this Court have said that "tax is a debt

due and owing, although not payable, notwithstanding that no

assessment has been made, ..." (Emphasis added)

56. The above statement of Mason J is binding in this case.

57. The question was raised as to whether s207 additional tax can be described as a "future contingent liability" to which Mrs Kavich was subject on 28 February 1978.

58. Any liability in respect of additional tax could not arise until 27 December 1978 when the primary tax remained unpaid after it had become due and payable. This date was after the date of bankruptcy. In these circumstances, I do not consider it can be said that Mrs Kavich was subject, on the date of bankruptcy, to any liability to pay additional tax.

59. The Commissioner submits that as at the date of the bankruptcy there was a liability under s17 to pay primary tax. As at that date, it is said, there also existed a contingent future liability. The contingency was said to be the possibility of non-payment of the primary tax after the date notified in the assessment for payment. However no "liability", within the meaning ascribed to that term in Clynes's case to make payment of primary tax arose until after bankruptcy and the liability to pay additional tax also first arose after that date. Any liability to pay additional tax could not properly be described as a contingent liability or debt to which Mrs Kavich was subject as at the date of bankruptcy.

60. Reliance was placed by the Commissioner on the decision in Community Development Pty Ltd v Engwirda Constructions Co (1969), supra at 459 where Kitto J, who gave the principal judgment said:

"In In Re William Hockley Ltd, Pennycuick J suggested as a

definition of 'a contingent creditor' what is perhaps rather a

definition of 'a contingent or prospective creditor', saying

that in his opinion it denoted 'a person towards whom, under an

existing obligation, the company may or will become subject to a

present liability upon the happening of some future event or at

some future date.' The importance of these words for present

purposes lies in their insistence that there must be an existing

obligation and that out of that obligation a liability on the

part of the company to pay a sum of money will arise in a future

event, whether it be an event that must happen or only an event

that may happen. A building contract creates as soon as it is

entered into, an obligation upon the building owner to pay the

contract price, either as a whole upon a future event or, more

usually, by progress and final payments each of which is to be

made on a future event. The event or events may not happen, but

if and when one of them does happen the building owner, by force

of the contractual obligation, must pay the builder a sum of

money ...."

61. As can be seen from the above, that decision related to the question whether money payable under a building contract, where the parties were in dispute as to the work had been properly performed was such as to make the builder a contingent creditor entitled to petition for the winding up of the owner.

62. The obligation in that case, as Hill J pointed out, was significantly different from an obligation to pay s207 tax. In that case, the liability existed as from the date of execution of the contract, notwithstanding that it was contingent upon work being performed.

63. In the present case, the liability under s207 did not arise until there had been a failure to pay primary income tax, which itself was only quantified and became due and payable after 27 December 1978. So the additional tax cannot be described as a debt or liability to which she was subject at the date of the bankruptcy. I would answer the first question in the negative.

64. I am not persuaded that there was any error in the conclusion of Hill J that the additional tax was not a debt or liability which Mrs Kavich was subject as at the date of bankruptcy.

65. The second question is: whether, subject to the answer given to the third question, s207 tax is a debt or liability to which Mrs Kavich became subject before discharge, by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of bankruptcy?

66. I will address this question on the basis that Mrs Kavich became "liable" to pay additional tax. However, for reasons given below in relation to the third question I do not accept that Mrs Kavich ever became liable to pay additional tax.

67. By operation of s17 of the Tax Act, Mrs Kavich, upon deriving taxable income in the financial years from 1972 onward, was obliged to pay primary income tax. This liability can properly be described, in my view, as an "obligation" incurred before the date of the bankruptcy. "Obligation" is a wide general term. This basic obligation, in respect of primary tax arises in respect of each financial year in which taxable income is derived as a result of the derivation of that income.

68. The obligation to pay additional tax under s207 does not arise from or by reason of the underlying obligation to pay the primary tax. It is a separate and distinct obligation which only arises from the non-payment of the primary tax.

69. The argument advanced on behalf of Mrs Kavich is that the reason she became liable to additional tax was the fact of non-payment of the primary tax and that this failure occurred after the date of sequestration, that is, after 28 February 1978. I consider this argument is correct.

70. The words used in s82 are by reason of an obligation incurred before the date of bankruptcy and in my view, the only obligation to pay s207 tax arose from the fact of non-payment. As at the date of bankruptcy the only obligation on Mrs Kavich was an obligation to pay primary tax which at that time had not been assessed and quantified so that it was not then due and payable. The obligation to pay additional tax only arose when that primary tax was not paid by the due date after it had been assessed. Although, in a sense, the underlying obligation to pay primary tax was a sine qua non of any liability to pay additional tax it did not itself give rise to that liability. It was only after failure, for whatever reason, to pay primary tax within time, that liability to pay additional tax came into existence. It did not arise from the derivation of taxable income. This non-payment occurred after bankruptcy. I therefore answer this question in the negative.

71. The third question raised is: whether s207 tax was not payable after bankruptcy because on bankruptcy Mrs Kavich ceased to be the person liable to pay the tax?

72. This question requires consideration of the effect of the bankruptcy in relation to the alleged post bankruptcy obligation of Mrs Kavich to pay additional tax. The question arises because s207 tax is payable "by the person liable to pay the tax". "The" tax referred to is the primary tax.

73. The legal effect of a sequestration order is well settled. In Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 44; (1984) 154 CLR 589 at 594-595, Gibbs CJ and Murphy, Brennan and Dawson JJ said:

"But since the debtor was already bankrupt when the petition

came to be heard, the remedies against the person and property

formerly available to the Deputy Commissioner had been taken way

and there was substituted a right to prove against the estate

which had become vested in Mr. Andrew as trustee: see In re

Thomas; Ex parte Commissioners of Woods and Forests ((1888) 21

QBD 380 at 383). At that time the Deputy Commissioner "was not a

mere creditor. (He) was a creditor whose claim was in proof.

(His) claim was no longer a mere right of action for a debt.

(He) could no longer have maintained an action as for a debt.

The debt had been, at any rate provisionally, merged in an

equitable execution ...": see In re Higginson and Dean; Ex parte

Attorney-General ((1899) 1 QB 325 at 333); Ex parte Trustee of

the Property of Cork ((1932) 5 ABC 1 at 6); and In re Cole; Ex

parte Richards ((1966) 9 FLR 190 at 191). Amounts which were

owed by a debtor at the date of the bankruptcy may,

notwithstanding his bankruptcy, still be described as debts, and

the Act refers to them as such: see, e.g., ss. 58(3), 84(1),

85(1), 86(1), 513(1), 154(1)(b). They are "debts" from which the

bankrupt is not released until he is discharged from bankruptcy:

s. 153. However, in our opinion, they are no longer debts "still

owing" within the meaning of s. 52(1)(c). Although, as was

rightly observed in the Federal Court, one dictionary meaning of

"owing" is "that is yet to be paid", the word connotes a sense

of obligation to make the payment. The effect of the bankruptcy

however is that the debtor is no longer obliged to pay his

creditors; indeed he is disabled from doing so. If he offered

payment they could not safely accept it; their right is a right

of proof against the estate." (Emphasis added)

74. More recently, in Cummings v Claremont Petroleum NL (unreported, 20 June 1996, High Court), Brennan CJ, Gaudron and McHugh JJ, referred to the decision of the Court of Appeal in Heath v Tang ((1993)) 1 WLR 1421 and said at 11:

"After canvassing the authorities, his Lordship concluded

that they demonstrated 'that in principle a bankrupt cannot in

his own name appeal from a judgment against him which is

enforceable only against the estate vested in his trustee'. In a

later unreported case, Hoffman LJ said:

'The essence of (the) decision in (Heath v Tang) is that a

bankruptcy order divests the bankrupt of any further interest in

what debts he owes because it provides that he shall no longer

be under any personal liability. An appeal from the judgment

against him or an application to set aside the judgment against

him is therefore a matter for his trustee, but does not concern

the bankrupt.'

So far as a judgment entered in an action against a bankrupt

creates or evidences a provable debt, we respectfully agree that

the bankrupt has no financial interest which would confer locus

standi to appeal in his own name against the judgment. That is

because it is fundamental to the law of bankruptcy that the

bankrupt is divested of both his interest in his property and

liability for his provable debts." (Emphasis added)

75. Dawson and Toohey JJ referred to Heath's case and said at 23:

"In the course of his judgment Hoffman LJ referred to

Rochfort v Battersby ((1849) [1849] EngR 429; 2 HL Cas 388 (9 ER 1139)) which

concerned the locus standi of a bankrupt to appeal against a

judgment in foreclosure proceedings commenced after the

bankruptcy. Lord Cottenham LC said:

'There cannot be a stronger proof therefore that the Courts have

always considered these acts of Parliament as divesting the

insolvent of all title and interest in the property, which would

authorise and justify him in entering into any litigation

respecting it.'

Hoffman LJ referred to some earlier authorities relating to

challenges against the judgment on which the bankruptcy petition

was founded and concluded:

'The insolvency law has of course changed a great deal since the

time of Lord Eldon ... Nevertheless, the principle that the

bankrupt is divested of an interest in his property and

liability for his debts remains fundamental in the new code. The

consequences for the bankrupt's right to litigate do not seem to

us inconvenient or productive of injustice. The bankruptcy court

acts as a screen which both prevents the bankrupt's substance

from being wasted in hopeless appeals and protects creditors

from vexatious challenges to their claims.'

Those comments are apposite so far as the Act is concerned. They

reinforce the conclusion we have reached that each right of

appeal vests in the trustee." (Emphasis added)

76. The submission for Mrs Kavich is that upon her bankruptcy all obligations to pay debts or meet liabilities were converted to rights to prove in the estate of Mrs Kavich and therefore Mrs Kavich could not be described after 2 February 1978 (the date of bankruptcy) as being "the person liable to pay the tax to the Commissioner" and so any liability to pay additional tax could not arise. Indeed, the Commissioner, after bankruptcy, was absolutely prohibited from enforcing any remedy for recovery of the pending tax in respect of which he has lodged a proof. Mrs Kavich had no control over the bankrupt estate such that she could pay the additional tax from that estate.

77. Where a debtor becomes bankrupt the property of the bankrupt, not being after acquired property, vests in the official trustee. See s58(1) of the Bankruptcy Act.

78. Section 58(3) of the Bankruptcy Act provides that:

"58(3) Except as provided by this Act, after a debtor has

become a bankrupt, it is not competent for a creditor:

(a) to enforce any remedy against the person or the property of

the bankrupt in respect of a provable debt; or

(b) except with the leave of the Court on such terms as the

Court thinks fit, to commence any legal proceedings in respect

of a provable debt or take any fresh step in such a proceeding."

(Emphasis added)

79. The appellant submitted that the effect of s58 was that the bankruptcy did not extinguish the liability of Mrs Kavich to pay additional tax but rather barred the enforcement of remedies and limited the pursuit of legal proceedings by operation of s58(3). It was said that after bankruptcy, Mrs Kavich was not prevented from paying her debts because she might, for example, pay debts from assets which were not included in property divisible among her creditors. Therefore, it was said that the underlying liability to pay primary tax continued after bankruptcy and was only extinguished on her discharge, and that up to that time she had the capacity, if she so desired, to pay that debt. However, it seems to me that this does not advance the appellant's case, because a payment in such circumstances would be voluntary and consistent with absence of "liability". It is somewhat odd to speak in terms of a continuing liability to pay a debt after bankruptcy when there is an absolute statutory prohibition on enforcement of that debt. In any event, the statements of the High Court, set out above, make it quite clear that the bankrupt is divested not only of property, but also of liability to creditors for provable debts as a result of the bankruptcy and submissions as to possible capacity to lawfully pay the debt are therefore of no relevance or assistance to the appellant.

80. Applying this reasoning to the present case, the position is as follows. As at the date of the bankruptcy there was an obligation under s17 of the Tax Act to pay primary tax. During the bankruptcy an assessment was made of that tax and by reason of s204 that tax became due and payable, on the due date, namely 27 December 1978. The bankrupt became "liable" to pay the primary tax by reason of an obligation incurred before the bankruptcy (ie by reason of s17). However, upon bankruptcy the liability of Mrs Kavich was converted into a right to prove and she was "divested of both (her) interest in her property and liability for her provable debts". The primary tax was a provable debt. Therefore, after bankruptcy she no longer had any liability to pay that tax. Additional tax is only payable by "the person liable to pay the (primary) tax". The result is that as at the date when the primary tax was assessed Mrs Kavich was not the person liable to pay the primary tax so that she could not incur any liability to pay the additional tax. In other words, she never became liable to pay additional tax.

81. For the above reasons I answer the third question in the negative and I agree with the conclusion reached by Hill J on this question.

82. The fourth question is whether s207 tax should be treated as analogous to interest?

83. As I have concluded that no claim for s207 tax has arisen, it is not necessary to consider whether the principles in Mckenzie v Rees [1941] HCA 21; (1941) 65 CLR 1 at 8-12 per Dixon J, and Re Hyman and Anor; Ex parte Law (1930) 3 ABC 61 should be applied to claims for additional tax by analogy to interest.

84. A submission was made that the trustee, upon sequestration, became "the person liable to pay the tax within the meaning of s207". The basis advanced was that the Commissioner could prove in the estate in respect of the taxation debt and under the Bankruptcy Act the trustee was bound to administer the estate. The result was said to be that the Commissioner was entitled to be paid, by the trustee, a proportionate amount of any assets of the estate. Hence, it was said, that since the trustee was "the person liable to pay the tax" as from the date it became due and payable up to the date of payment, then the full amount of the tax and additional tax could be recovered from the trustee.

85. It is simply not correct to say that the trustee ever became liable to pay any tax to the Commissioner because the only person who arguably became liable to pay the tax was Mrs Kavich. There was never any debtor-creditor relationship under the Tax Act or the Bankruptcy Act as between the Commissioner and the trustee.

86. The obligations of the trustee arise as a result of the Bankruptcy Act and the duty are to administer the estate and pay the provable debts out of the estate. The trustee does not come under any liability in the capacity of trustee or otherwise by reason of the Bankruptcy Act, or the Tax Act, to pay any primary or additional tax to the Commissioner. Accordingly, I reject this argument.

Conclusions

87. Accordingly, I would dismiss the appeal and order that the appellant pay the costs of the appeal.
