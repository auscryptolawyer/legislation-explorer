Madeleine Lloyd and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 116; (1993) 17 AAR 285 (1993) 29 ALD 905 (15 April 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

MADELEINE LLOYD v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V92/837

AAT No. 8649

Number of pages - 6

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 116; (1993) 17 AAR 285

[1993] AATA 116; (1993) 29 ALD 905 (extract)

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.M. Forrest(1) (Deputy President), J. Brassil AM(1) (Member) and D. Elsum AM(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - nomination of beneficiary - personal representative - whether a discretion exists in s. 111(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to pay entitlements of a deceased member to a person or persons other than the personal representative of the deceased member.

Superannuation Act 1976 s. 111(1)

The Queen v Anderson; ex Parte Ipec-Air Pty Limited [1965] HCA 27; 113 CLR 177

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 17 March 1993

15:4:1993

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the superannuation benefit of the deceased member, Michael Radysz, be paid to the applicant Madeleine Lloyd, pursuant to sub-section 111(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

B.M. FORREST, J. BRASSIL AND D. ELSUM This is an application by Madeleine Lloyd for a review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, to pay the superannuation benefit of a deceased member to his personal representative, the State Trust Corporation of Victoria, hereinafter called 'State Trustees'.

2. The jurisdiction of the Tribunal arises pursuant to sub-section 154(6) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act").

3. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to s. 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (numbered T1-T19). The applicant and Mr Novak, a sales representative with AMP Society, gave oral evidence. Mr Robertson appeared for the applicant and Ms Munoz for the Commissioner. There was no appearance of behalf of State Trustees, although given notice of the application and of the hearing date.

4. Sub-section 111(1) of the Act provides:

"111. (1) Where:

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee because of death; or

(b) ...

being a person in respect of whom benefit is not payable under Part VI,

a lump sum benefit of an amount equal to the person's accumulated

contributions must be paid out of the Fund to his or her personal

representatives or to such person or persons (if any) as the

Commissioner determines."

5. The issue before the Tribunal is whether on the proper construction of subsection 111(1) the Commissioner has a discretion to pay the entitlement of a deceased member to a person or persons other than the personal representative of the deceased member.

6. Part VI of the Act which concerns benefits payable to spouses and children is not relevant for present purposes.

7. Michael Radysz ("Radysz") was an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act. He was an employee of Telecom. After a short illness, Radysz died on 22 March 1991, intestate. He was then 62 years of age and had never married. He had no known dependents or relatives in Australia.

8. The applicant, Madeleine Lloyd, was not aware until after the death of Radysz that he had nominated her as beneficiary, on 26 June 1990, on a form (T6/23) headed 'Nominations of Beneficiaries' supplied to him by Telecom Australia on which he wrote her name (given name misspelt "Madalene") her address and beside relationship "niece" and at the foot signed it with his usual signature. As an existing member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS) he was required at this time to remain in the Scheme or else to transfer to the newly established Telecom Superannuation Scheme (TSS). On 29 June 1990, he signed another Telecom form electing to remain in the CSS. Although there is no requirement that members of the CSS nominate a beneficiary this was apparently, a practice introduced in the TSS. The forms indicating his wishes were retained by Telecom and after his death, were made available to the applicant who provided a copy to the Commissioner.

9. On 28 May 1991, the applicant brought to the attention of the Commissioner her claim for Radysz's superannuation benefit.

10. On 10 July 1991, Letters of Administration of the estate of Radysz were granted by the Supreme Court of Victoria to State Trustees. The value of the estate is approximately $250,000.

11. Radysz had lost contact with his immediate family. Efforts to trace his mother and brother in the Ukraine have been fruitless it is not known if they are alive.

12. By letter dated 19 June 1991 to the Commissioner, State Trustees inquired about the superannuation of Radysz and in particular sought confirmation that the superannuation did not form part of his estate and would be paid direct to the nominated beneficiary.

13. The Commissioner replied that a superannuation benefit of approximately $44,000 was due to the estate. In the letter to State Trustees the Commissioner made no mention of the nominated beneficiary. However in later correspondence to the applicant's solicitors (23 August 1991), the Commissioner wrote in relation to the "nomination of beneficiaries' form:

"It should be noted that this form would only have had effect if the

deceased had elected to join the Telecom Superannuation Scheme (TSS).

Since the deceased elected to remain with the CSS the payment of his

refund of accumulated contributions is made in accordance with that

scheme."

14. On 29 July 1991, State Trustees requested that the CSS forward the proceeds of the Superannuation to the estate.

15. On 23 August 1991, a delegate of the Commissioner decided that Radysz's superannuation benefit would be paid to State Trustees. On reconsideration, at the request of the applicant, another delegate confirmed the original decision of 23 August 1991.

16. In 1990, Mr Novak gave Radysz advice about the respective superannuation schemes prior to his election to remain in CSS. Radysz made it clear he wanted to leave everything to the applicant, and Mr Novak advised him to make a Will.

17. The applicant gave evidence of the relationship of her family and herself with Radysz over more than 40 years. It was a poignant story of three young male Ukrainians who left their homeland at the end of the Second World War after service in the Russian Army. They eventually joined a group, mainly Ukrainian exiles in Switzerland.

18. Her father married there and in 1950 her parents were part of a group of migrants who were re-settled in Melbourne. After some time in a migrant camp her parents, together with her father's two friends, Radysz and Nevolony, moved to a house in Coburg, sharing it with other families. The applicant was born while they were living there and another child, a sister, was born in 1954. The father worked on the railways but the other two gained positions with the Postmaster-General's Department, later Telecom Australia.

19. Myhal, the applicant's father, left the family while the two girls were still young and the two friends, Radysz and Nevelony remained. Each took responsibility associated with the raring of the children with Radysz caring, in particular, for Madeleine and Nevelony for the younger sister. She called Radysz "uncle". After divorcing Myhal in 1958, the applicant's mother continued to accept the assistance of the two other men and whole group moved to a house in Broadmeadows in which two other families also lived.

20. Following her own marriage, the applicant moved to Craigieburn and Radysz used to visit three week-nights and most of the weekends. He contributed to the cost of holidays, as he had done for the family when she was a child. He bought a block of land at Craigieburn and eventually built a house for which he asked the applicant to choose the furnishings. He continued to visit regularly, eating most evening meals with the applicant's family and showing his interest in the development of her three children. He had little command of English and depended on the applicant and her family when he needed assistance in filling out forms. The applicant was not cross examined. The Tribunal accepts her evidence without reservation.

21. It was contended on behalf of the applicant that:

(a) The delegate of the Commissioner should have taken into

consideration the expressed intention of Radysz in nominating the

applicant as beneficiary, and made payment of the lump sum benefit to

the applicant even though there was no requirement that CSS members

make such a nomination.

(b) The delegate was wrong in law in contending that the discretion

given to the Commissioner in subsection 111(1) only operates in the

absence of a personal representative, and further contends that the

language of the subsection clearly gives the option or discretion to

the Commissioner to pay the lump sum benefit to the applicant.

22. The Commissioner in maintaining the correctness of the decision under review, contended:

(a) The discretion given to the Commissioner in subsection 111(1) only

operates in the absence of a personal representative of the deceased.

The language in subsection 111(1) does not allow the Commissioner the

option of paying the lump sum benefit to some other person or persons

where there is a personal representative.

(b) There is no provision in the Act for the Commissioner to take into

account the nomination of a beneficiary in these circumstances.

23. In our opinion, the view of the Commissioner that the discretion to pay out "to such person or persons as the Commissioner determines" only operates in the absence of a personal representative is to read down the ordinary meaning of the provision. Expressed another way, the contention of the Commissioner is that if there is a personal representative he is precluded from making a determination to pay to any other person. Such an interpretation is, in our opinion, to limit the meaning of the express language of the provision. The obligation of the Commissioner is mandatory; he must pay the benefit to the personal representative or to such person or persons (if any) as he determines. The word "or" after personal representative is used disjunctively. In common parlance "or" is used as an alternative. The subsection does not provide a descending order for payment. It does not provide for instance that payment be made to such person or persons only in the absence of a personal representative.

24. While it would be a matter of importance how the Commissioner exercises this discretion when there are personal representatives, the Commissioner would be expected to exercise his discretion in accordance with the often quoted principle expressed by Kitto J in The Queen v Anderson; Ex Parte Ipec-Air Pty Limited [1965] HCA 27; 113 CLR 177 at 189:

"...a discretion allowed by statute to the holder of an office is

intended to be exercised according to the rules of reason and justice,

not according to private opinion; according to law, and not humour, and

within those limits within which an honest man, competent to discharge

the duties of his office, ought to confine himself:"

25. In our opinion, the plain meaning of the language of the provision does not operate to fetter the discretion of the Commissioner in the manner as submitted on his behalf.

26. The further contention that the Commissioner cannot pay the lump sum benefit to a nominated beneficiary because the legislation makes no provision for a nominated beneficiary cannot, in our view, be sustained. The Act makes provision for payment to persons other than personal representatives. It does not follow, that a nominated beneficiary is excluded from the category of "such person or persons" to whom a benefit may be paid.

27. The Tribunal was invited to consider the Explanatory Memorandum to the Superannuation Bill 1976 and to the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Bill 1991. That course is permissible for the purposes set out in paras. (a) and (b) of s. 15AB(1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901. The only possible need for resort to extrinsic material in this application is under para. (a) to confirm that the meaning of the provision is the ordinary meaning conveyed by the text as the purposes specified in para (b) do not apply and therefore there is no justification to refer to extrinsic material in that context. However in our view, the ordinary meaning is plain enough. We think we should add that the words used in sub-section 111(1) are not at odds with the text of the extrinsic material referred to.

28. Both parties agreed that in the event the Tribunal were to find the Commissioner has a discretion, then it would be appropriate as a matter of administrative convenience that the Tribunal, having heard the evidence and standing in the shoes of the Commissioner, exercise the discretion rather than remitting the matter back to the Commissioner with any direction or recommendation. We are satisfied it is within our power to do so.

29. On the totality of the evidence before the Tribunal, we have come to the conclusion that it would be a proper exercise of the discretion to be found in subsection 111(1) of the Act for the Commissioner to pay the lump sum benefit of Radysz to the applicant.
