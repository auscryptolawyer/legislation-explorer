ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX assesable income trading stock on hand whether goods in transit under contracts of sale from overseas constitute "trading stock on hand" before arrival in Australia for sale by purchaser in Australia.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – ss. 6(1), 28, 48, 51(1) & (2), 82D, 166;

Commonwealth Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 - s. 16(a);

Finance Act 1921.

Kwei Tek Chao v. British Traders and Shippers Limited (1954) 2 Q.B. 459;

Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v. The Executor Trustee and Agency Company of Couth Australia Limited [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 C.L.R. 108;

REASONS FOR DECISION

21 September 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. At midnight on 30 June 1985 shipping containers filled with a variety of frozen products as diverse as chow mein from Taiwan; cooked and peeled prawns from Malaysia, hake fillets from Uruguay; calamari from Japan; vegetables from Belgium; and fish fingers from New Zealand were en route by cargo ships to Australia. They were in the course of being delivered to the Applicant with a view to sale by the Applicant. They were to become part of the "trading stock" of the Applicant if they were not already part of the trading stock of the Applicant. Each delivery was being made pursuant to a contract entered into between a supplier (as vendor) and the Applicant (as purchaser). The price in each case had been paid. The question for determination is to what extent the total cost of the goods in transit ($1,389,517) is to be taken into account in calculating the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1985.

  1. The Applicant is a company which carried on business as a dealer in such frozen products. In the ordinary course of its business it received such goods delivered from its suppliers; it received them into storage under the control of the Applicant; it de-containerized the products delivered; it tested them for conformity to standards; and it marketed them to other wholesalers and to retailers within Australia. I accept that it was no part of the business of the Applicant to deal in such products outside Australia and no part of its business to deal in such products while in transit. I find that in one instance, as an alternative to having one container delivered to its own store, the Applicant had that container delivered direct to the store of a purchaser (Order 4139 $30,396).
  2. According to the documents presented in evidence, the contracts fell into the following categories:-

1. FIS (free into store)

7 contracts $ 177,238

2. CIF (cost insurance freight)

3 contracts $ 102,943

3. C&F (cost and freight)

21 contracts documentation

pre-1 July $1,067,250

2 contracts documentation

post-30 June $ 42,086

TOTAL $1,389,517

4. I find that, on or before 30 June 1985, all contracts for purchase had been in all respects completed as to their terms; payment of the purchase price had been completed; none of the goods had reached Australia; and none of the goods were in the direct physical control of the Applicant. I further find that, of the CIF and CF contracts, in all instances save two (for $42,086), bills of lading had been delivered to the Applicant before 30 June 1985. I find that the physical delivery of goods into Australia and into the control of the Applicant (or in one instance, its customer) only occurred after 30 June 1985.

5. The question was argued as to what findings of fact should appropriately be made as to the "passing of property" in the Sale of Goods Act sense. It was common ground that, as a general rule, "property" passes only when it is intended to pass; that ordinarily what has been referred to as a "conditional property" (of post) passes on delivery of a Bill of Lading in relation to CIF and C&F contracts; and that ordinarily "property" only passes on FIS contracts on delivery of the goods to the purchaser. It was argued for the Commissioner that the evidence presented before the Tribunal was not sufficient to enable the Applicant - as the party bearing the burden of proof -to satisfy me in relation to any of the 33 contracts that the terms of agreement had prevented the passing of an unqualified title to the goods on or before 30 June 1985. I am not persuaded that that is so, even though I accept that no witness testified directly as to the negotiation of the terms of the agreements for sale. I am not so persuaded because I accept that the documents presented before me did constitute the documentary record of the transactions as maintained by the Applicant. I also accept that it formed the basis upon which the goods were accepted and that it constituted, not only a record of payments made, but was the record which was relied on in arranging to effect the payment.

6. At this point it is appropriate to deal with the question of "conditional property", the phrase used by Devlin, J. (as he then was) in Kwei Tek Chao v. British Traders and Shippers Limited (1954) 2 Q.B. 459 (at p.487) when, in speaking of a CIF contract, his Lordship said:-

"I think that the true view is that what the buyer obtains, when the title under the documents is given to him is the property in the goods, subject to the condition that they revest if upon examination he finds them to be not in accordance with the contract. That means that he gets only conditional property in the goods, the condition being a condition subsequent."

  1. If "property" rather than "possession and control" be the test for determining what constitutes "trading stock on hand" I am not persuaded that the circumstance which prompted his Lordship to hold as he did presents a relevant or significant qualification to the concept of the passing of property in the context in which it needs to be considered here. As I understand the authority of the decision, what his Lordship referred to in using the phrase "conditional property" was a situation in which the "property" had passed between the parties but had passed in such circumstances that, by reason of the operation of certain factors and the subsequent exercise of power at the option of the purchaser, the purchaser might elect that property in the goods revert to the vendor.
  2. The Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") requires of the Commissioner that he shall "make an assessment of the amount of the taxable income of any taxpayer, and of the tax payable thereon" (cf. s.166 and following). S.48 provides that:-

"In calculating the taxable income of a taxpayer, the total assessable income derived by him during the year of income shall be taken as a basis, and from it there shall be deducted all allowable deductions."

9. Sales generate assessable income for the Applicant. As a result, in each year it is necessary for the Commissioner to determine the assessable income proper to that year. That is determined by considering what "assessable income has been derived" during the year of income. Depending upon the circumstances of the Applicant the measure of derivation might be an "accruals" or a "cash receipts" basis [Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v. The Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Limited (Carden's Case) (1938) 63 C.L.R. 1083. Once the appropriate method of calculation had been chosen the computation of assessable income for the year in question would be determined by the events relevant to that year of income. But s.48 also provides that taxable income is only to be determined after bringing to account "all allowable deductions". For a trader, such as the present Applicant, one of the most obvious expenses - not necessarily the largest - is the cost of goods sold. The cost of goods sold constitutes an allowable deduction in the year in which such expenses are "incurred". S.5K2) expressly provides that:_

"Expenditure incurred or deemed to have been incurred in the purchase of stock used by the taxpayer as trading stock shall be deemed not to be an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature."

It is acknowledged in these proceedings that the Applicant claimed to have incurred such an allowable deduction in the sum of $1,389,517: a claim which has been allowed by the Commissioner. There was no suggestion in these proceedings that the claim was not allowable.

  1. In so far as the Act makes provision for the "derivation" of assessable income and for allowance of losses and outgoings "incurred" as provided for in s.51(l), both the Commissioner and taxpayers are strictly bound by the provisions of the Act. Neither has an option as to whether or not things which have happened shall be treated as relevant to one year in preference to another. So it is that if, for example, a taxpayer chooses to defer the derivation of income until a later year, he may do so [Brent v. F.C.T. (1971) 125 C.L.R. 4183. Similarly, when faced with a recurrent expense which might properly be paid in one year or in a later year, the test as to when the liability was "incurred" may well be to consider when the liability was actually met rather when it might have been met.
  2. That being so, special provision is made in sub-division B of the Act, to deal with problems of trading stock, a term defined by s.6(1) to mean:-

"In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears trading stock includes anything produced, manufactured, acquired or purchased for purposes of manufacture, sale or exchange,..."

S.28(1) addresses the problem of trading stock in the following terms:-

"Where a taxpayer carries on any business, the value, ascertained under this subdivision, of all trading stock on hand at the beginning of the year of income, and of all trading stock on hand at the end of that year shall be taken into account in ascertaining whether or not the taxpayer has a taxable income."

Recognizing that values may rise during the year, sub-section (2) provides:-

"Where the value of all trading stock on hand at the end of the year of income exceeds the value of all trading stock on hand at the beginning of that year, the assessable income of the taxpayer shall include the amount of the excess."

On the other hand, the Act recognizes that values may fall, with the result that sub-section (3) provides:-

"Where the value of all trading stock on hand at the beginning of the year of income exceeds the value of all trading stock on hand at the end of that year, the amount of the excess shall be an allowable deduction."

It has long been recognized that the stock of a trader as "on hand" at the beginning and end of a fiscal period may need to be brought to account in determining his "income" for taxation purposes. Dixon, J. (as he then was) addressed some such problems in Carden's Case where he said, at p.154:-

"In the present case we are concerned with rival methods of accounting directed to the same purpose, namely, the purpose of ascertaining the true income. Unless in the statute itself some definite direction is discoverable, I think that the admissibility of the method which in fact has been pursued must depend upon its actual appropriateness. In other words, the inquiry should be whether in the circumstances of the case it is calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income. We are so accustomed to commercial accounts of manufacturing or trading operations, where the object is to show the gain upon a comparison of the respective positions at the beginning and end of a period of production or trading-, that it is easy to forget the reasons which underlie the application of such a method of accounting to the purpose of ascertaining taxable income. Although the field of profit-making which it covers in practice is probably much greater than any other among the manifold forms of income or revenue, it is a system of accounting which does not represent the primary or basal position from which an investigation of income for taxation purposes begins. Speaking generally, in the assessment of income the object is to discover what gains have

during the period of account come home to the taxpayer in a realized or immediately realizable from....."

"The reasons which underlie the practice of estimating for taxation purposes the income from trade or manufacture by means of a commercial profit and loss account consist in the impracticability of computing income in any other way and in the adoption for fiscal purposes of recognized commercial principles. The computation of profits from manufacture and trading has always proceeded upon the principle that the profit may be contained in stock-in-trade and 'outstandings'. Whether this is to be explained on some view that the purpose is to ascertain what is the detachable increase in circulating capital, or more simply on the ground of common sense and the teachings of experience, the result for the purposes of taxation is the same. The result is that a tax upon the profits or income of such a business must be understood as a tax upon the profits or income computed according to the system, because, according to common understanding and commercial principles, that is the method of determining the profits. The basis of a trading account is stock on hand at the beginning and end of the period and sales and purchases. In such an account book debts represent what before sale was trading stock and it is almost inevitable that they should be taken into consideration upon an accrual and not a cash basis. But nearly all income tax legislation is against the practice which obtains commercially of making a reserve for bad debts or discounting the amount of book debts by a percentage for bad or doubtful debts. Specific provision is usually made for the deduction from the book debts accruing during the accounting period of such debts only as have proved to be bad during that period and have been written off. Usually the deduction is authorized also of book debts included in a previous accounting period which in the year under assessment prove to be bad and have been written off. Then book debts written off as bad which in a subsequent accounting period are nevertheless paid are to be brought in as receipts of that subsequent period."

12. At the time his Honour was speaking, the South Australian legislation, which was the subject of his Honour's deliberations, made no express provision to take account of a trader's stock. But the relevant provision in the Commonwealth Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 was s. 16(a) which, so far as is material, provided:-

"The assessable income of any person shall include -

(a) profits derived from any trade or business and converted into tock-in-trade or added to the capital of or in any way invested in the trade or business: ..."

13. Although the phrases "trading stock" and "trading stock on hand" (as used in the Act) and "trading stock-in-hand" as used in the Finance Act 1921 are familiar phrases, they have rarely been the subject of judicial comment. That being so, it is appropriate to review all of the decisions which have been referred to in the course of argument.

J. W. Green & Co. v. The Revenue Commissioners is a decision of the High Court, affirmed on appeal to the Supreme Court, reported in 1927 I.R. 240. The Appellant was a grain merchant and importer. In association with others who carried on similar businesses, they entered into contracts in rotation for cargoes or part-cargoes of grain to be imported. Most of the grain was generally sold "forward" before its arrival in Ireland: the remainder after it had been put into store. Any taxpayer who could prove a loss on "trading stock-in-hand" within a particular period would be advantaged. In those circumstances the Appellant claimed that the unsold portions of the grain still at sea constituted "trading stock-in-hand" at the relevant date. It was found as a fact that the goods in question were "not in the actual possession of" the Appellant at the material date; and that no bills of lading, invoices or insurance policies or certificates in relation to those goods had been delivered to the Appellant on or before that date. n those circumstances O'Byrne, J., in dismissing the claim of the Appellant, said:-

"It was contended on behalf of the appellants: first, that the property in the goods in question had passed to them, and, alternatively, that they had acquired a disposing power over the same, and that, in either case, the goods formed part of the appellants' trading stock in hand. It is not necessary for us to determine whether either of these contentions is well founded, as we are of opinion that neither the passing of the property in the goods nor the acquisition of such disposing power, as was contended for by the appellants' counsel, is, in the absence of delivery, sufficient to entitle the appellants to treat the goods as part of their trading stock in hand."

His decision was affirmed on appeal. FitzGibbon, J., with whom Kennedy, C.J. and Murnaghan, J. concurred, said:-

"Upon the whole case, we are of opinion: (a) that the expression 'trading stock in hand,' in Part II, Clause 1, of Schedule 2 of the Finance Act, 1921, means trading stock which is in the actual possession or under the control of the trader; (b) that none of the goods comprised in the cargoes with which this case is conversant was in the possession or under the control of the appellants upon August 21st, 1921; (c) that, if the test whether stock be 'in hand' or not be property and not possession or control, the property in the goods in question was not vested unconditionally in the appellants upon the 31st August, 1921; and that the order appealed from should be affirmed, and this appeal dismissed."

14. The same provisions came before the House of Lords for consideration in Benjamin Smith & Son v. C.I.R. C(1928) 139 L.T. 973. The principal speech was delivered by Lord Sumner who described (at p.99) the background to the claim:-

"The business of the appellants at the time in question was that of grain importers, grain merchants, and millers. The commissioners have found the facts relating to their course of business in some detail, but I do not think that anything turns on the various forms, under which in practice their business was carried on. Its general features were of a very familiar type, and the two points now material were these. Contracts were entered into for the purchase of foreign grain to be shipped abroad, and, having thus covered themselves, the appellants proceeded to make contracts for the sale of these shipments, sometimes in parcels of various sizes, sometimes in entire cargoes. When the grain had been shipped and generally while it was still afloat, shipping documents were, as usual tendered to the appellants to be taken up for cash. Till then, following regular mercantile practice, the jus disponendi was reserved to the shippers by the form of the bills of lading. The grain did not become the property of the appellants nor was it at their disposal or in their hands till the documents had been duly taken up. On the 31st Aug. 1921 they had naturally a considerable quantity of grain either contracted for but not yet shipped or already shipped but still in the reserved disposition of other parties."

His Lordship went on to say:-

"The Legislature selected a date, the 31st Aug. 1921, at which a line should be drawn for the purpose of this particular relief. Not only was that line necessary in order to limit the relief which was being granted, but it is evident that, as between their shippers and the appellants, in cases where both parties were subject to excess profits duty, it would depend on this line whether the one party or the other obtained the enhanced relief by bringing these shipments into account as 'trading stock in hand.' It can hardly be doubted that shippers, whose trading stock included grain, which they had sold and had to deliver, could claim to treat it as part of their stock in hand, when the property was still vested in them and the grain was still at their disposition being the actual taking up of the shipping documents. If so, the same grain could not at the same time be also the trading stock in hand of the appellants, to whom it did not yet belong, and to whom the right of present disposition had not yet passed, whatever the future intentions of the parties might be in the matter, unless either double relief was to be obtained by two taxpayers on the same grain or two different and mutually exclusive tests of 'trading stock in hand were to be within the meaning of the Act.

I think that at the initial date the grain in question, whatever else it was, clearly was not 'in hand'. Unless the contract, under which the appellants had bought, was expressly made part of the description of the goods sold by them, they could satisfy their selling contract with any other goods, that they could procure, which answered to that description, and, conversely, the original sellers could dispose of the grain in favour of third parties by endorsing and delivering bills of lading, taken to their own order, even though they might become liable to the appellants for failure to perform their contract with them. The matter does not depend on contractual arrangements to get possession, for breach of which the remedy lies in damages, but on having the grain 'in hand.' To say that they had the grain 'in hand,' merely because, by business arrangements which were in course of performance, they had put themselves in a position to deal with buyers with full security of being able to perform in their turn, is merely a figure of speech like 'having ten minutes in hand to catch a train' or 'finishing a race with several lengths in hand at the winning post.' "

Once again, the claim to a fiscal advantage based upon a claim to property, or to be entitled to property, in the subject goods failed.

15. Similar issues arose in Australia for the first time in Farnsworth v. F.C.T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 504. On that occasion, it was contended by the Commissioner that the goods in question constituted "trading stock on hand" of the appellant. The Appellant in that case was a berry fruit producer. She forwarded her crop to a packing house for grading, processing, packaging and sale. It was the unanimous view of the High Court of Australia that, having delivered her fruits to the packing house, they no longer constituted "stock on hand". As at 30 . June 1943, the taxpayer had been advised that it was "estimated" that over time she would receive $1,296 over and above the $680 she had received to that date. The Commissioner sought to assess the value of the estimated excess. Their Honours were unanimous that, with the delivery of the fruit to the packing house, it ceased to be part of her "stock on hand". Rich, J. said at p.515 that:-

"After a fruit grower has delivered his fruit to the packing company and it has been processed he has lost all control and power of disposition of it and his fruit has become so inextricably mixed with the fruit of other growers as to be incapable of identification. The property in the fruit passed to the packing company and became converted into a claim for an account."

Dixon, J. said at p.518:-

"In my opinion it is not possible to treat the sum as the value of the taxpayer's stock in trade on hand at the end of the accounting period. The taxpayer's dried fruit had ceased to be on hand' within the meaning of s. 28 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1943 when she delivered it to the packing shed. The packing house cannot be treated as a mere agent of the taxpayer holding her stock in trade for sale. The taxpayer's dried fruit had been delivered irrevocably to the packing house upon terms, complicated no doubt, which entitled her to a money sum and left her with no dispositive power over the fruit and no power to direct, or control the disposal by the packing house of the fruit either alone or in combination with other suppliers. Of course the packing house was contractually bound to process, pack and sell the fruit in the appointed manner. The obligations it incurred have, I imagine, a fiduciary character. For the packing house is bound to account for the proceeds of the fruit pooled and to make a proper distribution. But, if it matters, I should have thought the property in each parcel of fruit delivered into the pool passed to the packing house. Whatever contractual and equitable rights each grower or all growers possessed to enforce the obligations of the packing house they could not be considered 'stock in trade on hand' of each individual taxpayer." (The emphasis is mine).

16. The most recent decision of the High Court on the matter came to that Court on appeal from the Supreme Court of Victoria by way of appeal from a Full Bench of the Federal Court of Australia. The facts are set out in detail in the decision of Tadgell, J. in Suttons Motors (Chullora) Wholesale Pty. Limited v. F.C.T. (32 A.T.C. 4415). I do not propose to refer to them in detail. It is sufficient to say that what was at issue was whether a wholesaler of motor vehicles which had as its only commercial activity the sale of the vehicles to its parent company for sale by retail to the public could claim the fiscal advantage of a trading stock valuation adjustment pursuant to s.82D of the Act, although it had no claim to ownership of the vehicles; no legal obligation to buy them; had incurred no financial commitment for them; and merely held them as bailee. Among other things, his Honour said (at p.4430):-

"The facts that the appellant had not on 1 July 1976 made any financial outlay in respect of any of the vehicles in its hands on that date, that it had not then incurred a legal obligation to make one, and that it had not bought or agreed to buy or become committed to any vehicle, are not in my opinion decisive against the conclusion that the vehicles were trading stock. In the circumstances these considerations appear to me to be relatively insignificant."

17. His Honour went on to express the view that ss.28 to 37 could not be finally determinative of the construction of the phrase "stock on hand" in s.S2D. But, having said that, he went on to say (at p.4432):-

"Subject to a qualification I shall mention the vehicles in question were held by the appellant a dealer in motor vehicles, as assets of its business, they were trading stock in ordinary commercial parlance and they were available for immediate sale by the appellant in the ordinary course of its business. They were in my opinion (subject to the qualification) trading stock on hand in relation to the appellant's business in terms of s.82D(1)". (The emphasis is mine).

The qualification to which his Honour referred was to the claim which had been made to treat vehicles " in transit from the manufacturer" as being "stock on hand". Once recognized, the claim in relation to that stock was abandoned.

18. Bowen, C.J., in delivering his reasons for decision upon the appeal (83 A.T.C. 4304) summarized the argument for the Commissioner as follows:-

"Counsel for the Commissioner argued that Suttons did not have any trading stock on hand at the relevant time for the purposes of s.82D(1). His argument was based on a proposition that s.82D(l) refers to trading stock of the taxpayer and that merchandise cannot be classed as trading stock of the taxpayer where he does not own it, has not legal obligation to become the owner of it and has incurred no financial commitments in respect of it."

Later, in his reasons for decision, his Honour said (at p.4307);-

"Where goods have not been delivered they generally cannot be treated as trading stock on hand despite the fact that property in the goods may have passed to the taxpayer."

"The fact that Suttons does not immediately become the owner of the vehicles when they are delivered is not necessarily determinative of the question whether the vehicles may be classed as trading stock. The question is whether Suttons has 'acquired' the goods for the relevant purpose? There seems to be no obstacle as far as the purpose of the acquisition is concerned – Suttons obtains the vehicles with a view to their ultimate sale to a customer. Counsel for the Commissioner argued however that it could not be said that Suttons had 'acquired' the vehicles, interpreting 'acquired' to mean 'acquired the legal title or right thereto'. 'Acquired' is a word with a wide range of possible applications. I can find no justification for limiting it to situations where what has been acquired is the legal title or right thereto."

"The situation here was that the taxpayer had the possession and control of the vehicles, had the right to acquire title to them and had assumed the risk with respect to them. Commercially and practically the taxpayer was committed to the ultimate sale of the vehicles to consumers and thus to the ultimate purchase of the vehicles from G.M.A.C. It seems to me somewhat artificial and strained to suggest that Suttons had not acquired the vehicles for the purposes of sec.82D(1)."

His Honour went on to reject the argument that it was incorrect to treat goods held under a floor plan or similar arrangements as trading stock, saying that:-

"The Court is entitled to look at accounting practice as an aid to discovering the appropriate commercial treatment of the goods in question. This is so, as Dixon, J. explained, because income, profits and gains are conceptions of the world of affairs and particularly of business."

19. A Full Bench of the High Court of Australia dismissed the Commissioner's appeal [(1984-1985) [1985] HCA 44; 157 C.L.R. 277 Brennan, J. dissenting]. In a joint judgment, the majority, Gibbs, C.J., Wilson, Deane, and Dawson, JJ., said that it was

"not necessary for present purposes... to explore the outer limits of the area covered by (the) ordinary meaning of the term 'trading stock' but its traditional and narrower denotation still lies at the centre of that meaning and is adequate for present purposes. That denotation is of goods held by a trader in such goods for sale or exchange in the ordinary course of his trade. When used in relation to 'a business', as the term is used in the Act generally (see s.28) and in s.82D(l) in particular, that central meaning comprehends the goods held on hand in the business for the purpose of sale or exchange in the ordinary course of trade. The fact that particular goods may not have been paid for or may not be owned by the trader does not preclude there being trading stock on hand in relation to his business if, notwithstanding lack of payment or ownership, they are legitimately held in the possession of the trader as or as part of the body of stock to be sold or exchanged in the ordinary course of the trade of that business." (The emphasis is mine.)

The majority explicitly rejected the Commissioner's primary argument that vehicles could not be stock-in-trade because the taxpayer had not made any payment in relation was not either the owner of them or under any legal enforceable obligation to buy them. Their Honours said:-

"The relevant vehicles were, at the commencement of the tax year, plainly in the possession and at the risk of the Suttons group. They were held by the group for the purpose, and only for the purpose, of being offered for sale in the ordinary course of the compass of the business within which that group, looked at as a whole, carried on. They represented the stock which the group held to offer for sale and to sell in the course of that overall business and which it had become entitled to, and commercially though not legally obliged to, purchase from GMH at GMH's wholesale price at the time it took delivery."

  1. Counsel for the Commissioner strove to overcome the effect of the words of the High Court in their application to the circumstances of the present Applicant but, in my view, he has failed to do so.
  2. Having regard to all of the decisions which I have reviewed, in my view it is clear that what is to be considered as "stock on hand" is not to be measured by considerations of property or contractual entitlement to the property. Rather, the issue is to be determined by enquiring whether the trader is in a position in the ordinary course of his business to trade by selling or exchanging the goods. It might well have been that the Applicant was in a position to effect transfer of title to a particular container load, or a particular shipment, or to all of the container loads being carried on all of the ships, and to do so on 30 June 1985 but no such transaction would have been in the ordinary course of its business. The ordinary course of its business was to sell and deliver de-containerized product to customers in the wholesale and retail trade within Australia, and to do so by effecting or directing the delivery of the goods under its immediate control.
  3. It may be that, by reason of the "passing of property" the frozen products in question became "trading stock" of the Applicant at 30 June 1985. But, even if that were so, it was not trading stock "on hand". On 30 June 1985, the products in question could not have been sold in the ordinary course of business. In the words of Dixon, J., the product on the high seas did not constitute a gain which had "come home to the taxpayer in a realized or an immediately realizable form" (Carden: ante). Nor is that finding to be vitiated by the circumstance that the taxpayer may have "incurred" a loss or outgoing in relation to the purchase price payable for those goods in one year rather than another with advantage to the taxpayer. In my view, the objections of the Applicant should be upheld.
  4. Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal will be that the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection under review shall be varied and that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1985 should be reduced by $1,389,517.
