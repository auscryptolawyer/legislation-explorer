Last Updated: 26 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - Taxation Appeals Division Applicant not appearing in person or by representative at the hearing - whether Tribunal should dismiss application considerations applicable.

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 - ss.34, 42A(2);

Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 - s.221;

Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982.

Case V56 88 A.T.C. 415;

Morgan v. Secretary to the Department of Social Security (Decision No. 2732/27 June 1986);

Nolan v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (Decision No. 3557, 29 April 1987).

REASONS FOR DECISION

17 June 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. In this matter the Tribunal convened at the time it had appointed for the hearing of an application for review made by the Applicant. The application was in relation to a decision made by the Commissioner of Taxation upon the objection of the Applicant to an assessment of income tax for the year of income ended 30 June 1984. When the Tribunal convened the Commissioner was represented by one of his officers but there was no appearance by or on behalf of the Applicant. In those circumstances, in accordance with common practice on the part of the Commissioner's representatives, application was made for the Tribunal to exercise its powers under s.42A (2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the Tribunal Act"). That sub-section, in its material terms, provides as follows:-

"If a party to a proceeding before the Tribunal in respect of an application for the review of a decision (not being the person who made the decision) fails either to appear in person or to appear by a representative ... at the hearing of the proceeding, the Tribunal may

(a) where the only other party to the proceeding is the person who made the decision - dismiss the application without proceeding to review the decision; or

(b) in any other case - direct that the person who failed to appear shall cease to be a party to the proceeding.

In the circumstances of the case, the latter sub-paragraph is of no relevance.

  1. Such difficulties as the present more commonly come under notice at an earlier stage when a Preliminary Conference is scheduled to take place. But they do not always do so. Quite often the reason for non-representation of the Applicant at either a Preliminary Conference or a Hearing is simply unknown; and an application for dismissal such as the present may be made. Whether or not such an application should be granted is a matter which calls for an exercise of discretion on the part of the Tribunal. That exercise of discretion must be made in light of what is known to the Tribunal from its own resources and from such further evidence as is placed before it on behalf of the person in whose interest the application is made.
  2. Before considering the evidence available in relation to the particular application I think it appropriate to consider what different types of factor might have caused the Applicant to neither attend nor be represented at the hearing. Those circumstances might be broadly classified as follows.

IGNORANCE:

  1. Never having been advised of the time appointed by the Tribunal for the Preliminary Conference or Hearing.

In the case of a taxpayer representing himself that might be -

* because, despite the existence of a copy notice addressed to the Applicant on the Tribunal file, it was never posted to that address. (It is known that there have been instances in which notices to one applicant have been forwarded to another applicant; and other instances in which notices which should have been directed to the Commissioner, Sydney were directed to the Commissioner at Newcastle); or

* because the postal address of the Tribunal was incorrect, either because the Tribunal had incorrectly recorded the information supplied by the Commissioner as to the taxpayer's address for service or because the Commissioner had incorrectly communicated that address; or

* because of a failure on the part of Australia Post to deliver to the nominated address; or

* because the taxpayer had left his address without the mail being returned to the Tribunal either by Australia Post of its initiative or at the instance of the person to whom the post was delivered; or

* because the Applicant, being absent from the address provided up to the appointed date, had arranged for his mail to be held by Australia Post or a neighbour pending his return.

5. Other difficulties arise where the Applicant has been using as his address for service, the address of the persons representing him at the time the request for reference was made. It is known to the Tribunal:-

* that newly provided addresses for service are not always recorded or correctly recorded and thereafter used in giving notice to Applicants;

* that such have been the delays in the tax litigation system that it is a commonplace experience that the professional firm which lodged the request for reference has been dissolved or reconstituted by amalgamation or has changed address or has ceased to act for the Applicant or has been removed from the register of tax agents - sometimes instead of an address for service being supplied, the Commissioner has only advised that the agent who lodged the request for reference has been de-listed;

* that some firms do not pay the Tribunal the courtesy of advising that they are no longer acting. It may be that they make their best endeavours to pass on the substance of the communication, or the communication itself, to the Applicant by sending it to the last-known address available to them, being either the residential or business address of the Applicant or the address of his new representatives. Unfortunately those endeavours may fail to bring the matter to the attention of the Applicant; and

* that unfortunately, it sometimes happens that, because the firm is no longer acting, it ignores the communication.

INADVERTENCE:

6. Even if the Applicant was made aware of the hearing date, it is always possible that his failure to attend at the appointed time is due to inadvertence. He may confuse the date, the time or the place. He may forget. There are even occasions, admittedly not common, in which the Commissioner's representative has failed to appear at the appointed time and place due to inadvertence. In one instance to my knowledge it was in fact a Member of the Tribunal who was the person in default in that, when both parties assembled at the appointed place, he was not to be found. He was awaiting the appearance of the parties at another venue and, while waiting, was endeavouring unsuccessfully to ascertain why the parties had not been represented before him. In the event no damage was done because when the plight of the parties came to be known to Registry staff, another Member attended to the Preliminary

Conferences - all before it came to be known that the Member appointed to conduct them was inadvertently in the wrong conference room.

IMPOSSIBILITY:

7. Factors beyond the control of the Applicant may prevent his attendance at the appointed time and place. I have in mind situations in which the Applicant sets out on his journey, so timing his programme as to reasonably expect to present himself at the appointed place and time, only to suffer some experience in the course of that journey which prevents him from attending. Some hardy characters have managed to arrive despite being severely bitten by a dog en route to the Tribunal; some are involved in or delayed by reason of motor accidents, but in circumstances which have left them able to transmit a message advising of the likely delay. (In no more than three days that was the experience of a taxation officer who came to be unable to attend before the Tribunal at the appointed time. On one day his commuter train was delayed by reason of a passenger suffering an epileptic fit; and two days later his train was delayed by a fatal accident on that line).

8. However, sometimes when such delays occur it is not even possible for the Applicant, with the best will in the world, to give notice of likely delay. Telephones are not readily available when congestion stops the movement of traffic on the Sydney Harbour Bridge or a train is held up in the Wynyard tunnel. Nor do difficulties end there. In recent days a power failure in the building occupied by the Tribunal in Sydney caused one Applicant's representative to be late for an appointment because he was unable to move from one floor to the other and, there being no telephone available to him where he was, he could not communicate with the Registry.

INDIFFERENCE;

9. Some parties decide at some stage in the process that they will not proceed to a hearing. Many do not do the Tribunal the courtesy of advising it of such a decision. No doubt in some cases no discourtesy is intended. It is rather that such persons are not aware of the cost to the community and, more particularly, to the tax-paying community of which they are members, which is involved when one or more Members of this Tribunal assemble, attended by an associate (should they have one) and an attendant, with a recording monitor provided by the Commonwealth Court Reporting Service present to record the evidence. Similarly, they may not think of the cost to the community borne through the office of the Commissioner of Taxation in having his representatives, commonly two in number and sometimes more, in attendance to no purpose.

10. On the other hand, unfortunately, sometimes the failure to give notice of intention to withdraw is contemptuous in that the Applicant adopts an attitude that he shows no concern to help confine the costs of Government, as to the excessiveness of which he is quite likely to be a vocal critic.

PROCEDURAL STANDARDS

11. It is also significant to bear in mind that, although it is often contended that the simplicity and informality of Tribunal proceedings provides a more appropriate procedural system than that provided for by the Courts, in many ways it falls short of the high standards prescribed by the rules of procedure of the Courts. It is also true that in the Taxation Division, unlike most civil litigation, instead of the control of, and responsibility for control of, the litigation being primarily cast upon the person seeking relief - the taxpayer; that control in the Taxation Division largely rests with the Commissioner. In all ordinary cases it is the Commissioner who determines at what point this Tribunal will first be vested with an opportunity to discharge its responsibilities. The simple fact of that matter is that, by transferring in recent times the vast numbers of references which had been suffered to accumulate with the Commissioner over more than a decade, as all too often happens, the Tribunal has not been capable of promptly discharging its responsibilites. As a result, there have commonly been very substantial delays which tend to render information as to address for service of the Applicant inappropriate. So it is that with the Tribunal, in contrast to the Courts -

(a) the Applicant is not the person to initiate the involvement of the Tribunal;

(b) he is not obliged to provide an address for service within close proximity to the Registry;

(c) he is not confined to nominating as his representative, should he choose to have one, a solicitor subject to the authority of the Court;

(d) there is no provision requiring that a change of representative be notified to the Registry;

(e) there is no provision requiring that a change in the address for service is to be notified to the Registry; and

(f) there is no system whereby service must be strictly proved as a prerequisite to the grant of any application to dismiss proceedings for want of prosecution.

12. Far more significantly, there is the difficulty that, although the superior Courts have the power to set aside such orders of dismissal as they may have made when persuaded they ought do so, the accepted view is that, once an order of dismissal has been made by this Tribunal, it can do nothing to reinstate the application (cf. Case V56 88 A.T.C. 415). For present purposes I accept that decision as being sound.

13. There are yet further difficulties. It follows from the decision in Case V56 that, if an order of dismissal has been made then, no matter how reasonable the case for reinstating the matter, that could only be achieved upon proceedings before the Federal Court of Australia. Success in such proceedings would either depend upon establishing a lack of jurisdiction in the Tribunal to make the order that was made or, far more difficult, establishing that the exercise of discretion was so unreasonably exercised as to have been improperly exercised. Any such course would be quite beyond the capacity of many taxpayers, and involve expense quite disproportionate to the issue. Many taxpayers have problems which are very substantial to them but not so substantial as to have justified trusting the conduct of the litigation to professional litigators in tax, whether they be members of the legal profession or members of the accountancy profession well experienced in tax litigation.

14. An alternative procedure would be for the Applicant to institute an application to request a fresh reference of the disallowed objection for determination by the Tribunal, and to do so out of time. However, even if it be assumed that such a power could be properly exercised after a request for reference had been complied with, it raises yet further problems. First, if the Applicant had had to pay the fee of $240 for each initial request for reference, the order of dismissal would have the effect of those initial fees being forfeit to the Crown. Then the Applicant would be called upon to pay a further $240 in relation to each assessment in relation to which he sought an extension of time. Even for many taxpayers who were concerned enough and affluent enough to afford the initial fees, the prospect of putting a further set of fees at risk may well prove to be too much. If the imposition of fees on requesting reviews will deny many people the opportunity to challenge determinations of the Commissioner as unquestionably it will, then assuredly the imposition of further fees on the same scale with no other immediate end in view than to secure permission to re-establish the situation which previously existed will surely also act as a deterrent.

  1. However, even then the problems are not over. In a case such as the present where the last day for requesting a reference for independent review fell well before 1 July 1986, s.221 of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 provides that no such extension may be granted under any circumstances.
  2. Those circumstances distinguish the Tax Division from most other Divisions of the Tribunal - Divisions to which most come without liability to pay fees and with no procedural limtis preventing fresh applications being made (cf. Morgan v. Secretary to the Department of Social Security - Decision No. 2732, 27 June 1986; Nolan v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs Decision No. 3557, 29 April 1987).
  3. Having made those observations it is appropriate to say-that, although I believe large numbers of matters have been dismissed by this Tribunal and, in earlier years, by the Taxation Boards of Review, until recently I was not aware of many instances in which it might be thought that the dismissal had worked injustice. I also acknowledge that the Commissioner and this Tribunal both have a legitimate interest in bringing such applications to a conclusion with all due economy of cost and effort consistent with fairness and justice. So long as a matter is merely "de-listed" by removal from the active list of matters before the Tribunal, it will always be the responsibility of the Tribunal to determine the application should any person ask it to do so. In those circumstances, the Commissioner cannot automatically rely on the "de-listing" order as providing a justification or excuse for the closure of his file and destruction of relevant records. When an application is "de-listed" the future action (if any) of the Commissioner should be determined by the nature of the issue:
* if an application relating to an assessment of a target company under the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 is "de-listed", it will be essential for the Commissioner to proceed until the application is dismissed. If that is not done, it will not be possible to issue any Vendor or Promoter Recoupment assessment;

* if the Commissioner proposes to advance a positive case in support of an assessment upon a hearing, he may wish to seek "re-listing" and a hearing lest his witnesses and evidence might come to be unavailable at a later date;
  • * if some terms of settlement have been agreed, but not given effect to by an order of the Tribunal pursuant to s.34 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, he may have to rely upon such arrangements (if any) as may have been entered into and the issue and acceptance of the agreed amended reduced assessment;
  • * if, as is most commonly the case, the Commissioner only contends that the taxpayer cannot make out a case for the relief he seeks, he may be content that the matter remain "de-listed": in those circumstances, what cannot be proved at the time will not become possible of proof in later years.
  1. The formality with which the Tribunal records "de-listing" has no equivalent in most procedural systems of the Courts. That would seem to be because the Courts on the whole only act when called on to do so. If neither party in civil litigation request action, the Court does not initiate any. Nothing is done and, so far as I am aware, no significance is attached to any statistics of such inactivity. There seems to be no reason why the same approach should not be adopted in relation to applications to the Tribunal.
  2. But whatever may be in the best interests of institutions such as the office of the Commissioner and this Tribunal may be of little interest to the Applicant. It will be no consolation for him to know that in 99% of cases in which such applications on the part of the Commissioner are granted that no harm is done. If it was true that the principal objective of this Tribunal is merely to finally dispose of matters, the risk of injustice to some individuals may he of little moment. On the other hand, if the principal concern of this institution is to bring in a just determination on matters in issue, and to act fairly in doing so, then the matter of dismissal needs close attention in each case.

THE CASE IN HAND

  1. Against that background I proceed to consider the exercise of discretion in the particular case. The circumstances which appear on the evidence before the Tribunal established the following.
  2. The Applicant, born in 1949, prepared his income tax return to 30 June 1984 on 29 June 1984 and on 16 July 1984 lodged it with the Commissioner. In doing so he nominated as his postal address for service of notices a post office box at a suburban Sydney Post Office. In his return of income he described himself as a "professional air pilot and child-care worker". Nothing in his return of income suggested that during that year of income he had derived any income from flying activities. However, he claimed income tax deductions described as follows:-

"Pilot - Medical $43

Acft. - Hire & Test $366"

In support of the Commissioner's application for dismissal, his representative produced copy returns said to be those of the Applicant for other years. I am sure that they were. The return for the year of income ended 30 June 1983 disclosed that, in the period March 1982 to March 1983, the Applicant had conducted a flying school but that, having suffered a loss of $7,220, the flying school closed down in March 1983; and that from that date until mid-May 1983 he had been unemployed, although drawing unemployment benefits for a period of some eight weeks following the closure of the school. Among the deductions claimed in that year was an item "Pilot - medical, tests, aircraft hire, revision $343". In addition, the Commissioner's representative tendered copies of the Applicant's income tax returns for the years of income ended 30 June 1985 and 1986. Neither of those documents suggested that any flying was being undertaken as an income-producing activity although, in one year, the form identified the Applicant's occupation as "child-care worker/professional pilot" and, in the other, as a "pilot instructor".

22. Those circumstances prompted the Commissioner's representative to submit that it was quite improbable that the Applicant would be successful with his application should he proceed to a hearing. That may be so, but the question to be determined is not whether the Applicant would be likely to succeed upon a hearing but whether he should be heard, or at least to have been provided with an opportunity to be heard and then deliberately or irresponsibly failed to avail himself of that opportunity. However, although that distinction is quite fundamental to the determination of the application to dismiss, that is not to say that considerations as to whether the Applicant has a reasonably arguable case are not relevant.

23. In ray view it is first necessary to assess what is known as to the failure of the Applicant to attend. In the particular circumstances of this case much more is known than would usually be known. That happens because, in the week leading up to the appointed hearing date, the Tribunal endeavoured to make contact with the Applicant with a view to re-scheduling the hearing date. In doing so it called in aid the assistance of the Commissioner. As a result, what is known is:-

* the Applicant requested reference to a Taxation Board of Review by delivering a request in writing to the Commissioner dated 4 November 1985;

* on a date unknown the Commissioner referred the request to this Tribunal as successor to the Taxation Boards of Review;

* on 18 December 1987 the Tribunal wrote to the Applicant, addressing its letter to the Applicant care of the post office box stated by the Commissioner as the address of the Applicant;

* the Applicant replied to that enquiry indicating that he was ready to proceed to a hearing on reasonable notice;

* by notice from the Tribunal, similarly addressed, dated 4 February 1988, the Applicant was called on to be available by telephone for a Preliminary Conference and to notify a telephone number at which he would be available;

* he gave notice of telephone number and participated in the Preliminary Conference. At that point he wanted to proceed to a hearing;

* on 29 March 1988 notices were prepared by this Tribunal for despatch to the Applicant, addressed as before, and also to the Commissioner, appointing a hearing date;
  • * enquiries instituted in the week preceding the hearing indicated that the Applicant was no longer available by telephone at the number previously used, because he had sold his home and moved to another address which was unknown;
  • * enquiries through the Post Office indicated that, very shortly before enquiry was made, he had cancelled his post office box;
  • * at that point no enquiry had been made of his last-known employer although it is to be noted that indications from the return are that he was unemployed at 30 June 1986. Therefore, it is unlikely that his new whereabouts will be established easily before he presents his income tax return to 30 June 1987 - a return seemingly already substantially overdue.

24. In face of those circumstances I am conscious of the possibility that the Applicant may well have deliberately absented himself from the hearing. I am also conscious of the possibility that, had he appeared, he may not have been able to present any arguable case in support of the deduction claimed. However, I am also conscious that, in the circumstances, his failure to attend may have been occasioned by ignorance (not learning of the assigned date because of some default on the part of this Registry in posting the notice; because of some deficiency on the part of Australia Post in delivering the notice? or because of a failure on the part of the Applicant to take possession of the notice delivered to his post office box).

  1. I am also conscious of possible inadvertence: that, in the confusion which usually attends a transfer of residence, he may have simply completely overlooked the matter.
  2. In addition, I observe that at the time I was being asked to make the order - an order which would have had irrevocable consequences - attendance of the Applicant may have been rendered impossible by circumstances beyond his control: circumstances such as I have previously described. With the passage of time which has since elapsed I discount that as a possibility but, at the time the order would have been made, had the Commissioner's application been immediately acceded to, it is likely that that rather than any other factor would have been the explanation for non-attendance.
  3. In my view, particularly bearing it in mind that the principal responsibility of this Tribunal is to provide both parties with an opportunity to be heard, and thereupon to determine matters in issue, I think that justice requires that the application made should not be acceded to.
  4. In consequence, I propose to direct that the application be removed from the list of matters awaiting hearing. By following that course, the way will be open to either party to request reinstatement.
  5. As to the future, if the true explanation for non-attendance is that the Applicant deliberately chose not to proceed to a hearing, it is extremely unlikely that he will ever seek reinstatement in relation to such a relatively small matter. On the other hand, if the Commissioner is sufficiently concerned to be rid of the risk that at some future time he may be called upon to justify his assessment, the way will be open to the Commissioner to give notice to the Tribunal of a fresh address for service. Upon that being done, fresh endeavours may be had to appoint a hearing date and to ensure that the fact of that appointment is brought to the attention of the Applicant.
