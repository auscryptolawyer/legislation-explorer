Re Fay Olive Scott and Commissioner of Superannuation [1989] AATA 51 (9 March 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: FAY OLIVE SCOTT

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q85/107

AAT No. 5013

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

K.L. Beddoe(1) (Senior Member), B.C. Lock(1) (Member) and

Mrs H.M. Pavlin(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Spouse of deceased eligible employee - wholly or substantially dependent - whether spouse so dependent and eligible for spouse pension.

Superannuation Act 1976 S.s.3(1), 81.

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Scott & Others (1987) 71 ALR 408

Schlatter v. Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1985) 8 ALD 133

Johnson v. Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 825

HEARING

BRISBANE

9:3:1989

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and the Tribunal decides that the applicant is a spouse entitled to benefits within the terms of section 81 of the Superannuation Act and remits the matter to the respondent Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with that finding.

DECISION

The applicant in this matter seeks a review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") that she was not a 'Spouse' within the meaning of paragraph 3(1)(b) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act").

2. "Spouse" in sub-section 3(1) is defined as follows:

"'Spouse', in relation to a person who has died and

was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee or

a retirement pensioner, means -

(a) a person who was legally married to the deceased

person at the time of the person's death and who,

at that time, was living with the person on a

permanent and bona fide domestic basis;

(b) a person who was legally married to the deceased

person at the time of the person's death but who

was not living with the person on a permanent and

bona fide domestic basis at that time, and who,

in the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly or

substantially dependent upon the deceased person

at that time;

...".

3. The question at issue is whether in the opinion of the Tribunal, the applicant was wholly or substantially dependent upon her husband at the time of the husband's death.

4. This matter was previously decided by the Tribunal in May 1986.

5. The Tribunal then constituted by Deputy President Thompson, Mr F.A. Pascoe (Member) and Dr N C Davis (Member) in a decision dated 15 May 1986 decided that the applicant was substantially dependent on her deceased husband at the time of his death and was therefore entitled within the terms of s.81 of the Superannuation Act to be granted a spouse pension. Accordingly, the Tribunal as then constituted set aside the decision under review and remitted the matter to the respondent Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with a direction that the applicant was so entitled.

6. By Notice of Appeal dated 12 June 1986 the respondent Commissioner appealed against the decision of the Tribunal on the ground that it had misdirected itself in holding that the applicant only had to show substantial dependence by showing that there was a dependency which was more than trivial, minimal or nominal. In an amended Notice of Appeal subsequently filed at the Federal Court the respondent Commissioner sought to have reviewed the meaning of the phrase "wholly or substantially dependent" as it appears in paragraph (b) of the definition of "spouse" in sub-section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act. However, on the hearing of the appeal the respondent as appellant in those proceedings limited the submission to the contention that the Tribunal was wrong as to its interpretation of the word "substantially".

7. In Commissioner for Superannuation v. Scott (1987) 71 ALR 408 the Federal Court came to the conclusion that the Tribunal had erred in law in directing itself that the word "substantially" meant something more than trivial, minimal or nominal. The Court found that it was necessary to construe the word "substantially" alongside the word "wholly" and not by reference to what the word "substantially" does not mean. The Court took the view that the word "substantially" meant "in relation to a person wholly or substantially dependent, that that person is primarily essentially or in the main dependent on another person."

8. The Federal Court remitted the matter to the Tribunal for reconsideration in the light of the Court's reasons for allowing the Commissioner's appeal.

9. Upon coming on for further hearing before the Tribunal, as now constituted, the parties agreed that the findings of fact found by the Tribunal, as previously constituted, should be the findings of fact before us with one exception. It should be noted that this Tribunal has not had the advantage of seeing the exhibits in these proceedings as those exhibits were returned to the parties by the Federal Court.

10. In addition Mr Whithear for the respondent made the following point:

"... the Tribunal is obliged to reconsider. ... this

is a fresh Tribunal ... and this Tribunal is obliged

to satisfy itself as to all the ingredients required

under the definition of spouse in s.3 of the

Superannuation Act, 1976. ... so that to reconsider

... it is necessary for the Tribunal to look at all

the evidence and to come to its own conclusion on the

facts and the law."

11. We agree with that submission. We have taken that evidence into account (with the exception of the exhibits mentioned above), have adopted the findings of fact as found by the earlier Tribunal and have considered submissions on those facts by counsel for the parties.

12. One additional exhibit was tendered in the proceedings before us and that is a copy of an affidavit sworn in relation to the grant of probate in the estate of the deceased Daryl Leslie John Scott by a trust officer of Queensland Trustees Limited. The relevant part of that affidavit sets out the assets of the deceased as at the date of his death showing assets of $36,445 all of which may be described as liquid assets and liabilities of $3,488 including fees estimated to be payable to Queensland Trustees Limited of $2,000. The affidavit also disclosed two other benefits which had accrued on the death of the deceased but which do not form part of his estate. One of these benefits was a death benefit payable by the Public Service Death Benefit Society to the beneficiary nominated by the deceased - a Mrs Delvadene Dorothy Briars. The amount payable to Mrs Briars was said to be $7,372. There was also an amount payable by Australia Post in respect of outstanding long service leave. This amount was paid to the applicant, Mrs Scott in trust for their children, Kaylene Fay Scott and John Daryl Scott in an amount of $8,757. In the light of that affidavit the Tribunal, as now constituted, finds that the deceased was in a position to contribute to the support of himself, his wife and his children as at the date of his death.

13. The first question is as to the meaning of "dependency". The meaning of "dependency" was canvassed at length by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authorty (1985) 8 ALD 133. The Tribunal summarised its view at page 145:

"(87) In summary therefore, dependency is a financial

concept which exists if the claimant satisfies the

Authority (or on appeal a tribunal), that as at the

date of death the deceased was either paying money or

providing support to the claimant for the basic

necessities of life or lower order needs;

alternatively, was under a legal obligation to provide

such support to the claimant pursuant to a maintenance

order which probably would be effectively enforced if

not paid voluntarily; in the further alternative that

the deceased had a prima facie legal obligation to

provide such support to the claimant as at the date of

death which probably would have been effectively

enforced if not paid voluntarily. In the case of de

facto spouses the fact of a maintenance order (with

the exception of Tasmania) and the existence of a

legal entitlement to support is inapplicable at the

present time.

(88) It would also seem from the cases previously

referred to that, in considering whether the above

factors exist, the Tribunal must look at the whole

relationship between the parties as it existed at the

date of death taking into account both past events and

future probabilities which would include matters such

as any financial arrangemens, written or verbal

agreements, provisions in a will and all matters which

may affect the financial affairs between the parties

and not merely limited to the payment of money but

includes the provision of support other than by

payment of money."

14. It is not in dispute that after Mr Scott left the matrimonial home in May 1982 he paid no money and supplied no goods to support either the applicant or their children. It is also not in dispute that until her husband's death the applicant received a supporting parent's benefit under Part IVAAA of the Social Security Act 1947. That benefit was paid to the applicant at a rate of $200.30 per fortnight. It was found by the Tribunal, as previously constituted, that the applicant's day to day living expenses were in the order of $180 per week which included $40.00 per week for tennis expenses. The result was that the applicant's normal day to day expenses exceeded the amounts which she received from the Department of Social Security as supporting parent's benefit and it was only because of financial assistance from her parents that she had been also able to afford other things such as a motor car, television set, washing machine or a stove and to pay the rates and meet the tennis expenses. The applicant only succeeded in balancing her domestic budget before the deceased's death because of the amount paid to her by the Department of Social Security and that supplement paid by her parents out of their own relatively limited resources. If the Department of Social Security pension had not been paid the only recourse the applicant would have had would be against her husband.

15. We take the view that the gratuitous amounts paid to the applicant by her parents were just that and do not reflect dependency by the applicant upon her parents. Those amounts were made as gifts to the applicant and the parents were not under any obligation to provide for their daughter although it is of course understandable that the parents wished to provide for their daughter because of her situation. We do not regard that as reflecting upon dependency but rather reflecting the parents desire to assist their daughter and her children.

16. That brings us to the question as to whether the applicant was wholly or substantially dependent on her husband.

17. Mr Warnick of counsel who appeared for the applicant before the Tribunal submitted that the only issue of fact or law for the Tribunal to consider is whether, in the light of the definition which the Tribunal must accept of the meaning of the word "substantially" as interpreted by the Federal Court, Mrs Scott was substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death? As we will go on to show we think this may not adequately reflect the issues before the Tribunal because in our view the Tribunal should also consider whether the applicant was wholly dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death. Mr Warnick seemed to recognise this situation later on in his submission when he said that the issue was whether Mrs Scott was wholly or substantially dependent. It was the essence of Mr Warnick's case that Mrs Scott was entirely dependent upon the deceased, even though the deceased may not have been able to entirely meet her needs but nevertheless she was dependent on him and no one else. In the alternative Mr Warnick submitted that even if it was wrong to say that the applicant was entirely dependent upon the deceased, she was certainly substantially dependent upon him.

18. On the other hand Mr Whithear who appeared for the respondent Commissioner submitted that the Tribunal, being reconstituted for the purpose of dealing with this matter on remission from the Federal Court, was obliged to consider the whole issue and come to its own conclusion on the facts and the law, and not to rely upon the findings of the Tribunal as previously constituted. As we have already stated this Tribunal is not in a position to consider the documentary evidence afresh because the exhibits are in the hands of the parties and not in the hands of the Tribunal and neither party made any effort to tender those exhibits to this Tribunal. Furthermore the parties agreed at the commencement of the proceedings that the Tribunal as now constituted could proceed on the basis of the findings of fact of the Tribunal as previously constituted. It is our view that we are only required to consider the facts as previously found with the addition of Exhibit D.

19. Mr Whithear's submissions may be summarised as follows. He submitted that while S.72 of the Family Law Act gave the conditional right to support, Mr and Mrs Scott had agreed otherwise and hence there was no prima facie right to support vesting in Mrs Scott at the death of Mr Scott. The fact that Mrs Scott was in the position to have the maintenance agreement set aside by a further order of the Family Court was considered to be irrelevant for the purposes of Mr Whithear's submissions. He submitted that until the maintenance agreement was set aside there was no prima facie right to maintenance existing in the applicant. We reject those submissions. The evidence of Mr Andrews before the earlier Tribunal to the effect that the agreement was meaningless as regards future maintenance has not been disputed. We hold the view that the agreement did not therefore take away the prima facie right to maintenance.

20. In the event that that proposition did not find favour with the Tribunal Mr Whithear submitted that despite the existence of the maintenance agreement there was no probability of enforcement in respect of periodical maintenance and therefore Mrs Scott was not dependent upon the deceased. Mr Whithear placed some weight upon the fact that the evidence showed that Mrs Scott was more concerned to get a lump sum settlement from the deceased under s.79 of The Family Law Act rather than orders for periodical maintenance. We do not accept the substance of this submission as it seems to us that the fact of seeking a lump sum settlement, only asserts the applicant's claim that she was entitled to regard herself as a dependent of the deceased.

21. Mr Whithear also submitted that it was unlikely that the applicant would seek to enforce the periodical maintenance agreement or even to obtain such an order from the Family Court if she was financially better off receiving supporting parent's benefits or, we assume, in the event of the divorce, some other benefit payable under the Social Security legislation. No doubt the applicant would have some difficulty in obtaining periodical payments in the form of maintenance from the deceased which equalled the weekly supporting parent's benefit and it may be correct to say that she would therefore prefer to receive the social security benefit rather than pursue her husband through the Family Court by seeking to obtain a maintenance order. To our mind such considerations are irrelevant to determine the applicant's dependency upon the deceased. The Tribunal agrees with Mr Whithear when he submitted that it would be unsafe for the Tribunal to try to calculate any periodical maintenance which could have been paid by the deceased. It would make no difference as to whether such maintenance was paid voluntarily or under an order of the Family Court. We are satisfied, on the evidence of Mr Andrews, that the applicant intended to enforce her right to maintenance at the appropriate time.

22. The question as to what the Family Court may do when the separated wife sought to enforce her legal right to maintenance was argued at some length before us. In summary Mr Whithear submitted that there should not only be a legal right but there should be some hope of having that legal right enforced and that having regard to the attitude of the Family Court there is a possibility that the legal right may not have been enforced by the Court because Mrs Scott, the applicant, was in receipt of Supporting Parent's Benefit (pp 18 to 25 and p 32 transcript).

23. Mr Whithear also submitted that the Tribunal must look at legally enforceable rights. He made these submissions to us in answer to the comment in the Federal Court decision that the Tribunal should not need to consider what might happen in the Family Court. The question of legal obligation to support and the attitude of the Family Court and its decisions was also argued in Re Schlatter (supra). The Tribunal had this to say at page 140:

"The question is whether this Tribunal, in considering

whether or not a claimant is a dependant and whether

the deceased had a legal obligation to support the

claimant, is required to put itself 'in the shoes of'

the Family Court in order to decide whether or not the

deceased was liable to support the claimant pursuant

to sections 72 and 75 of the Family Law Act (supra).

The Tribunal thinks not. It is not necessary for a

Family Court to make an order for maintenance before

the legal liability of one spouse to maintain the

other exists; the legal liability exists if the

conditions set out in s.72 in conjunction with s.75

are fulfilled. It is not for this Tribunal, nor does

this Tribunal have jurisdiction, to make any legal

declaration that such legal obligation to maintain

exists nor is it able to make any order for

maintenance which is solely within the province of the

Family Court. This Tribunal is required merely as

part of its consideration of dependency, to look at

whether or not a deceased, in the absence of there

being any payment of money or order for maintenance,

had a prima facie legal obligation to support the

claimant as at the date of death having regard to the

criteria set forth in ss.72 and 75 of the Family Law

Act (supra). It is therefore only a prima facie legal

right which is looked to and which must then be

coupled with the probability of it being effectively

enforced."

24. It is not for us as the Tribunal in this matter to put ourselves "in the shoes of" the Family Court. It is sufficient in our view that a prima facie legal obligation existed. To answer the question of the probability of it being enforced successfully, would be speculation on our part. Such an answer must come from the Family Court. It follows, therefore, that we are of the view that speculation as to the outcome of maintenance proceedings in the Family Court should not be taken into account when deciding the question of dependency under the Superannuation Act and we refer to the words albeit obiter of the Federal Court at 71 ALR 415:

"It is our opinion that concepts relevant to the

Family Law court together with the practices and

decisions of that Court also do not necessarily

determine conclusively the question of dependency

under the Superannuation Act."

She therefore meets the definition of dependency as set out in Re Schlatter.

25. Mr Whithear also submitted that the Tribunal should not ignore the fact that the applicant was in receipt of the supporting parent benefit from the Department of Social Security. It is clear to the Tribunal that that benefit was being paid to the applicant because she had no other support for herself and her children. It is also clear to the Tribunal that if the deceased had been making adequate arrangements for the day to day wellbeing of his family the supporting parent benefit would not have been paid under the Social Security legislation.

26. Although we were invited by Mr Whithear to take the Social Security Benefit into account, we are convinced that we should not. These issues have previously been dealt with in Re Johnson v. Commissioner for Superannuation, (1979) 2 ALD 825 and Re Schlatter where it was held in each case that payment of Social Security benefits does not preclude a finding of dependency.

27. The deceased contributed to a superannuation scheme one of the by-products of which is to reduce the payments the contributor and his dependents receive from the welfare payroll. It would be ironic indeed if one's presence on the welfare payroll is used to deny dependence and therefore keep them on the welfare payroll. Such a philosophy would be self-defeating. We therefore follow what was said in Johnson and Schlatter and conclude that Social Security payments of a welfare nature should not be taken to exclude dependency.

28. There is no basis for finding that the applicant was wholly dependent upon the deceased. This is because under the property arrangement entered into between the deceased and the applicant, the applicant was entitled absolutely to the family home and she thereafter had full use of the family home. She was independent insofar as providing accommodation for herself and her children was concerned. She was not, however, independent in respect of the other daily costs of living and for this she depended in fact upon her husband. That these needs were met by the generosity of her parents and the benefit received from the Department of Social Security is, in our view, irrelevant.

29. Taking these factors into account we therefore find that Mrs Scott was dependent upon her husband in respect of all the basic necessities of life except accommodation. She was dependent, however, upon him for the payment of rates and for maintenance on the family home although no doubt there was very little prospect that she would ever recover those amounts from Mr Scott. That does not mean that she was not dependent upon him, only that he was not discharging his obligations.

30. We take the view that the applicant was substantially dependent upon the deceased up until the time of the deceased's death. The reason for saying this is to be found in the reasons for decision in the Federal Court in these proceedings and we refer in particular to the report of the decision at 71 ALR 408 at page 413 where their Honours said:

"In our opinion, the Tribunal erred in law in

directing itself that the word 'substantially' meant

something more than trivial, minimal or nominal. It

is necessary to construe it alongside the word

'wholly' and not by reference to what it does not

mean. It has, in our opinion, the meaning, in

relation to a person in the expression 'wholly or

substantially dependent', that that person is

primarily, essentially or in the main dependent upon

another person."

31. We find that the applicant, Mrs Scott, was primarily and in the main dependent upon the deceased. This in our view is sufficient to bring her within the definition of "spouse". It cannot be said that she was essentially dependent because it has been demonstrated that her welfare was maintained by others in the absence of the maintenance by the deceased.

32. We find therefore that at the date of Mr Scott's death the applicant was substantially dependent upon him and is, therefore, a person entitled under s.81 of the Superannuation Act to be granted a spouse's pension.

33. The decision under review is set aside and the matter will be remitted to the respondent Commissioner for reconsideration with a direction that the applicant is so entitled within the terms of s.81.
