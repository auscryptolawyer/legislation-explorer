ST86/342 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 112 (26 March 1987)

Last Updated: 16 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION - LUMP SUM COMPENSATION PAYMENT - time of assessment of lump sum - notional interest - capital, income distinction considered - purpose of statutory payment relevant to whether receipt constitutes income in hands of taxpayer - s.45 of Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 considered - principles regarding time of assessment of income considered - "loss of income" within s.51(1) of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 must be "real", not "notional" loss.

Words & Phrases: "loss of income"

REASONS FOR DECISION

26 March, 1987 Deputy President R.A. Layton

Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)

Dr D.B. Williams (Member)

At the conclusion of the hearing of this application for review, Deputy President Layton stated orally the terms of the decision intended to be made, and the reasons therefor. Those reasons are set out below.

  1. This application for review concerns a decision of the Commissioner that a lump sum of $25,743.80 received by the taxpayer in October 1983 for arrears of weekly payment of compensation, was assessable income in the year ending 30 June 1984 and, further, that an amount of $9,732.00 representing notional interest lost due to wrongful deductions of installments from the lump sum payment, was not an allowable deduction pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act").
  2. At the hearing, the taxpayer represented himself, and the Commissioner was represented by counsel, Mr McCullough.
  3. The facts giving rise to this application are that the taxpayer was an employee of the Australian Telecommunications Commission and suffered a compensable injury due to which he became totally incapacitated for work as from 30 January 1980. He did not receive any weekly payment of compensation until a hearing of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, after which he was paid a lump sum of $25,743.80, representing weekly payment of compensation from 30 January 1980 until 19 October 1983 inclusive, pursuant to s.45 of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 ("the Compensation Act"). Thereafter, weekly payment of compensation was made commencing on 20 October 1983 and payment has continued up to the time of this hearing. The details as to the payment of compensation are set out in Determinations of the Commissioner for Employees' Compensation, which formed part of the documentation before the Tribunal at this hearing.
  4. The taxpayer made a number of submissions; first, that the lump sum of $25,743.80 was a payment of capital and not income; second, that the lump sum represented payment over a period commencing 30 January 1980 and should not have been assessed in the financial year ending 30 June 1984; third, that as a result of the wrongful deduction of income tax instalments from the lump sum payment, by his employer, he was unable to use that sum to earn interest and had therefore suffered a loss of income which was an allowable deduction under the Assessment Act. (The calculation of that interest was detailed by the taxpayer in his annexure to his income tax return.) Further, the taxpayer argued that, as the compensation paid was less than his real wages, the difference between the amounts should be allowed as a deduction for loss of income.
  5. Mr McCullough submitted that the lump sum payment of compensation representing arrears of weekly payment of compensation had the character of income in the hands of the taxpayer; that character of income was not lost by the fact that it was paid as a lump sum. The lump sum was therefore assessable income within the meaning of s25 as well as sub-s26(j) of the Assessment Act.
  6. Whether or not a particular receipt is income depends on its quality in the hands of the recipient. The quality of such receipt was considered by the Full High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540. This decision is a suitable starting point in considering the law in this area. In Dixon's case (supra), the High Court was concerned with the character of an amount paid by an employee during the war years to make up the difference between the military pay of an employee who had enlisted and the payment he would have received in his civilian occupation. It was held that sums so received were income and the comments expressed by Fullager, J at p568 are apposite:

"What is paid is not salary or remuneration, and it is not paid in respect of or in relation to any employment of the recipient. But it is intended to be, and is in fact, a substitute for - the equivalent pro tanto of - the salary or wages which would have been earned and paid if the enlistment had not taken place. As such, it must be income, even though it is paid voluntarily and there is not even a moral obligation to continue making the payments. It acquires the character of that for which it is substituted and that to which it is added."

  1. Previous Taxation Boards of Review have regarded weekly payments of compensation as assessable income, for example, in 1958 8 TBRD 358 Case H76 and 1959 10 TBRD 187 Case K34, the Board expressed the view that the fact that arrears of weekly compensation payments were payable in a lump sum and paid retrospectively did not alter the nature of the receipt. They were still payments of an income and not of a capital nature.
  2. More recently, the characterisation of compensation payments has been considered by both the Victorian Supreme Court in Tinkler v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 78 ATC 4565 and by the Federal Court on appeal from that decision reported in 79 ATC 4641. That case concerned a taxpayer who was unable to work in her employment as a result of injuries received in a motor car accident. She received payments pursuant to sub-ss 25(1) and (2) of the Victorian Motor Accident Act 1973 which provided that a person who was injured as a result of an accident and suffered a "loss of income", was entitled to payments in respect of that loss. The statutory payment was calculated as a proportion of the taxpayer's average weekly earnings multiplied by the number of weeks of incapacity. In upholding the Commissioner's assessment of the payments as income, Jenkinson, J. in the Victorian Supreme Court decision found, at p.4571:

"...that the amount has been measured by reference to the diminution of income caused by the injury, but in the sense also that the amount is, substantially, a statutory substitute, pro tanto, for the salary or wages lost."

The taxpayer's appeal from that decision was unanimously dismissed by the Full Federal Court who were in substantial agreement with the findings of Jenkinson, J and his conclusion that the payments received by the taxpayer were made in substitution for income and therefore had the character of income. Further, Deane and Fisher, JJ, in considering the character of the payment of compensation, not only considered the particular statutory wording, but also stated:

"The method of calculation or quantification may provide a quite misleading guide to the character of the payment. Where however, as in the present case, the method of calculation comprises an integral part of the provisions under which the entitlement arises, it can legitimately be regarded as a relevant factor in determining the character of the payment."

(79 ATC 4648)

9. The importance of considering the particular statutory provisions under which compensation is paid was highlighted in the case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Slaven 84 ATC 4073. That case, as with Tinkler's case (supra), was also concerned with payments made pursuant to the Victorian Motor Accidents Act (supra), but concerned amendments which had been made following Tinkler's case which were specifically devised to exclude such payments being regarded as assessable income. The Full Court of the Federal Court expressed the view that the purpose of a statutory payment as disclosed by the terms of the statute must be a powerful, although not conclusive, aid to the determination of the character of the payment and, in particular, in determining whether its receipt constitutes income in the hands of the taxpayer.

  1. Further comments were made by the Full Court which drew a distinction between loss of earnings and loss of earning capacity. On the facts before it, the statutory payment made to the taxpayer specifically provided for compensation for loss of earning capacity. In assessing the amount of such payment, the Board considered the nature of the injury, the nature of the person's professional business and the medical evidence.
  2. Turning to the relevant legislation in this case, the lump sum payment representing weekly payment of compensation, was paid pursuant to s45 of the Compensation Act which provides for weekly payment of compensation:

"Where an injury to an employee results in the employee being totally incapacitated for work "

Thereafter follow a number of provisions setting out the calculation of the rate at which weekly payment of compensation is payable. The rate for the first 26 weeks is payable at the same rate as an entitlement to sick leave pay, and thereafter at a prescribed rate or average weekly earnings, whichever is the lesser.

12. It is to be noted that the calculation of the rate of weekly compensation is not referrable to the nature of the injury, the specific disability or loss of function arising from the injury, nor any future loss of capacity to earn. In this respect s.45, which is contained in Part III of the Compensation Act, differs from the provisions for lump sum compensation payable pursuant to ss 39, 40, 41 and 42 of the Compensation Act which are concerned with the type of injury and the extent of the disability or loss of function arising from that injury. Section 43 of the Compensation Act is concerned with injury resulting in death. Section 49 of the Compensation Act is concerned with the redemption of weekly payment of compensation to an employee who was partially incapacitated and in sub-s.(4), regard is to be had to:

" the nature of the injury .... the age and occupation of the employee and any other relevant matters."

  1. Section 45, in the context of the whole of the Compensation Act, is confined to a payment of compensation which is related solely to loss of earnings which arise from injury but not to exceed the prescribed amount.
  2. Unlike the fact situation facing the court in Slaven's case (supra), this is not a payment for a loss of earning capacity. Instead, it is a payment for loss of income or, as Mr McCullough described it, s45 is an "income maintenance provision". It is a payment which is calculated to reflect a loss of income following an incapacitating injury. We therefore conclude that receipt of weekly payment of compensation pursuant to s.45 of the Compensation Act is a receipt which has the character of income in the hands of the recipient, and is assessable income pursuant to ss25 and 26J of the Assessment Act. The fact that it was paid in a lump sum does not alter its character. It still represents income loss in arrears as was stated in the cases of Dixon (supra) and Slaven (supra).
  3. As to the taxpayer's submission that the Commissioner should not have assessed the full amount of compensation in the financial year ending 30 June 1984 because it represented payment over a period commencing from 1980, the principles regarding the time of assessment of income have been the subject of a number of decisions. In an early caase of Leigh v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (1927) 11 Tax Cases 590, Rowlatt, J., at 595, said, "....receivability without receipt for the purposes of income tax is nothing at all". Subsequently in the case of Brent v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4195, Gibbs, J. (as he then was) found that the taxpayer was entitled under an agreement, to be paid a certain sum in 1969 for personal services, but this was not paid until July 1980. It was his view that the money was not assessable income in the year in which payment was due, but was assessable income when the payment was received.
  4. The effect of the time of receipt of a lump sum in arrears of weekly payment of compensation was specifically considered in the case of H76 1958 8 TBRD 359. It was held by the Board, after considering the relevant authorities that such a lump sum is derived in the year of its receipt and is assessable therefore in the income tax year of receipt.
  5. On the basis of these authorities, we conclude that the Commissioner was correct in regarding the lump sum payment to the taxpayer as being assessable in the year ending 30 June 1984. In reaching this conclusion, we are mindful of the taxpayer's concern that by having the lump sum regarded as assessable income in that year, he has been placed in a position in which his tax rate on that amount is higher than it would have been if the same sum had been paid to him over the relevant period. This, however, is not the fault of the Commissioner of Taxation, who is properly acting within the provisions of the Assessment Act. This problem arises because of a dispute as to liability to pay compensation in conjunction with a later payment of a considerable amount of arrears of compensation by way of a lump sum.
  6. The third matter raised by the taxpayer concerns what we understand to be a claim for the sum of $9,732.00 as an allowable deduction for loss of income by way of interest on installments wrongly deducted. The taxpayer claims that, because the employer wrongly deducted tax installments from the lum sum, he lost income from interest which he could have obtained by investment of that money. As to this argument, s.221C of the Assessment Act obliges an employer to deduct installments of income tax from the "salary or wages" of employees. "Salary or wages" is defined in sub-s.221A(1) as including any payments made and under placitum (f), "by way of compensation or of sickness or accident pay in respect of incapacity for work, being payments calculated at a weekly or other periodical rate". Although the word "instalments" (plural) is used in S.221C, that does not preclude the plural from including the singular according to sub-s.23(b) the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 (as amended) but, in any event, the definition contained in sub-s.221A(1) specifically resolves any possible inconsistency in interpretation. The employer was therefore correct in making the installment deductions.
  7. As to whether the taxpayer has a loss or outgoing in respect of gaining or producing assessable income pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Act because of his inability to invest the installments, this argument must fail for two reasons: first, that the installments were a legitimate deduction and the taxpayer had no right to their payment and, second, the word "loss" in sub-s.51(1) refers not to notional loss, but to a real loss (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Western Suburbs Cinema Limited (1943-1952) Vols. 7, 8 and [1952] HCA 28; 9 ATD 452, and Commercial Banking Company of Sydney Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 83 ATC 4208).

20. In the latter case, which was concerned with interest due on unpaid accounts, David Hunt, J. stated at 4214:

"I do not accept that such a non-receipt [of a revenue item] is denied the character of a loss within the meaning of secl. 51. It is common ground that the amounts in question here were owing by the to the .... taxpayer, and that the taxpayer was entitled to expect to be paid that interest. When the position was reached that the taxpayer no longer expected to be paid, and the debt was written off, it suffered a loss of the amount which it previously expected to be paid. That is a real loss which is suffered."

  1. In this case, the taxpayer was not entitled to be paid the installments, there was no legal entitlement to interest and he has not foregone any rights to interest which he may have had such as would convert a notional loss into a real loss. He has not, therefore, suffered a loss of income which would be an allowable deduction pursuant to sub-s51(1) of the Assessment Act.
  2. For similar reasons, we also reject the taxpayer's claim that the difference between his wages entitlement and the compensation payable should also be a deduction. He has no legal entitlement to this difference. It is a notional loss only which is not covered by sub-s51(1) of the Assessment Act.
  3. As to the final concerns of the taxpayer about the effect of delays of his payment of compensation and delay in the final determination of his taxation objection, we understand those concerns, together with the emotional and economic effect that they have had on him. However, the delay in compensation payment can in no way be attributable to the Commissioner of Taxation and is an unfortunate but inevitable delay which can occur when questions of liability to pay compensation are in issue. In this case, such issues were not resolved until after a hearing before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The second delay concerns the determination of the taxpayer's objection and as to that, it is hoped that this Tribunal, because of the greater resources made available to it than to the previous Taxation Boards of Review, will be able to expedite finalisation of cases, this taxpayer's case amongst them.

24. For these reasons, therefore, we consider that the Commissioner's decision should be upheld.
