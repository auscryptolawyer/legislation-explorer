Bulie and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2026] ARTA 1003 (4 June 2026)

Applicant: Ragnar Bulie

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Numbers: 2025/2067, 2025/2068, 2025/2069, 2025/2070, 2025/2071

Tribunal: General Member M Burch

Place: Sydney

Date: 4 June 2026

Decision: The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution decides that the objection is allowed in full.

Catchwords

TAXATION – income tax – where applicant a dual resident of Australia and Singapore – where applicant has permanent home and habitual abode in both Australia and Singapore – whether applicant to be treated as resident of Australia or Singapore under tax treaty ‘tie-breaker’ rule – country with which applicant has closest personal and economic relations

Legislation

Agreement between the Government of the Commonwealth of Australia and the Government of the Republic of Singapore for the Avoidance of Double Taxation and the Prevention of Fiscal Evasion with respect to Taxes and Income [1969] ATS 14



Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) s 14ZZK

Cases

Pike v Commissioner of Taxation [2019] FCA 2185

Pike v Commissioner of Taxation [2020] FCAFC 158

Secondary Materials

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The issue in this case is whether the Applicant, Mr Ragnar Bulie, is to be treated as a tax resident of Australia or Singapore for the income years in dispute. Under the domestic tax law of the respective countries, Mr Bulie is a resident of each of them; the tax treaty between the two countries[1] provides ‘tie-breaker’ rules that determine of which country Mr Bulie is to be treated as a resident.
  2. Between May 2019 and March 2023, the Respondent issued a series of income tax assessments and Division 293 assessments (Assessments) with respect to Mr Bulie’s taxable employment income earned in Singapore for the income years ending 30 June 2018 through 30 June 2022 (Relevant Period) on the basis that Mr Bulie was a tax resident of Australia. The Assessments were based on the relevant income tax returns lodged by Mr Bulie.
  3. Mr Bulie lodged an objection to the Assessments on 13 April 2024 (Objection), contending that under the Treaty he was to be treated as a tax resident of only Singapore during the Relevant Period, meaning that his taxable employment income was taxable only by Singapore[2] (and that the Assessments were thus excessive and incorrect).
  4. The Respondent disallowed the Objection in full on 16 December 2024 (Objection Decision) on the basis that Mr Bulie was to be treated as a tax resident of only Australia under the Treaty tie-breaker rules.
  5. Mr Bulie applied for review of the Objection Decision on 19 March 2025.
  6. As the taxpayer, Mr Bulie bears the burden of proving that the Assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessments should have been.[3]

IS MR BULIE TO BE TREATED AS A TAX RESIDENT OF AUSTRALIA OR SINGAPORE DURING THE RELEVANT PERIOD?

Applicable Law

  1. Mr Bulie’s tax residency is to be determined by the Treaty’s ‘tie-breaker’ provisions in Article 3(2), which provides in relevant part:

(2) Where by reason of the provisions of paragraph 1 of this Article an individual is both a Singapore resident and an Australian resident —

(a) he shall be treated solely as a Singapore resident:

(i) if he has a permanent home available to him in Singapore and has not a permanent home available to him in Australia;

(ii) if sub-paragraph (a)(i) of this paragraph is not applicable but he has an habitual abode in Singapore and has not an habitual abode in Australia;

(iii) if neither sub-paragraph (a)(i) nor sub-paragraph (a)(ii) of this paragraph is applicable but the Contracting State with which his personal and economic relations are closest is Singapore;

(b) he shall be treated solely as an Australian resident —

(i) if he has a permanent home available to him in Australia and has not a permanent home available to him in Singapore;

(ii) if sub-paragraph (b)(i) of this paragraph is not applicable but he has an habitual abode in Australia and has not an habitual abode in Singapore;

(iii) if neither sub-paragraph (b)(i) nor sub-paragraph (b)(ii) of this paragraph is applicable but the Contracting State with which his personal and economic relations are closest is Australia.

  1. It is common ground between the parties that Mr Bulie is a dual tax resident of Australia and Singapore and that, for Treaty purposes, he has a permanent home available to him, and has a habitual abode, in each country. Thus, the confined issue for determination in this case is with which country Mr Bulie’s personal and economic relations are closest.
  2. The leading authorities on this issue are the decisions in Commissioner of Taxation v Pike [2019] FCA 2185 (Pike FCA), and Commissioner of Taxation v Pike [2020] FCAFC 158 (Pike FCAFC) (upholding the decision in Pike FCA) (together, Pike), which involved a substantively similar tax treaty tie-breaker rule and a similar set of facts (as discussed below). Pike FCAFC makes clear that the taxpayer’s personal and economic relations are to be considered in a holistic way, and that the test is a ‘composite’ one.[4]
  3. The decision in Pike FCAFC[5] acknowledges the OECD commentary on the test that provides:

[R]egard will be had to his family and social relations, his occupations, his political, cultural and other activities, his place of business, the place from which he administers his property, etc. The circumstances must be examined as a whole, but it is nevertheless obvious that considerations based on the personal acts of the individual must receive special attention.[6]

Background Facts

  1. The following background facts are not in dispute.
  2. Mr Bulie was born in Norway in 1961 and is a Norwegian citizen.
  3. He moved to Australia in 1989 as a permanent resident and married his wife, Elise, an Australian citizen, in 1994. Mr Bulie and his wife have two children; the elder daughter is in her late 20s and the younger daughter was born in 2001 in Singapore (when Mr Bulie and his family were living there). Mr Bulie holds a Resident Return 5 Year (Subclass 155) visa, which requires him to be physically present in Australia for a total of two years (730 days) out of the five-year visa term.
  4. Starting in 1986, Mr Bulie has worked in a variety of jobs in the oil and gas industry all over the world (i.e., Norway, Australia, United States, England), including a previous stint working and living in Singapore from 1999 until 2004 (during which time his wife lived with him in Singapore and his younger child was born there). During his career, Mr Bulie has moved internationally nine times for work and has held twelve different roles in five different countries.
  5. Mr Bulie and his wife bought a home in Sydney in July 2013 (Sydney Home), where Mr Bulie lived with his family until 2017. During that period, Mr Bulie worked in Sydney in a variety of roles.
  6. In November 2017, Mr Bulie started a full-time job working in Singapore for a company now called Equinor and he continues to work there, earning approximately $700,000 per year, as the Head of Crude Oil Trading for Equinor Asia Pacific. Mr Bulie and his wife decided that Mr Bulie would relocate to Singapore for the job so that he could earn enough to secure the family’s finances and fund their day-to-day living, pay for his children’s education and care, pay for the mortgage on the Sydney Home, and fund his and his wife’s retirement. Since starting the job, Mr Bulie has held a Singapore Employment Pass and associated residency permit.
  7. Mr Bulie also receives modest amounts of pension and passive income from Norway (which, during the Relevant Period, amounted to less than 10% of Mr Bulie’s total income). During the Relevant Period, Mr Bulie earned a negligible amount of interest from his Australian bank accounts. His wife works but earns a modest amount that is not enough for the family’s needs in Australia.
  8. From January 2018 until the end of August 2022, Mr Bulie lived in a leased residential property in Singapore; since September 2022 he has lived in another leased residential property in Singapore. Mr Bulie’s wife and youngest child have continued to live in the Sydney Home since 2013 (his eldest child lived in Canberra during the Relevant Period and has recently moved to Denmark).
  9. In Singapore, Mr Bulie has his closest personal friends and is a member of the British Club, where he regularly socialises and is an active member of its squash club.
  10. Mr Bulie spent 173 days physically present in Australia in 2018; 78 days in 2019; 189 days in 2020; 144 days in 2021; and 108 days in 2022. Overall, during these years, Mr Bulie spent approximately 38% of his time (as measured by days of physical presence) in Australia.
  11. During the Relevant Period, Mr Bulie contributed a total of $127,500 to his superannuation account, and at the end of the 2022 income year that account had a balance of $137,339.17. Mr Bulie also maintains Australian bank accounts and private health insurance. The Sydney Home has appreciated in value significantly since it was purchased in 2013 for $1,825,000 and is subject to a mortgage. Mr Bulie also had a boat in Sydney during the Relevant Period, for which he incurred a variety of boat-related costs.
  12. During the Relevant Period, Mr Bulie sent large sums of money (over $1.7m during the Relevant Period) from Singapore to Australia for the support of his family and to make mortgage payments on the Sydney Home.

Consideration and Decision

  1. While acknowledging that ‘[e]ach case must be fact specific’,[7] a comparison between the fundamental facts and circumstances in Mr Bulie’s case and those of Mr Pike (the taxpayer in the eponymous case) is warranted given the authoritative nature of Pike and the basic factual similarities between the cases.
  2. Like Mr Bulie, Mr Pike moved overseas to live and work (in Thailand) while his family remained in Australia. Though he spent the majority of his time overseas, Mr Pike spent a significant amount of time visiting his family in Australia during his years living overseas.[8] Mr Pike’s foreign earnings supported his family in Australia (as well as his extended family in Zimbabwe). Mr Pike maintained bank accounts in Australia and, during his time living overseas, bought land in Australia on which he planned to build a home for himself, his partner, and his sons. Mr Pike ended up selling the land without building or living on it.
  3. While Mr Pike ‘had a life’ overseas that ‘included an active social and recreational dimension,’[9] at an emotional level, his closer personal relations were with Australia on account of his partner and children living in Australia. However,
    In contrast and overwhelmingly, Mr Pike’s economic relations were closer to Thailand. It was this Thai sourced income stream, derived from Mr Pike’s ongoing employment there, which not only supported his life and lifestyle there but also, all the more so after [Mr Pike’s partner’s] employment with Ernst & Young came to an end, supported his family in Australia, including him, when he was able to be with them. Contrary to his original aspiration, Mr Pike had never been employed in Australia. ... His journeys here were wholly personal, never in the additional nature of reporting in person to, or working for a time at, a “head office” or even controlling regional office.[10]
  4. I agree with Mr Bulie’s contention that the Court in Pike gave ‘paramount weight to the taxpayer’s primary source of ongoing income’.[11] Likewise, I consider that the primary source of Mr Bulie’s ongoing income is the primary consideration when considering his closest economic relations. Aside from the relatively small amount of passive income earned from his native Norway, Mr Bulie earned the bulk of his income during the Relevant Period from his employment in Singapore. As such, Singapore is the ‘centre of his day-to-day life and source of economic lifeline for himself and his family’.[12]
  5. In Pike, the taxpayer’s earning of income overseas outweighed both his personal connections to Australia and his other economic connections to Australia. With respect to Mr Pike’s other economic connections to Australia, the decision in Pike FCA emphasised that Mr Pike’s assets in Australia were not income-producing:

For part of the period between 2010 and 2013, Mr Pike owned a capital asset in Australia, the Brookwater land. While I accept that this land can, on the basis of ownership alone, be regarded as evidencing an economic relationship with Australia, it did not have the additional feature of being a competitor of any sort with the Thai employment for income production. Mr Pike maintained bank accounts in both Thailand and Australia but these were but conduits through which living and other expenses in Thailand or Australia for him or, as the case may be, him and his family were met. Mr Pike jointly owned cars in Australia but, even taken in conjunction with the Brookwater land, as a foundation for relative closeness of economic relations, these pale into relative insignificance when compared with Mr Pike’s Thai employment.[13]

  1. The Respondent contends that Mr Bulie’s ongoing ownership (with his wife) of the Sydney Home can be distinguished from Mr Pike’s temporary land ownership on the basis that in Mr Bulie’s case,

that ownership is much longer lasting and of higher value. Both of these factors mean that, even though the [Sydney Home] did not generate income for the Applicant as Mr Pike’s land did not generate income for him, the [Sydney Home] has appreciated very substantially and forms the Applicant’s most significant asset.[14]

  1. I acknowledge that Mr Bulie’s Sydney Home, on account of its value and nature, provides for stronger economic relations to Australia than what Mr Pike had via his land ownership. The decision in Pike FCA also acknowledges this point, albeit with respect to the ordinary meaning of the word ‘resident’ in s 6(1) ITAA 1936 rather than the relevant treaty tie-breaker rule:
    [I]t is a striking feature of that case and hardly likely to be coincidental that ... Mr Pike and [his partner] have never owned a home in Australia. Renting carries with it a flexibility in relocation that home ownership may not.[15]
  2. However, while I accept that Mr Bulie’s ownership of the Sydney Home is quantitatively a more significant economic relation to Australia than Mr Pike’s ownership of land was (in part because it affords Mr Bulie less flexibility with respect to relocation), I do not consider it to be qualitatively different. The most relevant feature of both assets for current purposes is that they were not income-producing during the relevant time periods. That the Sydney Home appreciated in value during the Relevant Period does not substantially alter the analysis. Simply put, capital appreciation (especially in this case, where the amount of annual appreciation is equal to a small fraction of Mr Bulie’s annual realised employment income)[16] is not the same thing as realised income as a measure of annual economic relations for the present purposes, as the decisions in Pike make clear.[17] I agree with Mr Bulie’s contention that, as a non-income producing asset, the Sydney Home ‘does not alter [Mr Bulie’s] actual, recurring economic activity’, which is centred in Singapore.
  3. The Respondent contends that a primary economic concern of Mr Bulie’s is retirement planning and that he has been building significant assets in Australia for that purpose; specifically, the Sydney Home and his superannuation accounts (to which Mr Bulie made the maximum allowable contributions during the Relevant Period). In this regard, the Respondent contrasts Mr Pike’s situation: Mr Pike did not own a home in Australia or have superannuation savings in Australia and the evidence indicated that his wife was planning to join him overseas after their children had grown.
  4. I do not accept the premise that Mr Bulie has a definitive plan to retire in Australia. Mr Bulie’s evidence is that his retirement plans are not settled and that he and his wife might wish to spend time in Europe in the future. Mr Bulie’s superannuation savings are available to support his retirement wherever it may be spent. In any event, while Mr Bulie’s superannuation savings are not insignificant, and provide an economic relation to Australia that Mr Pike did not have, his superannuation accounts were not income-producing (in the relevant sense) during the Relevant Period and were not large in comparison to his earned income during that period. Furthermore, as Pike indicates, it is the source of income rather than its destination that is the more relevant consideration with respect to the instant tie-breaker rule. As such, Mr Bulie’s superannuation accounts are not qualitatively different from Mr Pike’s ownership of assets in Australia (or Mr Bulie’s ownership of the Sydney Home) and do not alter the analysis that Mr Bulie’s earned employment income is his strongest and most important economic relation.
  5. I give relatively little weight to Mr Bulie’s remaining economic relations with Australia. That he has maintained private health cover, Medicare access, and bank accounts in Australia is a function of the administrative realities and conveniences of dual residency and does not have a significant bearing on the tie-breaker test in this case.[18]
  6. Mr Bulie concedes that, like Mr Pike, his personal relations with Australia are closer than his overseas personal relations, mainly because his family lives in Australia. However, Mr Bulie’s evidence is that his closest personal friends are in Singapore (and his wife also has close friends there and visits regularly). Cumulatively over the course of his career, Mr Pike has spent a significant amount of time working and living in Singapore; in addition to his current stint, he lived there with his wife for over five years at the turn of the millennium, during which time his youngest daughter was born there. For these reasons, even though his personal relations with Australia are stronger than his personal relations with Singapore, I consider that Mr Bulie has developed personal relations overseas that are stronger than Mr Pike’s personal relations overseas. I also note in this regard that Mr Pike was an Australian citizen, and Mr Bulie is not.[19]
  7. In terms of physical presence, during the five-year period from 2018 through 2022, Mr Bulie spent a significant amount of time in Australia visiting his family: he was physically present in Australia for approximately 38% of the five-year period (compared to approximately 25% for Mr Pike during the period to which his tax dispute related).[20] I consider this difference too to be quantitative rather than qualitative and in the ultimate analysis, I consider it significant that Mr Bulie spent the majority of his time during the Relevant Period physically present in Singapore.[21] Mr Bulie did not earn any employment income from Australia and none of his visits were for work or business purposes. The amount of time he spent in Australia supports that his personal ties to Australia were stronger than his personal ties to Singapore but does not alter the ultimate analysis of his economic relations.
  8. Finally, I consider that the most important factor to Mr Bulie was the earning of employment income. He has spent his career moving around the world in order to earn his income using his specialised industry knowledge. That he spent the Relevant Period living away from his family so that he could work in Singapore indicates that earning income was the most significant factor to him. I consider that Mr Bulie’s decision to spend the bulk of his time living overseas away from his family in order to best exploit his skills and experience in earning his income to be the ‘personal acts’ of his that are most deserving of ‘special attention’. Thus, as in Pike, I give this factor the greatest weight. And, as in Pike, I find that Mr Bulie’s economic and personal relations to Singapore were, in composite, stronger than his economic and personal relations to Australia during the Relevant Period. During the Relevant Period, Mr Bulie spent the bulk of his time in Singapore and earned the bulk of his income in Singapore. Though his personal relations to Australia were stronger, he had meaningful personal relations in Singapore as well. And though his ownership of assets in Australia was not insignificant, they did not produce income and paled in their significance when compared to the primacy of his income-earning activity in Singapore. For these reasons, I find that Mr Bulie’s strongest personal and economic relations during the Relevant Period, when considered holistically, were with Singapore.

DECISION

  1. Mr Bulie discharged his burden of proving that the Assessments were incorrect and what they should have been. It follows that the Objection Decision, being the decision under review, is set aside and substituted with a decision allowing the Objection in full.
