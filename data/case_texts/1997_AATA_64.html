DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION NO 11670

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant



And Commissioner of Taxation

DECISION



Tribunal Mr B.H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Mr W.G. McLean, Member

Date 4 March 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal varies the decision under review by allowing the applicant’s objection to the extent of allowing a deduction for interest incurred of $14,234 and mobile telephone expenditure of $1,107 in the year ended 30 June, 1992.



.........(Sgd. B.H. Pascoe)........

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - Interest - employer share scheme - no payment made whether loss or outgoing incurred - cost of newspapers - whether allowable deduction - cost of dry cleaning - whether allowable deduction.

Henderson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4016

REASONS FOR DECISION

4 March 1997 Mr B.H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Mr W.G. McLean, Member

  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection to an amended assessment of income tax based on income of the year ended 30 June, 1992. The amendment had been issued to disallow $19,256 of expenses claimed as deductions in the return lodged by the applicant. At the hearing the respondent conceded that a deduction of $1,107 relating to mobile telephone expenditure was allowable and the applicant did not seek to pursue claims for certain other expenditure. The items which remained in contention were:

Interest $16,509

Newspapers 188

Dry cleaning 42

  1. At the hearing the applicant was represented by his accountant Mr I. Ross and the respondent by Mr G. Davies of counsel. Evidence was given by the applicant.
  2. In the relevant year the applicant was employed as the national merchandising manger of a major clothing manufacturer. The claim for interest related to a bank loan for the purchase of shares in an associated company of his employer. The claim for newspapers was the cost of having The Age delivered to his home daily and The Australian on one day per week. The dry cleaning claim was said to relate to the dry cleaning of suits soiled in visits to factory and stores.
  3. In January, 1990, the applicant was invited to participate in what was described as the “Bank Finance Share Purchase Scheme”. Under this scheme the employer had negotiated with the bank for loans to be made available to nominated employees for the purpose of acquiring shares in O Pty Ltd. Under the terms of the arrangement the bank held the shares as security together with an undertaking by the employer to repurchase or refinance the shares on the death, resignation, retirement or cessation of employment of the employee. The principal was to be repayable over a ten year period commencing twelve months after the granting of the loan. The employer was to be liable for a maximum rate of 13.5% interest with any excess interest being charged to the employer. Interest for the first year of loan was to be added to the original principal and, thereafter, dividends on the shares and tax saving from negative gearing were expected to finance the interest and principal repayments. Attached to the letter of offer from the employer was a table setting out expected cash flows from the scheme based on initial dividends of 10% increasing to 20% by 1994.
  4. The applicant accepted the offer to acquire 75,000 shares and signed an undertaking that, in the event that he terminated employment with the company, he would repay outstanding principal and interest and dispose of the shares to a purchaser nominated by the directors. A loan agreement with the bank was entered into on 26 January, 1990 which provided for a fully drawn advance in the name of the applicant for $86,000. The agreement provided interest at 13.5% with the employer to be debited with any excess over this rate. It is assumed that the $86,000 stated in the agreement represented the initial $75,000 plus the first twelve months interest. No dividends were paid by O Pty Ltd and the bank continued to increase the amount owed by the applicant by monthly interest charges. On 8 November, 1991 the applicant’s employer advised by letter that the terms of the share purchase scheme appeared to be in breach of The Corporations Law. The letter advised further that, as a consequence:

(a) the arrangement for the employer to advance loan funds to the applicant to meet any excess of capitalised bank loan interest over the total loan facility ceased as of 7 June, 1991.

(b) amounts advanced by the employer to date would continue to attract interest at 13.5% from 7 June, 1991.

(c) the actual interest rate charged by the bank became the responsibility of the applicant as of 7 June, 1991 with the employee no longer being liable for any excess over 13.5% interest.

(d) the guarantee from the employer to the bank for repayment of any loan money outstanding to the bank may be voidable at the option of the employer.

  1. By letter of 27 August, 1992, the employer advised that the applicant’s commitment under the loan scheme as at 30 June, 1992 was:

Shares in O Pty Ltd 75,000.00

Balance of bank loan at 1 July, 1991 87,887.54

Interest and bank charges for year 12,328.33

Balance of bank loan at 30 June, 1992 100,215.87

Employer loan at 1 July, 1991 2,274.82

Interest in excess of 13.5% charged by bank 610.49

Interest at 13.5% for year 394.92

Balance employer loan at 30 June, 1992 3,280.23

Total liability at 30 June, 1992 103,496.10

The applicant claimed a deduction for interest in his return of income for the year ended 30 June, 1992 of $15,608 being $12,328 to the bank and $3,280 to the employer. At the hearing both parties agreed that the correct figure for the bank interest was $13,229 to include interest of $901 debited to the account by the bank on 5 July, 1991. The employer’s letter of 27 August, 1992 had incorrectly commenced with the balance at that date rather than the balance prior to the interest debit.

  1. By letter of 18 January, 1993 from the bank countersigned by the employer and the applicant the bank and employer released and discharged the applicant from any liability in respect of the bank loan and his participation in the share scheme.
  2. In his evidence, the applicant said that he entered into the scheme to purchase shares as it was seen to provide a benefit to senior executives who would be participating in the results of their efforts to achieve profitable growth by their employer company. The projections provided to him indicated that the negative gearing of the investment would enable the loan to be paid off within ten years from the dividends and income tax savings. He had expectations that the company would be profitable and would pay the projected dividends. Dividends had been paid in prior years. He said that the letter of 8 November, 1991 was a unilateral decision of his employer and the changes to the scheme had not been discussed with him prior to the receipt of this letter. His understanding was that the doubts on the validity of the scheme arose out of discussions relating to the possible sale of the employer company. He said that he was advised by the management of the company that there was no requirement for him to make repayments of the debt as it was anticipated that negotiations for sale of the company would include negotiation for termination of the share scheme and discharge of the debt. The discharge of his debt on 18 January, 1993 was understood by him to have arisen out of the sale of the employer company and as part of the overall negotiation by the Board of the employer company with the bank in relation to the company’s exposure generally to the bank. The applicant stated that he had accepted that he was personally responsible to the bank to meet the interest costs and debt repayment obligations and that he had been unsure whether any other personal assets or bank accounts were at risk. He was uncertain as to when he became aware of negotiations between his employer and the bank but insisted that, until shortly before the debt was extinguished, he had retained an expectation of dividends. He was not aware whether he still owned shares in the company but said that he had been advised that they were worthless and that the company was expected to be wound up after June, 1994.
  3. It was submitted for the applicant that the interest debited to the applicant’s account with the bank and his employer during the year ended 30 June, 1992 constituted an allowable deduction under s. 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). It was said that the interest was incurred by the applicant for the purpose of deriving assessable income from dividends. It was submitted that, although no interest was paid by the applicant in the year, he had committed himself to a liability for such interest and was personally liable for such interest when debited to his account by the bank and his employer.
  4. For the respondent it was submitted that the applicant had not incurred the interest claimed in the year ended 30 June, 1992. It was said that he was not committed to pay such interest. The scheme entered into by the applicant was said to rely on payment of dividends by the company in order to meet interest on the bank loan and would only operate if substantial dividends were paid. The respondent argued that the applicant was never asked to make payments and the fact that the bank did not at any time regard the applicant as being in default in meeting repayments set out in the original loan agreement inferred that the bank had no expectation of payment where dividends were not received. It was said that the applicant had been told by his employer that it was not necessary to make payments. The respondent produced internal memoranda of the bank indicating that a doubtful debt provision for the balance of the applicant’s account at 26 May, 1992 had been created and that such provision had been reduced to nil preparatory to writing off the debt in September, 1992. The result of the discharge in January, 1993 was said to have left the applicant in the same position then as he had been before entering into the loan scheme and that he had suffered no loss. It was argued that, albeit with the benefit of hindsight, the applicant had not incurred any loss or outgoing and that the commercial reality of the situation was that the arrangement was contrived to ensure that outgoings would be incurred only if dividends were received. It was submitted that if a loss could have been said to have been incurred, then the applicant must be said to have made an equivalent gain in the second year as a result of the discharge of his liability. The respondent produced the income tax returns for the company in which the applicant held shares, O Pty Ltd, for the years ended 30 June, 1990 to 1993 inclusive to show that in each of those years the company had a nil taxable income and did not and was not likely to pay dividends in that period. It was further argued that the letter of 8 November, 1991 was a unilateral action by the employer without the agreement by the applicant and could not have had the effect of moving the employer’s liability to interest to the applicant.
  5. For the applicant to succeed the amount claimed must come within s. 51 of the Act which provides:
“All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”

The questions to be determined are whether the interest claimed constituted a loss or outgoing, whether it was incurred in the year of income and whether it was incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income. It is clear from numerous decisions of the Courts that for an amount to constitute a loss or outgoing does not require an actual outlay of cash. As observed in the joint judgment of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v James Flood Proprietary Limited [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492 at p. 506:

“The word “outgoing” might suggest that there must be an actual disbursement. But partly because such an interpretation would produce very strange and anomalous results, and partly because of the use of the word “incurred”, the provision has been interpreted to cover outgoings to which the taxpayer is definitively committed in the year of income although there has been no actual disbursement.”

Their Honours referred to the decision of Lord Cooper in James Spencer & Co v Commissioners of Inland Revenue (1950) SLT 266 at p. 268 and said:

“This passage must be qualified in its application under the Commonwealth Act. For under our law the facts must satisfy the expression ‘losses and outgoings incurred’. These words perhaps are but little more precise than the word ‘established’ or the expression used above ‘definitively committed’. But they do not admit of the deduction of charges unless, in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income or carrying on the business, the taxpayer has completely subjected himself to them. It may be going too far to say that he must have come under an immediate obligation enforceable at law whether payable presently or at a future time. It is probably going too far to say that the obligation must be indefeasible.”

The respondent sought to argue that this was a matter of expenditure which was not even impending, threatened or expected in the year of income and referred to the oft quoted statement of Dixon J., in New Zealand Flax Investments Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 when he said (at p. 207):

“ “Incurred” does not mean only defrayed, discharged, or borne, but rather it includes encountered, run into, or fallen upon. It is unsafe to attempt exhaustive definitions of a conception intended to have such a various or multifarious application. But it does not include a loss or expenditure which is no more than impending, threatened, or expected.”

In this case the applicant entered into a legal agreement with the bank under which he was liable to pay interest on the amount of the loan. The applicant was personally liable for that interest, at least to the extent to which it did not exceed 13.5%. Whilst the bank may not have demanded payment at the time the interest was debited to the account and was prepared to await the expected receipt of dividends to fund the discharge of the liability, this does not mean that the applicant was not definitively committed to the payment of interest. The evidence was unclear as to when the applicant became aware of the possibility that no dividends would be received and that the bank would forgive the debt. However, his oral evidence was that, as at 30 June, 1992, his expectation was that the share scheme was continuing and dividends would be forthcoming. Whilst the internal memorandum of the bank of 26 May, 1992 may indicate that the bank had little expectation of the applicant meeting his commitment, this did not change the then liability of the applicant. The letter of 27 August, 1992 from the employer reinforced the applicant’s understanding of a then presently existing liability for accumulated interest on the loan funds. It would appear likely that, prior to this date, and certainly, after this date, discussions had taken place between the employer and the bank regarding the position of the share scheme loans within the total bank-employer relationship if negotiations to dispose of the company or its undertaking were successfully concluded. Much was made by the respondent of the fact that the applicant had been advised by his employer that there was no need to make payment to the bank and that the debt was forgiven in the subsequent year of income. As said by Newton J., in Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4151 (at p. 4161):

“The authorities also show, in my opinion, that a taxpayer may completely subject itself to a liability, notwithstanding that the liability is defeasible: See Flood’s case at pp. 506-7: it may incidentally be remarked that all, or almost all, unpaid liabilities are in a sense defeasible, because they could in the future be forgiven by the creditor, or cancelled by Act of Parliament, or barred by any applicable statute of limitations.”

The question here is whether, at the dates during the year ended 30 June, 1992 when interest was debited to the applicant’s account with the bank, such interest could be regarded as “no more than impending, threatened or expected”. On the evidence presented we are satisfied that it was more. We find that the applicant was definitively committed to the liability for interest on the bank loan which liability was enforceable at law and that such interest was a loss or outgoing incurred in the relevant year of income.

  1. We accept also the evidence of the applicant that, at 30 June, 1992 he continued to have an expectation of deriving dividends from his investment in the shares. As such we find that the interest was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. It is clear from the authorities that the assessable income to which a loss or outgoing is directed need not be derived in the year in which the loss or outgoing is incurred provided such loss or outgoing is relevant and incidental to the derivation of assessable income. (See Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47). The respondent sought to argue that, at least in part, the interest was incurred to gain an income tax deduction and the “negative gearing” attributes of the scheme and, as such, s. 51 requires an apportionment. We do not accept that such an apportionment can apply here. In our view the total bank interest was incurred with the expectation of generating assessable dividend income. The fact that there was an expectation that the interest would exceed the dividends in the early years does not derogate from this. Objectively, the transaction was a straight forward commercial transaction. (See Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Janmor Nominees Pty Ltd 87 ATC 4813).
  2. The claim for interest debited to the account of the applicant by his employer needs separate consideration. Here the claim of $3,280 consisted of three separate items:
    1. Amount paid to the bank by the employer prior to 30 June, 1991 to meet the amount of interest which would bring the account over the pre-agreed total loan facility. This was treated on an advance to the applicant by the employer.
    2. Interest in excess of 13.5% charged to the employer by the bank but not accepted by the employer after 7 June, 1991.
    3. Interest at 13.5% for the year ended 30 June, 1992 on the preceding two items.

Whilst there might be reservations on the apparent action of the employer to seek to unilaterally exclude itself from liability for amounts of bank interest in excess of 13.5% and impose a charge for interest on such amounts, it is clear that such action was taken and accepted by the applicant. Although it does not appear in any prior document relating to the scheme, we accept that the employer did advance funds to the applicant as set out in 1. above. However, that amount was not an amount in the nature of interest and, in any event, was incurred prior to the commencement of the year ended 30 June, 1992. The other two items set out in 2. and 3. above represent interest incurred by the applicant in relation to his participation in the share scheme. In our view he was definitively committed to the liability for that interest totalling $1,005.

  1. The result of the above findings is that the applicant was entitled to a deduction under s. 51 of the Act for $14,234 in respect of interest incurred during the year ended 30 June, 1992 being $13,229 to the bank and $1,005 to his employer. Whilst it is true that the events in the subsequent year produced the result that the applicant was not required to discharge the liability incurred in the earlier years, it is only the year ended 30 June, 1992 which is before us and we have found that the outgoing was incurred in that year. In the case of Henderson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4016, the High Court was considering the effect of a change from a cash basis to an earnings basis and the question of assessability of fees collected in the year of change which were earned in the prior year and could potentially escape being brought to tax. In a decision on the first appeal, Windeyer J., (reported at 69 ATC 4049) considered that the exclusion of such fees in the year of change would not “reflect the true income over the period of two years combined”. On appeal to the Full Court, Barwick C.J., said (at p. 4019):
“But, with due respect to his Honour, there cannot be any warrant in a scheme of annual taxation upon the income derived in each year of taxation for combining the results of more than one year in order to obtain the assessable income for a particular year of tax. Of course, the experience of a prior year may be reflected in the opening figures of the relevant year but they become and are figures of that year and not figures of two years in combination.”

Although His Honour was there considering assessable income, we are of the opinion that his views have equal effect in relation to allowable deductions.

  1. The remaining deductions claimed by the applicant and in dispute were $188 per newspapers and $42 for dry cleaning. The applicant contended that in his capacity as National Merchandising manager he had a need to keep up with news relevant to his work and, in particular, notifications of tenders being called. He accepted that his wife and son may well have read the newspaper also and that the account at the newsagent was in the name of his wife. On the basis of the evidence we do not find that this expenditure has the necessary nexus with the derivation of the applicant’s salary income. The cost of normal daily newspapers delivered and read at home has much more of the attributes of normal private expenditure. While it was possible that occasional items within the newspapers may have been useful within the applicant’s occupation this is not sufficient to provide a deduction under s. 51 of the Act.
  2. Similarly, the remaining item of dry cleaning appears to have the attributes of private expenditure. The applicant was unable to provide any specific details of the soiling of clothes beyond that which would result from normal daily activities. He produced two receipts for dry cleaning and maintained that only business suits were treated by that particular establishment. The suits in question were normal clothing and the costs of maintaining such suits is private expenditure. The applicant said that his position required him to maintain a high standard of appearance and visits to factories and stores resulted in soiling. He was not able to provide details or satisfy us that visits to clothing factories or stores have a greater likelihood than any other form of employment to produce the need for out of the ordinary dry cleaning costs. Again we find that this claim must fail.
  3. The decision under review is varied to allow the applicant’s objection to the extent of allowing a deduction for interest incurred of $14,234 and mobile telephone expenditure of $1,107 in the year ended 30 June, 1992.
