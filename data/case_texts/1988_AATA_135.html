NT87/5685-5689 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 135; 88 ATC 520 (27 April 1988)

Last Updated: 25 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - assessable income - profit on sale of town-houses constructed by taxpayer company following purchase of site from shareholder - whether "profit-making undertaking or scheme".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s. 26(a).

Chapman v. Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 C.L.R. 167;

Steinberg v. Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 63; (1975) 134 C.L.R. 640;

Buckland v. F.C.T. (1960) 12 A.T.D. 166;

Kratzmann v. F.C.T. 70 A.T.C. 4043.

Respondent Nos. NT87/5685-5689

) Nos. NT87/5685-5689

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

27 April 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. The references before me for determination relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1982 inclusive but the substantial issues which must be determined relate to an earlier period, particularly the years ended 30 June 1969 to 1971 inclusive, and to events which followed during the years of income ended 30 June 1973 to 1975 when the assessable income giving rise to the dispute is said by the Commissioner to have been derived. In the years following 1973 the questions for determination lay dormant, hidden by the circumstance that the Applicant on any view was not deriving taxable income. That that was so was because the Applicant had incurred substantial losses in the course of farm development. However, when the Applicant presented its 1978 return of income, its claim to still have no taxable income was rejected by the Commissioner. Similar claims in relation to the four years following were also rejected.
  2. Commencing with a notice of assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1978 which issued on 21 December 1978 the Commissioner claimed that the taxable income of the company was $5,064, $19,749, $24,232, $13,953 and $10,876 for the years ended

30 June 1978 to 1982 inclusive. Each assessment was explained in terms such as:-

Loss as returned $61,939

DEDUCT:

Previous years' losses brought forward

reduced from $70,147 to $3,144 67,003

ADJUSTED TAXABLE INCOME $ 5,064

Notice in such terms was hardly apt to identify the substantive problem. However, the Commissioner's assertion was that the allowable carried-forward losses were to be reduced by reason of the Applicant having derived assessable income from sales of suburban town-houses in the years of income ended 30 June 1973 to 1975. From the lodgment of its 1974 return of income in December of that year, the Applicant had consistently asserted that the moneys received on the sale of those town-houses constituted receipts of capital.

  1. Having been so assessed in each instance the Applicant, within the defined period of only sixty days allowed to it for the purpose, objected in writing to each assessment so made. After intervals of time ranging up to thirty months, the Commissioner gave notice to the Applicant that its objections had been disallowed. Within the defined period of only sixty days then allowed the Applicant requested that its objections be referred for independent review. The first such request for reference was made on 24 March 1980. It was not complied with for seven and a half years. Other requests for reference followed as objections to assessments for later years were disallowed. Then on 20 November 1987 the Commissioner referred to this Tribunal all of the objections of the Applicant. The matters came on for hearing in March 1988.
  2. The Applicant is a Company: Beta. It is a company which serves the interests of a married couple and their two children. (I shall refer to the parents as Andrew and Margaret). I am satisfied that Beta was at all material times controlled by Andrew; and that Andrew also made all decisions in relation to the financial affairs of and involving his wife and family.
  3. Andrew is now approaching his mid-fifties. He was engaged in the share-broking industry. In 1964 he married Margaret and within a few months he took his bride to a suburban house he had purchased in his own name. It occupied a select site with marvellous views over one of Australia's finest harbours. It was a most attractive building. It had been built in the mid-nineteenth century and, sited as it was with splendid harbour views, it was in a most favourable location. However, in one respect it was in a most unfortunate position. An adjoining property on the same street frontage not only bounded the homesite on one side but also to the rear or harbour side of the homesite. That might have been of no great consequence but for the fact that the substantial structure erected on that adjoining site was an architectural disaster and structural nightmare. In the words of Andrew it was "very shabby". On any view it was an eyesore. Further, in contrast to the neat, single-storey cottage occupied by Andrew and Margaret, it was a four-storey structure with three residential floors divided into five flats. Fortunately for Margaret and Andrew it was so sited to the side of their home that it did not obstruct their view as it might have done had it been sited to the rear of their home. At the time Andrew purchased the home there was no immediate or short-term prospect of the appearance or condition of the adjoining property being altered because of the security of tenure in the premises of one tenant.
  4. Nonetheless, because of its siting and the manifest deficiencies of its existing development, it was well-recognized by Andrew and by others as a site with substantial development potential: a potential only capable of being realized when the tenure of an elderly lady ceased. She seems to have been a hardy soul. It was said that she had already outlived two owners of the property who had hoped to have the opportunity to develop it. In 1966 when one of those would-be developers died, his widow placed the property on the market for sale.
  5. At that point Andrew and Margaret discussed the possibility of buying the property. They had good reason to do so. They recognized that sooner or later someone would redevelop the site and that when they did so the method of redevelopment could result in the existing family home losing much of its view: a view which contributed greatly to its unique qualities. They recognized that that would particularly be so if development occurred on the lower ground to the rear of their property, between the site of their home and the harbour. Their belief at the time was that any developer would readily obtain permission to redevelop the site to construct five units matching the existing number of units; and with three storeys also matching existing development. They believed that such development would be permitted even if it involved steeply pitched roofs. Any such development could easily be sufficient to cause the disadvantages they feared, particularly if decisions as to siting and elevations were unsympathetic to the interests of the family.
  6. With the immediate objective of protecting the position they had and thereby preserving the market value of their home Andrew contemplated the purchase of the adjoining site. I find that at the time of doing so he was conscious of the quality of the site and of its development potential.
  7. In considering the possibility of purchase it was not essential that the property should be self-sustaining financially, although Andrew was not prepared to over commit himself to secure the long-term advantages he desired for himself as owner of the homesite and, for the entire family, the advantages of living there. In the event, he was able to arrange the purchase at what he considered to be a cheap price. That was in 1966 - some two years after establishment of the family home. With a view to providing Margaret with some resources of her own in time, Andrew arranged for the purchase to be made in her name but with funds provided by himself. Repayment of his advances was secured by an unregistered mortgage.
  8. Late in 1968 the protected tenant died and very shortly thereafter Margaret received notices from the Council calling upon her to effect substantial and extensive repairs and other works on the premises. That notice was issued on 26 November 1968. Andrew promptly took advice from real estate agents and they persuaded the Council on 11 December 1968 to extend time for compliance until 20 February 1969. However, having then considered the prospects in detail, Andrew decided that the required works would not be carried out and that instead Margaret would cease using the building. The Council was advised to that effect by letter of 21 December 1968. Early in 1969 Notices to Quit were given to the remaining tenants and in due course the property fell vacant. On 3 December 1969 the Council gave formal notice condemning the property as being unfit for human habitation.

11. I am satisfied that Andrew had not been idle throughout 1969 but that he had been carefully considering the possibilities open to them. One possibility was to construct a new residence on the site for themselves. Together Andrew and Margaret decided against that course as there was no certainty that they would achieve a more satisfactory home than the one they had. Another possibility was to dispose of the property for development by others. To simply sell without more would have exposed them to the risk of unsympathetic development, with the possible consequence of substantial detriment to the value of the home. Another course involving sale would have been to protect their own interests before selling by first effectively imposing restrictions on future development by an appropriate Deed of Covenant. The third possibility was to effect a development themselves thereby protecting their interest in the homesite. In that event they could rely upon the development as ensuring the protection they sought while they controlled the site, and in addition ensure the running-on of that protection by the provision of effective restrictive covenants. Andrew decided upon the latter course. However, he also decided that, before undertaking that work, the site would be transferred to Beta to be developed by it.

  1. In about 1972 Andrew had conferred with the family accountant about the establishment of an investment vehicle for the family. He was conscious of the desirability of such a course in the way of an "estate planning" project and in recognition of his need to ensure that some resources were available to his family independently of himself should misfortune befall him in the broking business. His extensive experience in that industry made him conscious of prospects for loss as well as gain and had also made him well familiar with investment possibilities, financing techniques and taxation considerations. The accountant made available to the couple a Memorandum of Association and Articles of Association which had initially been prepared for another client, and thereon Andrew and Margaret applied for, and secured, the incorporation of Beta. Following its incorporation, the Articles of Association were amended to render the company better suited to the "estate-planning" purposes of the family.
  2. Having been established, Beta engaged in investment activities and was sufficiently successful to enable Andrew to report to his bankers in March 1970 that, following a market boom:-

"Profits from share trading transactions and capital accounts so far this year are approximately $80,000."

14. As 1970 approached Andrew was also investigating the possibility of Beta entering into primary production. The reason for doing so was explained in the same letter to the bank in the following terms:-

"From the Directors Report you will note that there is no provision for tax, due to the allowance for subscriptions of capital to various oil and mining companies. These deductions were taken in case the Commissioner should treat certain capital gains as profits. If he does so, the capital profits reserve will be largely taxable profit but this will be offset by the deductions referred to above.

Profits from share trading transactions and capital gains so far this year are approximately $80,000. If he applies the same treatment to the capital gains element in this figure, this amount will be taxable. Accordingly the company is in the process of acquiring a pastoral property near...... on which is proposed to spend up to $80,000, which will be almost entirely deductible from taxable income."

  1. Those investigations were to result in a situation whereby, by March 1970, Beta had contracted to purchase an undeveloped interstate property comprising 2,826 acres at a cost of $16 per acre. Andrew proposed that, with the aid of the tax concessions available to developers in such circumstances, Beta would develop 2,000 acres of the property, bringing them to full development at a cost of $56 per acre. Beta purchased the property in April 1970, paying one-half of the purchase price ($22,608) at that date and carrying forward an obligation to pay the balance of the purchase price in two further installments of 25% each ($11,304) to be paid at one and two-year intervals. I am satisfied that Andrew recognized by March 1970 that the farm project would call for the provision of substantial funds.
  2. While that substantial farming venture was in prospect Andrew continued to address the question as to what was to be done with the suburban site which had been available for redevelopment since early 1969. He engaged the services of an architect to advise as to design and development and conferred with an estate agent as to the likely market response of tenants and purchasers to particular developments which were suggested. Enthusiasm was such that by March 1970 he was advising his bankers that development would commence in April and that two of the proposed five town-houses would be sold before the calendar year passed.

17. That was proposed when Andrew sought and gained a commitment of financial support from his bankers. Such support would be necessary to enable him to finance the project. He prepared a detailed written submission dealing with the past successes of Beta and its proposals both in relation to the interstate farming development and the town-house project. In relation to the latter he said (inter alia):-

"At the same time as (the farm project) is being undertaken the company is to build for resale five town houses at........... This project is next door to my own house and was undertaken so that we can control the development next door and so protect our own views and privacy and hence the value of our house.

It is planned that building will begin on April 1st. Development approval has already been granted by Council and working plans are presently before them for building approval.

The land has been valued by the architects @ $56,000 and we are presently waiting for the valuer-general's figure.

The architects estimate the building costs @ $110,000, plus fees of $11,000.

The actual building costs, assuming starting on April 1st, are expected to be drawn as follows:

mid May $10,000

mid June $15,000

mid July $20,000

mid August $15,000

mid September $20,000

end October $ 2,750

6110,000 (sic)

The estimated building time is 30 weeks.

From the above you will calculate that the total value of the project is $171,000 (sic). The selling of the houses has been undertaken by........who have not yet attempted to estimate selling prices. However, I and the architects are confident they will sell for no less than an average of $50,000 each. This is based on the recent sales of lesser quality houses with lesser harbour views for up to $46,000."

  1. In evidence he explained the chosen forms of words as less than apt to describe his intentions. He explained that what he was seeking to do was only to assure his bankers that they would not be troubled by any financial default on the part of Beta. He sought to do that, he said, by assuring the bank that, even if long-term finance should not be available such as would enable Beta to keep all five town-houses indefinitely and rent them out, it would be possible to sell two units by December 1970; and that two sales, such as he contemplated in the letter, would provide all the funds necessary to satisfy the bulk of the claims of the bank. However, in addition, when cross-examined, he acknowledged that, at that point of time, he had foreseen that it could well be necessary or desirable for Beta to sell two of the proposed five units; that he had instructed the same agency as had advised earlier to sell two - any two - of the town-houses; and that doing so would enable Beta to fulfill its aim of holding the remaining units long term.
  2. His optimism as to prospects of sale was well-founded. Later it was encouraged by the circumstance that, once demolition of the old structure was underway, an approach was made by prospective purchasers. Andrew recorded in the Minutes of Beta at 10 November 1970 the willingness of the company to sell the first town-house to the enquirers at $60,000. It was not to be. Despite having initiated negotiations the purchasers did not persist with them. However, it was the next day - 11 November 1970 - that Beta committed itself to a contract for construction of five town-houses at a total cost of $144,970. That contract fairly promptly fell by the wayside and another contract was entered into with another builder in February 1971 at a cost of $156,795. But the record of those two days provides clear evidence that, before Beta made any irrevocable commitment to the construction programme, it already had planned the sale of one specific town-house - and, in consequence, the derivation of profit. That record is also consistent with the representations made to the bank some eight months earlier. The delay in proceeding to construction was not explained.
  3. It was put to Andrew that the design and standard of construction proposed for the units was such that it too indicated that at all times it was his intention that Beta would sell the units; and that if his intention had been a long-term holding of the properties with a view to rental income as he had claimed, the standard of design and construction would have been lower. I accept the genuineness of Andrew's response. In short he said that "economies" such as were suggested by the cross-examiner were false economies; and that well-built and we11-designed premises were as likely to attract top rentals from tenants as much as top prices from purchasers. In any event, there is no evidence before me which would enable me to say that the adoption of other standards for design and construction would have had any effect on either the cost of construction or market values.
  4. The approaches to the bank having been successful, in due course, but only after an unexplained delay of some eight months, construction proceeded to a successful conclusion and the then fully developed site was in a condition which preserved the views of the family home. That had been achieved by constructing two units in a single-storey building to the side of the family home on the approximate site of the original development; and by building three other units in a single-storey construction on the lower ground level on the harbour side of the home, but so constructed with flat roofs as not to impede the view from the residence.
  5. Having obtained a commitment from the bank other relevant developments also occurred. First and foremost was the transfer of title to the development site from Margaret to Beta at a cost of $50,000. (The purchase price was left on loan by Margaret to the company). The price was based upon a valuation provided for stamp duty purposes by the Valuer-General. His valuation was $10,000 in excess of that provided by the estate agent. It was decided not to effect a sale at a higher price -something which would have reduced the profit to Beta and, at the same time, increased the non-taxable gain to Margaret. That was explained as attributable to a concern to avoid liability to any form of gift duty such as might have been levied as such or by way of stamp duty at gift duty rates. The transfer was executed by Margaret on 15 April 1970 and, in due course, was registered. It was not preceded by any written contract or any other agreement which might be construed as having conferred an entitlement recognizable in law or equity for Beta to purchase the property. That being so, it follows that Beta did not "acquire" any interest in the land prior to 15 April 1970. However, I accept that Andrew had been confident at all times that whatever his wishes might be in the matter they would be implemented; and would be implemented upon such terms and at such times as he wished. those expectations were fulfilled on 15 April 1970.
  6. Another relevant matter had been a decision to have strata titles issued - a matter which, in my judgment, was a matter of simple commercial prudence. Further, a decision was taken to establish a legally enforceable basis for protecting the view from the homesite. The latter was achieved by having Beta enter into a restrictive covenant in favour of Andrew and "his heirs, executors, administrators and assigns" as holders of the estate in fee simple in the homesite. The provision of the covenant was made gratuitously on the part of Beta and conferred no advantage on that company.
  7. The evidence did not establish with any precision the date of completion of the construction programme. However, on the basis of the depreciation schedule to the 1973 income tax return of Beta, I find that construction was completed in about November 1972. Following the completion of construction Beta let the properties to tenants and its unchallenged trading accounts show the following results:-

Year Ended 30 June 1973 1974 1975 1976

$ $ $ $

Rents Received 11,936 19,517 5,828 568

Interest 10,062 13,097 2,685 -

Other 5,610 8,344 2,850 94

15,672 21,441 5,535 94

Profit (Loss) for the year (3,736) (1,924) (293) 474

  1. Construction had been financed by a line of commercial bills made available through the bank - a course which was convenient and appropriate for a short-term financial programme calling for progressive draw-down of funds. Andrew's evidence was that once the project was completed the bank was content to continue the facility indefinitely. It follows that at that point Beta was in a position to hold the five properties and let them indefinitely should it choose to do so. It did not follow that course. For some time it continued to finance its holding by commercial bills at costs which increased as market interest rates rose rapidly in the community. The evidence before me shows that, although at March 1970 the bank had been prepared to provide finance at an effective interest rate of eight and one-quarter per cent, that rate was to rise to fifteen and one-half per cent before August 1974.
  2. What also unquestionably happened is that by 30 June 1974 one unit had been sold for $60,000, generating a profit of $13,861. In September 1974, a second sale took place at $61,000. That generated a profit of the order of $12,000. At that point two sales such as had been spoken of as being seen as sufficient to nearly fully satisfy any requirements of the bank should long-term finance not be available, had been effected. The gross proceeds were nearly enough to repay the $130,000 initially advanced by the bank. (In November 1972 there was a further advance of $10,000). But the selling did not stop there. Further sales occurred. Sales at $60,000 in October 1974 and at $62,500 in November 1974 brought "profit" for the year to $35,589; and a fifth and final sale at $65,000 in September 1975 generated a profit for the 1976 year of $17,553. In all, Beta had derived what Andrew described as a "modest profit" of $67,003 - something less than had been foreshadowed in March 1970.
  3. In this case there is no dispute about the amount of the profit or the timing of its derivation. The difference between the parties is that the Applicant contends that no part of the profit derived constitutes assessable income. The Commissioner disagrees. He contends that, in all the circumstances, the profit does constitute assessable income by force of one or other of the two limbs of s.26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") which may be expressed as follows:-

(a) First Limb: "the assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale"; and

(b) Second Limb: "the assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme".

Although the matter was argued under both limbs, I find it sufficient, and therefore appropriate, to resolve the matter by reference only to the second limb of s.26(a).

28. Under cross-examination, when confronted with the terms of the letter which he had written to the bank, Andrew acknowledged that, from the time Beta embarked upon construction of the five town-houses, it was expected that two at least of the units would be sold and that the agents were instructed accordingly; but that it was intended that those remaining would then be held indefinitely as properties to be let at a rent. In my view, it was clear that in those circumstances there was a "profit-making undertaking or scheme". That scheme embraced at least the first two sales which were effected. Further, I am satisfied that the scheme was a profit-making one in that it was intended to produce a return to Beta in excess of the costs of development appropriate to each sale. It follows, that in so far as at least the first two sales are concerned, that the profit actually derived constituted assessable income. To that extent, there is merely an application of the principles expressed in relation to the first limb in Chapman v. Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 C.L.R. 167: a decision sufficiently summarized for present purposes by the headnote, which provided as follows:-

"Where a parcel of land is purchased with the intention of retaining part and selling the remainder at a profit, two independent purposes may be found in the purchase, and the profit on the sale of the remainder may be included in the assessable income of the purchaser."

29. However, the more difficult question is whether in the circumstances of this case the profit arising from further sales constituted assessable income. I am not persuaded that there was any precise plan of action contemplated on behalf of Beta at the commencement of construction such as would enable the Tribunal to be satisfied that, at that point, there was a well-defined plan or concept which involved the prospect of selling or only some identified or identifiable units and retaining the others, whether for occupation as a private residence or as properties to be let. But as Gibbs, J. (as he then was) said in Steinberg v. Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 63; (1975) 134 C.L.R. 640 at p.700:-

"Schemes may be precise or vague; every detail may be arranged in advance, or the working out of the plan may be left for decision in the light of the circumstances as they arise. It is no objection to a plan that it allows room for manoeuvre."

30. In my view, the imprecision and vagueness of which his Honour spoke may as much relate to the identity of what is committed to the scheme as to the ways in which the desired profit is to be sought. On the evidence before me, I am satisfied that not only was there an initial intention to sell two of the town-houses at a profit but also that, as admitted by Andrew in cross-examination, it was at all times recognized that the sale of the others was not only possible but, as represented to the bank, was proposed. Furthermore, I am not satisfied that, at the outset, any decision had been taken not to sell either any or some of the town-houses, however they might be identified.

  1. This is not a case in which a plan - whether well-defined and precise as to extent and method or, on the other hand, vague and ill-defined - was later altered, let alone radically altered by reason of the later development of other circumstances. I am not persuaded that rising interest rates rather than any other consideration prompted any of the sales. Rather it is a case in which the plan was initially imprecise and came to be fully defined by its implementation [cf. Buckland v. F.C.T. (1960) 12 A.T.D. 166 at p. 169]. Viewed in that way my conclusion is that there was a plan which envisaged the construction of five units and the certain sale of some, and the possible sale of all of them, with the intention that, upon each sale - should it be effected - a profit would be derived. In the event, all were sold.
  2. What actually occurred constituted no less the implementation of a "profit-making undertaking or scheme" because, pending the sale of units, they were let. It might have been that, despite an initial intention to sell all five town-houses, in the event some were retained and let to tenants for so long a period that the appropriate finding would be that the scheme originally contemplated had been abandoned (cf. Kratzmann v. F.C.T. 70 A.T.C. 4043). But, in my view, it would be quite unreasonable to draw such an inference upon consideration of the evidence before me.
  3. Furthermore, the motivation which led to the original decision that Margaret would acquire the site in order to preserve the character of the home-site, is not to be attributed to Beta. Nor is that original motivation to be allowed to determine the characterization for income tax purposes of what was done by Beta.
  4. In the circumstances, I am not persuaded that the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections under review were incorrect. As the only other claim - a claim to a deduction for $139 - was abandoned, the determinations of the Commissioner will be affirmed.
