Re David Searle Mitchell and Commissioner of Superannuation No: A91/48 Aat [1992] AATA 285 (22 September 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: DAVID SEARLE MITCHELL

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No: A91/48

AAT No. 8265

Superannuation

COURT

Mr J.A. Kiosoglous(1) (Senior Member), Dr D. Travers(1) (Member) and H. Julian(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - BCC issued in respect of back condition - whether 'real risk' that applicant would retire before the age of 65 years because of back condition - Decision set aside

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.16(4), 154(6)

Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN N93

Re Nicholson and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 7 AAR 371

Re Gelsthorpe and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 9 AAR 361

Re Harris and the Commissioner for Superannuation [1992] AATA 22; (1992) 15 AAR 37

Re Bergin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1989) 19 ALD 279

Perrett v Commissioner for Superannuation (1991) 23 ALD 257

The Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (1989) 10 AAR 288

Re Kirby and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 6173, 4 September 1990)

Re Nix and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 7347, 6 September 1991)

HEARING

ADELAIDE

22:9:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the BCC issued by the delegate of the respondent on 7 March 1991 be revoked.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision made by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ('the Commissioner') on 7 March 1991 (T2/2). The decision was in the following terms:

' I, Ronald Charles WHITHEAR, a delegate of the

Commissioner for Superannuation (the Commissioner),

under subsection 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976

(the Act) CONFIRM the decision taken by a delegate on

9 Feb 1990 to issue, under subsection 16(4) of the

Act, a benefit classification certificate (BCC) in

respect of Dr Mitchell, specifying the condition

"History of Lumbar Disc Removal". '

2. The Tribunal received in evidence the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 (T1-T19), together with one exhibit tendered by the applicant (Exhibit A1). The applicant represented himself and gave oral evidence. The respondent, who was represented by Ms T. Munoz, called two orthopaedic surgeons, Mr R.A.B. Vance and Mr I. Pike.

3. The relevant legislation is contained in sub-s.154(6) and sub-s.16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ('the Act'), and provides as follows:

' 154(6) Applications may be made to the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of

reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or

varied under sub-section (4) and for review of

decisions of the Superannuation Board, or delegates of

the Superannuation Board, made under section 141 of

the superseded Act. '

' 16(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or

reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the

opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or

mental condition or conditions referred to in the

report or reports, will not continue to be an eligible

employee until the person attains his maximum retiring

age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit

classification certificate to that effect, being a

certificate in which the relevant condition or

conditions is or are specified. '

4. The issue before this Tribunal is whether or not there is a real risk that the applicant will retire on invalidity grounds before reaching the maximum retiring age, by reason of, or for a reason connected with a specified condition; the specified condition in this case being lumbar disc removal. The applicant seeks review of the decision on 9 February 1990 to issue a Benefit Classification Certificate ('BCC'), pursuant to sub-s.16(4) of the Act.

5. The following facts are not in dispute and are accepted by the Tribunal. The applicant who is an Aquatic Ecologist, was born in Bulawayo, Rhodesia (now known as Zimbabwe) on 19 March 1935. He attended primary and secondary schools in Zimbabwe before undertaking tertiary studies in South Africa at the Capetown University, where he graduated in science, majoring in botany and zoology. He completed an honours degree in botany and an education diploma at Capetown University before returning to Zimbabwe to take up a teaching post in a secondary school for two years. The applicant was appointed to a teaching post at the University of Zimbabwe, where he taught for about 16 years. During that time he obtained a PhD with the University of London and took two periods of sabbatical leave; the first in England for about 12 months and the second in Australia, for some ten months.

6. While in Australia on sabbatical leave in 1975/76, the applicant obtained employment with the Bureau of Environmental Studies, in what he believed to be the Department of Conservation and Housing. He spent ten months looking at water weed problems in Australia and wrote a major report on aquatic weeds. He stated that soon after his return to Zimbabwe he was advised there was a vacancy in Australia for a principal research scientist with the CSIRO Division of Irrigation Research at Griffith in New South Wales, for which he applied successfully. He stated that he was 42 years of age when he left Zimbabwe with his wife and children to take up the position at Griffith. The applicant is currently the Director of the Murray-Darling Freshwater Research Centre.

7. Before taking up the appointment the applicant was required, pursuant to sub-s.16(2) of the Act, to undergo a medical examination which he did at Andrew Fleming Hospital in Zimbabwe on 23 November 1976. The examination was carried out by the Medical Superintendent of the Hospital, Dr L.J. Binnie. In his report (T5/18-22) Dr Binnie stated that he observed while the applicant had 'some restriction of Lumbar Spine movement', he met the general medical standard. The results of this examination were reviewed by Dr Breitner, a Commonwealth Medical Officer in Sydney, after the applicant had taken up the appointment at Griffith in 1976. The applicant stated he was notified that he met the general medical standard and was eligible for superannuation. Apart from the removal of two lumbar discs in 1965 the applicant was notified that the examination results were fairly normal.

8. The applicant's initial appointment was for three years and he was given a three year visa. When he had been in Australia for about eighteen months a position became available in South Africa. However, after discussing the matter with the appropriate personnel, he declined the South African position. Within four months his position at Griffith was made permanent and on that basis he was granted permanent residence. He obtained Australian citizenship in late 1979.

9. In his oral evidence the applicant stated that he worked at CSIRO as a principal research scientist until about 1981, when he was invited to act as chief of the division. Subsequently, changes were made in the division and it became a centre for irrigation research with the applicant being the officer-in-charge. Later it became the centre for irrigation and fresh water research, the applicant again being in charge. In 1986 he was appointed Director of the new Murray-Darling Fresh Water Research Centre and for about a year he did both jobs. However, in 1989, after he had moved to Albury, New South Wales, he was seconded full time from the CSIRO as Director of the Murray-Darling Fresh Water Research Centre.

10. The only evidence before the Tribunal as to when the applicant became an eligible employee for superannuation for the purposes of the Act is that set out in the delegate's reasons (T2/2). The parties do not dispute the date and the Tribunal is satisfied and finds that the date the applicant joined the superannuation scheme is 5 August 1977.

11. For a variety of reasons not all the medical reports of members who joined the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme ('the CSS') since 1 July 1976 were immediately examined and assessed. It is not disputed by the respondent that the applicant's medical report was in that category; it was not until 9 February 1990 that a delegate of the respondent, having considered the report and such other matters as were considered relevant, issued a BCC in respect of the applicant, specifying the condition 'History of Lumbar Disc Removal' (T3). A delegate of the Commissioner notified the applicant of the issue of the BCC on 9 February 1990 (T6).

12. On 23 February 1990 the applicant wrote to the Commissioner seeking a reconsideration of the delegate's determination (T7). The respondent asked the applicant to set out the reasons for making the request, to which the applicant replied on 4 June 1990 (T8) in the following terms:

' I apologise for not replying earlier to your letters

of the 5 April and the 9 May. Unfortunately since my

return from Africa I have been extremely busy and have

not had many days in my office.

I confirm that I would like to request reconsideration

of the Benefit Classification Certificate which it is

proposed to issue to me. My reasons for making this

request are that since the operation to the lumbar

region of my spine in 1965, I have had only 2

incidents of trouble with my back that necessitated me

being away from work. One was in about 1972 when I

was working for the University of Zimbabwe, and the

other was in about 1982 when I was working for CSIRO

in Griffith. On both occasions I missed less than a

weeks work, though I spent a couple of days in bed.

While the removal of two disks from my spine clearly

means that my back is weaker than a normal back, I

contend that I compensate well for this and there is

no evidence of it interfering with my work, or of any

decline.

It also causes me considerable concern that a

Certificate such as this could be issued some 13 years

after I was accepted into the Superannuation Scheme.

I would also contend that this period of time is much

too long for such an alteration to be made. '

The applicant also advised the respondent of the medical practitioners who had treated him in Australia, namely Dr D. Johnson, of Griffith, and Dr C.K. Ingle, of Thurgoona, New South Wales (T10). The change of doctors occurred in April 1988 when he moved from Griffith to Albury. Both doctors were requested to provide medical reports.

13. In his report dated 6 July 1990 (T14), Dr Johnson stated that the applicant was a patient of his between 1977 and 1988. The report provided as follows:

' I understand that he suffered from sciatica between

1958 and undergoing the removal of the L4,5 and L5,S1

discs in 1965. He then only had relatively minor back

discomfort, which caused one episode of more severe

pain in October 1981, following some unaccustomed

manual work. He was away from the C.S.I.R.O. for a

week and treated with bed rest and analgesics. At

that time, he was somewhat overweight and dieted very

effectively with rapid resolution of significant low

back ache. I understood that he'd only been required

to undertake bed rest twice since his surgery. In

addition, he received some Naproxen and was fitted

with a new corset. An x-ray of his back taken in

November 1981, showed evidence of the past surgery

with mild narrowing of both disc spaces and some

associated mild arthritis of the facetal joints.

Spinal alignment remained normal. He made no further

complaint to me about his back ache, though he

mentioned it was variable at the time of a general

check-up in August 1986. His consultation dates

concerning this matter were as follows:- 19th October

1981, 29th October 1981, 12th November 1981, 4th

December 1981, 27th August 1982, 24th May 1985 and the

1st of August 1986. He was not referred to any

specialist concerning his back, while he remained my

patient. '

14. In his report dated 6 July 1990 (T13), Dr C.K. Ingle stated that the applicant consulted him for backache and seborrhoeric dermatitis on 31 January 1989. He prescribed Naprosyn for the backache but did not refer him to a specialist. Dr Ingle stated further that:

' The patient certainly appears to have no problems or

sequelae associated with his lumbar spine surgery (nor

should he). '

Dr Ingle's report also indicated that the applicant first consulted him on 30 July 1988 for hayfever and the last occasion prior to this report was on 14 June 1990 for 'Pruritus Ani'.

15. In relation to the applicant's sick leave history since his appointment on 10 May 1977, Mr G. Hore, Administrative Officer with the Murray-Darling Freshwater Research Centre, advised the respondent on 6 September 1990 (T15) that supporting certificates for absences in 1981 and 1985 had been destroyed, but it was understood they were in relation to viral infections. A medical certificate covering absences in 1988 for viral infection was attached. It was advised further that during his appointment to CSIRO the applicant had no compensable conditions and had submitted no claims.

16. The applicant was also requested to attend an examination by an orthopaedic surgeon, Mr I. Pike, on 5 November 1990. As a result of this examination Mr Pike provided a medical report to the respondent dated 21 November 1990 (T17). After setting out the applicant's history, both factual and medical, and his present complaints Mr Pike set out his observations of his examination as follows:

' He appeared to be a fit looking fellow but was

slightly over weight. Height 167 cms, weight 84 kgs.

BP 130/80.

Lumbar Spine: The surgical scar was non tender, it

was a midline wound from L5 to S1. His range of

Lumbar movement was restricted. On forward flexion he

could reach only to mid calf. Lateral flexion and

extension were normal.

Both knee jerks were present. Both ankle jerks were

depressed.

Normal light touch sensation in both legs. Slight

weakness of extension of the left great toe.

No X-rays were available for review.

This 55 year old scientist underwent spinal surgery 25

years ago for treatment of Lumbar disc protrusion. He

made an apparently satisfactory recovery from his

surgery and has had three episodes of significant back

pain the last 25 years. At present he has

intermittent low grade symptoms that are well

controlled with exercise, occasional anti-inflammatory

medications.

He is currently involved in administrative and

scientific work which is not taxing in a physical

sense. On the basis of the history given I would

estimate that the risk of recurrence of significant

back pain that would provoke early retirement on

medical grounds prior to the age of 65 would be in the

order of 1% to 10%.

A history of previous disc excision at two levels in

the Lumbar Spine would indicate that degenerative disc

changes are present and after that period of time

almost certainly secondary degeneration of the facet

joints at that level. These degenerative changes will

deteriorate very slowly in time and may provoke a

slight increase in his level of symptoms. Injury or a

high level of physical activity may exacerbate this

deterioration but given the stability of his symptoms

in the last 25 years that risk does seem small.

Generally symptoms of his present intensity are

controlled by anti-inflammatory medications. There

are numerous alternative medications.

I don't think it would be beneficial to list there

(sic) side effects which, if they do occur, are

generally mild.

I do not believe the risk of (the applicant's)

retirement is any greater now than it was prior to

1977.

In summary, I believe that (the applicant's) back

condition is reasonably stable at present and would

represent only a small risk of possible premature

retirement prior to the age of 65.

I trust that I have covered the matters outlined in

your letter. Please let me know if I can be of

further assistance. '

17. The respondent wrote to the applicant on 8 March 1991 (T18) informing him of the result of the reconsideration by the delegate and attaching a copy of the decision and reasons (T2) which is the subject of the appeal to this Tribunal (T1). In arriving at his decision the delegate took into account all the evidence before him, including the report from Mr Pike.

18. Mr Pike and Mr Vance both appeared before the Tribunal and gave oral evidence. Mr Pike stated that following his examination of the applicant on 5 November 1990, he prepared the medical report (T17), a portion of which is set out above. He stated that the history given to him by the applicant was that he has intermittent attacks of back pain, which are aggravated by sitting for long periods, such as when travelling, and for which he takes anti-inflammatory medication. The applicant had told him that the back pain did not cause him to lose any time from work and he kept fit by walking every day for half an hour to an hour. Mr Pike stated that the applicant does not have a major disability, is not prevented from doing what he wants to do and is not limited in his day to day activities at home.

19. Mr Pike estimated the risk of a recurrence of the applicant's back pain that would provoke early retirement on medical grounds prior to the age of 65 years would be in the order of 1-10%. He regarded the risk as small but identifiable and believed the applicant's symptoms to be very low grade. He stated further that he would be surprised if the applicant had to retire early due to his back; he did not think the risk was very great and certainly not likely to cause the applicant to retire prematurely. Mr Pike stated that the applicant has gone through a sequence of changes where his two inter-vertebral discs have bulged and generally that is followed by a sequence of degenerative changes in the disc and secondary degenerative changes in the surrounding bone and joint. He was of the view that the applicant has degenerative disc disease. He stated further that the applicant's symptoms are well controlled with exercise and anti-inflammatory medication. Mr Pike arrived at his conclusions as a result of the history given to him by the applicant and his examination of the applicant. He had not seen an x-ray of the applicant's spine nor had he taken any.

20. Mr Pike stated that he was aware the applicant was taking Naproxen as a preventative measure and he had no objection to this as long as the applicant understood the necessary precautions to be taken and the side effects. He would not prescribe a long course but would recommend the use of Naproxen on an intermittent basis: this is the applicant's current practice.

21. Mr R.A.B. Vance stated in his medical report dated 19 September 1991 (T19) that he examined the applicant on 16 September 1991 at the request of the respondent following his appeal to this Tribunal. In part Mr Vance's report read as follows :

' ON EXAMINATION

He is short and thick set. His spine shows a

restriction of all movements and he experienced no

pain. He has to a large extent avoided flexion

movement over the years as I have pointed out.

Both knee jerks were present but both ankle jerks were

absent. Dorsiflexion of his L big toe was restricted

a little but the muscle was actively functioning. He

had no sensory loss.

OPINION

Dr Mitchell injured his lower back many years ago and

subsequently underwent a laminectomy and excision of

two lower lumbar discs. Since then he has had several

acute low back episodes which settled rapidly. He

does not believe there has been any significant change

in his low back condition over the last 10-15 years.

I believe he does have a small, but identifiable risk

of retiring before the age of 65 years for reason of

his lower back condition. I would assess the risk at

between 1-10%. The most appropriate description of

his condition is that he has suffered from post

laminectomy low back symptoms and restrictions. The

condition could deteriorate in the future. This could

lead to increasing frequency of these episodes and the

possible necessity for him to retire early. There are

no particular circumstances which may exacerbate this

deterioration. '

22. In his oral evidence Mr Vance stated that he believed the applicant had a small, but identifiable, risk of retiring before the age of 65 years for reasons of his lower back condition. He considered that the applicant had a possible increased risk between 1-10% given the history of his back condition and this risk was at the lower rather than the upper end of the scale. He stated he was not surprised that occurrences of acute trauma were infrequent in the applicant's case or that there was rapid recovery. This is quite normal for people who have had discs removed and the applicant had a healthy attitude towards his back condition. As far as Mr Vance was concerned the applicant has not had further sciatic pain following the spinal surgery, although the effect of the original sciatic pressure from the disc bulge or protrusion must have affected the muscles to the applicant's left big toe. When he saw the applicant he observed a restriction of dorsa flexion of that toe which he regarded as not uncommon after sciatic symptoms. On examination he found that the applicant's spine showed a restriction of all movements but no pain was experienced.

23. The applicant submitted that the assessment of real risk in his case is very small, of the order of 1-10%. He supported this by submitting that since the removal of the discs there have only been two incidents which resulted in him being absent from work, namely in 1974 and in 1981. In each case it took him only about a week to recover. He attributed these more to muscular spasm rather than evidence of further disc degeneration. He submitted that he has adjusted his lifestyle in a preventative way and hence is of the view that he is probably less at risk than someone who thinks they have a normal back. The applicant admitted it is his intention to retire earlier than 65 years of age, but that this was not because of a problem with his back, rather his wish to pursue other fields of interest, including the writing of a book on areas of his expertise, namely water plants. He does not want his intention to retire at an earlier date to be associated with the back problem.

24. In deciding whether the BCC should be revoked, the Tribunal needs to consider whether or not there was a 'real risk' of the applicant being unable to continue as an employee until he reached the maximum retiring age. Some indication of what is meant by 'real risk' can be found by reference to the facts in Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN N93. In that case Mr Linden was found to be suffering from 'labile hypertension' and he had significantly reduced the risk of developing a permanent hypertensive condition by bringing his weight problem under control. Notwithstanding this, the Tribunal considered that it was too early to form any confident prognosis that a permanent hypertensive condition would not develop. It concluded that there was a real risk that he (Linden) would not continue to be an eligible employee to his maximum retiring age. In Re Linden the Tribunal in considering the meaning of the word 'likely' stated:

' When, in sub-ss (4) and (8) of s 16, parliament has

referred to a person who is not "likely", by reason of

or for a reason connected with some physical or mental

condition, to continue to be an eligible employee

until attaining maximum retiring age, it seems to us

that, in the context, parliament must be referring to

conditions which give rise to a "real risk" and not

merely to conditions which create a "probability" or

"better than even chance" that the person will not

continue until attaining maximum retiring age.

There is no question in our minds that where the

medical evidence establishes a probability or "better

than even chance" that a particular condition will

prove disabling before a person attains maximum

retiring age, the Commissioner is entitled, if not

obliged, to issue a certificate. But we are unable to

accept that the section limits the Commissioner to

issuing a certificate only where such a degree of

certainty is established. The general medical

standards developed for the guidance of Commonwealth

medical officers, which were designed at establishing

acceptable levels of risk, are as we understand them,

consistent with that broader view. Failure to meet

the general medical standards may not render it

"probable" that a person will not continue in

employment until maximum retiring age. But such

failure does suggest that, if the condition persists

early death or retirement on grounds of invalidity is

an event which might well happen.'

25. In Re Nicholson and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 7 AAR 371 at 373 the Tribunal considered the meaning of 'real risk' as follows:

' Both Mr Lorkin and Mr Whithear drew a distinction

between a bare possibility and a real risk. We

consider that that is a proper distinction to make.

We were invited to examine the meanings of the

individual words "real" and "risk" given in various

dictionaries, and have done so; however, we are

satisfied that the mere conjunction of any of the

meanings of "real" with any of the meanings of "risk"

does not capture the essence of the phrase "real

risk", which is a phrase having connotations of its

own distinct from the dictionary meanings of the two

words comprising it. In the phrase "risk" refers to a

chance that something detrimental will occur. There

is a "real risk" where a factual situation exists

which has inherent in it a particular potential to

result in that detrimental occurrence. Usually the

phrase is used in relation to the process of making an

assessment of whether precautions are warranted to

prevent either the occurrence of the detrimental thing

or the consequences of it occurring. We are satisfied

that in the context of the Act s 16 is intended to

provide that a benefit classification certificate

should be issued where an eligible employee is

suffering from an identified condition which has a

particular potential to result in his or her dying, or

becoming so incapacitated as to be unable to continue

in employment, before reaching maximum retiring age. '

26. In Re Gelsthorpe and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 9 AAR 361 at 377 the Tribunal, in commenting on the above extract from Re Nicholson, stated as follows:

' This Tribunal finds some difficulty in applying only

the test which is set out in Re Nicholson. The

Tribunal does not consider that the words "particular

potential" for a condition to result in a detrimental

occurrence, are a substitution for the words "real

risk". The words "particular potential" in turn would

require some definition. Does the word "particular"

mean "specific" or does it mean "something special"?

It could be said that any mental or physical condition

has inherent within it a particular potential to

result in a detrimental occurrence, and the word

"particular" says nothing about the gravamen or the

extent of that potential. This Tribunal considers it

preferable to view the words "real risk" not as a term

of art but rather to regard them as a matter of common

parlance. The word "risk" refers to a chance or

hazard (see Macquarie Dictionary) and the word "real"

in that context would mean something other than

nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful. Therefore

a "real risk" would be a chance which is more than

nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful but less

than a probability; how much less than a probability

it is not possible to specify. '

27. In Re Harris and the Commissioner for Superannuation [1992] AATA 22; (1992) 15 AAR 37 at 46 the Tribunal stated that Re Gelsthorpe provided four principles which are of assistance in deciding when a condition constitutes a 'real risk'. These are as follows:

' (a) The term "real risk" is not a term of art and

should be interpreted in accordance with the

dictionary meaning.

(b) "Real risk" refers to a chance of an occurrence

which is real and is more than nominal,

imaginary, fictitious or fanciful but less than

a probability.

(c) "Real risk" requires consideration and assessment

of the characteristics of the person, the

nature, gravemen and characteristics of the

condition in the context of employment to

maximum retirement age.

(d) Although percentages may sometimes assist in

assessing the seriousness or otherwise of

certain conditions in relation to employment,

"real risk" is not a quantitative assessment

such that it can be reduced to percentage terms

in order to determine its existence. '

28. The Tribunal, as well as giving consideration to the authorities already mentioned, also had regard to the cases of Re Bergin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1989) 19 ALD 279; Perrett v Commissioner for Superannuation (1991) 23 ALD 257; The Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (unreported decision No. 2886, No. ACT G68 of 1986, dated 14 April 1988); Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (1989) 10 AAR 288; Re Kirby and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 6173, 4 September 1990); and Re Nix and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 7347, dated 6 September 1991).

29. In arriving at its conclusion the Tribunal has taken into account the evidence as a whole, including the authorities and submissions of both parties. The Tribunal is satisfied and finds that the applicant's medical condition has been in existence since 1965 and is hence of long standing. In that time he has experienced pain on two occasions which necessitated taking only a few days off work. On other occasions the applicant has thwarted the problem by taking Naproxen as a preventative measure. The applicant has also been very careful with his diet and exercise, in the form of walking.

30. The applicant's general practitioner between 1977 and 1988 was Dr D. Johnson, who stated in his report that the applicant suffered one episode of severe back pain in October 1981, as a result of which he was away from work for a week. Dr Johnson did not refer the applicant to a specialist during the time he was under his care. Likewise, Dr C.K. Ingle, the applicant's next general practitioner, stated that the applicant consulted him for back ache on 31 January 1989, for which he prescribed Naprosyn but did not refer the applicant to a specialist. Dr Ingle was satisfied that there appeared to be no problem or sequelae associated with the lumbar spine, nor should there be.

31. The two specialist doctors, Mr Pike and Mr Vance, both agree there is a small but identifiable risk. The Tribunal is satisfied and finds this to be the case. Mr Vance stated in his oral evidence that there was a small risk of the applicant retiring before the age of 65 years for reasons of his lower back condition. As far as he was concerned the applicant has not had sciatic pain since the spinal surgery. He stated further that retirement before 65 need not necessarily be as a result of a back problem. He was not surprised with the applicant's infrequent occurrences of back pain and his rapid recovery, due to his careful attitude towards his back condition.

32. In his medical report dated 21 November 1990 (T17) Mr Pike stated that he believed the applicant's back condition is reasonably stable at present and would only subject him to a small risk of possible premature retirement prior to the age of 65. During his oral evidence he stated that the applicant does not have a major disability and is not prevented from doing the things that he wants to do. Mr Pike also estimated the applicant's risk of recurrence as very low and he would be surprised if the applicant retired early due to his back as he did not think there was a very great risk and it was most unlikely to cause the applicant to retire prematurely. He stated further that the applicant's symptoms are well controlled. He was of the view that the applicant would not retire early due to a back problem. Whilst Mr Vance was not prepared to go so far, the Tribunal is satisfied that his evidence supports that of Mr Pike, in respect of the applicant's symptoms and condition.

33. The Tribunal is satisfied that although there is a risk of the applicant retiring prematurely because of his back condition, it is not a 'real' one. Guided by the medical evidence, the relevant authorities and the evidence of the applicant, the Tribunal is satisfied and so finds that the applicant's back condition will not prevent him from continuing in his employment until the age of 65 years.

34. Accordingly, pursuant to sub-s.43(1)(b) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the BCC issued by the delegate of the respondent on 7 March 1991 be revoked.
