Income Tax - tax avoidance - income splitting - accountant carrying on practice in partnership commencing to carry on practice as employee of a unit trust - arrangement to divert accountant's income through unit trust to family members - whether arrangement to alter incidence of income tax - whether arrangement void as against the Commissioner under s260.

REASONS FOR DECISION

MESSRS. K.P. BRADY and J.E. STEWART (SENIOR MEMBERS): 23RD APRIL, 1987

  1. The question requiring our decision in this application is whether an arrangement entered into by a practising chartered accountant and others is struck down by the provisions contained in sec. 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. The year of income in issue is that ended 30th June 1981, and it was in that year that the applicant and his partners sold their accountancy practice to a nominee company acting as a trustee of a unit trust. That sale occurred on 31st August 1980, and it seems that thereafter the business was conducted by the nominee company in its capacity as trustee, with the units in the trust being held by the family trusts of the erstwhile partners.
  2. In raising the applicant's 1981 assessment, the Commissioner took the view that the agreements and transactions pertaining to the above transfer of ownership represented an arrangement caught by s.260 and that, accordingly, the terms and conditions contained therein were void as against himself. On that basis, he, after increasing the net income of the trust by the amount of salaries and superannuation contributions made either to or on behalf of the erstwhile proprietors, shared that adjusted surplus equally between the applicant and his former partners. The effect was to increase the applicant's taxable income by an amount of $22,097. The applicant objected against the Commissioner's action and, upon the disallowance of same, requested that the matter be referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board concluded its hearing of the matter prior to 1st July 1986, and reserved its decision. Under the provisions of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, the functions of Taxation Boards of Review were transferred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal with effect from 1st July 1986. As a consequence, the decision in the instant case, together with reasons in writing for the decision, are given pursuant to the provisions of s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

3. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by counsel and gave evidence under oath. The Commissioner was represented by senior counsel. The applicant was the only witness called by his counsel; the Commissioner's counsel did not call any witnesses.

  1. The facts in the instant case bear a close resemblance to the facts in Case WT85/951 which decision has been delivered contemporaneously with the present one. We shall endeavour to avoid repetition as far as possible.
  2. It is well settled that cases requiring a decision under sec. 260 must be determined on their own particular facts, (see FC of T v Gulland 85 ATC 4765 at p 4796) and so it is necessary to go into the fact situation in some detail. Before embarking on that exercise however, it is well to make the point that the applicant's counsel conceded that there was an arrangement entered into by his client although, as was to be expected, that concession was quickly qualified by the submission that the arrangement did not fall within the ambit of sec. 260. Also it was common ground that the arrangement was made prior to 27th May 1981 so there was no question that Part IVA - Schemes to Reduce Income Tax, might have application.

6. We turn first to examine the modus operandi of the practice prior to its sale to the unit trust on 31st August 1980. Up to that time the practice had been conducted by a series of partnerships of which the applicant had been a member since approximately 1965. In 1977 the partnership changed its name to coincide with the surnames of the then three partners and that same composition of the firm continued until 30th June 1980 when a new partner was admitted with effect from 1st July 1980. It seems however that no further change was made to the firm name, because a name comprising the surnames of the three erstwhile partners had some time previous been registered as a business name with the Corporate Affairs Office. Up to 30th June 1980 the three partners shared profits equally. For the last year of income before the sale of the practice took place, i.e. the year ended 30th June 1980, the applicant's share of the firm's net income was $25,320. That share however was after deduction of certain outgoings made in favour of three related trusts, all of which were involved in the provision of either assets or services to the partnership. Whilst the activities and functions of those entities are not under challenge, it is, we believe, necessary to examine those inter-related transactions in order to be more closely acquainted with all of the circumstances surrounding the arrangement entered into by the applicant and his fellow partners.

7. Accordingly, we turn to consider the first of those associated entities which performed the function of a service trust. The trustee was a nominee company, the directors and shareholders of which were the three partners. It seems that the activities of the trust comprised the provision of typing and photocopying services to the practice and that the methodology applied in determining the charge-out rates from which its profit was derived had been modelled upon those which were used in the well known case of FC of T v Phillips 78 ATC. 4361. 9. The ownership of the office building which was used to house the principals and staff of the accountancy practice, as well as other tenants, also requires some comment. For a period of several years until its sale in August 1980, that property had been registered in the name of yet another company under the control and direction of the three partners; it seems that the company held that asset on behalf of certain trusts and individuals. Whilst the beneficial owners in that arrangement changed from time to time, it seems that those participating over the final years of the tenure were the individual family trusts of the three partners. Although the financial statements for this venture were not tabled, it appears that the original acquisition and improvements thereto were funded in the main by borrowed moneys. No doubt that highly geared situation explains the share of losses brought to account in the records of the applicant's family trust and which were offset against income derived from other sources. That share of loss for the 1980 year was a figure of $3444. In concluding this aspect, we would add that the introduction of a new partner into the firm on 1st July 1980 and his inability to fund the purchase of an equal interest in the office building because of a marked increase in its value, motivated its sale in August 1980. It was considered by the partners, so the evidence ran, that an unbalanced ownership in the property might cause inequities in the new partnership (now comprising four partners), and thus the decision was taken to realise the asset. Subsequent to that disposal, the unit trust, which came into operation on 31st August 1980 for the express purpose of taking over the accountancy practice, entered into an agreement with the new owner to lease back that portion of the premises previously used by the partnership and its associated entities.

10. It is both convenient and relevant that we pause here to consider the composition and operations of the two trusts established for the benefit of members of the applicant's family, and used to derive income from a variety of sources. It seems that a need for the second family trust arose from a break-down in the applicant's first marriage. Thus we find that the original family trust was operational until the end of the 1981 year of income but its activities were then wound down following upon the divorce of the applicant from his first wife who, together with their four children, represented the beneficiaries nominated in the trust deed. The second trust, although established in June 1980, did not appear to fully carry out its functions until the 1982 year. The beneficiaries comprised the applicant, his children, and his new wife, the marriage taking place sometime during the 1981 financial year.

13. There appears little doubt that the issue by both the Chartered Institute of Accountants and the Australian Society of Accountants of ethical rulings on the use of incorporated companies for operating accounting practices initiated the notion of such a change. It seems that subject to compliance with certain requirements, the Chartered Institute would consent to the incorporation and use of a company in the rendering of services ordinarily provided by a member in practice and that such a function could be undertaken by a company either as principal or as trustee. Although it was intended that the new arrangement for conducting the practice would commence on 1st July 1980 to coincide with the introduction of a fourth partner, it seems that the firm encountered delays in obtaining the consent of the Chartered Institute and that approval was not forthcoming until 5th August 1980. Even then, the approval received was subject to making an amendment to the company's memorandum of association. Notwithstanding that the required alteration was not effected until some time later, the recently formed company, acting as the trustee of a new unit trust, acquired on 31st August 1980, the following assets from the partnership:-

Book Debts $134,218

Additionally, it seems that the functions previously performed by the Service Trust were merged with those taken over from the partnership, and that the combined operation was, from that day forth, conducted by the trustee company for and on behalf of the unit trust. The involvement of parties outside the accountancy practice caused a delay in transferring the activities of the Computer Trust, and it was not until October of the following year that such a consolidation took place. It seems that the partnership was dissolved on 31st August 1980.

An equal number of units in the trust which was to be used in the conduct of the practice was held by each of the trusts settled for the benefit of the families of those same parties. The additional partner introduced into the firm on 1st July 1980 also became a director and shareholder, and his family trust was issued a like number of units in the trading trust. Contemporaneously with the change of ownership the four partners, together with the former employees of the partnership and service trust and numbering in all about 26 became employees of the unit trust. For the remainder of the 1981 financial year, a salary at the annual rate of $17,000 was paid to each of the four directors, and it seems that the level of such remuneration was increased to $25,000 for the 1982 year and remained at that figure until 1985 when it was increased to $40,000.

16. Copies of the deed dated 1st May 1981 establishing the superannuation fund together with two deeds of variation were tendered at the hearing. The scheme of the fund was internal by choice and designed to comply with the criteria set out in sec. 23F of the Income Tax Assessment Act. It appears that the original intention was the provision of pension benefits to retiring members and that the amounts so payable were to be calculated at 70% of the person's average salary over the last three years of service. In later years, differing categories of membership were brought into existence and it seems that employees of the firm were admitted to the fund in the second year of its operation, i.e. in the year ended 30th June 1982. In our understanding of the evidence, all amounts contributed from that time onward were determined on the basis of furnishing retiring members with a lump sum payment. The company which controlled the operations of the applicant's second family trust and the Computer Trust also acted as trustee of the fund.

17. 19. It seems to us that the business which the partners sold on 31st August 1980 could be described as a medium-size accounting practice. According to the applicant's evidence, the number of employees at that time including the four directors totalled twenty-six and all but four or five support staff were engaged in attending to the demands of clients, and that the time spent on those tasks was costed and charged out to those concerned. Furthermore, it seems that the applicant's contribution to gross fees was to the order of 9% and that such a relatively low percentage resulted in part from his position as office administrator and partly from his involvement on an honorary basis with a number of charitable and professional organisations. The above evidence was not disputed and we accept it without hesitation.

20. We turn now to an examination of two of the many documents adduced in evidence before us viz. the Memorandum of Association of the trustee company, and the Unit Trust Deed. The objects of the company were expressed in its Memorandum of Association to be as follows:-

As indicated earlier in these reasons, the four erstwhile partners were its directors and shareholders.

21. The unit trust deed was of a kind which is in common use and was created by a person called a Founder. His name would suggest that he was a relative of the applicant, and he settled on the trust the sum of $51. The schedule to the deed specified the termination date to be 30th June 2059 in order that the perpetuity period might be no longer than the period of BO years as required by the State's relevant statute. As the trust was established to buy a business with assets totalling $187,218, the indebtedness would normally be met from loan moneys or remain in the books of account as a debt due to the partners. It was the latter situation which in fact appertained. Under the terms of the deed the trustee company was given very wide powers indicating the power to invest moneys and use them as if it were the absolute owner thereof. As regards the distribution of income. it had no discretion but was obliged to apply the whole of the net income of a given accounting period for the benefit of the unit holders in proportion to the number of units respectively held by them at the end of that financial period It will be recalled that all units in the trust were held by family trusts of the erstwhile partners. The deed gave to the unit holders the power to remove the trustee and appoint a substitute, or appoint an additional trustee or trustees. As it was the unit holders who had an interest in all the property and income of the trust as soon as it was derived, it seems to us that the control of the unit trust was structured in such a way that the full control of the business including control of its finances remained with the erstwhile partners.

Thus the evidence of the applicant was that he viewed the arrangement as an ordinary business arrangement possessing normal commercial purposes. In answer to questions put to him by his own counsel and in cross examination, he denied that the arrangement evidenced a tax avoidance purpose.

23. It is appropriate that we now turn to the relevant law. As regards an arrangement such as this entered into prior to 28th May 1981 it is necessary to examine sec 260, the then main anti-avoidance provision of the Act, to see whether or not it (the arrangement) is caught by its terms. The section provides as follows:-

"Every contract, agreement, or arrangement made or entered into, orally or in writing, whether before or after the commencement of this Act, shall so far as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly -

(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;

(b) relieving any person from liability to pay any income tax or make any return;
  • (c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or liability imposed on any person by this Act; or


(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any respect,

be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, or in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but without prejudice to such validity as it may have in any other respect or for any other purpose."

24. The basic test, we consider, for determining whether an arrangement is one to which the section applies is the predication test employed by Lord Denning in giving judgment for the Privy Council in Newton v FC of T [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1957-58) 98 CLR 1. At page 8 he said:-

".... the section is not concerned with the motives of individuals. It is not concerned with their desire to avoid tax, but only with the means which they employ to do it. It affects every 'contract, agreement or arrangement' (which their Lordships will henceforward refer to compendiously as 'arrangement') which has the purpose or effect of avoiding tax. In applying the section you must, by the very words of it, look, at the arrangement itself and see which is its effect - which it does - irrespective of the motives of the persons who made it In order to bring the arrangement within the section you must be able to predicate - by looking at the overt acts by which it was implemented - that it was implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax. If you cannot so predicate, but have to acknowledge that the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax, then the arrangement does not come within the section."

The learned judge went on to say at p 10:-

"It is clear from this analysis the avoidance of tax was not the sole purpose or effect of the arrangement.......But nevertheless the section can still work if one of the purposes or effects was to avoid liability for tax.... This seems to their Lordships to import that it need not be the sole purpose."

Thus two important principles emerge from the case:-

first, one must look at the overt acts of the taxpayer which evidence the arrangement under consideration, and see whether they indicate that the arrangement was implemented so as to avoid tax, and

secondly, the avoidance of tax need only be a main purpose (according to the way Lord Denning's words on this aspect have been interpreted by the High Court, see FC of T v Gulland (supra) at p 4772).

25. The case of DFC of T v Purcell [1921] HCA 59; (1920-21) 29 CLR 464 is the one normally cited as the first of a long line of cases to show that in the absence of special circumstances a unit trust or discretionary trust will not be caught by sec. 260. Our colleague, Mr Trowse has set out the basic facts of that case and it is not therefore necessary for us to repeat them. In our view however the case, which in fact dealt with sec. 53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915, the predecessor of sec. 260, is authority only for the proposition that where there is an arrangement leading to an alteration in the incidence of tax but the tax falls precisely as the Act intended, such arrangement is not caught by the section. It is well to note that in an earlier case on the same facts, viz Purcell v DFC of T [1920] HCA 46; (1920-21) 28 CLR 77, Isaacs J (who did not sit in the later court) criticized the trust instrument executed by Mr. Purcell describing it as "the most extraordinary production of the kind that within my judicial and professional experience has ever been brought under the consideration of a Court of Justice." And of course it is true to say that the concept of the modern unit trust could never have been in the mind of the High Court sitting in 1920. We do not question, and indeed we cannot question the authority of Purcell, but we believe that it cannot be cited to support any wider proposition of law than the one stated above. Certainly we do not regard the decision as persuasive as regards the issues we are now considering because of the material differences in the respective fact situations.

26. The case of Hancock v FC of T [1961] HCA 90; (1962-63) 108 CLR 258 which followed soon after Newton was a case where unlike Purcell it was held that special circumstances existed as evidenced by the overt acts of the parties so as to indicate that the arrangement was of a kind that should be characterized as a means of avoiding tax. Hancock was one of a number of dividend stripping cases decided in favour of the Commissioner in the wake of Newton but it is helpful to us because of various statements made on the application of sec. 260 by Mr Justice Kitto when handing down his judgment. At p 283 he stated:-

"If those [overt] acts are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary dealing, such as business or family dealing without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax, the arrangement does not come within the section."

A few sentences later he added:-

"If an arrangement has been a means for the avoidance of tax, the fact (if it be a fact) that it has been a means to other ends as well does not prevent the application of s 260."

27. The latter point was examined in greater detail in the Full High Court decision in Peate v FC of T (1963-64) 111 CLR 443. As with the fact situation before us, that case was also concerned with a group of entities. The taxpayer there was a doctor in a partnership encompassing eight doctors. In order to split his income, an arrangement was entered into whereby the partnership was dissolved and a structure of companies and trusts was formed which purchased the partnership's business and equipment. The main new entity was a company called Westbank Pty Ltd It took over the role previously performed by the partnership and supplied medical services to the erstwhile partnership's patients and contracted with family companies established for the various doctors for the supply of their services. The family companies paid the doctors' salaries. In this way the proceeds from fees were divided as they had been under the partnership but were now split amongst the various doctors' families. The Court unanimously held that the arrangement was caught by sec. 260. Mr Justice Kitto who delivered the principal judgment stated at p 469:-

"The arrangement in the present case, considered objectively as is thus required, may well seem to be characterized by several purposes and effects, some of them unconnected with taxation, including the protection of individual members of the group against liability for negligence; the making of superannuation provision for employees, including doctors employed to assist the group; the better organisation of the group's activities and particularly its methods of accounting; and the making of provision for the doctors' families. (All of these purposes, indeed, the appellant swore were actually contemplated in the formation of the plan). But the question remains, whether the overt acts that were done under the plan are fairly explicable without an inference being drawn that tax-avoidance is a purpose of the arrangement as a whole............

An understandable purpose of providing for the doctors' families, and doing so quite honestly, is perfectly evident; but what is equally evident is a purpose of doing so by a method which will divert income away from the participating doctors to or for the benefit of their families, to the end that a substantial part of the tax might be avoided which would have been incurred if the income had first been derived by the doctors and then applied by them for the benefit of their families.

The case therefore falls, plainly as I venture to think, within the application of s 260."

It is interesting that many of the stated purposes for the structural re-organisation of the medical practice in Peate are similar to those of the applicant in the case before us. Basically Kitto J considered that the plan to achieve the doctors' purposes was too elaborate in the absence of imputing to it a tax avoidance practice. In a separate judgment, Windeyer J. stated his agreement with the views of his fellow Justices but based his decision on the unusual nature of the plan's operation, for example he considered it unusual for Westbank to act as a provider of medical services and he thought it most odd for a doctor to work for his family company and then be hired out to another company. Upon the taxpayer's appeal to the Privy Council, that Court concurred with the judgment of the High Court.

28. A New Zealand decision of relevance to the matters in issue before us is the case of Manqin v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (N.Z.) 70 ATC 6001. There again special circumstances were said to appertain calling for the implementation of sec. 108, the New Zealand equivalent of sec 260. Mr Mangin was a farmer and he leased for £3 an acre a wheat-producing section of his farm to trustees for one year. The taxpayer was employed by the trustees to sow, harvest and sell the crop. They then held the net proceeds on trust for the taxpayer's wife and children. Thus part of the taxpayer's total income became the income of his family who could claim allowances and reduced rates of tax which led to less tax being paid on the profits of the total acreage. The Commissioner made amended assessments on the taxpayer to restore his income tax liability to what it would have been but for the "paddock trusts". On one view it might be said that the transactions were capable of explanation by reference to ordinary family dealing. The Privy Council held however on the basis of the predication test enunciated in Newton, that the short period of the lease and the unrealistically low rental pointed to the fact that the arrangement was a scheme devised for the sole purpose, or at least a principal purpose, of escaping liability for tax on a substantial part of the taxpayer's income. Thus the arrangement was caught by sec. 108. Any reference to Mangin is not complete without mention being made of the strong dissent of Lord Wilberforce who was not persuaded that the special circumstances adverted to by the majority indicated that the arrangement should necessarily be labelled as a means to avoid tax. His Lordship's conclusion, citing Purcell as authority, was that the arrangement amounted to no more than a disposition of an income-earning asset, with the liability to assessment shifting to the members of the taxpayer's family.

29. In the same year, 1971, the High Court handed down its decision in Hollyock v FC of T 71 ATC 4202. There, the taxpayer was a pharmacist. He had for some years carried on the business on his own. He decided to sell half the business to his wife at valuation. To that end, he and his wife executed a deed which contained a declaration that he held the business, its assets, and income on trust for himself and his wife in equal shares. It also contained the unusual provision that the taxpayer covenanted not to demand the price from his wife during her life unless she disposed of her interest. The taxpayer retained the sole right to carry on the business whether alone or with a partner, or by means of a manager. For the years under review the taxpayer was treated by the Commissioner as having derived the whole of the net income of the pharmacy business. In support of his assessment, the Commissioner claimed, first, that the deed was illegal and void, being designed to circumvent the Pharmacy and Poisons Act, secondly even if valid, the deed did not effectively divest the taxpayer of his right to receive the income from the pharmacy business, and thirdly, and in any event, sec. 260 operated so as to avoid the deed. The High Court, per Gibbs J (as he was then), agreed that the arrangement was caught by sec 260 and relevant for present purposes is his finding that because of the requirements of the Pharmacy Act, the business could only be lawfully carried on by the taxpayer, and thus it was unlawful for his wife to be part of the business. Accordingly there was a commercial unreality in giving a half-share in the practice to a wife who could not work. Also the learned Judge found that the trust instrument contained wide and unusual powers. Thus, whilst he found that the arrangement considered objectively had several purposes, including provision for the wife and avoidance of death duty, he held that the avoidance of tax was an essential purpose.

30. In giving judgment, the learned Judge drew a distinction between the fact situation which appertained in the case before him and the facts of Purcell where it will be recalled sec 260 was said to have no application. At p 4206 he said:-

"Finally, it is not possible to regard this case as one in which the appellant has simply disposed of income-producing property. It is true that a considerable quantity of trading stock was included in the assets of the business, but the income derived by the appellant was derived from carrying on the business, and the trading stock yielded income only because the business was carried on."

Thus it was significant in Gibbs J's view that it was a business that was the subject matter of the.trust, and not simply an income earning asset.

31. Earlier in his judgment, the learned Judge took issue with an example given by Lord Denning in Newton of an arrangement that could not be regarded as one to avoid tax, viz. a declaration of trust made by a father in favour of his wife arid daughter.

Gibbs J. put his position as follows:-

"If this were intended as a universal proposition it would be too wide, for it must depend on all the circumstances whether one can predicate of a trust that it was created to avoid tax, but it cannot be supposed that it was so intended. Their Lordships had already remarked (at p. 465) that the word 'arrangement' in sec. 260 comprehends 'not only the initial plan but also all the transactions by which it is carried into effect - all the transactions, that is, which have the effect of avoiding taxation, be they conveyances, transfers or anything else.' (See also Bell v F.C. of T. [1952] HCA 34; (1951-1953) 87 C.L.R. 548 at p. 573). Declarations of trust which have the purpose of avoiding taxation must be within the scope of the section, and Mancrin v IR Commr. 70 ATC 6001, (1971) 2 WLR 39 affords a recent example of trusts made by a father in favour of his wife and children that were struck down by the New Zealand section equivalent to sec 260." (emphasis added)

32. Lastly, Hollyock is of interest as regards questions raised by Gibbs J. on the general issue of assignment of income, and indeed he seemed to foreshadow the sorts of matters that would be examined a decade later by the High Court in FC of T v Everett 80 ATC 4076. Sec. 260, of course, was not argued in that case and we make mention of it at this stage only to express agreement with the view put forward by our colleague, Mr Trowse, that the shares of the net income passing to the unit trust holders cannot be regarded as income arising from the rendering of personal services by the applicant and his fellow directors, so as to remain assessable income in their hands. However the manner in which the taxpayer's accounting practice was conducted did not differ in material respects (as far as we can gauge) from the practice of the accountants in Case WT85/951, and accordingly we affirm the same sorts of reservations that we made on that particular matter in giving reasons for our decision in that case.

33. Another case concerning a unit trust where sec. 260 was involved, (at least in the court of first instance), and to which oblique reference has already been made, is Phillips v FC of T 77 ATC 4169. The taxpayer in that case was a member of a firm of chartered accountants which established a unit trust to carry on the commercial activities of the partnership such as the lease of plant and equipment, the provision of clerical and secretarial staff and the availability of share registry services. Those services were offered to the partnership at certain rates of charge, but not in excess of commercial rates. The units in the trust were held by wives, family trusts, and/or family companies of the partners. The trust was established by the execution of a trust deed between a manager and a trustee and the allotment to the manager of 100 units of $1.00 each. Subsequently, further units were created and taken up by the wives, trusts, and companies of the various partners. Upon the agreed sum of capital being received, the unit trust began operations providing services as detailed above, and rendering accounts for same to the partnership on a monthly basis. For the years in issue which were those ended 30th June 1972 and 1973, the Commissioner assessed the taxpayer on the basis that the amounts incurred by the partnership to the unit trust were not deductible in determining the net income of the partnership. Upon the taxpayer objecting, the Commissioner advised his disallowance of the objection upon the following alternative bases:-

first, the amounts claimed were not allowable at all,

secondly, so much of those amounts- as represented the profit of the unit trust was not allowable,

thirdly, sec. 260 operated so as to render the arrangement between the partnership and the service trust void as against him (the Commissioner).

34. In allowing the taxpayer's appeal, the Supreme Court of New South Wales per Waddell J., found that the partnership in engaging the services of the unit trust and in accepting the obligation to pay for them did not acquire any legally enforceable right otherwise than in the performance of those services. The Court considered that the services rendered by the unit trust were employed in the partnership business and so were necessarily incurred in gaining the assessable income; thus they were properly deductible under sec 51(1). As regards the sec. 260 aspect, the Court was of the view that the High Court decision in favour of the taxpayer in Cecil Bros. Pty. Limited v F.C. of T. [1964] HCA 82; (1963-64) 111 CLR 430 operated so as to preclude the Commissioner from relying on that provision. In particular, the Court relied upon dicta of Mr Justice Menzies that the Commissioner could not substitute a different price for that actually paid in accordance with the contracts he was attacking under sec. 260, and so came to the view that in the situation before it:

"Section 260 cannot be regarded as a warrant for disregarding part of the price actually paid for the hire of staff, assets and services pursuant to that agreement, the validity of which remains unaffected."

35. In Cecil Bros., the High Court was required to determine the deductibility to the taxpayer company, a retailer of shoes, of expenditure incurred by it in buying stock at a price in excess of commercial rates from an associated company the shareholders of which were either shareholders in, or relatives of shareholders in, the taxpayer company. The effect, of course, was to divert profit to the family company. At first instance, Owen J. rejected the Commissioner's submission that sec 51(1) should not apply to allow a deduction for the mark-up to the interposed company, but on applying the predication test propounded in Newton, he found that sec. 260 applied so as to reduce the amount of the taxpayer's outgoing, and thus increase its taxable income. On appeal to the Full High Court, the decision of Owen J. was reversed, the Court finding that upon all of the facts there was no arrangement to which the taxpayer company was a party falling within sec. 260, with Mr. Justice Menzies in a separate judgment finding that sec. 260 was purely an annihilating section and so does not authorize the Commissioner to substitute a different price than that agreed between the parties.

  1. It is of interest that the Judge at first instance in Phillips did not refer to the Newton decision although that decision was examined closely by Owen J in Cecil Bros. Another interesting sideline to the Phillips case is that sec. 260 was not argued by the Commissioner in his appeal to the Full Federal Court, where in an unanimous decision, the decision of Waddell J. was confirmed.
  2. A different result to the decision in Phillips was arrived at by the New Zealand Court of Appeal on similar basic facts in the decision of Wisheart & Ors v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (N.Z.) 71 ATC 6001. There, two solicitors assigned a lease of their premises and equipment to a company whose shares were held by family trusts following upon the retirement of a third partner and the later admission of a new partner. All the staff were dismissed from the firm but were then re-employed by the company. That entity then contracted to provide staff premises and equipment to the partnership at 15% above cost. It was held by the Court that the arrangement was of the kind where one could predicate, by looking at the overt acts, that its effect was to avoid tax and so was caught by sec. 108, the New Zealand equivalent to sec. 260. The decision is of interest because the Court studied all aspects of the arrangement in issue and preferred to base its decision on the broad sweep of Newton rather than on the more restricted line of reasoning in Cecil Bros.
  3. We now turn to a consideration of the "doctor cases", FC of T Gulland (supra) also Watson v FC of T and Pincus v FC of T, both latter cases having the same citation as Gulland. The fact situations of the three doctors have been described in some detail in Case WT85/951. Accordingly, for present purposes we content ourselves with saying that all three doctors transferred their practices to a unit trust and arranged for superannuation benefits to be obtained for them as employees of the trust; also they arranged for the units in the trust to be held by family trusts so that the relevant shares of income of the practice could be distributed to members of the family of each practitioner. As is now well known, the Full High Court held, with Deane J. dissenting, that the arrangement as made by each doctor was caught by sec. 260. The case is notable for a heavy emphasis placed on the predication test formulated in Newton and on the reading down of the choice principle as enunciated in Mullens & Qrs. v F.C. of T. 76 ATC 4288, Slutzkin & Ors. v F.C. of T. 77 ATC 4076 and Cridland v F.C. of T. 77 ATC 4538.
  4. Similarly to the view of the majority of the Court in the doctor cases, we are of the view, as regards the instant case, that the desire of the partners to secure superannuation benefits was certainly one of the purposes for re-organizing the structure of their practice. But we find it difficult to see it as the main purpose, or as one of the main purposes, as contended by the applicant, basically because it was not necessary to have the partners' family trusts owning all the units in the unit trust in order to obtain that particular benefit. Also, one would have thought that if the need to provide for superannuation had been seen to be acute, the applicant and his colleagues would have attended to the matter well before 1st May 1981 (eight months after the unit trust was established) at which date the superannuation trust deed was executed. Furthermore, in accepting a low salary of $14,166 for the first ten months of the unit trust's operation, one would have thought that the partners would have taken formal steps to protect their rights, if in fact the attraction of joining a superannuation fund merited the priority which the applicant contended all the partners accorded to it. As a final point on this same matter we consider that the partners' lack of action was inconsistent with their demonstrable energy and enterprise in establishing their service trust, computer trust, and trust for the more lucrative operation of their business premises. Their overt acts are what we must be concerned with, and certainly in this matter they run counter to the evidence given by the applicant and stated earlier in these reasons (see para 22).

40. In Gulland, Dawson J. stated at p. 4796 that the whole of the circumstances must be considered in every case where sec. 260 is in issue, and added that often there will be special features pointing to the proper conclusion to be drawn. In our view, the operations of the service and computer trusts are here most relevant. It will be recalled that the activities of the service trust were absorbed into those of the unit trust in September 1980 with those of the computer trust being necessarily absorbed some time later. Hence the constraints on the upper level of charge-out rates imposed as a result of Phillips case with its consequential limitation on disposable income available to the family trusts, could have no application with the trusts operating as part of the unit trust. Whatever the size of the income of the unit trust, (which after 1st September 1980 included the profit made by the service trusts), the whole of it was required to be paid over to the partners' family trusts in accordance with the terms of the trust deed. With the admission of the fourth partner on 1st July 1980, it could be expected that the practice's gross fee income would rise, and indeed the profit and loss statements adduced in evidence reveal that such was the case. Accordingly the conclusion is irresistible that the partners saw that the measures introduced by way of the service and computer trusts to divert income were limited in their scope, and that a re-organisation of the practice to remove those constraints and substitute a more lucrative structure was eminently desirable.

We are of the view that the re-structuring of the practice could not amount to a family dealing according to the meaning given to those words in Newton even though, as was the case in Peate, one of the purposes of the re-organisation was to make provision for the partners' families We say this because all that was done by the partners went far beyond (using dicta from Peate) "an ordinary family settlement."

41. Also we cannot accept that the establishment of the unit trust, and the attendant issue of the units to the principals' family trusts, coupled with the absorption of service trusts into that trust and the subsequent dissolution of the partnership, could be regarded as an ordinary business dealing. The whole process, involving as it did frequent discussions with the erstwhile firm's solicitor, was far too elaborate conceptually and in terms of time and money to merit that description. The fact that such arrangements had become relatively commonplace amongst professional people by the early nineteen-eighties is, in our view, not to the point. In Gulland, Gibbs C.J. made it clear that the words "ordinary business or family dealing" as enunciated by Lord Denning in Newton mean normal or regular rather than commonplace or prevalent. After making that point at p 4773, the Chief Justice went on to say:-

" in any case that reference was made by way of contrast to the words without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax' and it is the latter words which give the clue to Lord Denning's meaning; see Mangin v IR Commr. at p 751."

42. Accordingly we believe that at the very least, a principal purpose, if not the main purpose, of the re-organisation of the practice was to divert income away from the partners to the unit trust so that, first, deductible contributions could be made to a superannuation fund for the partners' future benefit and the future benefit of their staff, and secondly, distributions of the enterprise's surplus could be made to the partners' family trusts. Accordingly we consider that the purpose and effect of the arrangement was to alter the incidence of income tax and relieve the applicant and his erstwhile partners from a liability to pay income tax which would otherwise have been imposed on them. The fact that distributions to the family trusts in a particular year were less than appertained in certain years before the re-organisation of the practice was implemented, (as the applicant contended), is, in our view, beside the point. Certainly, that sort of matter did not persuade Gibbs CJ in Gulland that sec 260 should not apply (see p 4770), and it is really the capacity to make distributions to which sec 260 is directed, rather than the distributions in themselves. Also the fact that the partners' professional association approved of the arrangement has no bearing, in our view, on the central issue of whether there was a purpose to avoid tax. That body was concerned with ethical considerations particularly as affecting members' relationships with clients; it had no mandate to concern itself with members' income tax matters which necessarily would vary from member to member.

43. It will be recalled that some of the purposes for the re-organisation of the various doctors' practices in Peate included "the better organisation of the group's activities and particularly its methods of accounting." The High Court there considered that that could well have been so, but it regarded the method by which those purposes were implemented as involving a diversion of income leading to an avoidance of tax. Accordingly, the Court held that the arrangement came within sec. 260. As regards the instant case, in considering the applicant's alleged commercial reasons for the unit trust arrangements, one needs to bear in mind that when the partnership was in operation there existed three separate business enterprises comprising the professional practice, the service trust, and the computer trust. Under those arrangements, each enterprise was no doubt required to provide some internal recording systems for control purposes, for example, time sheets, as well as standard type financial reporting systems, to enable the partners to have an awareness of the nature and level of activities carried on by the trusts, and of the profitability of those activities.

  1. As a general proposition, it would seem probable that the service and computer trusts, as relatively small undertakings, would have fewer administrative problems than might be encountered in a larger enterprise, which had not hived off the secretarial aspects of the business. Accordingly, whilst, in the instant case, the consolidation of the activities of the trusts and professional practice into the complex enterprise comprising the unit trust may have, (as stated by the applicant in evidence), effected some reductions in the administrative and accounting work previously carried on, there would still have been a need per medium of some form of columnar-type bookkeeping to have an awareness of the profitability of the service type functions (particularly in relation to outside parties), performed under the single structure. In these circumstances, it is difficult to make any worthwhile appraisal of the added efficiencies and cost savings that may have arisen with the amalgamation of all functions under the control of the unit trust. In this context, it is also worth adding that the creation of the unit trust structure gave rise to further financial tasks associated with the payment of the purchase price of the partnership business and of the superannuation contributions.
  2. It therefore seems improbable to us that cost efficiencies played an important part in the decision taken by the partners to re-structure their practice. The aim of obtaining operational efficiencies seems prosaic compared with the advantages, (which arose out of the unit trust structure), of vesting full control of the profit making operations in the partners, and at the same time requiring the recipients of those profits to be the partners' family trusts.

46. For the above reasons, we consider that the amount in issue of $22,097 was correctly included in the assessable income of the applicant for the year of income ended 30th June 1981 pursuant to the provisions of secs. 19 and 92, and to the definition of "partnership" contained in sec 6(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

REASONS FOR DECISION

MR. D.J. TROWSE (MEMBER): 23RD APRIL, 1987.

I have had the advantage of reading the findings of fact and reasons in law for the conclusions reached by my colleagues, Mr KP Brady and Mr JE Stewart. However, whilst I am in substantial agreement with those findings of fact and some of their conclusions, I am unable to accept their decision as to the applicability of s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

2. The provisions contained in s.260 and which, as amended by Act No. 110 of 1981, are restricted to contracts, agreements and arrangements made or entered into on or before 27th May, 1981, are as follows:

"Every contract, agreement, or arrangement made or entered into, orally or in writing, whether before or after the commencement of this Act, shall so far as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly -

(a) altering the incidence of any income tax; i

1 (b) relieving any person from liability to

pay any income tax or make any return;

(c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or liability imposed on any person by this Act; or

(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any respect,

be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, or in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but without

prejudice to such validity as it may have in any other

respect or for any other purpose."

Because of the expansiveness of the language used in the above provision, the Courts have recognised the need to impose limitation on its operation. Some of its attendant difficulties were alluded to by Fullagar J. in the following terms when sitting as one of the Full High Court in the well-known case of F.C. of T. v. Newton & Ors. (1956-57) 96 C.L.R. 578 at p.646:

"Section 260 has been regarded as a difficult section. It should be mentioned that before the Act of 1936 the section did not contain the words 'as against the Commissioner or in regard to any proceeding under the Act'. The added words make it quite clear that there is avoidance only for the purposes of the Act. But there is difficulty with regard to the construction of the section, and there is difficulty with regard to its operation. In the first place, the 'purposes' or 'effects' which will attract its operation are stated very vaguely. If we interpret it very literally, it will seem to apply to cases which it is hardly conceivable that the legislature should have had in mind. On the other hand, any limitation which we may seek to imply may appear to deprive the section of all practical effect. In the second place, even when we have discovered one of the 'purposes' or 'effects' which bring a case within the section, the actual effect of the section on the vitiated transaction is not immediately obvious. Something is 'avoided' though only against the Commissioner - i.e. only so far as it affects ostensibly the incidence or quantum of income tax. But is anything left, and if so, what, when effect has been given to the 'avoidance'?"

3. In considering the possible application of s260 to the re-arrangement effected on 31st August, 1980, it is also necessary to appreciate that the purpose referred to in the section, and of which Fullagar J. spoke, goes to the main purpose, or one of the main purposes. Whilst that contention is not completely free from debate, support for it is to be found in Europa Oil (N.Z.) Ltd. (No 2) v Commr. of IR (NZ), 76 ATC 6001, and Manain v. Commr. of I.R. (NZ., 70 ATC 6001. Additional judicial comment on this subject is to be found at p.4206 in the decision of Gibbs J. (as he then was) in the case of Hollvock v FC of T, 71 ATC 4202:

"On the other hand, if tax avoidance is an inessential or incidental feature of the arrangement, that may well serve to show that the arrangement cannot necessarily be labelled as a means to avoid tax."

4. In situations of the kind currently under consideration, it seems that the matter in issue should be tested against the relevant criteria laid down by the Courts over the years, (varying though that criteria may be), and that the sequence of that action be ordered in such a way so as to coincide with what I see as a series of tests which must be progressively passed before moving to the next stage.

5. The initial point to be established is whether the shares of net income from the partnership and the unit trust and passing to the applicant and his family trust represent income from his personal exertion activities. It appears from the majority decisions handed down by members of the High Court in FC of T v Gulland; Watson v F.C. of T.; Pincus v. F.C. of T., 85 ATC 4765 (see particularly pp.4773, 4780 and 4781), that such a characterisation could be fatal to the applicant's cause. If the income flows from the applicant's personal exertion, then it follows that its derivation attaches to him and forms part of his assessable income, and of no one else. It seems that the basis of such a view is to be found in the remarks of Henry J in Spratt v Commr. of IR (N.Z.) (1964) N.Z.L.R. 272 at p.277; (1963-65) 13 A.T.D. 308 at p.311:

"No taxpayer can, by way of assignment, escape assessment of tax on income resulting from his personal activities - such income always remains truly his income and is derived by him irrespective of the method he may adopt to dispose of it."

6. In deciding this question in favour of the applicant, I have been assisted by comments made by Barwick C.J., Stephens, Mason and Wilson JJ. in their joint judgment in the case of FC of T v. Everett. 80 ATC 4076. In considering the inability to assign a right to income from personal exertion, and after indicating that such a principle would apply to income earned by a sole trader who operates a business and a professional man who practises on his own account, the learned Judges went on to say, at p.4083:

"In this context it is correct to say that the taxpayer's remuneration is the product of his personal exertion and that all that he has to assign are his future receipts as distinct from any right to receive those receipts. But this is not true of partners in general or of the respondent as a partner in this case. The respondent's entitlement under the partnership agreement was to a proportionate share of the partnership profits as disclosed by the partnership accounts. The relevant proportion of the partnership profits was payable to the respondent because he was a partner and the owner of a share in the partnership. The respondent was entitled before the assignment to his proportionate share of the partnership profits, however much or however little energy he devoted to the practice, so long as the partnership remained on foot. Accordingly, it is a misnomer to speak of the respondent's share of the income as having been gained by his personal exertion."

The above pronouncement, when taken in conjunction with -

the relatively large number of employees engaged in the business and responsible for the earning of gross fees;

the relatively small degree of contribution made by the applicant in that same period; and

the extent of the income producing assets (the clientele as acquired from the partnership, together with office furniture and equipment) applied in the earning of the business income,

leads me to the conclusion that the share of net income passing to the holders of the units in the unit trust should not be treated as income arising from the rendering of personal services by the applicant and his fellow directors. Rather, it represents a stream of income which flowed from a joint source consisting of capital and labour, with the labour component being a mix of services rendered by all employees including the four directors. On this basis alone, I distinguish a variance of some considerable significance as between the factual position now under examination and those which existed in the cases of Gulland, Watson and Pincus.

7. As I see it, the next step is to decide whether the transactions of 31st August, 1980, between the four former partners and the nominee company acting as trustee of the unit trust, and purporting to refer to the sale and purchase of the accountancy practice, were entered into in good faith. If they were, then assistance in determining the matter in dispute is to be found, in my opinion, in the judgment of Knox CJ and members of the High Court in DFC of T v Purcell [1921] HCA 59; (1920-21) 29 CLR 464. In that matter, Purcell, a primary producer and owner of certain pastoral property and livestock pastured thereon, had declared himself a trustee of those assets for himself, his wife and his daughter equally and, in declaring the trust, he reserved to himself wide and unusual powers of management, control and investment. The Commissioner treated the income yielded from the grazing activity undertaken on that property, and conducted by Purcell in the same way as before, as having been derived by Purcell alone, and in doing so he invoked the provisions then contained in s53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915-1916, and which were the equivalent of s260. In holding in favour of the taxpayer, Knox CJ had the following to say at p466 on the operation of the anti-avoidance section:

"It does not extend to the case of a bona fide disposition by virtue of which the right to receive income arising from a source which theretofore belonged to the taxpayer is transferred to and vested in some other person. The section is intended to protect the revenue against any attempted evasions of the liability to income tax imposed by the Act - that liability is imposed on the taxpayer in respect only of his income ...; and the bona fide gift or sale by a taxpayer of assets producing income is therefore in no sense an attempt to evade his liability to income tax."

The additional comments of Gavan Duffy and Starke JJ. at p.473 are apposite:

"The Commissioner next contended that, even if the declaration evidenced a real, genuine and valid transaction, yet it was struck by sec 53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915-1916. If the argument be sound the assessment is of course unimpeachable. It is therefore essential to consider the true construction of sec 53. The section, as the Chief Justice says, does not prohibit the disposition of property. Its office is to avoid contracts, etc. which place the incidence of the tax or the burden of tax upon some person or body other than the person or body contemplated by the Act. If a person actually disposed of income-producing property to another so as to reduce the burden of taxation, the Act contemplates that the new owner should pay the tax. The incidence of the tax and the burden of the tax fall precisely as the Act intends, namely, upon the new owner. But any agreement which directly or indirectly throws the burden of the tax upon a person who is not liable to pay it, is within the ambit of sec. 53. It follows, from what we have said, that there is no contravention of sec. 53 in the present case."

The decision in Purcell was referred to with approval by the Privy Council in Newton v FC of T [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1957-58) 98 CLR 1, and more recently by Gibbs C.J., Brennan and Dawson JJ. in the cases of Gulland, Watson and Pincus (supra), particularly by the latter Judge at pp.4793 and 4794.

  1. As I understand the position, Purcell stands for the authority that s.260 will have no application where income producing assets are transferred in terms of a bona fide arrangement, and that that situation prevails notwithstanding a continuation of the same management and control as before. Furthermore, as it will be seen from the joint decision of Duffy and Starke JJ., the existence of a purpose to reduce the burden of taxation does not alter that position. However, as demonstrated by the High Court in its majority decision in the doctors' cases, all such arrangements involving transfers of assets are not protected from the force of s.260. In this regard, it seems to me that a finding that the income was, and continues to be, generated from the personal exertion of the former principals takes the matter beyond that contemplated in Purcell. But that is not the case here (see paragraph 6), and thus it is my view that the limitation adverted to has no application.
  2. After a consideration of the whole of the evidence, I am satisfied that it was the genuine intention of the former partners to sell their accountancy practice to the unit trust and that, likewise, the trustee of the unit trust intended to acquire the practice and the assets attaching thereto, and that all of the parties to the agreement accepted the binding nature of the contract entered into. Furthermore, I accept that those assets were acquired by the new owner at the values agreed upon and that the possession of those assets was fundamental to the derivation of the professional income. On that last point, it should be acknowledged that the taking over by the new owner of the existing clientele, i.e. the goodwill of the practice, enabled, to a large extent, the generation of gross fees of $435,882 in the first 10 months of trading. In short, I am not able to perceive any justification for the conclusion that the disposition was not genuine. Accordingly, I find that the income earned after 31st August, 1980, was derived by the unit trust and that the incidence and burden of tax falls precisely as the Act intends, namely, upon the new owner. Perhaps, before passing on to the next stage, I should mention the dicta of Kitto J. in Hancock v. F.C. of T. [1961] HCA 90; (1962-63) 108 C.L.R. 258 at p.283, wherein he expressed the view that in certain nominated situations a larger transaction of which a disposition of property forms but a part may nevertheless be caught by the operation of s.260. In my opinion, the circumstances referred to by the learned Judge do not prevail in the present reference.

10. Having regard to the difficulties and uncertainties of interpreting s.260, I am conscious of the possibility of having misconstrued the breadth of application resulting from the Purcell decision and, with that thought in mind, I consider it appropriate to proceed to an examination of the predication test which had its origin in the judgment of Lord Denning in Newton's case (supra) and its rebirth in the majority decision of the High Court in Gulland, Watson and Pincus (supra). I should add that some of the judgments handed down in those same cases place considerable limitations on the application of the choice principle (see Mason J. in Cridland v FC of T, 77 ATC 4538 at p.4542) and the recent decision of the Privy Council in IRC v Challenge Corporation Ltd. (1986) STC 548-60 (albeit of persuasive authority only) would indicate further judicial misgivings as to its application. However, on the view that I take of the evidence it is not necessary for me to refer further to that principle.

11. The often quoted terminology used by Lord Denning in describing the predication test, and which appears at p.8 in the citation previously mentioned, is as follows:

"In order to bring the arrangement within the section you must be able to predicate - by looking at the overt acts by which it was implemented - that it was implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax. If you cannot so predicate, but have to acknowledge that the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax, then the arrangement does not come within the section."

But additionally, and following directly upon the above extract, are examples used by Lord Denning in an effort to demonstrate possible situations where such a predication could not and could be made. As that part of the judgment is of assistance in developing a better understanding of the test, it is quoted below:

"Thus, no one, by looking at a transfer of shares cum dividend, can predicate that the transfer was made to avoid tax. Nor can anyone, by seeing a private company turned into a non-private company, predicate that it was done to avoid Div. 7 tax, see W.P. Keighery Pty. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Taxation [(1958) [1957] HCA 2; 32 A.L.J.R. 118; 11 ATD 3593. Nor could anyone, on seeing a declaration of trust made by a father in favour of his wife and daughter, predicate that it was done to avoid tax, see Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Purcell [(1927) [1921] HCA 59; 29 C.L.R. 464]. But when one looks at the way the transactions were effected in Jacrues v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1924) 34 CLR 3283; Clarke v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1932) [1932] HCA 46; 48 C.L.R. 56], and Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1953) 87 C.L.R. 5483 - the way cheques were exchanged for like amounts and so forth - there can be no doubt at all that the purpose and effect of that way of doing things was to avoid tax."

Recognising that the determination of the correct answer to this test may prove difficult in some (if not all) circumstances, the identification of other instances could be helpful, as for example the splitting of income per the medium of a family partnership where the arrangement is bona fide, or, likewise, through the operation of a service trust where the charges are commercially realistic. In both those cases, it appears that the Commissioner accepts that such arrangements do not attract the operation of s.260 (see his Ruling No. 2330), and I assume that he takes that stance either because their implementation cannot be predicated as a means of avoiding tax or such arrangements may be considered as ordinary business dealings. Furthermore, it seems that he has adopted a similar line where the whole of an architectural practice is incorporated as a company, provided that such entity complies with the relevant requirements of the Council of the Royal Institute of Architects concerning directors and controlling shares, and where the parties have taken adequate steps to ensure that all contracts, receipts, advertisements are in the name of the company (see his Ruling 139). Whilst it is appreciated that such rulings are of no binding effect, they do further elaborate upon a subject which, in my opinion, lacks both precision and ease of interpretation.

12. In examining the predication test as regards the instant case, there are several associated matters which require attention, the first of which relates to whether, in the situation now encountered, the provision of superannuation benefits bears prima facie the stamp of tax avoidance. The following extract taken from the judgment of Dawson J. in FC of T v Gulland, at p 4797, clearly negates such a proposition:

"Of itself and without more, the establishment and operation of a superannuation fund, notwithstanding the opportunity it offers to deduct from assessable income contributions to the fund on behalf of an employee, will not attract sec. 260."

At and since the hearing of this reference, I have experienced doubts as to the real purpose of the fund. Was it, in fact, established in a genuine attempt to create retirement benefits for employees, or was its prime function (recognising the loan-back arrangements entered into during the initial period) the maximisation of income tax deductions? In the end, I am persuaded that the real purpose was the provision of superannuation benefits to retiring and deceased members, and in reaching such a conclusion, emphasis has been placed on the subsequent action of the trustees in maintaining the required balance as between Government securities and other investments, as well as the admission of several other employees into the fund.

  1. Secondly, it would appear from the judgments handed down in the trilogy of doctors' cases that the presence of artificial, complex or contrived elements in an arrangement are factors which may infer a purpose of tax avoidance and to which s260 is intended to apply. It seems to me that those adjectives must be viewed in a relative context and that one should have regard to the extent and degree of those elements if, in fact, they are found to exist. The pertinent facts of the issue being considered are that four accountants, previously carrying on business as a partnership, sold their practice to a nominee company which acted as trustee for a unit trust; that unit trust employed and provided them with superannuation benefits and any profits remaining were to be shared equally between their family trusts. I do not perceive such an arrangement to be either artificial, complex or contrived to the extent of warranting the application of s260.
  2. Thirdly, the continued search for economies in conducting an accounting practice in what has become a very competitive area of endeavour is a vital matter, and on that point I refer to the applicant's evidence relating to the benefits resulting from the consolidation of activity. I accept that such a union would facilitate the preparation of more meaningful short-term financial reports and that that form of improvement was one of the reasons for the change. In my opinion, such a purpose was aimed at improving the performance of management and, on that basis, it is correct to conclude that a commercial advantage was being sought and that the presence of such a plan supports the view that the main purpose of the arrangement was not tax avoidance.

15. Fourthly, reference should be made to the fact that the whole of the arrangement was undertaken in accordance with the by-laws and approval of the Chartered Institute of Accountants as this is, in my opinion, a matter of some significance in assessing whether the series of transactions might be explicable as ordinary business or family dealings. In considering situations purporting to vest an activity in a person or body which is prevented by professional or statutory controls from pursuing that activity, the Courts have clearly indicated that that kind of breach stands in direct conflict with arrangements of a "normal or regular nature", and that accordingly it might be assumed that the real purpose was tax avoidance (see Hollyock v FC of T (supra) and Millard v FC of T [1962] HCA 28; (1962-63) 108 CLR 336). Conversely, it seems to me that the express provision that members of the Institute might conduct their practices through a trust adds considerable weight to the view that within the accountancy profession the arrangement had the features of being an ordinary business dealing. Additionally, the high degree of attention to detail and the businesslike approach adopted in the implementation of the arrangement lend further support to the notion that tax avoidance was not one of the main purposes.

16. The final and perhaps most contentious matter relates to the use of the applicant's family trust as a beneficiary in the unit trust and, more particularly, whether, because of that involvement and thus the opportunity for splitting income, it should be inferred that the main, or one of the main, purposes of the whole arrangement was the avoidance of tax. In deciding this issue, it is necessary to have regard to the commercial environment which prevailed in the late seventies and early eighties, and especially the types of business structures then coming into operation. From my memory of that environment, I believe it is fair to say that the utilisation of a variety of trusts was extremely popular with professional people in the conduct of their affairs. Furthermore, I would suggest that it would have been a rarity to find a unit trust of the non-public variety with individuals acting in their own right as beneficiaries. In my experience, it was "common, prevalent, normal and regular" to find the units in a trust held by a trustee of a discretionary trust. Notwithstanding those comments, it is clear that some of the majority of the High Court in Gulland, Watson and Pincus were of the view that the creation of a unit trust to conduct a professional practice and act as employer, with the units in the trust being issued to family trusts of erstwhile principals now employees in the practice could, in a given situation, amount to an arrangement to avoid tax and so be caught by s260. In my understanding of those cases, the Judges so minded only reached that conclusion after a full appraisal of all of the circumstances in the three situations before them, and it would be incorrect to conclude that the implementation of trusts to conduct businesses, even if they were to follow the same model as appertained in the above "doctor cases", would inevitably attract the operation of s260. As Dawson J stated in Gulland (see p4796): "the whole of the circumstances must be considered in each case and often there will be special features which will point to the proper conclusion".

17. Of itself, the issue of units to the applicant's family trust clearly does not, in my opinion, attract the operation of s260. After reviewing all of the evidence, I am of the view that the order of purpose was, first, the provision of superannuation benefits, second, to facilitate and improve upon management information and, a further distance back and in third position, the possibility of splitting income through the family trust. In the circumstances of this reference, I do not see the inclusion of the family trust as having a wider purpose of tax avoidance. It was, to my mind, a relatively inessential or incidental feature of the arrangement. In determining the above sequence, I have had due regard to all of the features previously referred to and also to the fact that the extent of the income flowing to the family trust in terms of the old arrangement, and which had been accepted by the Commissioner, seemed more than sufficient when one recognises the number and ages of available beneficiaries in that trust. Taken as a whole, I am unable to predicate - by looking at the overt acts by which the arrangement was implemented - that it was implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax. Conversely, it seems to me that the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax and, with those views in mind, my conclusion is that the arrangement entered into in August 1980 did not have a main purpose of tax avoidance, and on that basis s.260 has no application.

18. For the reasons stated, I allow the applicant's objection in respect of the year ended 30th June, 1981.
