Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The decision for review in this matter is an objection decision dated 4 October 2024 (the Objection Decision) made by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) denying input tax credits under the goods and services tax (GST) legislation to RQA Accountants Pty Ltd (the Applicant) for the quarterly income periods from 1 July 2016 to 30 June 2020 (the Relevant Periods).
  2. The director of the Applicant, Mr Surana, told the Tribunal about a complex set of circumstances occurring over several years which preceded the Objection Decision. These circumstances included litigation involving the Applicant and an Australian bank (the ANZ Bank), engagement with the Tax Practitioners Board (TPB) and other Tribunal proceedings involving the Applicant or Mr Surana and the TPB or Respondent. From Mr Surana’s evidence and submissions it was clear to the Tribunal that these matters have caused significant financial and personal stress to Mr Surana and his family, and continue to cause him considerable distress.
  3. However, the Tribunal wishes to make it clear from the outset that it is only able to make a decision in this proceeding as to whether the Objection Decision was the correct or preferable decision, that is, whether the Applicant was entitled to the input tax credits the subject of that decision. The Tribunal has identified below where historical matters may be relevant to the factual matrix in this proceeding. Otherwise the Tribunal is unable to make a decision in relation to matters in dispute arising under other Tribunal proceedings or engagements with other regulators involving the Applicant or Mr Surana.

FACTUAL BACKGROUND

  1. The following basic facts are not in dispute:
    (a) The Applicant was incorporated on 29 July 2010.[1] The Applicant also obtained an Australian Business Number (ABN) and was registered for GST.

    (b) Mr Surana has been a director of the Applicant since its incorporation.[2]

    (c) Around 2012 to 2014 the Applicant and the ANZ Bank were parties to litigation in the New South Wales Supreme Court relating to loans from the ANZ Bank to Mr and Mrs Surana which were secured by a mortgage over a property owned by the Suranas, being the home in which the Surana family lived at the time. Mr and Mrs Surana were also guarantors for a business loan and overdraft facility from the ANZ Bank to the Applicant. The outcome of the litigation was that an order was made in favour of the ANZ Bank for possession of the property and the property was sold by the ANZ Bank as mortgagee. The Applicant was ordered to pay certain costs of the ANZ Bank arising from the litigation.[3]

    (d) Around December 2014 the Respondent was advised by ASIC that the Applicant was no longer a registered corporation. The Respondent cancelled the Applicant’s ABN, GST and other tax related registrations on 12 December 2014.

    (e) The Applicant’s registration as a corporation was reinstated by ASIC on 9 October 2018.

    (f) On 19 October 2018 the Respondent reinstated the Applicant’s ABN and GST registrations, with an effective date of 29 July 2010.

    (g) The Applicant lodged business activity statements (BAS) for the Relevant Periods on 28 November 2018,[4] 9 June 2020[5] and 26 August 2020.[6]

    (h) Also on 9 June 2020 and 26 August 2020 the Applicant requested amendments to the BAS for some of the Relevant Periods.

    (i) In September 2020 the Respondent advised that it would be undertaking an audit of the Applicant’s BAS for the Relevant Periods.

    (j) After finalising its audit the Respondent issued a Notice of Amended Assessments of Net Amount for the Relevant Periods on 19 January 2021 (the Amended GST Assessments).

    (k) On 11 June 2024 the Applicant lodged an objection against the Amended GST Assessments (the Objection).[7]

    (l) The Objection Decision was made by the Respondent on 4 October 2024, disallowing the Objection.

  2. The input tax credits claimed in the Relevant Periods relate to:
    (a) Legal costs arising from litigation between the Applicant and the ANZ Bank (Legal Costs).

    (b) Other expenses relating to items identified by the Applicant as having been used in its business, including fuel and other motor vehicle expenses, postage, office supplies, technology, and telecommunications (Miscellaneous Expenses).

  3. During the Objection process the Applicant completed a ‘business profile questionnaire’ provided by the Respondent.[8] The Respondent relied on answers provided in that questionnaire in making its Objection Decision and in its submissions to the Tribunal. Mr Surana told the Tribunal that he did not necessarily agree that the questionnaire responses gave a complete or accurate picture of the Applicant’s circumstances. Disputed factual matters are discussed in further detail below, however the questionnaire answers included the following:
    (a) The Applicant’s main business activity was ‘Boutique Accounting, Consulting and Limited Purpose Tax Services. We have added Community Health Influencing blogging service on a Not-for-Profit basis through regular updates on various social media platforms to maintain public trademark visibility granted by IP Australia on 8-Dec-2010.’

    (b) The business originally commenced in April 2009 when the business name ‘RQA Accountants’ was registered with New South Wales Fair Trading, with Mr Surana operating as a sole trader under this business name.[9] The Applicant was subsequently incorporated to operate the business.

    (c) The Applicant’s registration as a corporation ceased temporarily between 2013 and 2018. Registration was reinstated by ASIC in 2018 with retrospective effect.

    (d) Following the reinstatement of the Applicant’s registration as a corporation with ASIC in 2018 the Applicant’s business activities were significantly reduced to ‘online operations limited to management consulting, community health services, related entities accounting/tax and administration services mostly related to tax dispute resolution services to comply with “Fit and Proper Test” under Tax Agent Services Act 2009.’

    (e) The Applicant did not have any employees, with the business being ‘owner operated self-employed’ (that is, by Mr Surana). The activities of the Applicant were restricted to home-based operations.[10]

    (f) Costs and business overheads had been significantly reduced since the business commenced in 2009. In the three years preceding the completion of the questionnaire, the:

    ‘ongoing costs were limited to basic software subscriptions and maintenance of domain name registration and unique one person email to meet ongoing demands of various dispute resolution services with numerous regulators. As our existing operations are limited to cloud-based operations, we can offer various services with/without contractors and alliance partners such as virtual CFO and related consulting services. We expect to provide taxation services and return to profit as soon as we gain our registration with the tax practitioners board of Australia. As discussed with [name] marketing and business development in various accounting services is subject to mandatory compliance and registration with TPB failing which we can expose ourselves to heavy penalties under TASA 2009.’

    (g) Ongoing business expenditure included subscriptions to software services, CCH secured portal cloud space with multi-factor authentication security and payments to entities such as Australia Post, TPG, CPA Australia. The Applicant also identified as an expense ‘proportionate rent in home floor space and related home based occupancy costs.’

    (h) A Yamaha motorbike was purchased in the 2022 financial year which was funded by an associate loan.[11]

    (i) In relation to qualifications, training, licences or registrations required to run the business the Applicant identified qualifications issued by or memberships with entities such as CA/CPA (relating to accounting practice), registration with the TPB, continuous professional development activities as required by membership bodies and ‘hands on work experience in local cloud-based accounting ecosystems such as XERO, MYOB and QBO.’

    (j) The types of products or services sold or provided were:

    ✓ Management Consulting including Virtual CFO services

    ✓ Accounting, Tax, Consulting and Administrative Services (Fee Income-based)

    ✓ Community health influencing services (Not for Profit Blogging) to maintain

    trademark visibility and professional networking to source future clients

    ✓ Social -media blogging and influencer services

    ✓ Technical workshops and webinars’

    (k) The main customers of the business were related entities and ‘verified Business to Business’ customers. Customers were sourced and the services of the business sold via referrals, word of mouth, presence on online platforms, professional networking, membership of local community sport organisations and chambers of commerce. Customers could be located within or outside Australia due to the cloud-based operations and ‘extensive online presence.’ The Applicant described various social media platforms on which it had a presence and identified a domain name it had registered for use in a business website after obtaining TPB registration.

    (l) The sales revenue of the business was described by the Applicant as fee income of $50,000 reported in 2009 and 2010, and thereafter losses were incurred to the end of 2022 ‘due to civil litigation matter involving its past financier.’ As of June 2024 the Applicant ‘expects to recommence trading at optimum capacity earning fee income ... upon finalisation of ongoing tax agent registration with TPB Australia.’

  4. Mr Surana provided the Tribunal with some further background to the events that had occurred, including in relation to the litigation with the ANZ Bank:
    (a) The litigation resulted in the Surana family losing their home in 2013.

    (b) When the Applicant’s corporate registration was reinstated in 2018 by ASIC with retrospective effect the Applicant was required to reconstruct records relating to the period during which it had been deregistered. The Applicant was also required by the Respondent to lodge BAS for those tax periods when corporate registration had ceased. The Applicant did not have all of the supporting documentation for those periods at that time.

    (c) Mr Surana had engaged with numerous officers of the Respondent over a period of some years in relation to the Applicant’s taxation matters. He had sought assistance from the Australian Small Business and Family Enterprises Ombudsman (ASBFEO) who had referred him to a barrister with experience in taxation matters. That barrister had advised that the ANZ Bank’s withholding of paperwork was a key problem.[12]

    (d) The Applicant and Mr Surana had sought documentation and other information from the ANZ Bank over a lengthy period, including tax invoices which would support the Applicant’s taxation claims and explain how certain funds had been allocated. The ANZ Bank did not provide material until 2022, at which time they provided a bundle of invoices.

    (e) The Applicant did not agree with how the Respondent had characterised aspects of the litigation with the ANZ Bank in its submissions to the Tribunal in this proceeding. He believed that the ANZ Bank had owed the Applicant a fiduciary duty, based on its knowledge of the Applicant’s business, to provide the Applicant with sufficient working capital. The ANZ Bank had also breached its duty by lending Mr and Mrs Surana money in circumstances where the Suranas could not repay it, and then not lending them more money when they needed it. The Applicant had also received advice from a barrister that the decision of Adamson J in the NSW Supreme Court was incorrect.[13]

    (f) The Royal Commission into Misconduct in the Banking, Superannuation and Financial Services Industry which finalised its report in February 2019 (the Banking Royal Commission) made findings consistent with the views of Mr Surana relating to the treatment of the Applicant and the Suranas.

    (g) The Respondent’s audit commenced in 2020 and was conducted during COVID-19 lockdowns when the Applicant had difficulty accessing tax-related documents. Mr Surana said that an early neutral evaluation (ENE) process had occurred around this time.

    (h) After the audit was finalised the Respondent imposed administrative penalties on the Applicant calculated on the basis of an intentional disregard of a taxation law. At this time Mr Surana was seeking re-registration as a tax agent with the TPB and because of the ‘intentional disregard’ finding by the Respondent, the TPB refused his application. These administrative penalties were subsequently remitted, then further penalties imposed which were also remitted.

  5. Mr Surana also drew to the Tribunal’s attention the significant adverse impact on his mental health that had followed the litigation with the ANZ Bank and extended engagement with regulators, providing documentation relating to treatment by a psychologist.

    (a) The Respondent’s objections officers had partly adopted an audit report that had been finalised in January 2021, which was during a COVID-19 lockdown period. At the time of the audit the Applicant did not have copies of tax invoices supporting around $9,000 of input tax credit related refunds.

    (b) The relevant tax invoices were released by the ANZ Bank to the Applicant in 2022.

    (c) Although the Respondent had previously said that the lack of tax invoices was the reason for the denial of input tax credits for tax periods between 2011 and 2022, the Respondent was now saying that the Applicant was outside the four-year time limit for claiming the input tax credits.

    (d) This was unfair and the Applicant had written advice from their late solicitor confirming its entitlement. Further advice obtained from a barrister indicated ‘defective administration of tax laws’ on the part of the Respondent.

PRELIMINARY MATTERS

  1. Before setting out its analysis of the substantive issues, the Tribunal believes it would be more efficient to address a number of evidentiary or procedural points raised by the Applicant in its submissions and earlier correspondence with the Tribunal.

Scope of this Tribunal proceeding

  1. Mr Surana told the Tribunal that two other Tribunal proceedings were related to and relevant to this matter. The first of these (the Previous Tribunal Proceeding) was a matter in the former Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT).[18] That proceeding related to input tax credits claimed for the tax periods from 1 July 2011 to 30 September 2014. The Respondent had disallowed the Applicant a refund of GST arising from those input tax credits on the basis that the Applicant’s claims were made more than four years after time allowed for making such claims.[19] The decision under review in that matter was dated August 2019, following an objection made in June 2019 relating to notices of assessments of net amounts of GST issued on 18 December 2018.
  2. From the reasons for decision in the Previous Tribunal Proceeding it appears that there was some confusion around which tax periods the relevant input tax credits should have been attributed to, with the Applicant telling the Tribunal that they should have been claimed in 2017 and asking the Tribunal to order that the Applicant lodge amended BAS to remedy this. The Tribunal was unable to make such an order and was therefore obliged to affirm the objection decision under section 14ZZK of the TAA 1953 on the basis that the Applicant had not discharged its onus to prove that the assessments for the tax periods from 1 July 2011 to 30 September 2014 were excessive or incorrect. The Tribunal stated that it was consequently not required to engage with questions around the entitlement to the underlying credits, such as whether the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise during the tax periods or whether creditable acquisitions had been made by the Applicant.
  3. Mr Surana also referred to his ongoing engagement with the TPB over a number of years, with the TPB apparently refusing his application or applications for registration as a tax agent. Mr Surana identified another Tribunal proceeding where he is apparently the applicant and the TPB the respondent, and which relates to a review of a decision of the TPB to refuse his registration. The Respondent (the Commissioner of Taxation) is not a party to that proceeding, nor is the Applicant.
  4. Mr Surana referred to TPB matters and the Previous Tribunal Proceeding at several points while he was making submissions and giving evidence on behalf of the Applicant to the Tribunal in the current proceeding. The Tribunal reiterated to Mr Surana that the current proceeding is limited to review of the Objection Decision relating to claims for input tax credits by the Applicant in the Relevant Periods. The Tribunal asked Mr Surana to clarify what he was seeking from the Tribunal. Mr Surana told the Tribunal that he believed the existence of disputed matters between the Applicant and the Respondent was a reason for the TPB refusing to grant his registration as a tax agent. He said that the TPB had acknowledged that a finding of intentional disregard of the law on his part would be a factor weighing against his registration as a tax agent.
  5. Around June 2021 the Respondent had imposed administrative penalties on the Applicant, calculated on the basis of ‘intentional disregard’ of taxation laws. Mr Surana told the Tribunal that he had successfully objected against these penalties such that they were ultimately remitted.[20] The Objection Decision the subject of the current proceeding does not cover the imposition of any administrative or other penalty, therefore matters of ‘intentional disregard’ or ‘recklessness’ or lack of reasonable care are not before the Tribunal.[21] The Respondent has not raised any issue of penalties in this proceeding. The Objection Decision relates to the Respondent’s denial of the Applicant’s entitlement to input tax credits, and therefore to a refund of tax for those credits. No amount has been refunded to the Applicant in relation to those input tax credits and therefore no debt is owed by the Applicant. No interest has been charged. No penalty has been imposed.
  6. The dissatisfaction of the Applicant and Mr Surana with the processes of the TPB and previous actions of the Respondent may provide context for the current proceeding but will not distract the Tribunal from determining the question in issue, namely the entitlement of the Applicant to the input tax credits claimed by the Applicant in the Relevant Periods, whether the Applicant has discharged his burden of proof in relation to the Amended GST Assessments under section 14ZZK and therefore whether the Objection Decision was the correct or preferable decision.
  7. The Applicant also made a number of submissions about the litigation involving the ANZ Bank. The Tribunal agrees that some matters relating to this litigation are relevant to the issues for consideration in this proceeding, particularly the characterisation and treatment of the Legal Costs for GST purposes as discussed in more detail below. The Tribunal accepts Mr Surana is deeply aggrieved by his experiences with the ANZ Bank. However it is not part of the Tribunal’s review of the Objection Decision to make broader findings about the actions of the ANZ Bank, including in relation to allegations made by the Applicant or Mr Surana of unconscionable conduct or breach of fiduciary duty on the part of the ANZ Bank. The Applicant made a number of assertions about the findings of the NSW Supreme Court in this litigation. The Tribunal will prefer the reported decisions of the Court as evidence of the outcomes of that litigation over Mr Surana’s understanding or view of the outcomes. The Applicant also submitted that the ANZ Bank had breached a 2014 settlement agreement.[22] There is no evidence to support this contention and in any event it is not relevant to this proceeding. Similarly, the Tribunal does not believe that the statements made in the Applicant’s SFIC about mediation attempts with the ANZ Bank and approaches to the NSW Small Business Commissioner over a decade ago[23] are relevant to the GST questions the subject of this proceeding.
  8. The Respondent raised a concern about Mr Surana’s repeated references to matters which were apparently discussed in the context of an ADR process facilitated by the Tribunal, in relation to another proceeding. Tribunal-led ADR events are held on the basis that matters discussed between the parties are confidential and must not be relied upon in any subsequent hearing (except in limited circumstances where the Tribunal has given leave to do so). Even if the ADR process had occurred in the course of this proceeding, the Applicant would have required permission of the Tribunal to raise or rely upon such matters at the hearing. In any event, the matters raised about the ADR event discussions are not viewed by the Tribunal as relevant to its decision in relation to the Objection Decision the subject of this proceeding.
  9. The Applicant submitted that the Respondent had not complied with model litigant obligations or ‘ART practice directions’, referring to paragraphs of an ‘SBT Neutral Evaluation’ process document.[24] This document does not constitute a practice or other direction of the Tribunal. The Applicant’s reference to neutral evaluation appears to be in the context of a process that occurred in another Tribunal proceeding.
  10. In certain email correspondence and at the hearing Mr Surana said that the Respondent should not be permitted to raise the issue of whether the Applicant was ‘carrying on an enterprise’ in the Relevant Years. He said that the Respondent’s decisions in relation to input tax credits for periods before the Relevant Years and the outcome of the Previous Tribunal Proceeding were based on the Applicant not having documentation such as tax invoices to substantiate its entitlement to the credits and consequential refunds. He contended that the Applicant had since obtained this documentation and therefore the question of entitlement to input tax credits for the Relevant Periods could be resolved in favour of the Applicant on that basis. He said that the Respondent should not introduce a new basis for arguing that the Applicant was not entitled to input tax credits, and the question of the existence of an enterprise was not relevant to these proceedings.
  11. The Tribunal controls the scope of a proceeding before it for review of a decision: section 53 of the ART Act. The Tribunal must ensure that each party to a proceeding is given a ‘reasonable opportunity’ to present their case, make submissions and adduce evidence: section 55 of the ART Act. The question of whether an issue or ground is properly raised in the course of a proceeding is a matter for the Tribunal to determine.
  12. The Applicant did not make any application to the Tribunal to limit the scope of the proceeding in this manner. Mr Surana’s concerns were initially raised in correspondence he sent to the Respondent, which he copied to the Tribunal. The Applicant referred to ‘new grounds of denial’ in its SFIC[25] in the context of a discussion of the Previous Tribunal Proceeding and its complaints about the Respondent’s actions. However even if the Applicant had made a formal application for the Respondent to be prevented from relying on the ‘carrying on of an enterprise’ ground, the Tribunal is unlikely to have agreed to limit the scope of the proceeding in such a significant way.
  13. The Tribunal reiterates that the application for review lodged by the Applicant is the Objection Decision. That Objection Decision sets out at length the issue of ‘whether you are carrying on an enterprise’ as a basis for the Respondent’s decision. In turn, the Objection Decision addresses the matters in the Applicant’s Objection of 11 June 2024. The Applicant’s Objection included a set of submissions in which it expressly addressed the issue of whether it was carrying on an enterprise.[26] The issue of whether the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise was central to the Objection Decision under review. The Tribunal cannot ignore this issue, particularly as the question of whether the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Years is a key element of the statutory provisions giving rise to an entitlement to an input tax credit.
  14. The Tribunal’s position therefore is that the Respondent should have been, and was, permitted to argue in this proceeding the question of whether the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise. This procedural point may be academic as, for the reasons given below, the Tribunal believes that even if it had found or assumed that the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Years, it would not have been satisfied that the Legal Costs and Miscellaneous Expenses represented creditable acquisitions of the Applicant, such that input tax credits would have arisen in relation to those items.
  15. The Applicant told the Tribunal that there was an additional amount in relation to which the Applicant sought an input tax credit, supported by an invoice for $13,000 from the real estate agent (Laing) which handled the sale of the Suranas’ home following the ANZ Bank litigation. The Laing invoice appears to have been obtained by the Applicant after the Objection Decision was decided. It does not appear to have been the subject of the Amended GST Assessments or the Objection Decision. The Tribunal would not have been satisfied that was a basis for expanding the grounds of Objection or the scope of this application to include a consideration of the amount of the Laing invoice. In any event, the Tribunal has determined that the Applicant was not carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods such that an input tax credit could not have arisen for this amount.
  16. The Applicant also submitted that a particular barrister expert in tax matters had provided advice to the effect that any inability for the Applicant to claim input tax credits in the Relevant Periods arose from the ANZ Bank not giving the Applicant tax invoices. The Applicant provided a copy of a three paragraph email dated 17 January 2024 sent by the barrister (and copied to Mr Surana) referring the Applicant’s matter to a solicitor colleague.[27] The referral is in relation to an ‘ex gratia’ application being made on the Applicant’s behalf and says that it involves ‘a GST case where the client’s bank’s lack of paperwork has got him into trouble with the ATO (which sounds familiar).’ The Tribunal does not find support for the Applicant’s position in this brief email which does not suggest any reasoned basis for the Applicant being able to claim input tax credits.

Which authority is precedential or persuasive?

  1. Mr Surana contended several case authorities cited by the Respondent were irrelevant and/or a decision of an Indian court should be preferred over those authorities. This was on the basis that:
    (e) Cases cited by the Respondent[28] were many years old and in some cases were decided prior to the introduction of the GST Act or prior to the current version of the GST Act. For the same reason the Respondent’s public ruling GSTR 2001/4, which referred to case law decided between 1953 and 2001 reflected ‘outdated legal principles.’[29] Old case precedents did not reflect accounting practices or the accounting and tax services industry as it existed today.

    (f) Indian case law identified by Mr Surana in the Applicant’s SFIC was more recent. Newer case authorities should be preferred over older ones.

    (g) The Indian Central Goods and Services Tax Act 2017 (CGST Act) contained provisions that were ‘similar’ to the Australian GST Act and therefore Indian cases considering the CGST Act should be relied upon in interpreting the provisions of the (Australian) GST Act.[30] The Applicant submitted that:

    ‘In fact, the Indian court ruling aligns with prior judicial interpretations under earlier tax regimes (e.g. CENVAT Credit Rules) like Australian GST Act 1999 based on principles of value added tax regimes followed in mostly all countries based on English laws.’

    (h) The authorities cited by the Respondent were decided before the Banking Royal Commission, and therefore did not take into account the findings and outcomes of that forum, which were directly relevant to the Applicant’s circumstances. The Applicant contended that:[31]

    ‘... giving full effect to 2013 common law incomplete/foregone proceedings after ARC findings of banking misconduct in 2017 – 19, will lead to substantial infringement of legislative intent in 2025 and promote miscarriage of justice through following hopelessly outdated case precedents due to new code and revised NCCP Act 2009 after ARC in 2019.’

and

‘the respondent’s long-standing refusal of ITC claims is unfair, consistent with the principles of GST as a value-added tax and based on outdated legal precedents in pre-Australian Royal Commission enquiry into banking misconduct era promoting cascading effect of GST on to us as applicants.’[32]

  1. He contended that the Respondent’s submissions were therefore based on ‘incorrect and irrelevant’ principles and of ‘limited academic value’ such that the Tribunal should ignore them.[33]
  2. The Tribunal explained to Mr Surana at the hearing that the age of a decided case was not necessarily determinative of its relevance and authoritative status. For example, decisions of the Australian High Court which may have been decided some decades ago remain relevant to, and potentially binding on, other Courts and on tribunals and are properly cited by those Courts and tribunals.
  3. The Tribunal also told Mr Surana that it accepted that case law from some jurisdictions may be regarded as persuasive in the context of issues before the Tribunal. There are historic reasons for decisions of United Kingdom courts frequently being cited in Australian courts and Tribunals, and this extends to decisions of other jurisdictions where those decisions are relevant to the issues under consideration.[34]
  4. However the Tribunal is unwilling to place greater precedential weight on the Indian case authority cited by Mr Surana simply on the basis that the Indian case was decided more recently.[35] That case was said by the Applicant to establish the proposition that unutilised input tax credits can be refunded upon business closure even where not expressly covered by statute, such that taxpayers had the right to recover ‘stranded’ credits to improve cash flow during discontinuation.[36]
  5. The Tribunal is obliged to apply the provisions of the Australian GST Act. The Tribunal will have primary regard to case authorities which have considered those provisions and will be obliged to follow decisions of Australian courts which are binding on it. The Tribunal observes that many relevant case authorities[37] were decided after the introduction of the GST and considered provisions of the GST Act, including the definition of an ‘enterprise’ specifically under that legislation. These cases did not deal with banking matters and therefore any changes to banking law or regulation that occurred after they were decided do not affect the relevance of those cases to the question of whether the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise.
  6. The Tribunal also observes that based on the Applicant’s description of the Indian case authority, it does not appear to be relevant to this proceeding. The ability to obtain a refund from unutilised input tax credits is predicated on an entitlement to those credits in the first place. The dispute in this proceeding is whether the Applicant has established its entitlement to any of the input tax credits it claimed during the Relevant Periods.

Relevance of policy underlying the statutory provisions

  1. Mr Surana provided extensive submissions on his understanding of how value added and similar taxes, including the Australian GST, should operate. He referred to, and said that the Applicant relied on:
    (a) A submission made by Chartered Accountants Australia on 22 September 2008 to the Board of Taxation which referred to input tax relief as a fundamental notion in a VAT and made observations about the policy objective of Australia’s GST.[38]

    (b) A quote from the judgment of Hill J in HP Mercantile Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation[39] in which his Honour described the concept of value added taxation systems, of which GST was an example, observing that the ‘system of input tax credits thus ensures that while GST is a multi-stage tax, there will ordinarily be no cascading of tax.’

  2. Mr Surana suggested to the Tribunal during the hearing that the policy behind statutory provisions may or should take precedence over the language of the provisions. He said that legislation cannot override the government policy, which was not to burden small business with the cascading effect of value added taxes. The Tribunal does not accept this contention. The Tribunal explained to Mr Surana during the hearing that it was obliged to apply the relevant legislation, in accordance with case authorities although it recognised there might be differing views on policy matters, including as to whether legislation and policy were consistent.
  3. In Commissioner of Taxation v Consolidated Media Holdings Ltd the High Court said:[40]
    "This Court has stated on many occasions that the task of statutory construction must begin with a consideration of the [statutory] text" ... So must the task of statutory construction end. The statutory text must be considered in its context. That context includes legislative history and extrinsic materials. Understanding context has utility if, and in so far as, it assists in fixing the meaning of the statutory text. Legislative history and extrinsic materials cannot displace the meaning of the statutory text. Nor is their examination an end in itself.
  4. Reference to extrinsic material, such as Explanatory Memoranda, may be appropriate where there is uncertainty or ambiguity as to the meaning of the provision and it is relevant to consider the intention of Parliament as context for the enactment of the provision. However, for the reasons given below, the Tribunal believes that the issues in contention are resolved by reference to the facts and evidence of the Applicant’s circumstances. The Tribunal does not believe that there are matters of uncertainty or complexity relating to the interpretation of the relevant statutory provisions.

ISSUES AND CONTENTIONS

  1. The Respondent outlined its position as follows:
    (a) Its primary contention was that the Applicant had not been carrying on an enterprise during the Relevant Periods. Entitlement to an input tax credit is dependent on the making of a creditable acquisition, which in turn is dependent on the existence of a creditable purpose which requires the claimant to be carrying on an enterprise. If the Applicant had not been carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods this meant that input tax credits were not available to the Applicant without the Tribunal needing to consider further the characterisation or nature of amounts claimed.

    (b) Nevertheless, if the Tribunal found that the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise during any of the Relevant Periods, then the Respondent contended that the Legal Costs and Miscellaneous Costs were not consideration for creditable acquisitions made by the Applicant. The Legal Costs did not represent consideration for any taxable supply made to the Applicant, instead being a payment or payments of the ANZ Bank’s legal costs pursuant to orders of the NSW Supreme Court. Further there was inadequate evidence that the Miscellaneous Expenses represented creditable acquisitions of or taxable supplies to the Applicant.

    (c) Finally, if the Tribunal found that the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods and that the Legal Costs did represent consideration for a taxable supply made to the Applicant, then the Respondent contended that such a supply would be input taxed and the Applicant would be taken not to have a creditable purpose in relation to that acquisition, and therefore it would not be a creditable acquisition that could give rise to an input tax credit.

  2. The Applicant’s position was that:
    (a) The Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods, being a continuation of its prior business or enterprise, albeit a downsized business compared to that which it had previously conducted.

    (b) The Applicant rejected the Respondent’s characterisation of the litigation between the Applicant and the ANZ Bank, and also disputed some of the findings of the NSW Supreme Court. The Applicant did not agree that costs amounts were deducted from the Applicant’s bank accounts but viewed the ANZ Bank as having diverted funds held in a trust account, before the orders of the NSW Supreme Court were finalised. The Applicant disputed the Respondent’s contention that the Legal Costs were debited to the Applicant’s bank account in relation to the litigation. These amounts at most were ‘blackhole expenditure’ or ‘overheads’ passed on to the Applicant in the course of carrying on its enterprise.

    (c) In relation to the issue of the Legal Costs being an input taxed supply, the Applicant generally refuted the Respondent’s contentions and said that provision or receipt of services such as legal, accounting or taxation advice related to financial supplies are not considered to be financial supplies under the GST Act.[41] The Applicant drew an analogy between these amounts and ‘blackhole expenditure’ for income tax purposes.

RELEVANT LAW AND GUIDANCE

  1. An entity is entitled to the input tax credit for any creditable acquisition that it makes: section 11-20 of the A New Tax System (Goods and Services Tax) Act 1999 (Cth) (the GST Act).
  2. A ‘creditable acquisition’ is defined in section 11-5 as follows:

You make a creditable acquisition if:

(a) you acquire anything solely or partly for a *creditable purpose; and

(b) the supply of the thing to you is a *taxable supply; and

(c) you provide, or are liable to provide, *consideration for the supply; and

(d) you are *registered, or *required to be registered.

  1. An ‘acquisition’ is ‘any form of acquisition whatsoever’, including an acquisition of goods or services: section 11-10. It also includes an acquisition of something the supply of which is a ‘financial supply’: paragraph 11-10(2)(f).
  2. Section 11-15 defines the term ‘creditable purpose.’ Subsection 11-15(1) says:
    You acquire a thing for a creditable purpose to the extent that you

    acquire it in *carrying on your *enterprise.

  3. Section 9-20 provides that an ‘enterprise’ is ‘an activity, or series activities’ done ‘in the form of a business’ or ‘in the form of an adventure or concern in the nature of trade’ or in other contexts not relevant to the Applicant’s circumstances.[42] Subsection 9-20(3) provides that the fact that activities of an entity are limited to making supplies to members of the entity does not prevent those activities being in the form of a business or an adventure or concern in the nature of trade.
  4. A ‘business’ includes any ‘profession, trade, employment, vocation or calling’ other than an occupation as an employee. Carrying on an enterprise includes doing anything in the course of the commencement or termination of the enterprise: section 195-1.
  5. The Respondent referred the Tribunal to the decision of the High Court in Spriggs v Federal Commissioner of Taxation[43] which identified several indicia of the existence of a ‘business’, including the existence of a profit-making purpose, the scale of activities, the commercial character of transactions, and whether activities are systematic and organised.
  6. However, courts have held that the GST statutory definitions of an ‘enterprise’ and ‘carrying on an enterprise’ appear to be ‘substantially broader’ than that of carrying on a ‘business’ for the purposes of income tax law. This is apparent from the wording of the definitions and is consistent with the Explanatory Memorandum to the Bill which introduced the GST Act.[44]
  7. The Respondent has issued public guidance in the form of Miscellaneous Taxation Ruling MT 2006/1 ‘The New Tax System: the meaning of entity carrying on an enterprise for the purposes of entitlement to an Australian Business Number’ (MT2006/1). The Tribunal is not bound by the Respondent’s published guidance although consistency of decision-making suggests that it should be taken into account provided that it is otherwise consistent with the law.[45]
  8. Importantly, the case authorities and the Respondent’s guidance are in general agreement that there is no single test to determine whether an entity carries on an enterprise (or a business), that it is a matter of fact and degree, and what is required is a consideration and weighing of the facts and circumstances of the particular case.
  9. An entity will not acquire a thing for a creditable purpose to the extent that:
    (d) the acquisition relates to making supplies that would be input taxed: paragraph 11-15(2)(a).

    (e) the acquisition is of a private or personal nature: paragraph 11-15(2)(b).

  10. Subdivision 9-A defines what are ‘taxable supplies.’ Section 9-5 provides:
    You make a taxable supply if:

    (a) you make the supply for *consideration; and

    (b) the supply is made in the course or furtherance of an *enterprise that you *carry on; and

    (c) the supply is *connected with the indirect tax zone; and

    (d) you are *registered, or *required to be registered.

    However, the supply is not a *taxable supply to the extent that it is

    *GST-free or *input taxed.

  11. A ‘supply’ is any form of supply whatsoever: section 9-10. The term ‘consideration’ is defined in section 9-15 to include any payment, any act or any forbearance in connection with a supply of anything or in response to or for the inducement of a supply of anything.
  12. An input tax credit will not be attributable to a tax period where the entity does not hold a tax invoice for the creditable acquisition at the time of lodging their BAS for the period: subsection 29-10(3).

ENTITLEMENT TO INPUT TAX CREDITS

Was the Applicant carrying on an enterprise?

  1. The Respondent told the Tribunal that it accepts that the Applicant had carried on an enterprise in the past, that is, at some time prior to the Relevant Periods. However in relation to the Relevant Periods the Respondent contended that the relevant indicia of the conduct of an enterprise were absent. In particular:
    (a) Only seven ‘supplies’ (as defined for the purposes of the GST Act) were made by the Applicant during the Relevant Periods, and those seven supplies were all made between December 2018 and June 2020 to an associate of the Applicant, being Mr Surana’s self-managed superannuation fund (the Superannuation Fund).[46] Between 2016 and 2020 GST of approximately $350 was disclosed, suggesting sales or supplies of only (approximately) $3,600 over that period.[47]

    (b) The Applicant ‘ceased to exist’ from 2013 to 2018 (being the period after ASIC cancelled the Applicant’s registration as a corporation until its reinstatement). Even though the Applicant’s registration as a corporation was subsequently reinstated, as a matter of fact when the Applicant undertook any activities during the Relevant Periods, including purchases of the items comprising the Miscellaneous Expenses, this was at a time when the Applicant did not exist as a corporation.

    (c) Mr Surana was not registered as a tax agent with the TPB during the Relevant Periods and therefore was prohibited by law from providing tax advisory services during that time.

    (d) Although the Applicant had registered a trade mark (Trade Mark) in March 2011, this lapsed and was not renewed.

    (e) There was a lack of documentation or other evidence demonstrating that the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise when it entered into transactions recorded in its BAS.

    (f) The activity undertaken by the Applicant lacked significant commercial character, profit-making intention and repetition and regularity.

    (g) The scale of the Applicant’s activity was ‘relatively small’ and lacked similarity to activities conducted in the ordinary course of trade.

    (h) The Respondent raised a question about the loan or loans the subject of the ANZ Bank litigation. The Applicant had described this as involving a ‘business’ mortgage but on further review the Respondent believed that the litigation related, at least in part, to a loan for personal or domestic purposes of the Suranas.

  2. The Respondent accepted that the Applicant had demonstrated action taken in relation to possessing the relevant knowledge or skill for the conduct of an accounting practice.[48]
  3. The Applicant submitted that the Tribunal should find that it had been carrying on an enterprise during the Relevant Periods based on the following matters:
    (a) The attributes of an accounting and taxation advisory practice are very different to other profit-making enterprises such as retail businesses. The Applicant pointed to the business industry code (as used by the Respondent to describe a taxpayer’s main business activity) ANZSIC 69320 which relates to accounting, auditing, bookkeeping and tax agent services. Public accountancy practices are focussed on the provision of professional services rather than commercial operations. Professional accreditation and membership is required, which in turn necessitates ongoing professional development, unlike other profit-making entities. Adherence to professional standards and regulatory oversight by professional bodies, the need to hold specific licences (such as registration with the TPB) and other fiduciary and public interest obligations are also recognised attributes.

    (b) During the Relevant Periods Mr Surana undertook professional development activities and maintained, or obtained further accreditations or qualifications such as in MYOB and Xero. He continued to be registered as a member with the CPA.

    (c) Mr Surana was actively involved in engagement with relevant regulatory authorities during the Relevant Periods, on work directly related to maintaining and developing the Applicant’s accounting practice. He engaged with ASIC in relation to reinstatement of the Applicant’s corporate registration, including the preparation of historical records. He was also working on applications for registration as a tax agent, including management of the dispute with the TPB. Between 2016 and 2020 he had five proceedings under his direction and had also lodged complaints with the Financial Services Ombudsman and Office of Australian Information Commissioner. Together with his CPD commitments he estimated that he spent on average between 35 and 40 hours each week on these matters.[49]

    (d) The Applicant did have clients during the Relevant Periods to whom accounting services were provided, being the associated Superannuation Fund and a not-for-profit entity.[50] The Applicant had been approached by potential clients and had over 500 contacts on its LinkedIn page.

    (e) Although the Applicant had ceased to rent separate commercial premises, it had moved to a home-based business. This was consistent with the manner in which it operated when the business was originally established in 2009. The fact that it was operating from its director’s home did not mean that an enterprise was not being carried on.

    (f) The Applicant had downsized its activities during the Relevant Periods as a result of financial pressures, including those resulting from the litigation with the ANZ Bank. Mr Surana told the Tribunal that he sought to take a prudent approach to the Applicant’s finances and did not want to over expand. The Applicant had adhered to a business action plan to keep trading on a limited financial budget after the ANZ Bank litigation. The Applicant sold the car previously used in the business and bought a motorbike which was cheaper to run. He had not wanted to end up in bankruptcy which could preclude membership of relevant professional bodies or re-registration with the TPB. The fact that the Applicant had downsized did not mean that it had ceased carrying on an enterprise during this period.

    (g) Although the Applicant had lost its registration as a company with ASIC around 2013/2014, its registration was reinstated with retrospective effect in 2018. Therefore deregistration or gap in registration was effectively nullified and the Applicant’s corporate existence should be treated as having continued during the Relevant Periods.

    (h) It is not appropriate for the Respondent to look at the activities of the Applicant by reference only to limited blocks of time (such as the Relevant Periods). The Respondent should have regard to the wider time range within which activities occurred. Low levels of sales activity disclosed in BAS lodged in relation to the Relevant Periods were explicable by other factors, such as the Applicant being occupied with the Respondent’s audit and COVID-19 conditions.

    (i) The business questionnaire completed by the Applicant provided only limited information. For example, it did not reflect the fact that Mr Surana had been a registered tax agent for 5 years previously. Similarly the other records identified by the Respondent did not provide a full picture of the Applicant’s activities.

    (j) The Applicant acknowledged that it was unable to provide tax advisory services during the Relevant Periods because TPB registration had not been granted. It was also for this reason that the Applicant could not renew the Trade Mark, which had been registered when the Applicant was originally formed as a corporation, as the Trade Mark referred to tax services.

    (k) The Applicant had established the existence of goodwill associated with its accounting practice. The Applicant referred to the Respondent’s Taxation Ruling 2019/1[51] which suggested that where a company held assets which generate ongoing returns, activities which are limited to its ongoing management, ensuring compliance with ASIC regulatory requirements, making decisions about the holding of a static investment portfolio, the receipt and distribution or retention of income, and other matters of an administrative nature could be sufficient to amount to the carrying on of a business. The Applicant submitted that such assets would include intangibles such as goodwill and a trademark. Although goodwill was not recorded in the Applicant’s books, it was the most significant intangible asset in the Applicant’s enterprise. It had not been valued because the Applicant had not intended to sell that enterprise.

  4. The Applicant submitted that it had continued to operate its pre-existing business during the Relevant Periods, albeit on a smaller scale.
  5. The decision of the High Court in Spriggs sets out a number of factors which may be relevant to a determination of whether an entity is carrying on a business. In that case the Court was considering whether the amount of a fee was necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, in the context of the deductibility provisions of income tax legislation. Those factors are still relevant to the question of whether an entity is carrying on an enterprise for the purposes of the GST Act, an enterprise including an activity or series of activities done ‘in the form of a business.’ However as mentioned previously an ‘enterprise’ is potentially a wider concept than a ‘business’ [52] and the Tribunal therefore agrees in part with the Applicant that Spriggs does not address all matters of relevance.
  6. For the purposes of the definition of an ‘enterprise’ in section 9-20 it is not necessary for there to be repeated or continuous activities. However the use of the words ‘carrying on’ would ordinarily imply a repetition of acts.[53] The term ‘enterprise’ was defined widely because ‘GST is intended to have a broad base ... Activities of a wide nature are expected to be regarded as enterprises so that input tax credits will be available to them.’[54] The use of the words ‘in the form of’ extend the reach of an ‘enterprise’ to activities which are in the form of a business but which would not on the ordinary meaning of ‘business’ be so.[55]
  7. The Tribunal has considered the activities which the Applicant has demonstrated were undertaken in the Relevant Periods and how those activities are to be characterised and weighted for the purposes of the definition of an ‘enterprise’, including in the context of the continuity of an existing enterprise. To this extent the Tribunal agrees with the Applicant that it may be appropriate to look at activities and events involving the Applicant during periods outside the Relevant Periods in determining whether the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise during the Relevant Periods. Such activities or events may provide an explanation of or context for activities and events in the Relevant Periods. However, the question for the Tribunal is whether the Applicant was carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods. On balance, the Tribunal cannot find that an enterprise was being carried on by the Applicant in the Relevant Periods.
  8. A highly significant matter is the lack of evidence of clients or customers of the accounting practice which the Applicant said it was conducting, such that there was an absence of evidence of any services being provided by the Applicant during the Relevant Periods. In this regard:
    (a) Mr Surana told the Tribunal that the only customers of the Applicant during the Relevant Periods were the Superannuation Fund and one or more not-for-profit entities. Those not-for-profit entities were not identified and any work done for them appears to have been on a pro bono basis with no evidence of fee or other income being derived (and no documentation or other material evidencing the nature of services provided to those entities or when any services were provided).

    (b) Subsection 9-20(3) provides that, for the avoidance of doubt, the fact that the activities of an entity are limited to making supplies to members of the entity does not prevent those activities being in the form of a business or an adventure or concern in the nature of trade. However, even if regard is had to services said to have been provided by the Applicant to Mr Surana’s family superannuation fund, these are also very few in number and value and there is no indication of what services were provided.[56]

    (c) The number of contacts on the Applicant’s LinkedIn page was not necessarily indicative of engagement with current or prospective clients and did not indicate services being provided by the Applicant or a plan for services to be provided. The Applicant did not provide evidence in support of Mr Surana’s claim that the Applicant had been approached by potential clients during the Relevant Periods. The responses to the Respondent’s business profile questionnaire suggested potential means of sourcing customers (for example, by having a social media presence or cloud based operations providing access for overseas customers) but this was of a very high level or general nature and no ‘verified Business to Business’ customers had been identified.

    (d) Mr Surana is correct in stating that it would not be permissible for the Applicant to advertise or seek to provide tax services in the absence of a TPB registration. However the Applicant’s own description of its business goes beyond the provision of tax services to outline a range of other accounting and consultancy services. It is not clear why the Applicant could not have provided those ‘non-tax’ services to clients during the Relevant Periods, particularly in circumstances where its director Mr Surana stated that he had maintained his membership of appropriate professional bodies such as CA or CPA and had undertaken further qualifications related to such services.

    (e) Although the Tribunal acknowledges Mr Surana’s concern that the Respondent’s ‘business profile questionnaire’ gave a complete or accurate picture of the Applicant’s circumstances, the Tribunal does observe that he told the Respondent that the services provided by the Applicant also included management consulting, community health services/community health influencing blogging services and tax dispute resolution services. The Tribunal was unable to see any evidence of management consulting or community health services being provided during the Relevant Periods. The only demonstrated tax dispute resolution activities were the Applicant’s own involvement in the subject of the Previous Tribunal Proceeding and this proceeding, and potentially Mr Surana’s involvement in a dispute with the TPB.

    (f) Mr Surana suggested that his available time during the Relevant Periods and subsequently had been taken up with engaging with various regulators (including the Respondent, TPB and ASIC) and other business-related disputes. He said that the Respondent’s 2021 audit led the Applicant to suspend his CPA membership and tax agent registration reinstatement work because of what might be inferred as allegations of professional misconduct, negligence or incompetence.[57] The Applicant’s statement that it provided taxation dispute resolution services appears to refer to activities it undertook in relation to its own disputes, and those of Mr Surana, with regulatory bodies rather than assisting third parties. The Tribunal does not accept that engagement in disputes with regulatory bodies is necessarily probative of the carrying on of an enterprise (or the continuation of an existing enterprise) even where those disputes are related to matters such as business registrations with ASIC and TPB. The link between engaging in complaint processes with bodies such as the OAIC and FSO and the conduct of an accounting enterprise is even more uncertain.

  9. Other than Mr Surana stating an intention or wish for the Applicant to engage in profitable activities when registration with the TPB was obtained at a future date, it was not clear to the Tribunal what plans had existed in relation to the Relevant Periods for this to occur.
  10. The Applicant did not provide any evidence in support of its assertion that it held a significant intangible asset in the form of goodwill. The Applicant acknowledged that no goodwill had been recorded in its books and had never been valued. Further the public ruling cited by the Applicant talks about businesses or enterprises which comprise the holding of assets, however those assets generate ongoing returns and there is some form of activity in relation to the management and administration of those assets directed towards profit making. The Tribunal is unable to place weight on the Applicant’s assertion of the existence of goodwill, as a matter of fact or as an indicator of the Applicant carrying on an enterprise.
  11. The Tribunal has not placed significant weight on the deregistration of the Applicant as a corporation for a period prior to the subsequent reinstatement of its ASIC registration. That is, the Tribunal does not necessarily view the deregistration as fatal to the Applicant’s case. However even where the Tribunal accepts that the reinstatement had retrospective effect such that the Applicant is taken to have continued in existence during the Relevant Periods, the Tribunal must still look at the activities that were said to be undertaken in relation to the conduct of an accounting practice. For the reasons given above the Tribunal is not satisfied that there was evidence of a sufficient level of activity to constitute an enterprise.
  12. In reaching its decision the Tribunal placed no or less significance on the following matters:
    (a) In relation to an absence of employees of or contractors retained by the Applicant, the Tribunal believes that an accounting practice could be conducted by a sole trader or service provider who is a qualified accountant.

    (b) In relation the premises from which an accounting practice is conducted, the Tribunal believes it is quite possible such a practice be conducted from the owner’s residential premises, particularly in circumstances where financial constraints or personal circumstances make working from separate rented commercial premises difficult.

    (c) In relation to the size and scale of activity, the downsizing or scaling down of a business, even if substantial, is not necessarily conclusive of an enterprise having ceased.

    (d) In relation to the fact that the Applicant had not renewed its previously registered Trade Mark, this could be explained by the Applicant’s financial constraints as well as the Applicant not presently being permitted to promote itself as a registered tax agent.

    (e) The decisions of the NSW Supreme Court indicate that the primary loan secured by the mortgage over the Westmead Property (described as a ‘consumer mortgage’) was a home loan provided to the Suranas in 2004, with the mortgage being extended to cover a car loan. The Suranas subsequently provided personal guarantees over a business loan and overdraft facility taken out by the Applicant, with their obligations under those guarantees being secured by the mortgage.[58] The Tribunal acknowledges the Respondent’s submission that the litigation with the ANZ Bank might be regarded as related, at least in part, to personal or domestic financial obligations rather than business ones. However, the Tribunal does not regard time spent by Mr Surana on the ANZ Bank litigation as activity directed towards a business or enterprise.

    (f) In relation to COVID-19 lockdowns restricting the ability of the Applicant to provide services or undertake activities, the Tribunal acknowledges that COVID-19 conditions had an impact on many businesses. However in the circumstances of the Applicant the Relevant Periods mostly occurred prior to the commencement of the pandemic restrictions.[59] The Applicant also indicated that its operations were home-based and/or online by this time.

  13. In summary, although the Tribunal could accept the proposition that an enterprise might continue in existence, even where there has been a significant decrease in size or scale or a change in where or how it operates, the evidence in the present matter was not consistent with the Applicant carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods. There was no evidence of provision of services or undertaking of work for ‘clients’ other than a few limited activities for the Suranas’ Superannuation Fund which may be compliance related. There was no evidence of other activities such as the management of assets for income generating or profit-making purposes. The Tribunal does not accept that Mr Surana’s engagement with multiple regulators and management of disputes, although it occupied a significant amount of his time, constituted activities in the form of a business or an adventure or concern in the nature of trade.

Was the Applicant entitled to input tax credits for the Legal Costs?

  1. The Tribunal has determined that the Applicant was not carrying on an enterprise during the Relevant Periods. In such circumstances the Legal Costs could not be in relation to a ‘creditable acquisition’ under section 11-5 of the GST Act because the Applicant could not have acquired the subject matter of the Legal Costs for a ‘creditable purpose’ under section 11-15. A thing is only acquired by a person for a creditable purpose to the extent that it is acquired in the carrying on of an enterprise by the person. This is sufficient reason of itself for finding that the Applicant was not entitled to input tax credits in relation to the Legal Costs.
  2. However both parties made substantial submissions relating to the statutory criteria for a credible acquisition other than the carrying on of an enterprise.[60] The Applicant also contended that obtaining tax invoice documentation from the ANZ Bank should have resolved the dispute over most of the input tax credits claimed for the Relevant Periods. Although the Tribunal does not agree with the Applicant that the question of ‘carrying on an enterprise’ was irrelevant or should have been excluded from consideration by the Tribunal, the Tribunal believes it is still appropriate to address the submissions made by the Applicant about the nature and characterisation for GST purposes of the Legal Costs. For the reasons set out below, the Tribunal’s view is that the Legal Costs were not consideration for a supply to the Applicant of anything acquired by the Applicant.
  3. The Tribunal has had particular regard to the following documents provided to the Tribunal:
    (a) The Tax Invoice dated 4 October 2013 rendered by Laing & Simmons to ANZ Banking Group relating to ‘Laing & Simmons Real Estate Sales Commission’ and ‘Auction Advertising Fees’ for a property in Westmead in the amount of $13,163.15 (which was stated to include GST of $1,196.65).

    (b) Invoices dated between 31 July 2012 and 31 October 2014 rendered by Kemp Strang to ANZ Banking Group relating to ‘professional fees’ and ‘disbursements’ re RQA Accountants Group.[61]

    (c) An invoice dated 26 September 2012 rendered by Kemp Strang to ANZ Banking Group relating to ‘professional fees’ and ‘disbursements’ re RQA Accountants Pty Ltd – Local Court Proceedings.[62]

    (d) Numerous items of email correspondence between Mr Surana and staff at Laing & Simmons and ANZ relating to these invoices and their subject matter.[63]

    (e) A document entitled ‘Annexure 2 - Details of Abridged Tax Invoices (2012-2014) Released by ANZ on 4 August 2022.’ This document appears to have been prepared by the Applicant or Mr Surana and includes the notation ‘Gross Legal Costs, Security Sale Commission, and Incidentals Passed by ANZ Bank to RQA (Excludes Fees, lnterest, and other Incidental Costs.’[64] It refers to amounts charged to an account ‘Business Loan Ending 515.’

    (f) A copy of a statement for a Business Loan account held in the name of the Applicant with ANZ with an account number ending 515, for the period February to August 2013 indicating several transactions described as ‘Loan Enforcement Expense’ in amounts which reflect amounts charged under six of the Kemp Strang invoices as above.[65]

    (g) Copies of statements or printouts of online statements for a cheque account held in the name of the Applicant with ANZ with an account number ending 926, for periods from December 2013 to December 2014 indicating several transactions described as ‘Details Advised Separately’ or ‘Solicitor’s Fee’ in amounts which reflect amounts charged under another eleven of the Kemp Strang invoices.[66]

    (h) Extracts of ‘director current account transaction’ records as reconstructed by the Applicant when its ASIC registration was reinstated. These records show debits for amounts described as ‘Legal Trust Account Expensed following NSWSC Order of 6 March 2017’ in amounts which correspond to amounts in some of the Kemp Strang invoices.[67]

  4. Mr Surana told the Tribunal that he did not agree with the Respondent’s description and characterisation of the litigation between the ANZ Bank, the Applicant and the Suranas. Mr Surana also suggested that the reported decisions of the NSW Supreme Court did not provide a complete or accurate picture of the dispute and the ultimate outcomes of the litigation. The Tribunal acknowledges that Mr Surana might not have agreed with the outcomes of the litigation in that Court, or that he believes that different outcomes might have arisen had the litigation occurred after the report of the Banking Royal Commission. However the Tribunal must place primary weight on the decisions of the Court as reported in relation to the findings, outcomes and orders in the litigation between the ANZ Bank, the Applicant and the Suranas.
  5. In Australian & New Zealand Banking Group v RQA Accountants Pty Ltd & Ors[68] Adamson J provided the following summary of factual matters:
    1. Mr and Mrs Surana obtained a home loan for about $310,000 from the ANZ for the purchase of the Property in 2004 (the Home Loan). It was a term of the home loan that a mortgage be granted to the ANZ as security (the Consumer Mortgage). Mr and Mrs Surana subsequently obtained a loan in October 2009 in the amount of $36,000 to buy a car (the Supplementary Loan). This loan was also secured by the Consumer Mortgage.
    2. Mr and Mrs Surana are both accountants. They obtained a licence to practise as tax accountants in Australia and to this end they incorporated RQA. From early 2009, the defendants operated the business of RQA from the Property but later, in August 2010, sought to lease premises from which they could operate the business (the Premises). They approached the ANZ for finance and RQA entered into a business loan agreement dated 18 October 2010 (the Business Agreement) for working capital up to an amount of about $30,000 for RQA's business (the Business Loan) and an overdraft facility (the Overdraft Facility) up to an amount of about $20,000. The $30,000 was drawn down on 6 August 2010. Weekly repayments were required pursuant the Business Loan but not by the terms of the Overdraft Facility. Mr and Mrs Surana guaranteed the indebtedness of RQA to the ANZ under the Business Loan and the Overdraft Facility.
    3. RQA was granted a lease of the Premises in about August 2010 (the Lease). It was a term of the Lease that RQA provide a bank guarantee in the amount of $15,950 to secure RQA's obligations...
  6. The ANZ issued a bank guarantee on 13 August 2010 (the Bank Guarantee) and transferred funds from the overdraft account to another account 905xxxxx9 in the name of RQA, as security for the Bank Guarantee.
  7. Adamson J also set out that:
    (a) The Suranas defaulted under the Home Loan and Supplementary Loan in August 2011.

    (b) The Bank Guarantee was called on in November 2011, with the ANZ Bank releasing the full amount of the Bank Guarantee by debiting the account 905xxxxx9 in the name of RQA. In July 2012 the Applicant filed a statement of claim against the ANZ Bank in the Local Court, and the ANZ Bank commenced proceedings in the NSW Supreme Court for possession of the Westmead Property.

    (c) The Consumer Mortgage secured monies owing under the Home Loan, the Supplementary Loan, any other amounts owed by the Suranas to the ANZ Bank (which included amounts outstanding under the Business Loan and Overdraft Facility) and ‘enforcement expenses’ (as defined in the Consumer Mortgage and Business Loan).

  8. The orders made by Adamson J included the following:
    (a) An order for possession of the Westmead Property against the Applicant and Mr Surana.

    (b) An order that RQA and the Suranas pay the costs of the ANZ Bank’s motion for summary judgment for possession of the Westmead Property, striking out of the defence of the Applicant and dismissal of the cross-claim of the Applicant and Suranas.[69]

  9. In Australian & New Zealand Banking Group v RQA Accountants Pty Ltd [70] Adams J refused an application filed by Mr Surana for a stay of execution of a writ of possession which followed the decision of Adamson J. Adams J made an order for Mr Surana[71] to pay the ANZ Bank’s costs of the stay application/motion.
  10. In Surana v ANZ Banking Group Limited[72] the NSW Court of Appeal dismissed a summons filed by Mr Surana seeking leave to appeal from earlier orders of Adamson J and Adams J. On the day of the hearing of the summons the Suranas’ advised the Court that they wished to discontinue the summons application and sought an order that they would not be liable for costs. The Court nevertheless ordered that they should pay costs.
  11. On the basis of these three reported decisions and the documentary evidence set out in paragraph 89 the Tribunal concludes that:
    (a) The invoices rendered by Kemp Strang to the ANZ Bank dated between 31 July 2012 and 31 October 2014 were for amounts payable by the ANZ Bank to Kemp Strang for legal services provided by Kemp Strang to the ANZ Bank in relation to the NSW Supreme Court litigation, and disbursements incurred by the ANZ Bank in that litigation. Kemp Strang was retained by, and acting for, the ANZ Bank in this litigation.

    (b) The Applicant and the Suranas were parties to that litigation but the invoices did not relate to any legal or other services provided to the Applicant or the Suranas.

    (c) The NSW Supreme Court made orders for the Applicant and/or the Suranas to pay the costs of the ANZ Bank arising from the various applications made during the litigation in that forum.

    (d) The terms of the various loan and security contractual arrangements between the ANZ Bank, the Applicant and the Suranas required the Applicant and the Suranas to meet enforcement expenses incurred by the ANZ Bank in enforcing its security.

    (e) Between February and August 2013 the ANZ Bank deducted amounts referable to the amounts of some of the Kemp Strang invoices from the Business Loan bank account (number ending in 515) held by the Applicant with the ANZ Bank.

    (f) Between December 2013 and December 2014 the ANZ Bank deducted amounts referable to the amounts of some of the Kemp Strang invoices from the cheque account (number ending in 926) held by the Applicant with the ANZ Bank.

    (g) Around April 2014 the ANZ Bank deducted further amounts from the Applicant’s cheque account for stamp duty arising on a transfer of land, which appears to be the Westmead Property.

  12. When the ANZ Bank deducted amounts from or charged amounts to the Applicant’s business loan account for legal costs and enforcement expenses the Applicant was not ‘reimbursing’ the ANZ Bank for expenses or acquisitions otherwise belonging to the Applicant. The legal services provided by Kemp Strang were provided to the ANZ Bank. No legal or other services or things were acquired by the Applicant by it satisfying the costs orders of the NSW Supreme Court. The recording or labelling of amounts by the Applicant in the ‘director current account’ as ‘legal trust account expensed’ does not turn these amounts into expenditure incurred by the Applicant for legal or other services. Other entities might have provided services to the ANZ Bank when the ANZ Bank enforced its security over the Westmead Property. However, no services or rights or things were acquired by the Applicant through the ANZ Bank enforcing its security. The amounts deducted from the Applicant’s bank account were not consideration for anything acquired by or supplied to the Applicant.
  13. In the Objection Decision the Respondent also set out a position that based on the dates of the Kemp Strang invoices for the Legal Costs the Applicant had not claimed input tax credits within the four-year time limit provided under Division 93 of the GST Act. This matter was not pressed by either party in their respective SFICs or at the hearing.

Was the Applicant entitled to input tax credits for the Miscellaneous Costs?

  1. The Tribunal has determined that the Applicant was not carrying on an enterprise during the Relevant Periods. In such circumstances the Miscellaneous Costs could not be in relation to a ‘creditable acquisition’ such that input tax credits arose to the Applicant under the GST Act.
  2. Further, if the Tribunal had found that the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise of a boutique accounting and tax advisory practice, the Tribunal would not have been satisfied on the basis of the very limited evidence provided by the Applicant that the Miscellaneous Costs represented amounts paid for things acquired in carrying on such an enterprise.
  3. ‘Tax invoices’ are not required for acquisitions below a certain value, that value currently being $75 (exclusive of GST).[73] The majority of the Miscellaneous Costs are below that threshold.[74] However the Tribunal must still be satisfied that the Applicant acquired those items for a creditable purpose, that is, to some extent in carrying on an enterprise. The Applicant provided summaries[75] of the Miscellaneous Costs in relation to which it sought input tax credits, but did not provide receipts or other supporting documentation. The Tribunal accepts that some time has passed since the acquisitions were made and the need to keep receipts for small purchases such as a few dollars’ worth of fuel or stationery may not have been front of mind at the time of purchase. However, it would not be unreasonable to expect documentation to exist for insurance policies and telecommunications subscriptions or purchases.
  4. In many cases the descriptions provided for the items the subject of the Miscellaneous Costs are very general[76] such that it cannot be determined exactly what was acquired, and therefore whether the items acquired were for business or personal use. In other instances the subject matter of the expenditure is clearer,[77] but the Tribunal cannot determine whether the goods or services were acquired for the purpose of an accounting practice, for other business purposes or for personal use. Some of the Miscellaneous Costs are identified with references applied by the Applicant such as ‘Bunnings – LRF Dispute’ or ‘Australia Post – RQA Reinstatement.’ Such labels provided by the Applicant are not of themselves sufficient to demonstrate a nexus to an enterprise. The Tribunal acknowledges the evidence of Mr Surana that he was obliged to sell the car that had originally been purchased for the business and instead acquire a motorbike which was more cost efficient. However, this was insufficient to demonstrate any nexus between the expenditure and the carrying on of an accounting practice as contended for by the Applicant.
  5. The Tribunal therefore finds that input tax credits do not arise to the Applicant in the Relevant Periods in relation to the Miscellaneous Costs.

Legal Costs as an input taxed supply

  1. Based on its finding that the Applicant was not carrying on an enterprise in the Relevant Periods, and in the alternative that the Legal Costs did not represent consideration for a taxable supply to the Applicant, it is not necessary to consider the question of whether the Legal Costs related to an input taxed supply.
  2. In relation to the Applicant’s submission that the Legal Costs might be regarded as blackhole expenditure or treated in a manner similar to blackhole expenditure:
    (a) The Objection Decision under review in this proceeding relates to amended assessments of net amounts of GST, and whether input tax credits arise under the GST Act. Blackhole expenditure was not raised by the Applicant as a ground of objection. Neither the Objection Decision nor those amended assessments relate to income tax.

    (b) The Applicant’s submissions did not provide any intelligible basis for adopting principles relating to blackhole expenditure in an analysis of the GST provisions.

OTHER PROCEDURAL MATTERS

Model litigant complaint in Applicant’s SFIC

  1. The Applicant stated the following in its SFIC:[78]
    On 7th May 2025, the respondent filed a 66-paragraph advance SFIC as opposed to the common procedure of the applicant filing a SFIC first under standing practice direction under ART Act 2024. The applicant’s SFIC scheduled to be filed on 16 April 2025 had to be postponed due to delays in approval of ART summons as suggested by on duty Registrar [Name]. This was clearly communicated to the respondent who simply proceeded with filing of advance SFIC despite being aware of delays in summons. ...

    The respondents prematurely timed advance SFIC of 7-May-2025 prepared before expiry of 11-June-2025 ART issued summons deadline to [Bank] aims to oppose the applicant’s claim for ITC relief.

  2. The Applicant then complained that the Respondent’s SFIC repeated matters that had been raised during the audit process, namely the questions of whether the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise, whether amounts constituted consideration for a supply and whether there was an input taxed supply. The Applicant stated that the Respondent had not followed model litigant principles.
  3. The Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to make decisions or offer a remedy relating to ‘model litigant’ matters but believes it is appropriate to make the following observations as they relate to Tribunal directions and processes:
    (a) Pursuant to Tribunal directions of 18 February 2025 the Respondent was directed to file its SFIC and material and evidence by 7 May 2025. This direction was not varied by the Tribunal until further directions were made on 18 May 2025. It may be that there was delay on the part of the Tribunal in making varied directions. However, if the Respondent had not filed an SFIC by 7 May 2025 it would have been in breach of existing Tribunal directions. The Respondent explained this to the Applicant in an email dated 6 May 2025.[79]

    (b) There is no requirement under the ART Act for an applicant to file its SFIC first. It is a matter for the Tribunal to determine dates for filing based on the circumstances of the case. The applicant usually files its SFIC first as it is the party that has made the application to the Tribunal. However, in some matters involving a self-represented applicant the Tribunal may ask the respondent to file its materials first to assist the applicant in preparing their case.[80]

    (c) Following the making of further directions on 18 May 2025, the Applicant was given the opportunity to file its SFIC by 9 July 2025 with the Respondent to file by 29 July 2025.

    (d) The Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant suffered any disadvantage or procedural unfairness as a consequence of the Respondent filing an SFIC on 7 May 2025. If anything, it gave the Applicant greater detail and understanding of the Respondent’s case prior to the Applicant being required to file its own SFIC.

    (e) There is nothing untoward in the Respondent’s SFIC reflecting matters that were in dispute at audit and objection. The Tribunal would have a greater concern if the Respondent sought to raise entirely new matters after objection and prior to the hearing. Further, in this case the Tribunal believes that the three issues relating to entitlement to input tax credits are the relevant issues in dispute in this proceeding.

Post hearing matters

  1. As discussed above, prior to the hearing the Tribunal had made directions for the filing of key documents and evidence by the parties including:
    (a) On 18 May 2025 directions were made for the Applicant to lodge with the Tribunal and serve on the Respondent its SFIC and any material and evidence upon which it intended to rely by 8 July 2025, with the Respondent to file its SFIC and any further material and evidence by 29 July 2025.

    (b) On 8 August 2025 directions were made for the Applicant to lodge with the Tribunal any written submissions it wished the Tribunal to consider four weeks prior to the hearing date, with the Respondent to file any written submissions two weeks prior.

  2. In its hearing certificate dated 8 August 2025 the Applicant indicated that it wished to call Mrs Surana as an ‘Expert Witness – BAS Agent.’ Persons who appear as expert witnesses before the Tribunal are required to comply with the Administrative Review Tribunal (Expert Evidence) Practice Direction 2024. Such a witness must provide impartial assistance to the Tribunal on matters relevant to their area of knowledge or experience and is not an advocate for a party. The Tribunal appreciates that the Applicant may not have understood the difference between a lay and expert witness.
  3. The substantive hearing of this matter was listed for 12 November 2025. As the Applicant had not filed any opening submissions as of 15 October 2025, the Tribunal contacted the Applicant who confirmed that it had nothing further to file. The Respondent also elected not to file written opening submissions.[81]
  4. On 11 November 2025 the Tribunal received from the Applicant a document described as a ‘witness statement’ of Mrs Surana.[82] Attached to this statement was a copy of a listing notice from the Tribunal sent to the Applicant in relation to an ADR event that took place in November 2019 in relation to the Previous AAT Proceeding. Mrs Surana’s witness statement addressed matters that were apparently discussed at that ADR event, which she had attended with Mr Surana on behalf of the Applicant. In her statement she expressed her view that because certain issues raised during ADR had since been addressed it was unfair for further input tax credits to be denied to the Applicant.
  5. The Tribunal had not given leave to the Applicant to file further evidence or submissions. Filing an additional witness statement the day before the hearing was also unfair to the Respondent.
  6. On the basis of the Applicant’s hearing certificate the Tribunal had made arrangements for Mrs Surana to participate in the hearing and be contacted on the mobile phone number provided in the hearing certificate. The Tribunal was advised at the beginning of the hearing that Mrs Surana was present in the same room as Mr Surana and would participate via his video link. The Tribunal could see Mrs Surana at various times during the hearing seated beside Mr Surana and they appeared to speak to each other on occasion. As it had been indicated in the hearing certificate that Mrs Surana might give evidence, the Tribunal asked her to make an affirmation at the beginning of the hearing.
  7. The hearing proceeded and the Applicant did not call Mrs Surana to give evidence.
  8. The Tribunal reserved its decision at the conclusion of the hearing on 12 November 2025.
  9. On 14 November 2025 the Tribunal was copied by Mr Surana into an email he sent to the Respondent’s representatives, which attached an email chain between the parties. That email chain indicated that at some point after the hearing Mr Surana had contacted the Respondent’s representatives in relation to scheduling a further hearing for the purpose of Mrs Surana giving evidence. The Respondent’s representatives had indicated to Mr Surana that they would not oppose a further hearing if it was only for the purpose of hearing evidence that Mrs Surana had intended to give at the original hearing.
  10. Mr Surana’s email also claimed that Mrs Surana had been prevented from giving evidence by the Respondent’s representative and that the Applicant had objected to the Respondent being given ‘extensive’ time to address the issue of whether the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise. Mr Surana repeated a number of claims relating to the TPB Proceeding and his engagement with the TPB, as well as repeating matters relating to the Banking Royal Commission and his litigation with the ANZ Bank. Mr Surana said that Mrs Surana should be allowed to make an ‘oral presentation’ to the Tribunal on the basis that she was a witness to these matters, her investment in the Applicant’s business and that she had ‘benefitted from Mr Surana’s guidance from time to time over the several decades to shape her accounting career and service Australian workplaces.’
  11. The Tribunal appreciates the Respondent’s openness to scheduling a further hearing however the Tribunal would not have given leave to reopen or resume the hearing of this matter for the following reasons:
    (a) Substantive hearings are expected to be final and it would be highly unusual for a hearing to be reopened. The Tribunal’s resources are limited and hearing time must be available for the range of parties who have matters before the Tribunal. Reopening hearings would not be consistent with the Tribunal’s objective as set out in section 9 of the ART Act to ensure that applications are resolved quickly and with as little cost as appropriate.

    (b) The Tribunal does not agree that Mrs Surana was stopped from giving evidence. The Tribunal believes that Mr Surana is referring to an instance where he was being cross-examined by the Respondent’s representative and sought to ask Mrs Surana to assist with the response. It was appropriate for the Respondent’s representative to request that Mr Surana answer the question that was put to him during cross-examination.

    (c) Although the Tribunal could have simply refused to receive Mrs Surana’s statement as received on 11 November 2025 on the basis that the Applicant had not been given leave to file additional material, the Tribunal did not do so. However, the Tribunal’s view of Mrs Surana’s statement is that it is not relevant to this proceeding on the basis that it essentially addresses matters that were canvassed during an ADR event in a different proceeding, being the Previous Tribunal Proceeding. In addition to the issue of relevance, Tribunal led ADR events are held on the basis that matters discussed between the parties are confidential and must not be relied upon in any subsequent hearing (except in limited circumstances where the Tribunal has given leave to do so).

    (d) The Tribunal made arrangements for Mrs Surana to attend the hearing and for her to make her affirmation as to the truth of any evidence she would give. The Applicant either chose not to call Mrs Surana or forgot to do so at the hearing. Towards the end of the hearing the Member explained to the parties that the next step would be for the Member to consider the materials that had been filed and the submissions and evidence put to the Tribunal during the hearing for the purposes of providing a final decision to the parties. The Member specifically asked the parties whether there were any further matters the parties wished to raise before the Member concluded the hearing. The matter of Mrs Surana giving evidence was not raised.

  12. Mr Surana sent a further letter to the Tribunal on 20 November 2025 in which he said that the Applicant would ‘decline second hearing request by the respondent which will add no further value.’ He again set out a number of matters relating to the Applicant’s dispute with the ANZ Bank and referring to an ADR process involving the TPB.
  13. The Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant would have suffered any procedural unfairness had the Tribunal refused to allow Mrs Surana to return at a later date to give oral evidence. Noting that Mrs Surana was present during the hearing, there may have been potential unfairness to the Respondent if the Applicant had been given leave to reopen its evidence after the scheduled hearing had concluded. In addition to the extensive written submissions and correspondence filed by the Applicant, the Tribunal agreed to have regard to Mrs Surana’s written witness statement.
  14. The issue of the time allowed to the parties to make oral submissions during the hearing is not relevant to Mrs Surana’s witness statement but the Tribunal wishes to address Mr Surana’s concerns. There is no formula or rule for the time to be allowed to a party to make submissions, although the Tribunal is able to make directions or orders limiting the facts, issues and evidence it considers.[83] The procedure of the Tribunal is within the discretion of the Tribunal,[84] however the Tribunal must ensure that a party is given a ‘reasonable opportunity’ to present their case, make submissions and adduce evidence.[85] The Tribunal believes that Mr Surana was given an appropriate amount of time during the hearing to present the Applicant’s case in relation to the Objection Decision. Again, his oral submissions were in addition to the extensive written materials filed by the Applicant.
  15. To the extent that Mr Surana wished to discuss matters relating to his personal engagement with the TPB or other litigation in which the Applicant has been involved, the Tribunal has indicated above the limited extent to which it believes such matters are relevant to its review of the Objection Decision. The Tribunal repeats its prior statement that the purpose of this proceeding is to conduct a review of the Objection Decision, which requires a determination of whether the Applicant was entitled to input tax credits in the Relevant Periods. The hearing of this application is not a forum for agitating matters relating to Mr Surana’s engagement with the TPB or the outcomes of the Banking Royal Commission. The Tribunal has had regard to particular documentary evidence arising from the litigation between the Applicant and the ANZ Bank to the extent that this was relevant to the characterisation of amounts in respect of which input tax credits were claimed in the Relevant Periods.

CONCLUSION

  1. The length of these reasons reflects the fact that this proceeding was made unnecessarily complicated by submissions on matters that were not relevant to the issues of the entitlement of the Applicant to input tax credits in the Relevant Periods and the refund of tax that would have been generated by those credits.
  2. For the reasons given above the Applicant has not satisfied the Tribunal that it was carrying on an enterprise during the Relevant Periods, even if it had carried on an enterprise in the past or intended to do so in the future should circumstances permit. On that basis the Applicant is not entitled to input tax credits in the Relevant Periods.
  3. The Tribunal has also found that even where the Applicant had been carrying on an enterprise of an accounting practice during the Relevant Periods, the Tribunal would not have been satisfied that the Legal Costs and Miscellaneous Costs gave rise to input tax credits for the Applicant.
  4. The Objection Decision under review is therefore affirmed.
