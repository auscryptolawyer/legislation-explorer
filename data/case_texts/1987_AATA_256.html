ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION - AMENDED ASSESSMENT - whether applicants had made full and true disclosure of all material facts necessary for assessment pursuant to sub-s170(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - whether full and true disclosure must be constituted by information contained in income tax returns alone - whether amended assessment was to correct a mistake of fact within the meaning of sub-s170(3) of Act - onus of proof on a review of assessment.

Words & Phrases: "mistake of fact"

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, sub-ss.170(2), 170(2)(a), 170(2)(b), 170(3)

Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986 (Act No 46 of 1986) s20

REASONS FOR DECISION

6 July, 1987 Deputy President RA Layton

Mr DJ Trowse (Member)

Dr DB Williams (Member)

  1. Both of these applications for review which were heard together at the request of the parties involve review of decisions made by the Commissioner of Taxation to issue an amended assessment in respect of each applicant, pursuant to sub-ss.170(2) and (3) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act").
  2. At the hearing, the applicants were represented by counsel, Mr Adams and the respondent was represented by counsel, Mr McCullough. The documentation before the Tribunal consisted of the documents lodged pursuant to the modified s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T documents") and, in addition, an agreed statement of facts tendered at the hearing (Exhibit A1).
  3. Through a combination of the agreed statement of facts and the information contained in the T documents, the following matters are not in dispute between the parties.
  4. The applicants are husband and wife and, together with their children, are beneficiaries in a discretionary Family Trust (see supplementary T documents, pp. 20 ff). In addition, the applicants are the sole shareholders and directors of the Trustee Company for the Family Trust. The Trust deed was executed on 31 October 1978 and a copy had been provided to the Commissioner of Taxation on 1 July 1981 (see supplementary T documents, p.18). Personal taxation returns for the applicants, as well as Family Trust and the Trustee Company had previously been lodged since at least 1981. The amended assessment the subject of this review relates to the financial year ending 30 June 1982.
  5. On 28 February 1983, the applicants' tax agent ("W") delivered to the respondent the personal tax returns for the applicants for the financial year ending 30 June 1982. Those returns did not refer to any income either taxpayer had_ been allocated from any Trust.
  6. On 10 March 1983, the applicants' tax agent delivered to the respondent a letter dated 9 March 1983 written in the following terms:

"Taxation Dept 9-3-83

Adelaide

Dear Sir,

R. [husband] 567.348.813

A.M. [wife] 567.409.267

The Dept is advised of the following income that should be included in the 1982 Income Tax Returns of the abovenamed:-

The income is from the [name of family trust] and the amount to be included in the returns of the above for 1982 is as follows

R. [husband] $5710

A.M. [wife] $5710

These figures have been advised only since the lodgement of the 1982 Returns of the abovenamed.

Yours Faithfully

W

  1. On 18 March 1983, the respondent issued assessments to the applicants, which assessments did not bring to account the amounts of trust income referred to in Ws letter dated 9 March 1983.
  2. Also on 18 March 1983, AM signed the income tax return for the Trustee Company, as well as the income tax return for the Family Trust. These two returns were received by the respondent on 21 March 1983. The Trust return verified that the applicants' share of income was that which was stated in W's letter of 9 March 1983. It is also to be noted that item 23 in the Trust return which asks the question, "Was the income of the trust estate distributed at the discretion of the trustee?" was indicated to be "Yes" and that item 23 provided, "If YES, attach a signed and dated copy of the trustee's resolution". This trustee resolution dated 28 June 1982 was not attached to the return, but was provided by the applicants at this hearing (Exhibit R1).
  3. On 18 April 1983, the respondent issued the amended assessments which are the subject of review, which then included the Trust income referred to in W's letter of 9 March 1983 as taxable income.
  4. It is agreed between the parties that the employee of the respondent who determined the applicants' taxable incomes for the purposes of the assessments dated 18 March 1983 was, at the time of his determination, unaware of W's letter dated 9 March 1983.
  5. It is upon those facts that this review is to be determined.
  6. The central issues in these applications for review are whether or not the applicants have made full and true disclosure of all material facts necessary for the assessment of taxation pursuant to sub-s170(2) of the Assessment Act and, if they have, whether an amendment to their assessments increasing their liability to pay tax, was an amended assessment to correct a mistake of fact within the meaning of sub-s170(3) of the Assessment Act.
  7. Mr McCullough, for the respondent, presented a forceful argument that the Commissioner was empowered to amend the applicants' assessments pursuant to sub-s170(2) of the Assessment Act or, in the alternative, pursuant to sub-s170(3) of the Assessment Act.

14. The section as it stood at the relevant time (being prior to amendments to that section by s.20 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986 (Act No 46 of 1986)) was as follows:

"170. (1) The Commissioner may, subject to this section, at any time amend any assessment by making such alterations therein or additions thereto as he thinks necessary, notwithstanding that tax may have been paid in respect of the assessment.

(2) Where a taxpayer has not made to the Commissioner a full and true disclosure of all the material facts -necessary for his assessment, and there has been an avoidance of tax, the Commissioner may -

(a) where he is of opinion that the avoidance of tax is due to fraud or evasion - at any time; and

(b) in any other case - within 6 years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable under the assessment,

amend the assessment by making such alterations therein or additions thereto as he thinks necessary to correct an error in calculation or a mistake of fact or to prevent avoidance of tax as the case may be.

(3) Where a taxpayer has made to the Commissioner a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment, and an assessment is made after that disclosure, no amendment of the assessment increasing the liability of the taxpayer in any particular shall be made except to correct an error in calculation or a mistake of fact; and no such amendment shall be made after the expiration of 3 years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable under that assessment."

(Balance of sub-section is not relevant)

15. Mr McCullough's primary submission was that the applicants had not made to the Commissioner a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment. His argument ran that the onus of proving that there had been a full and true disclosure lay on the applicants, and that there had been neither a full nor a true disclosure at the time of the assessment by the Commissioner on 18 March 1983 because of the following factors, namely, the omission of the Trust income from the personal returns of the applicants contrary to the declarations contained on their personal returns; the failure to comply with regs. 9 and 10 of the Income Tax Regulations; and further, that the letter of 9 March 1983 from W did not rectify the inaccuracy and insufficiency of disclosure of the personal returns.

  1. For the applicants, Mr Adams argued that a full and true disclosure had been made. Although the applicants admitted that both an inaccuracy and a lack of fullness of disclosure existed in their personal returns, it was submitted on their behalf that such inaccuracy or insufficiency was rectified by the information contained in the later letter from their tax agent dated 9 March 1983. Mr Adams submitted that the Commissioner had before him, as at 18 March 1983, all of the material facts necessary for his assessment, being the same information as he had before him at the time of the issuing of the amended assessment. Further, Mr Adams submitted that neither the decided cases nor the Regulations indicated that full and true disclosure must be constituted by the information contained in income tax returns alone and finally, he submitted that any information as contained in either the Family Trust return, the Trustee Company return or, alternatively, the resolution of the trustees, was merely a confirmation of the information contained in the letter of 9 March 1983.
  2. In considering these submissions, it is necessary to have regard to the appropriate onus of proof on a review of assessment. The case of McAndrew v The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 62; (1956), 98 CLR 263 is authority for the proposition that, on such a review, the onus is on a taxpayer to satisfy the Tribunal that full and true disclosure has been made pursuant to sub-s170(2) of the Assessment Act. In this case, that onus falls squarely on the applicants.
  3. The meaning of the phrase "full and true disclosure" has been discussed in a number of leading cases. The expression is a broad one. In The Scottish Australian Mining Company Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1950] HCA 16; (1950), 81 CLR 188, Williams J, at p198 said that the expression imposed ".... the duty of disclosing every fact which he knows or is capable of knowing material to a correct assessment". Fullagar J, in Australasian Jam Co Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation Vol [1953] HCA 52; 10 ATD 217 at 222, commented upon the above dictum in the following terms:

"The words "or is capable of knowing," if read quite literally, may be thought to go a little too far, but I would think that even a merely inadvertent omission of a material fact may be enough to enable the Commissioner to maintain that the full and true disclosure required has not been made. I think further - what is of some importance in the present case - that, if there is a material omission in the taxpayer's return, it is nothing to the point for the taxpayer to say to the Commissioner: "It was obvious on the face of the return that there was something I "had not told you: You could have asked me about it, and, if you had done so, the information would have been immediately and willingly supplied."

However, Kitto J., in Levy v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 12 ATD 360 at 364, commented that the true construction of the phrase "full and true disclosure" may impose a wider duty of disclosure than some expressions of opinion given in previous cases, tended to suggest. In his words:

"There are, however, some statements to be found in the cases suggesting that s. 170(2) should be given more benevolent construction. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Westcrarth a provision of the Commonwealth Estate Duty Assessment Act, similar to s. 170(2) was considered. At page 407, Latham, C.J. said that "a person cannot be said to fail to disclose something which he never knew". Williams, J., at page 412, considered that a full and true disclosure was made if the person required to make it "discloses all that he knows or is capable of knowing". And Fullagar, J, at pp 415-6, thought that the use of the word "disclosure" involved "the idea of revealing something which is known to oneself". In The Scottish Australian Mining Company Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation Williams, J again spoke of the duty to disclose as being a duty to disclose "every fact which he knows or is capable of knowing", and in Foster v Federal Commissioner of Taxation Latham, CJ said that a failure by a taxpayer to tell the Commissioner a material fact which he, the Commissioner, already knew did not constitute a failure to disclose material facts. The expression "capable of knowing" used by Williams, J. in Westgarth's Case was thought by Fullagar, J. in Australian Jam Co Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation to go too far if read literally. His Honour went on to say that "a merely inadvertent omission of a material fact may be enough to enable the Commissioner to maintain that the full and true disclosure required has not been made". Finally in National Trustees Executors & Agency Co. of Australasia Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation Kitto, J. spoke of the duty to disclose as not involving an obligation to disclose facts of which the Commissioner was already aware or facts which the person required to make the disclosure "does not know or believe or (perhaps) possess the means of knowing". Some of the observations which I have quoted seem rather to treat the section as though it spoke of a "failure to make a full and true disclosure", but these are not its words. A "failure to disclose" does not seem to me to mean precisely the same thing as a "non-disclosure", and it might well be thought that there was no "failure to disclose" if the undisclosed fact was unknown to the person upon whom the duty of "disclosure lay."

19. Whilst there may be some differences in judicial expressions of opinion as to the degree of disclosure which is required, in Westqarth's case (supra), Foster's case (supra) and Levy's case (supra), it is clear that none of their Honours' expressions of opinion go as far as that expressed by the Board of Review in Case 18, 9 CTBR. The Board in that case, when considering the disclosure required of a beneficiary of income from a trust estate, considered that the disclosure would be deemed to have been made in accordance with s.170 of the Assessment Act if the following requirements were fulfilled:

"In our opinion a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for an assessment involves something more than a true disclosure of the amount of the assessable income and of the deductions. In most cases, it would not be possible for a beneficiary in his own return, to make a disclosure which is literally in accordance with the terras prescribed. However, we consider that, in the case of a beneficiary, the required disclosure should be deemed to have been made in respect of his share of the trust income if -

(a) his return specifically refers to the trust estate and discloses a share in the trust income;

(b) the amount so disclosed corresponds with the amount of his share as disclosed in the trust return; and

(c) the trust return fully and truly discloses all the material facts necessary for the ascertainment both of the net income of the trust estate and for the proper assessment of the shares of the beneficiaries."

20. In our view, in the light of the decisions in Westgarth's, Foster's and Levy's case (supra), we consider that the degree of disclosure as expressed in Case No 18 goes beyond what we consider to be the legislative requirement. It is to be noted that in this application for review, the assessment which is the subject of review relates to the personal taxation returns of the applicants and not to the returns of either the Trustee Company or the Family Trust. Regulation 9 of the Income Tax Regulations provides:

"9. (1) Except as otherwise prescribed, every return under the Act shall -

(a) be made and furnished in such of the forms provided by the Commissioner for the purpose as is applicable;

(b) contain the information and particulars mentioned or referred to in that form;

(c) be verified by declaration as therein set forth; and

(d) be accompanied by all such balance-sheets, profit and loss accounts, statements and other documents, as are mentioned in the form or as are requisite."

(Other sub-sections not relevant)

21. Regulation 10 provides:

"10. (1) The form of return applicable in the case of persons other than companies shall -

(a) itemise the income derived from various sources and the deductions claimed;

(b) provide for separate Parts as follows:

Part A-Income from Personal Exertion (when

Part C or Part D is unsuitable);

Part B-Income from Property;

Part C-Income from a profession, trade,

business, manufacture or concern, not

shown in Part A or Part B;

Part D-Income of a farmer, pastoralist or

horticulturist, &c, not shown in

Part A or Part B;

(c) provide in each Part for particulars to be supplied under separate headings relating to income derived and deductions claimed;

(d) provide for the following particulars or such modifications or extensions thereof as the Commissioner from time to time requires, namely:

Statement by trustee or partnership;

(e) provide for a declaration by the person making the return that the particulars shown therein and also those stated in the accompanying statements are true and correct in every particular and disclose without reservation or exception a full and complete statement of the total income derived during the year of income from all sources in and out of Australia."

(Sub-section 2 not relevant)

22. The declaration which is required to be completed in relation to personal taxation forms, Form A, (being in similar terms to that required in company and trust estate returns), is in the following terms:

"I declare that the particulars shown in this return and in the accompanying documents are true and correct in every detail and disclose a full and complete statement of the total income derived by me from all sources in and out of Australia during the year of income."

23. We note that sub-s.170(2) of the Assessment Act is concerned with disclosure of all material facts "necessary for his assessment". The wording does not require full and true disclosure to be in any particular form. It does not prescribe that such disclosure should be contained in the taxation return form. It does not, therefore, appear to be necessary pursuant to sub-s170(2) of the Assessment Act that the full and true disclosure should he comprised of the information which is contained within the relevant income tax return but rather, that it may consist of additional disclosure made in other forms which would include documents, explanatory memoranda, covering letter or even, as in this case, subsequent correspondence.

  1. Whilst the income tax return declaration requires "a full and complete statement of the total income", that is a lesser and different requirement from "full and true disclosure of all the material facts for ... assessment" prescribed in sub-s170(2).
  2. Further, the income tax return does not provide spaces for explanatory material; it is mainly for figures, therefore in order for sub-s170(2) to be complied with, it may be necessary to provide additional documentation or memoranda.
  3. It is to be noted that, in relation to a taxpayer's personal taxation return (Form A), the requirements of the Regulations, in combination with the information sought on the form itself, requires disclosure of the full and complete income derived by a taxpayer. In particular, in item 11 of Form A, being the item relevant to a share in partnership income or income as a trust beneficiary, the form requires the full name and file number of the trust estate or partnership, and the amount of the income less any deductions. There is no requirement in item 11 of Form A that the beneficiary taxpayer is required to disclose the percentage share of interest in the trust or partnership.
  4. In the light of these observations, we consider that Case No 18 places a far greater requirement as to disclosure of information by trust beneficiaries than the Act, Regulations or the prescribed taxation form requires. Furthermore, the beneficiary of a trust may not be the person responsible for the preparation and lodging of the trust return and could not reasonably be expected to comply with or be responsible for the same requirements as the trustees on the lodgment of the trust return.'
  5. In this case, the information which was before the Commissioner at the time of his assessment on 18 March 1983, consisted of the applicants' Forms A which were inaccurate and incomplete in that the Trust income received by them was not disclosed, but the letter of 9 March 1983 corrected that deficiency by stating the amount of the Trust income received and the name of the Trust. This is precisely the information which was sought in item 11 of Form A. In addition, however, the Commissioner also had before him the Trust Deed and previous income tax returns, and would have been aware that it was a discretionary trust and that the applicants were the directors of the Trustee Company and therefore responsible for the apportioning and distribution of Trust income.
  6. Therefore, as at 18 March 1983, the Commissioner had before him all of the material facts necessary to assess the liability of the applicants for taxation. The subsequent lodging of the Trust return and the Company return on 21 March 1983 was merely confirmatory documentation and was not necessary for the assessment of the applicants' personal liability to pay taxation.
  7. Mr McCullough, in submitting that full and true disclosure had not been made, made much of the inconvenience and disruption which would be caused to the Australian Taxation Office if material other than the tax returns was placed before the Commissioner which either contained additional information or, alternatively, as in this case, amended information which is contained in taxation returns. Whilst this is undoubtedly an inconvenience, it does not prevent the Commissioner from amending an assessment. However, if Mr McCullough's submission is taken at face value, then if there is an omission or an error contained in a taxation return, such omission or error cannot be corrected by the taxpayer placing subsequent information before the Commissioner, without running the risk of the provisions of sub-s170(2) prima facie applying. In such situations, the injustice would not work against the Commissioner, but rather, it would work against the taxpayer who would be subject to the less favourable treatment of an amendment being made pursuant to sub-s.170(2) which, depending on whether para. (a) or (b) applies, allows amendment at any time or, alternatively, within six years of an assessment. On the other hand, if amendment is made pursuant to sub-s.170(3), the period of amendment is limited to three years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable under that assessment.
  8. For these reasons, therefore, we reject the respondent's argument and consider that, on these facts there had been a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment of the applicants' liability to pay taxation as at 18 March 1983, being the date of assessment.
  9. It is therefore not necessary for us to consider whether, as a result of any failure to disclose, there had- been an avoidance of tax. That matter was not seriously contested in this case, as it was quite clear that, on the authority of W. Thomas and Co. Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 54; 14 ATD 78, the mere fact that the failure to disclose income would have lead to a lower assessment of taxation was sufficient to indicate that any failure to disclose would have been interpreted as "avoidance of tax" within the meaning of sub-s.(2) of s.170 of the Assessment Act. There was no suggestion in this case that para, (a) applied but merely that para. (b) was the relevant paragraph, and we agree with this submission.
  10. We will now turn to consideration of sub-s.170(3) of the Assessment Act. As we have already found that the applicants have made a full and true disclosure of the material facts, the next matter to consider is whether or not an amendment of the assessment was required to correct a mistake of fact (there is no suggestion that the facts give rise to an error in calculation).
  11. The submission of Mr Adams was that there was no mistake of fact involved. The letter of 9 March 1983 was received by the Commissioner. It is not relevant that the particular assessor was not aware of that letter; it was within the knowledge of the Commissioner (see Levy (supra)). It was therefore submitted on behalf of the applicants that there had been no mistake made by the Commissioner; rather, ignorance of the letter's existence by the particular assessor was merely a matter of internal administrative arrangements.

35. A number of Courts have considered, in differing contexts, the meaning of "mistake" of fact. A useful discussion of the word appears in Lady Hood of Avalon v Mackinnon (1909) 1 Ch 476 where Eve, J., in concluding that forgetfulness amounted to mistake, stated at pp.482-483:

"Courts of first instance have pointed out, and the Court of Appeal has pointed out, that there is no satisfactory definition of what mistake is. Lord Esher M.R. in Barrow v Isaacs & Son says this: "Now, having thought over this matter, and looked into all the cases, I can find no definition of what 'mistake' is; but if you treat mistake in its ordinary sense in the English language, is mere forgetfulness mistake? Can you, in English, say 'I forgot', and is that the same thing as saying 'I was mistaken'?" Then his Lordship goes on to say: "Both those questions depend on something happening in the mind of the person, and you have to see what it is that happens in his mind. If he merely forgets, he does not assume that one state of things exists whereas some other state of things exists: it is a mere passive state of mind; he has forgotten - he has not thought that one thing was in existence, whereas something else was in existence". With the greatest possible respect to Lord Esher, I do not quite follow that. It seems to me that when a person has forgotten the existence of a pre-existing fact, and assumes that such fact did not pre-exist, he is labouring under a mistake, and he acts on the footing that the fact really did not preexist; and, venturing to criticize the language of Lord Esher, I should have thought that a man makes a mistake in forgetting an existing fact quite as much as he does in assuming a state of things to exist which does not in fact exist. However, that is the view which the learned Master of the Rolls then took, but it is not a view which I find is acquiesced in by Kay L.J., whose judgment was adopted by Lopes L.J., in the same case, because Kay L.J. says this: "The questions are (1.) whether the facts I have described amount to mistake; and, if so, (2.) whether in its discretion, the Court will relieve". The point there was that the solicitor, forgetting there was a head lease which ought to have been consulted upon the question whether the licence of the superior landlord was a necessary condition precedent to an assignment, had carried out a transaction without referring to the head lease and without obtaining the licence. I need not consider the second question which Kay L.J. asks, but he says with regard to the first: "Very wisely, as I presume to think, the Courts have abstained from giving any general definition of what amounts to mistake. It is an easily arguable question whether mere forgetfulness of such a covenant - even if not negligent - can properly be called mistake. The case of Kelly v Solari, however, and the observation of Lord Blackburn in the House of Lords in Brownlie v Campbell establish that, in an action to recover money-paid by mistake, it is sufficient to prove that at the time of the payment the person paying was actually ignorant that the money was not due, although he had the means of knowledge, and it was owing to his own carelessness or forgetfulness, that he was in fact ignorant. There undoubtedly forgetfulness of the previous payment is treated as mistake". Then he goes on, as Mr Whinney has quite properly pointed out, to say that in those cases, that is, the payment over again of money, there was this additional element, that the payee was in possession of money of which he ought not to be allowed to remain in possession, and therefore the Court could not allow him to retain that to which he was not entitled. But, on the other hand, there is the distinct statement by the Lord Justice that forgetfulness of the previous payment is treated as mistake in the cases to which he refers."

  1. There is also inherent affirmation in those Reasons that carelessness or forgetfulness which results in ignorance, is also a mistake.
  2. Russell, LJ, in Mitchell v Harris Engineering Company Ltd. (1967) 2 QB 703, at p721 said:

"It is suggested that mistake here means error without fault; but I do not see why the word should be so narrowly construed."

38. Also, in a decision given by Smith, J., in the Supreme Court of Victoria in Boyle v Wright [1969] VicRp 89; (1969) VR 699, His Honour at p702 said:

"For I take the word "mistake" in the section to include an error in conduct consisting of an unintended failure to perform correctly and effectively a task intended to be duly performed: compare Sandford v. Beal (1895), 65 LJQB 73; Garrett v Toohey's Ltd. (1949), 23 WCR (NSW) 80; Shorter Oxford English Dictionary, "mistake";"

  1. Finally, in the context of the taxation legislation, Latham, CJ, in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hayden 7 ATD 440 at 442 stated:

"A wrong supposition as to a fact is a mistake of fact. It may arise from ignorance, from misinformation, or from forgetfulness."

  1. On the facts in this application, there is no error of law involved. The question at issue is whether the respondent's failure to take into account the contents of the letter of 9 March 1983, amounted to a mistake of fact. Clearly, the letter of 9 March 1983 should have been taken into account in assessing the applicants' liability to pay income tax, and was not. Although the letter had been acknowledged by stamp as having been received, and is therefore deemed to be received by the respondent, the relevant assessor was "unaware" of its existence. It is not unreasonable to infer that following its receipt by employees of the respondent, it was either mislaid, overlooked or forgotten or even ignored, intentionally or otherwise, by employees of the respondent. Or, to use the words of Smith, J in Boyle's case (supra), there was "an unintended failure to perform correctly and effectively a task intended to be performed". Whichever of these situations existed amounted, in our view, to a mistake of fact on the basis of the above authorities. We therefore reject Mr Adams' submission.
  2. We further consider that this argument on behalf of the applicants is not only wrong but also opportunistic in this situation where, firstly, inaccurate and incomplete information was supplied to the Commissioner, secondly, that information was corrected by letter from the applicants' tax agent and finally, when the contents of that letter were not discovered in time in a department as large as the Australian Tax Office, an attempt was made on behalf of the applicants to use that error against the Commissioner in order to limit the applicants' liability to pay tax which would otherwise be payable.
  3. The other question which arises in this application for review is that of onus. It was submitted by Mr Adams that the onus of establishing the mistake of fact lay on the Commissioner. We reject this submission in the light of the case of McAndrew (supra) and consider that, for the reasons which are examined in the CCH Australian Federal Taxation Reporter Volume 6, 80-125 and 80-135, the onus at all times remains on the taxpayer pursuant to s.190 of the Assessment Act.

43. For these reasons, therefore, we consider that the Commissioner was empowered to make the amendment made and issued on 18 April 1983 pursuant to sub-s170(3) of the Assessment Act, and uphold the Commissioner's decision.
