Leane and Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] AATA 672 (18 November 1994)

Last Updated: 22 September 2008



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - preservation of superannuation benefits by a resigning eligible employee - election outside prescribed period - discretion to recognise late election - whether recognition of late election is just and equitable in all the circumstances



Superannuation Act 1976, ss 137, 157

Superannuation Act 1922



Commissioner for Superannuation and Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239

Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450



AAT Decision No 9844

Decision : The decision under review is set aside, and in substitution therefor it is decided that the election made by the applicant on 25 September 1992 pursuant to section 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: A94/9

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: Peter Desmond Leane



Applicant



And: Commissioner for

Superannuation



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



Tribunal: Dr DJ Grimes, Senior Member

Mr NJ Attwood, Member

Mr HG Julian, Member



  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the delegate of the respondent dated 6 January 1994 confirming a determination of 29 January 1993, under subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”), not to direct that the election made by the applicant on 25 September 1992 pursuant to subsection 137(1) of the Act, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section of the Act.

  2. The applicant attended the hearing before the Tribunal and was represented by Mr Ken Cush of counsel. Mr Simon Adams appeared on behalf of the respondent. Oral Evidence was heard from the applicant, Mr Steve Sollazzo and Ms Lorna Fowler. Documents produced by the respondent in accordance with section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1 -T35) were before the Tribunal, as were a number of additional documents tendered at the hearing.

  3. The applicant in these proceedings is seeking recognition of a late election to preserve his superannuation rights. Subsection 137(1) of the Act prescribes the period in which an employee is able to elect to preserve such rights after he or she ceases to be an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act. As is relevant, it provides:

"s137

(1) A person who -

  • (a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply, and upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

  • (b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect by notice in writing to the Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the person.



(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.



(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -

  • (a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of the employer; and

  • (b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."

  1. Subsection 157(1) of the Act, however, contains a discretionary power vested in the Commissioner for Superannuation, to recognise an election made outside the prescribed period of 21 days in certain circumstances. It provides:

S157

(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

  1. As the applicant’s election to preserve his superannuation right was made after the time prescribed by section 137(1), the Tribunal must consider whether it is desirable, in all the circumstances of the case, to treat the election as if it had been made within the allowable period.

  2. The applicant was born on 16 November 1954 and joined the then Post Master Generals Department as a junior postal officer in September 1972. He became a contributor to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (“CSS”) established by the Superannuation Act 1922 on 23 November 1973 in connection with his permanent employment with the PMG.

  3. In order to take up a position with the Canberra College of Advanced Education, the applicant resigned his position as Technical Officer Grade 1 with Telecom with effect from 21 April 1987. The applicant gave evidence that whilst he enjoyed his job at Telecom, he wished to pursue a career in computing. As he could translate his long service leave and sick leave, he also believed that his superannuation would be transferred. It was his belief that he was merely transferring from one Commonwealth government organisation to another.

  4. On 9 April 1987 the applicant completed and signed a superseded Form S2A, entitled “Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights”. He elected the option to “apply for a refund of accumulated contributions” by deleting the remaining options including election for preservation of superannuation rights.

  5. As a result, the applicant was paid a refund of the accumulated contributions to the value of $17,008.11 by the Retirement Benefits Office on 4 June 1987.

  6. The applicant gave evidence that it was his belief that his “employers contributions” would be automatically transferred into his new superannuation scheme.

  7. Whilst employed at the Canberra College of Advanced Education, the applicant became a contributing member to the Superannuation Scheme for Australian Universities (“SSAU”).

  8. In May 1989, the applicant rejoined Telecom, and following his permanent appointment on 1 September 1989, became eligible to be a member of the Telecom Superannuation Scheme (“TSS”). The applicant told the Tribunal that on leaving the Canberra College of Advanced Education, his funds were transferred to the CSS. He was told as a new employee of Telecom, he would be required to join the TSS. In evidence before the Tribunal the applicant stated that when he became a permanent employee of Telecom he should have been contributing to superannuation. However, this did not happen and upon enquiry, the applicant said he was told that there was some confusion at that stage. He should have been contributing to the CSS but was caught between his previous classification and the category of “new employees” who were required to contribute to the TSS.

  9. Consequently, the applicant withdrew his money from the CSS and attempted to transfer it to the TSS. However, he told the Tribunal that the TSS were unable to accept the funds as “they hadn’t quite formed”. He then attempted to pay the funds into the Superannuation Fund Investment Trust (“SFIT”) but was again rejected. Eventually, the applicant's funds were transferred to the TSS.

  10. On 25 September 1992, the applicant made a late election to preserve his benefits in accordance with section 137(1) of the Act. On 29 January 1993, a delegate of the respondent determined that in all the circumstances of the case, it was not desirable that the election be recognised. That decision was confirmed on 6 January 1994.

  11. The applicant submitted that on resigning from Telecom in 1987, he was not fully aware of the implications of electing a refund of contributions. In evidence, the applicant said that in 1987, he contacted the personnel section to enquire as to the procedure for leaving Telecom. He was advised that the required Form S2A could be obtained from the clerical assistant located at the office in Kent Street, Deakin, or from the City office. The applicant said he obtained the Form S2A from the clerical assistant at Kent Street. He received no advice as to the completion of the form. The applicant told the Tribunal that he read the form and thought he understood it. He believed the form had two applications, one being for those resigning and the other for those retiring. Accordingly, he believed the only option available to him was to accept a refund of the contributions.

  12. Ms Lorna Fowler, an officer in the personnel section of Telecom in Canberra at the time of the applicant's resignation gave evidence before the Tribunal. She stated that the Form S2A was available from the personnel section at the city office and should not have been available at the Deakin office where the applicant was based. Ms Fowler noted that the form provided to the applicant had in fact been superseded, although it appeared that this may not have been noticed at the time.

  13. Evidence was given that the personnel section provided the appropriate form, and apart from indicating which sections needed to be completed, they did not give advice as to available options. Although occasional visits were made by personnel section employees to Kent Street, Ms Fowler said she, and the personnel section, were essentially positioned in a different location to that of the applicant.

  14. The applicant told the Tribunal that at the time of withdrawal of his funds from the CSS, he had no immediate use for the money. He described his financial situation as “comfortable”. On the advice of his bank manager he invested the majority of his funds in his home mortgage. Contrary to statements made in a letter of 4 June 1989 (T13), the applicant said he was not “desperate” for money after rejoining Telecom. He stated that framed the letter in those terms on the advice of “a lady” at CSS, in order to transfer his funds into the TSS.

  15. Evidence was also given by the applicant that he experienced difficulties with reading and comprehending forms. He said he had difficulties at high school with English, and nearly failed his technical certificate because he repeatedly failed a subject based on comprehension.

  16. Mr Steve Sollazzo, a principal Technical Officer employed by Telecom gave evidence that he had known the applicant since 1977. He stated that he had observed the applicant was having difficulties at work. Although he was technically good, the applicant’s work was affected by him not reading the circulars distributed to inform staff of procedural changes. Mr Sollazzo stated that he became aware that the applicant had a “severe reading disability”. The applicant would he said, misinterpret work documents and would need to be corrected on occasions. Mr Sollazzo noted that in completing the Electronics and Communications Certificate course, the applicant repeatedly failed one of his 21 subjects - Digital Logic - which required comprehension translating English into algebra. The applicant required many attempts to pass the subject.

  17. Mr Sollazzo told the Tribunal he also helped the applicant prepare a number of letters in relation to his superannuation claim. He also commented that the applicant had difficulty comprehending the decision and had misread the outcome of Commissioner for Superannuation and Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239, that was obtained in relation to the decision under review.

  18. Counsel for the applicant submitted that the applicant was an “honest but simple man” who was not sophisticated in his dealings. He had reading problems and difficulties understanding forms which had affected both his work and his understanding of the Form S2A. It was submitted that the applicant was not alerted to the available options of the Forms S2A and was given no assistance or opportunity receive advice. The personnel section apparantly ignored the fact that he had been given a superseded form when moving to Canberra College. It was argued that the applicant was a loyal public servant who clearly intended to remain in Commonwealth employment. His withdrawal of contributions was made under a misapprehension rather than a desire to gain access to the funds.

  19. The respondent submitted that in considering whether it was desirable in all the circumstances to exercise the discretion contained in section 157 of the Act, it was necessary to adopt an objective test of balancing what is fair in order to do justice as between the applicant and the requirements of the administration of the fund.

  20. The respondent noted that the applicant elected to preserve his benefits some five years and four months after the expiration of the time limit. It was submitted that the applicant’s explanation for the delay - i.e he was not alerted to the possibility of preservation of benefits - was not reasonable. The respondent argued that whilst the lack of information was relevant to explaining the delay in making the election, any further consideration as to the existence of fault or otherwise on the part of the Commissioner, was not a relevant consideration in the exercise of the discretion.

  21. The respondent noted that in completing the Form S2A, the applicant admitted to reading it carefully, and submitted that there was no direct evidence that he misunderstood it.

  22. In deciding the question of exercise of the discretion under s157(1) the Tribunal has recourse to the principles laid down in a number of previous decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (see Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870 and Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307). It is widely accepted that guidance for the test of whether it is desirable in all the circumstances of the case to allow a late election is found in the decision of the Federal Court in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344. Those principles, as summarised, are as follows:

    • (i) prima facie, proceedings commenced outside the prescribed period will not be entertained. An extension of time will be granted however, if it is proper to do so;
    • (ii) it is relevant whether the applicant rested on his rights or took action to make the decision maker aware that the decision was being contested;
    • (iii) any prejudice to the respondent that would be caused by granting the extension of time is relevant;
    • (iv) any wider prejudice to the general public in terms of disruption to established practices is relevant;
    • (v) the merits of the substantial application are relevant;
    • (vi) fairness of granting the extension of time as between the applicant and other persons in a like position is relevant.



27. Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation and Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239 endorsed such guidance. He commented (at 247):



“It is understandable, indeed inevitable, that a decision-maker considering an application under s.157(1) will have close regard to the assistance which decisions such as Hunter Valley Developments and A’Hearn offer.”



Von Doussa J considered there to be no reason for applying different considerations to the exercise of the discretion in section 157 and that to extend a procedural time limit in litigation. In each case, he stated, the question was whether it is fair and just that the party be extended the rights which would have been enjoyed had they acted within the prescribed statutory time limit.

  1. The Tribunal is of the view that the discretion is not unrestricted in its terms as it specifies that the Commissioner must be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised...". In considering so, the Tribunal is also mindful that a wide discretion is curtailed by the general purpose and policy for which the discretion is granted. It is noted that the Tribunal in Re Lander considered that the discretion conferred by section 157(1) was, to some extent, limited by the use of the word "desirable". In Liddle it was said that the word "desirable" strongly suggested a positive connotation, that is something "worthy of achievement" rather then "merely advisable" and that "objectively good reasons" must exist which justify non-compliance with a statutory time limit. The applicant in essence claimed that the facts of his case amounted to objectively good reasons which therefore made the decision to allow a late election desirable.

  2. The Tribunal notes the more recent comments of Davies J and Cooper J in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450, and those of Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman (supra), on the discretion contained in section 157(1).

  3. Davies J in Chalk stated (at 456):

“Many such provisions, including s.157, are not concerned with rights. If an act is not performed within the prescribed time, there is no right to have it performed. But there is a discretion to permit time to be extended should it be appropriate in the circumstances to do so. In the exercise of this discretion, it may be more important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time. Section 157 merely requires that the Commissioner ‘be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised’. ‘All the circumstances’ include all relevant circumstances existing at the time the discretion is to be exercised.



In the application of s.157, it may be of little consequence that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s.137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case. Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s.157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances need not be of a medical character. In Mr. Chalk’s case, the change in circumstance was that he resigned from Telecom intending to establish himself in an entirely new career as a farmer but was unsuccessful in doing so. And so he again took up employment with Telecom, after a lapse of two years.



The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights. But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to receive the benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the s.157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded.”



  1. Also in Chalk, Cooper J commented (at 458):

“The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under section 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration. In the instant case the Tribunal found that the appellant did not know of the choices open to him under the Act and that neither Telecom nor the Commissioner advised him of the available choices. The Tribunal correctly treated as a fact relevant to its deliberations that the appellant was ignorant of the choices available and that ignorance was partly explicable by the failure of Telecom and the Commissioner to correctly inform the appellant of the available choices. However, section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault. Rather, as explained by Davies J. in his reasons, the section merely requires that the Commissioner ‘be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised’. Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for consideration and will include circumstances of the type identified by Davies J. as relevant to the statutory purpose for which the discretionary power in section 157 to treat an election as within time was enacted.”



  1. It was stated by Von Doussa J, in Boardman at 247:

“In s.157(1) ‘desirable’ is not referable to a state of mind - whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late election is ‘desirable’ is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case. The suggested meaning ‘something worthy of achievement’ does not imply other than an objective evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in the end, whether the recognition of a late election is ‘something worthy of achievement’ must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.”



  1. The Tribunal notes these recent findings of the Federal Court and in determining the question before it, has carefully considered all the evidence before it.

  2. It was the applicant's evidence, that upon resignation, he did not know of his rights to preserve superannuation benefits, and in such ignorance of the availability of this option, he requested a refund of his contributions. It is relevant, in the Tribunal’s opinion, that as a technical officer, the applicant was not located in an office where appropriate information was available. Rather he was based in an office located some distance form the personnel section. The Form S2A which the applicant completed was provided by a clerical assistant rather than the personnel officer. In obtaining the form, the applicant was provided with no assistance. The Tribunal notes that the applicant “understood” the form insofar as he considered “preservation” to apply to retiring employees only. It is clear to the Tribunal that acting within this assumption, the applicant did not consider it necessary to make further enquiries.

  3. Whilst the applicant may have discussed his superannuation rights with workmates, he does not appear to have relied on the advice of his workmates in making his decision to seek a refund of his contributions.

  4. The Tribunal notes that the applicant has been continuously employed in the public sector (Commonwealth) throughout his working life and has pursued a consistent career path. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant has desired and entertained the expectation that he would have uninterrupted superannuation cover and entitlements associated with his public sector employment to date.

  5. The Tribunal also notes that the applicant was provided with a Form S2A that had been superseded some years prior to him completing it. Whilst it cannot be assumed that the applicant would have chosen the preservation option if provided with the current form S2A, it nevertheless indicates to the Tribunal that there was a lack of contact between the applicant and the personnel section of Telecom.

  6. It is evident to the Tribunal that the applicant has severe reading difficulties. The evidence before the Tribunal indicates that as a result of such difficulties the applicant's work was adversely affected at times. The Tribunal accepts that whilst required to complete some administrative tasks in the course of his work, he nevertheless experienced problems understanding forms. It is this Tribunal’s opinion that such difficulties, combined with the ambiguous nature of the Form S2A used by the applicant, did not assist the applicant in alerting him to the fact that other options were available to him.

  7. The Tribunal recognises that whilst the applicant used the refunded contributions to repay part of his home mortgage, the evidence does not suggest that the applicant withdrew his contributions under financial pressure or motivation to repay the loan. His decision to use the contributions refunded in the manner he did was made on the advice of, amongst others, his bank manager.

  8. The Tribunal is satisfied on the evidence before it, that the applicant did not rest on his rights during the period after his resignation in 1987 and his late election in 1992. The applicant was initially of the mistaken belief that his employer’s contributions were automatically transferred in 1987 in the same manner as his long service leave. Since leaving the Canberra College of Education, the applicant made continued efforts to organise his superannuation entitlements. When the applicant was eventually made aware of his preservation rights, he acted accordingly to elect to preserve his superannuation rights.

  9. In considering the question before it, the Tribunal has had regard to a number of recent decisions in similar cases. It is mindful however, that it is necessary to determine each case in light of its individual circumstances. In doing so, the Tribunal has carefully considered the evidence before it and is mindful that the applicant's election to preserve was made some five years and four months after the expiration of the prescribed period. However, in light of the circumstances of this case, particularly the evidence of the applicant's profound reading problem and difficulties with administrative forms (both within the knowledge of his supervisor), his remoteness from the personnel section and the confusion surrounding his superannuation entitlements, it is the Tribunal’s opinion that it is appropriate to treat that election as if it had been made within the period allowed, in order to do justice between the applicant and the requirements of the administration of the fund.

  10. The decision under review is set aside, and in substitution therefor it is decided that the election made by the applicant on 25 September 1992 pursuant to section 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section.
