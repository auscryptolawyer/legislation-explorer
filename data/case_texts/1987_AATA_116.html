Re "Al" and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 116 (2 April 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: "AL"

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A86/104

AAT No. 3342

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

N.J. Attwood (Member)

Dr D.M. Sutherland (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - decision of delegate of Superannuation Board in 1967 - extension of time to request reconsideration - meaning of "come to the notice of" in section 154(2) Superannuation Act 1976 - principles applicable - Hunter Valley Development Pty. Ltd. followed

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 16, 154(1), (2) & (6)

Superannuation Act 1922 ss. 5, 141

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ss. 25, 29

Freedom of Information Act 1982

Re Schmack and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1981) 3 ALN N77

Hunter Valley Development Pty. Ltd. v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344

Re Kenah and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (Decision No. 2250: 26 July 1985)

Re Edward Souery and Co. Pty. Ltd. and Export Market Development Grants Board (Decision No. 2414: 18 November 1985

HEARING

CANBERRA

2:4:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

Section 154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act") provides that a person who is affected by a reviewable decision, as defined in section 154(1), and who is dissatisfied with that decision may request the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") to reconsider the decision. It provides that the request may be made within the period of 30 days after the day on which the decision first comes to his notice or within such further period as the Commissioner allows. On 30 January 1986 the applicant wrote to the Commissioner requesting him to allow a further period within which he might request the Commissioner under section 154(2) to reconsider "the alleged decision of the Superannuation Board under section 5(2) of the Superannuation Act 1922, allegedly taken on or after 7-4-67, to accept me as a member of the Provident Account". On 29 April 1986 a delegate of the Commissioner decided to refuse to allow the further time.

2. The applicant wrote to the Commissioner on 8 May 1986 requesting him under section 154(2) to reconsider the delegate's decision refusing to allow him a further period within which to request reconsideration of the 1967 decision. On 29 August 1986 Mr P. J. Gunther, as acting Commissioner, decided to confirm the delegate's decision. The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal under section 154(6) for review of the delegate's decision that had been confirmed by Mr Gunther. "Reviewable decision" is defined in section 154(1); a decision of the Commissioner, of the Superannuation Board or of a delegate of either under the 1976 Act or the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act"), other than a decision under section 141 of the 1922 Act, is a reviewable decision. So the decision of the Superannuation Board in 1967 is a reviewable decision to which section 154(2) applies; and the Commissioner's delegate's decision not to allow the applicant a further period to request its reconsideration is also a reviewable decision. By virtue of section 25 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") the Tribunal has power to review the delegate's decision upon the application made to it.

3. Section 5 of the 1922 Act provided throughout 1966 for persons employed or about to be employed by the Commonwealth in the Public Service in a permanent capacity by the Commonwealth on a full-time basis to be medically examined to ascertain whether they were acceptable as contributors to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account established by that Act. It read as follows:-

"5. (1) Except as otherwise provided by this Act,

and notwithstanding anything contained in any other

Act, a person who becomes an employee after the

commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965 shall not

contribute to the Fund under Part III of this Act or

to the Provident Account unless -

(a) he has, before becoming an employee, or

within such period after becoming an

employee as the Board allows, undergone a

medical examination by a legally qualified

medical practitioner approved by the Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination

has been furnished by the medical

practitioner to the Board; and

(c) the Board is satisfied, after considering

the report of the medical practitioner,

that the health and physical fitness of the

person are such as to justify his being

accepted as a contributor to the Fund under

Part III of this Act or to the Provident

Account, as the case may be.

(2) Where -

(a) a person who becomes an employee after the

commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965

has, whether before or after becoming an

employee, undergone a medical examination

by a legally qualified medical practitioner

approved by the Public Service Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination

has been furnished by the medical

practitioner to the Public Service Board;

and

(c) the Public Service Board certifies, after

considering the report of the medical

practitioner, that the health and physical

fitness of the person are such as to

justify his being accepted as a contributor

to the Fund under Part III of this Act or

to the Provident Account,

the Superannuation Board may, in its discretion,

accept him as a contributor to the Fund under Part

III of this Act or to the Provident Account."

4. The applicant took up appointment as a Research Officer Grade 1 in the Commonwealth Public Service on 1 February 1966. During 1965 he had been living in the United Kingdom, where he had obtained a university degree. He had applied in the United Kingdom for appointment to the Commonwealth Public Service. In October 1965 he had completed a medical examination form and had been examined by a medical practitioner in the United Kingdom. He had then travelled to Australia, arriving in December 1965 or January 1966. On 14 January 1966 an officer of the Public Service Board had offered him permanent appointment as a Research Officer Grade 1. The offer was conditional upon his completing certain formalities referred to in a leaflet enclosed with the letter of offer; but it was stated in the letter that the section of the leaflet relating to his undergoing medical examination would not apply in his case as he had already been examined.

5. Following his medical examination on 29 October 1965 the doctor had recommended that he be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund; however, there is no evidence that the applicant was expressly informed of that. On 28 February 1966 a letter was sent to him by an officer of the Public Service Board informing him that his medical fitness for permanent appointment to the Public Service was "at present under review" and requesting him to attend the Commonwealth Medical Officer's rooms on 1 March 1966 and to complete another medical examination form similar to that which he had completed in the United Kingdom in October 1985. Although no reference was made in that letter to the fact that the applicant had become mentally ill while travelling from the United Kingdom to Australia by ship, that seems likely to have been the reason for the further medical examination being required.

6. On 1 March 1966 the applicant was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer in Canberra. The Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended that the decision whether he should be accepted for either the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account or totally rejected for superannuation should be deferred for 11 months. The reason for that recommendation was that he had ascertained that the applicant had a history of the type of illness which he had suffered on the voyage to Australia. On 18 March 1966 an officer of the Public Service Board wrote to the applicant informing him of the Commonwealth Medical Officer's recommendation. He wrote that the the recommendation had been accepted, that consequently it would be necessary for the applicant to undergo a further medical examination in November 1966 and that "if acceptable at that time, it is expected that the certification will be for Provident Account, rather than Superannuation Fund, benefits". In March 1967, after receiving a letter from a specialist medical practitioner who had been treating the applicant since the beginning of December 1966, the Commonwealth Medical Officer wrote that "it seems we should probably reject him"; but he added that "it could be a borderline case". An officer of the Public Service Board noted that the applicant had not taken sick leave and that the medical report was inconclusive. He considered that the applicant should be accepted for the Provident Account.

7. Among the documents lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to section 37 of the AAT Act is a photocopy of one which purports to be a certificate issued under section 5(2) of the 1922 Act in respect of the applicant to the effect that his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account. At the hearing, the applicant, who presented his own case, pointed out that the manuscript signature was not that of the person named as the signatory; he tendered a copy of another document which was signed by that signatory. He pointed out also that the date had been left blank. The document contains also a handwritten note reading "entered in Provident Account" and dated 7/4/67 but without further signature. Beside the signatory's typed name and the statement of the capacity in which he was signing are initials which are not those of the signatory. Mr M. Edwards, of the Australian Government Solicitor's Office, who represented the Commissioner at the hearing, showed to the Tribunal the document of which the photocopy had been made; it was apparent that it was itself a carbon copy and not the original certificate. He was unable to inform the Tribunal where the original certificate was; he said that it had been sought but not found.

8. The applicant gave evidence that a copy of the certificate was not sent to him and that he was not informed that it had been issued or that any decision had been taken by the Superannuation Board to accept him as a contributor to the Provident Account in consequence of its issue. However, one of the documents in evidence was a minute sent to him on 29 June 1967 by an officer of the Management Services Branch of the Department in which he was serving; in the minute the officer wrote that "advice has been received from the Public Service Inspector's Office that you are ineligible to contribute to the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund and, consequently, must contribute to the Provident Account". From the time when the applicant had commenced employment as a Research Officer Grade 1 deductions of the amounts appropriate as contributions to the Superannuation Fund had been made from his salary. In the minute dated 29 June 1967 it was stated that, due to the retrospective effect of the advice received, total arrears of $129.36 had accumulated. Details were set out of the deductions made and the amounts which should have been paid as contributions to the Provident Account.

9. The applicant gave evidence that, when he received that minute, he went to the Personnel Section and complained about it to an officer there, probably a staff clerk, but was told that no appeal was possible and that all he could do was to wait some time and then apply to be transferred from the Provident Account to the Superannuation Fund. Documents before the Tribunal show that in May 1971 he wrote to the Secretary of the Superannuation Board stating that he was contributing to the Provident Account but would like to apply for a review with the aim of transferring to the Superannuation Fund. He stated:-

"I joined the Public Service on 1.2.66 and initially I

contributed to the Superannuation Fund. However,

early in 1967 as a result of a deferred medical

examination I was transferred to the Provident

Account and I have been contributing to it ever

since."

His request for transfer to the Superannuation Fund was considered by the Superannuation Board at its meeting on 3 September 1971 but was not approved. A medical practitioner then wrote to the Commonwealth Medical Officer on behalf of the applicant asking for a statement of the reasons for the rejection of his application. The Secretary to the Board replied to him that all reports associated with medical examinations for the purposes of the Superannuation Act were regarded as privy to the Board and that its policy of non-disclosure of official confidential information was applicable in the applicant's case. The applicant gave evidence that he was unaware why he had been accepted only for the Provident Account and not the Superannuation Fund in 1967 and why his request for transfer into the Superannuation Fund was rejected in 1971.

10. On 1 July 1976 the 1976 Act came into operation. The 1922 Act was not repealed and it continued to govern the payment of benefits to employees who had retired before 1 July 1976. However, from that date onwards the 1976 Act applied to those who had been contributing to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account and who continued to be employed in the Public Service. The 1976 Act made no provision for a Provident Account; all those who had been contributing to the Provident Account under the 1922 Act were required by the 1976 Act to contribute to the Superannuation Fund; but benefit classification certificates were deemed to have been issued in respect of the medical conditions by reason of which they had not been accepted into the Superannuation Fund under the 1922 Act. The result of that was that, if any employee who had contributed to the Provident Fund was retired after 30 June 1976 on the ground of invalidity by reason of a medical condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, or by reason of a condition connected with it, the invalidity benefit to which he was entitled was less than that payable to an employee without a benefit classification certificate.

11. However, the respondent has power under section 16 of the 1976 Act in specified circumstances to revoke or vary a benefit classification certificate. On 17 February 1977 the applicant requested the Commissioner to review his benefit classification certificate. On 23 June 1977 he was informed by the respondent that a delegate of the respondent had directed that the benefit classification certificate remain in force in his case. A copy of the certificate was sent to him at that time. It specified one condition by reason of which it was issued. The applicant gave evidence that he was not informed that originally three other conditions had been specified and that it had been varied so as to omit them. He had, he said, not previously known any of the grounds for his having been accepted only for the Provident Account.

12. On 30 June 1977 he ceased to be employed in the Public Service. A delegate of the respondent directed that he should, for the purposes of the 1976 Act, be deemed to have been retired on that date on the ground of incapacity caused by certain conditions. One of them, the delegate was satisfied, had been substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate. Consequently the pension which he has received since then has been at a lower rate than he would have received if the benefit classification certificate had not been deemed to have been issued. It was deemed to have been issued because he had been a contributor to the Provident Account. He had been a contributor to the Provident Account because of the decision taken in April 1967 to accept him into the Provident Account and not the Superannuation Fund. That is why he wishes the Commissioner to reconsider that decision.

13. He submitted that the documents in evidence in relation to his medical examination in October 1965 and the offer of appointment made to him in January 1966 and the document showing that amounts appropriate for contributions to the Superannuation Fund and not the Provident Account were deducted from his salary from February 1966 until June 1967, when taken together with others tendered at the hearing which related to practices of the Public Service Board and the Superannuation Board during that period, supported an inference that he was accepted into the Superannuation Fund at the time when he was appointed to the Public Service. That being so, he submitted, the decision to accept him into the Provident Account purportedly made in about April 1967 was ineffective. As it was the fons et origo of his present reduced pension, it should be reconsidered by the Commission. Mr Edwards accepted, correctly in our view, that, if the applicant was accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund in 1966, the 1922 Act contained no provision permitting his transfer to the Provident Account in 1967. However, he submitted that the evidence did not show that the applicant was ever accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund; rather it showed that he had not been accepted.

14. The applicant, who although unrepresented had prepared his case thoroughly and presented it with considerable skill, referred the Tribunal to its decision in Re Schmack and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1981) 3 ALN N77 and to the Federal Court's decision in Hunter Valley Development Pty. Ltd. v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344. He sought to relate the facts to the principles to be derived from those decisions. Addressing the question of delay, he said that the extension of time which he was seeking was for a period of only about 10 months. He pointed out that in section 154(1) "decision" is defined as having the same meaning as in the AAT Act. He submitted that the legislature's intention when it used the phrase "come to the notice of" in section 154(2) of the 1976 Act was, therefore, to be ascertained by having regard to the provisions of section 29 of the AAT Act. He referred to the fact that section 29 provided that the time within which application for review of a decision might be made commenced on the day on which a document setting out the terms of the decision was furnished to the applicant. He then argued that a decision should not be regarded as having "come to the notice of" a person for the purposes of section 154(2) of the 1976 Act unless the notice was authoritative and certain.

15. He gave evidence that it was only after he had made several requests under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 for access to documents that he had become aware of the practices of the Public Service Board and the Superannuation Board in 1965, 1966 and 1967 and so had been enabled to understand the significance of information given to him at the time. Consequently, it had been only on 22 February 1985, he said, that he had received authoritative and certain notice that a decision had been made in 1967. He gave evidence that during the 10 months between that date and 30 January 1986, when he had asked to be allowed further time to request reconsideration of that decision, he had had to obtain a good deal of further information in order to be able to support with relevant facts a request for reconsideration. He submitted, therefore, that he had acted diligently at all times.

16. We are unable to accept that in section 154(2) the phrase "the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person" is to be given the meaning for which the applicant argued. The AAT Act was enacted before the 1976 Act. As the applicant pointed out, one word used in section 154 of the 1976 Act is defined by reference to its meaning in the AAT Act. If the legislature had wished to make in section 154(2) provision having the same effect as the provision it had made in section 29 of the AAT Act, it could readily have done so, either by using the same words as in section 29 or by express reference to section 29. As it did not do so, it is not proper to construe section 154(2) by reference to section 29, except possibly for the purpose of contrasting their provisions.

17. Neither section 29 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 nor section 154(2) of the 1976 Act is concerned with notice being given of the reasons for a decision; it is sufficient that the person concerned should know of the decision. In June 1967 the applicant was sent the minute informing him that he "must contribute to the Provident Account". Although there was no express statement that he had been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account pursuant to any particular section of the 1922 Act, nevertheless there is implicit in the statement that he must contribute to the Provident Account a further statement that he had been so accepted. The applicant gave evidence that he was not aware of the statutory basis of either the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account; all he knew was that deductions were being made from his salary for superannuation. Consequently, as the minute did not refer to any legislation, he was unaware that any decision had been taken under any statute.

18. Although he is now 20 years older than he was at that time and it would be unfair to judge his understanding of events then by reference to his very considerable grasp of them now, nevertheless it is apparent that he possessed then a keen mind and had been educated to use it. His appointment to the Public Service was not into the lowest Clerical Administrative grade but as a Research Officer, appointed at a higher level because of his above-average education and intellectual capacity. We cannot accept as credible his evidence that he was unaware of the relevant legislation relating to superannuation. He had the mental capacity and, as he worked in Canberra, the facilities readily to ascertain the statutory basis of the deductions which were being made from his salary. Similarly, we are unable to accept as credible his evidence that he simply accepted the word of a staff clerk that there was no way in which he could seek to have the question of his being required to contribute to the Provident Account instead of the Superannuation Account reviewed. Even if the staff clerk gave him incorrect information, there is a world of difference between the applicant's situation at that time and that of, for instance, the applicant in Re Kenah and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (Decision No. 2250: 26 July 1985), who was a private soldier trained to obey orders and to rely on information supplied to him by officers and who relied on information, in fact incorrect, given to him by an officer. In such a case such reliance may be reasonable if no positive action has been taken to correct the information. But it was not reasonable for the applicant to rely solely on information given to him by the staff clerk. If, as he has asserted, he really did not understand why he had been accepted only as a contributor to the Provident Account, he should, in our view, have pursued the matter further at the time. Provision was contained in section 141 of the 1922 Act for disputes to be dealt with by the Superannuation Board and for appeal to a single Justice of the High Court of Australia when a person was dissatisfied with the Board's determination of a dispute. The applicant gave evidence that he was unaware of that provision; that evidence is, we consider, credible only if he understood at that time why he had been required to contribute to the Provident Account and he accepted that it was a valid reason. Otherwise, we are satisfied, he would have acquainted himself then with the provisions of the 1922 Act and he would have sought his remedy then. We have come to the conclusion, therefore, that, in spite of his assertion of diligence in pursuing a remedy ever since the decision to accept him as a contributor only to the Provident Account came to his notice, we find that it came to his notice in 1967 and that before 1986 he had failed to take reasonable steps to seek to have it reviewed.

19. The next matter which the applicant submitted should be considered and we accept as relevant is whether the applicant would benefit if, upon a reconsideration of the decision made in 1967 to accept him as a contributor to the Provident Account in 1967, it were decided either that in 1966 he was already a contributor to the Superannuation Fund or that he should have been accepted as a contributor to the Fund. As that would have the effect that no benefit classification certificate would be deemed to have been issued and he would be entitled to receive pension at a considerably higher rate than he is receiving at present, it would clearly benefit him substantially.

20. The next matter required to be considered is prejudice to the respondent if it is decided to allow further time to request reconsideration of the 1967 decision. Mr Edwards pointed out that, unless it were found that the applicant had been accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund in 1966, reconsideration of the 1967 decision would require investigation of the state of his health at that time. He said that the only documentary evidence of that which was likely to be available would be the reports of his two medical examinations. Even if the medical officers who conducted them were still alive, it was doubtful if they would have any personal recollection of the examinations which might enable them to add to what was in the reports. The specialist who was treating him in 1967 might possibly, if still alive, have notes made at the time and some recollection of the applicant.

21. The applicant submitted that the Commissioner could not be prejudiced because the only argument which he would present on a reconsideration of the 1967 decision would be that the Superannuation Board had no power to make it because it had already accepted him in 1966 as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. However, while we do not query the applicant's bona fides in respect of his statement of his present intention, reconsideration of the decision made in 1967 could not be limited to the question of its legality. If the Commissioner found that it was within power, he would then have to consider the merits of the decision to accept the applicant as a contributor to the Provident Account. To do that he would need to investigate the state of his health at that time and to ascertain from what medical conditions, if any, he suffered and how serious they were. Very little expert evidence would be likely to be available; but the applicant would be able to give personal evidence of the state of his health which it might be difficult to challenge. In those circumstances the Commissioner would be prejudiced in his ability to conduct the reconsideration and make a properly informed decision on the matter.

22. In his statement of the reasons for the decision under review, which was lodged with the Tribunal in pursuance of section 37 of the AAT Act, Mr Gunther stated that, if a further period of many years were allowed for the applicant to apply for reconsideration of the Superannuation Board's decision, the respondent would be prejudiced also because it would open the door to applications by others for lengthy extensions of time for reconsideration of decisions of the Board made before July 1976. He said that many persons had been contributors to the Provident Account; any of them might wish to have the decision reconsidered by which he was accepted for the Provident Account. The applicant stated that there were very few persons whose circumstances were similar to his own, that is to say who were accepted as contributors to the Provident Fund after deductions from their salaries had been made for some time at the rate of contributions to the Superannuation Fund. Whether that is so we cannot verify from the evidence available to us. But even if it is, not only persons with circumstances similar to his own may wish to have the decisions reconsidered which resulted in their being accepted as contributors to the Provident Fund. Expert medical evidence is likely to be as unavailable in their cases as it is in the applicant's case. We are satisfied, therefore, that, unless the grounds for allowing the applicant further time of many years were found to be much stronger than the grounds which might be relied on in other cases, allowing him the further time he is seeking would be likely to result in a considerable additional administrative burden for the Commissioner and his staff. On the evidence before us we find that the grounds on which he seeks to rely are not strong.

23. Finally we turn to consideration of the merits of the applicant's case for reconsideration. That was not referred to in Re Schmack but was considered by the Federal Court in Hunter Valley Development Pty. Ltd. to be a factor to which regard must be paid. We consider that the applicant would have an arguable case. However, it would not be overwhelmingly strong, as was the case in Re Edward Souery and Co. Pty. Ltd. and Export Market Development Grants Board (Decision No. 2414: 18 November 1985). There an extension of time of two years was granted because the Federal Court had decided that a similar decision of the Export Market Development Grants Board in respect of another person was ultra vires. Consequently, the person applying for the extension of time was practically assured of success upon reconsideration.

24. In the present case some evidence might be seen as supporting an inference that the applicant was accepted into the Superannuation Fund in January 1966. Notwithstanding the provision in section 5(2) of the 1922 Act that, after the Public Service Board had issued a certificate in respect of an employee, the Superannuation Board had a discretion whether to accept the employee as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, the documents disclose that the Superannuation Board had developed a practice of automatically accepting as contributors employees in respect of whom certificates had been issued. When in January 1966 the Public Service Board offered the applicant appointment as a Research Officer Grade 1, it informed him that the section of the leaflet relating to medical examination would not apply in his case as he had already completed the requisite examination. That might be understood as indicating that it had decided to issue a certificate that his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund in accordance with the recommendation of the medical practitioner who had examined him in the United Kingdom.

25. However, there is other documentary evidence that, although (as was eventually made clear to the Superannuation Board in an opinion of an officer of the Attorney-General's Department in November 1970) the obligation to contribute to the Superannuation Fund did not arise until an employee had been accepted as a contributor, in 1966 and 1967 the Superannuation Board misunderstood section 5 of the 1922 Act. It believed that section 5 required the backdating of acceptance of an employee as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account to the date when employment commenced, so that the obligation to contribute to the one or the other existed from that date. It thought that, even if a decision to accept an employee as a contributor were not taken until after employment had commenced, the decision had to be made to have effect from the date of commencement of employment. Consequently, in order to avoid employees having to pay large amounts in respect of arrears of contributions when decisions whether to accept them as contributors to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account were delayed for some months, the Superannuation Board and the Public Service Board had devised a procedure by which deductions of the amounts of contributions to the Superannuation Fund were made from their salaries from the time when they commenced their employment. In December 1965 the Public Service Board issued an instruction for there to be inserted in copies of the leaflet sent with an offer of appointment a slip containing a statement that deductions from salary would be made from the commencement of employment but that the employee would be advised by his department when the medical certificate had been given and that until then the deductions did not establish any entitlements under the 1922 Act.

26. In view of the lapse of time, the weight to be given to the fact that there is on file no copy of a certificate issued under section 5(2)(c) of the 1922 Act to the effect that the applicant's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, may not be great. However, a copy of the certificate issued in April 1967 is on file; also in the letter to the applicant offering him appointment there was no mention of any certificate in respect of acceptance for the Superannuation Fund having been issued. The documentary evidence presented to us, therefore, could support an inference that no certificate was issued and that the deductions from salary were made in accordance with the administrative instruction issued by the Public Service Board in December 1965. In our view the evidence supporting that inference is at least as strong as that supporting the inference that they were deducted because he had been accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund.

27. Having taken into account the factors referred to in Re Schmack, Re Kenah and Hunter Valley Development Pty. Ltd., we have no doubt that the further period of nearly 20 years which the applicant asks to have allowed so that he may request the reconsideration of the Superannuation Board's decision in 1967 should not be allowed and consequently that the decision under review should be affirmed.
