the Taxpayer and Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 266 (29 July 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

THE TAXPAYER v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. NT 95/180, NT 96/107-108,

NT96/312-317, NT96/386

AAT No. 074.6

Number of pages - 11

Income tax



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

J BLOCK (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income tax - amended assessments - unsatisfactory books and records and unsatisfactory evidence - onus of proof under section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63

Denley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1986) 86 ATC 4423

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco (1990) 90 ATC 4088

McCormack v FCT [1979] HCA 18; (1979) 143 CLR 284

Vale Press Pty Limited v FC of T (No 2) [1994] FCA 1335; (1994) 124 ALR 210

McCauley v FC of T (1988) 88 ATC 4605

HEARING

SYDNEY, 16 July 1997 (hearing), 29 July 1997 (decision)

29:7:1997

Appearances

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr P Lever-Naylor

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr P Roberts

Solicitor for the Respondent: Mr M Donohoe of the Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The decisions under review are affirmed.

DECISION

J BLOCK

These matters, heard together, relate to a decision of the Respondent issued on 23 September 1994 to disallow objections against amended assessments in respect of the years ended 30 June 1987, 30 June 1988 and 30 June 1989, and a decision of the Respondent issued on 14 June 1996 to disallow objections against amended assessments in respect of the years ended 30 June 1981, 30 June 1982, 30 June 1983, 30 June 1984, 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986; the years referred to in this paragraph 1 are collectively referred to as the "relevant years"; the term "1981 year" will be used to refer to the year ended 30 June 1981, and the other relevant years are referred to correspondingly.

2. (a) The Applicant was represented by Mr Peter Lever-Naylor of Counsel and the Respondent was represented by Mr Paul Roberts of Counsel, instructed by Mr Michael Donohoe of the office of the Australian Government Solicitor. The Tribunal admitted into evidence the documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Act 1975; the term "First T-Documents" refers to the T Documents in respect of case No.NT96/312-317 in relation to the 1981 year - 1986 year (both years inclusive); the term "Second T Documents" refers to the T Documents in respect of case No.NT95/180 in relation to the 1987 year - 1989 year (both years inclusive). Each reference to the T Documents will be preceded by either the number 1 or the number 2 so as to refer to the First T Documents or the Second T Documents as the case may be; the number succeeding the letter T will in each case refer to the page number of the First T Documents or the Second T Documents, as the case may be.

(b) The Tribunal also received (prior to the hearing) a number of statements, and in particular:

(1) a statement by the Applicant dated 30 June 1987, which is referred to as the "Applicant Statement"; (2) a statement dated 26 June 1987 by an Appeals & Review Officer in the Respondent's office in Adelaide; that Officer is referred to as "H", and her statement is referred to as the "H Statement"; the H Statement includes an attachment marked "A" which is the record of an interview ("Interview Record") with the Applicant on 21 November 1990; (3) a statement dated 13 June 1987 by a Senior Officer Grade C in the Respondent's office in Adelaide; that Officer is referred to as "P" and his statement is referred to as the "P Statement"; the P Statement includes a number of annexures and in particular annexure "D" which sets out in respect of the relevant years, an analysis of proposed adjustments; (4) a statement dated 15 July 1997 by an Auditor in the Respondent's office in Hurstville; that Auditor is referred as "B" and his statement is referred to as the "B Statement"; the B Statement sets out in particular that B agreed with Annexure D to the P Statement.
(c) Other statements were received, but it is unnecessary to refer to them specifically because the persons who made those statements were not called to give evidence before the Tribunal.

(d) Oral evidence was given by the Applicant and also each of H, P and B.

3. In respect of the Applicant's evidence:

( a) In general terms, the Applicant's evidence was unsatisfactory. In many instances he answered questions with a reply to the effect that he could not remember; given the length of time which has ensued since the relevant years, his inability to answer certain questions was understandable, but, having regard to all the circumstances and the onus on him under section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act ("TAA"), it did not assist his case; he was moreover on some occasions evasive, and on at least two occasions, in the opinion of the Tribunal, untruthful.

(b) Clause 3 of the Applicant's Statement (edited to preserve anonymity) reads as follows:

"Owing to the sudden death of my previous accountant, ["previous accountant"], no tax returns were filed on my behalf for the tax years 1981 to 1989"
Paragraph 4 of that statement (again edited for the same reason) then goes on to say that:
"Because of the backlog it took ["the Accountant"] until late 1991 to prepare and lodge my tax returns for those years and meanwhile section 167 assessments based in part on an examination of my bank deposits and draft profit and loss statements prepared by ["the Accountant"], issued in August 1991."
During the course of the Applicant's evidence, it appeared that the previous accountant had at some point in time moved away from Sydney, but that he had maintained an office in Sydney; the Applicant could not place a date on when the previous accountant died or when the move took place. The statement to the effect that no tax returns were filed in respect of all of the relevant years "owing to the sudden death of my previous accountant" is clearly, in the view of the Tribunal, untruthful.

(c) The last paragraph of paragraph 13 of the Applicant's Statement (again edited) reads as follows:

"Regarding the use by the Commissioner of financial figures submitted by my solicitor to ("Finance Company") for the purposes of raising money for a joint new building project, these were optimistic projections only and the actual assessable income was as provided in my signed amended income tax return of 30 April 1993."
2T47-59; 2T47 is a fax by a bank (referred to as the "Bank") to the Respondent attaching "Valuation of Security Property, and 1988; 1989 P/L Statements"; 2T48 is a Management Profit & Loss Statement in respect of the Applicant for the 1988 year which reflects fees received of $934,971.27 and a net profit (exclusive of interest received) of $137,854.80; 2T49 is a Management Profit & Loss Statement for the 1989 year reflecting fees received of $864,407.95 and a net profit (exclusive of interest received) of $109,659.95; 2T50 (and following) is a valuation of certain property situated in the Eastern Suburbs of Sydney (the "Eastern Property") referred to later in these Reasons.

The Applicant denied all knowledge of the two Profit and Loss Statements; he attributed their preparation and despatch solely to his solicitor (the "solicitor"), who has since left the country, and with whom he had an association which, while it was initially a solicitor and client relationship, apparently became, at some later stage (not specified) a rather closer involvement and involving an incorporated joint venture. It is relevant to note that there was no evidence by the solicitor; nor for that matter was there any evidence (apart from his statement) by the Accountant, who prepared a number of returns by the Applicant, referred to later in these Reasons.

The Profit & Loss Accounts contained at 2T48 and 2T49 were demonstrably not projections; they were in fact Profit and Loss Accounts for the 1988 year and the 1989 year respectively, and must have been received by the Bank at some point in time after the end of the 1989 year; the valuation which commences at 2T50 is endorsed to the effect that it was received in July 1989.

The Applicant's description of the accounts as "optimistic projections" was manifestly untruthful; moreover the Tribunal does not accept that he had no involvement in their preparation and submission to the Finance Company or the Bank (which appear to have been associated entities).

(d) 2T2 is the first page of the Respondent's Statement of Findings in relation to the 1987 year - 1989 year (inclusive). Clause 7 and 8 refer to two companies (neither of which has ever lodged returns) alleged to have been associated with the Applicant into which moneys were alleged to have been deposited; in respect of one of those companies the Applicant is referred to as the principal shareholder. The Applicant was evasive as to that company and the extent of his shareholding in it, although he was prepared to accept that he had some shareholding in it; it would appear that it was that company which was used in respect of the incorporated joint venture involving the solicitor.

4. (a) It is relevant at this stage to note that throughout the relevant years, the Applicant was the registered proprietor of the Eastern Property and also another real property which is referred to as the "Hill Property". The Hill Property consists of a timber and weatherboard cottage whereas the Eastern Property consists of three units and a shop; in respect of both properties, rental income was derived; specifically in respect of the Eastern Property, the Applicant and his family lived in one of the units for a period.

(b) The Applicant gave evidence that both properties were dilapidated and in need of repair; he also said that although the properties were registered in his name, they were owned by him and his wife in partnership; the Applicant's tax returns included references to partnership returns reflecting, in respect of some of the relevant years, a comparatively small loss; (although no partnership agreement was produced, and although the Applicant's wife did not give evidence, the Tribunal accepts that the properties were owned through a partnership in which the Applicant and his wife were partners).

(c) The Applicant stated (but could not produce any supporting evidence) that he had expended considerable sums on repairs to the properties. (There was no specific evidence as to the nature of those repairs or the amounts expended). The Respondent's audit (and see Annexure D to the P Statement) indicated that amounts referable to repairs to the properties and other capital expenditure in respect of them had been claimed as deductions by the Applicant in his own return. The Applicant's recollection in this regard was extremely uncertain; however it is clear that claims referable to the properties should have been included in the partnership return and not the Applicant's return. No partnership returns were produced to the Tribunal. It is convenient at this juncture to note that each of H, P and B gave evidence, in broad terms, confirming their respective statements. P was cross-examined, by Mr Lever-Naylor, as to his understanding of what constitutes a repair. The Tribunal notes in this context that the Applicant appears to have misconceived his position as regards expenditure referable to the properties; insofar as moneys were expended to retire debt (and the Applicant stated that vendor finance had been obtained) that expenditure would be of a capital nature. Repairs might be either capital or income depending on a number of factors none of which were traversed before the Tribunal. But the important point for this purpose is that to the extent that any repair costs were properly deductible on income account, they should have been claimed in the partnership returns and not in the Applicant's returns.

5. (a) An audit in respect of the Applicant commenced in April 1990. It revealed that the Applicant did not keep proper books and records for his contracting business. In particular he did not maintain a journal or a ledger or other books of account; on the contrary, his bank statements were his only records as to incomings whereas his cheque counterfoils were his only records of outgoings. Moreover it would seem that the cheque counterfoils were usually silent as to the name of the payee (assuming in any given case the cheque was not made out to cash) or in the case of a counterfoil, which purported to relate to a sub-contractor, the name of the sub-contractor, or the contract on which the sub-contractor had been engaged. The Applicant did not retain invoices or other vouchers in respect of payments to sub-contractors.

(b) There was some rather vague evidence as to the fact that building contractors, such as the Applicant, conventionally receive some money in cash; the Applicant in his evidence stated that, during the relevant years, he would not have received much in the way of cash, and that generally the receipt by him of cash was rare. Since the Applicant could remember virtually nothing in respect of the relevant years, and since his records are so inadequate, the Tribunal doubts whether much reliance can be placed on this statement. It may be noted in this context that page 5 of the Interview Record reveals the following interchange:

"Q. Is that all the cash that you actually get, is that banked? A. No it is not, some of it goes ..."
(c) The cheque counterfoils were clearly a most unsatisfactory method of maintaining a record of outgoings; even more to the point is the fact that the Applicant claimed that all of the cheque counterfoils related in effect to outgoings referable to his contracting business; although there was no specific evidence on this aspect, it seems likely that some at least of those payments would have referred to amounts drawn by the Applicant and his family for living expenses. The Applicant said in his evidence that living expenses were provided in general terms by his wife out of her earnings; the Applicant's wife in respect of each of the relevant years received an amount, usually approximately $6,000, by way of salary from the business. The Applicant in his evidence said that his wife had other income; however, the Interview Record (page 6) indicated in answer to a question: "Has she any sort of other income" that the answer was "No". Moreover the P Statement indicates that a spouse rebate was allowed for each year based on a statement by the Applicant, in the interview on 21 November 1990, that his wife did not receive any income during the period under review. There was other (equally vague) evidence as to the involvement of the Applicant and members of his family in the acquisition of racehorses and racing, but there was no evidence as to amounts expended for this purpose.

(d) The Tribunal notes that these matters relate to the amended assessments issued by the Respondent; the Tribunal need not (as the Respondent's Counsel confirmed) be concerned with the fact that a spouse rebate was consistently (and incorrectly) allowed to the Applicant; nor need the Tribunal be concerned with an arithmetical error made by the Respondent in one of the amended assessments.

6. (a) Although no specific evidence in this regard was produced to the Tribunal, the Applicant admitted that he had been prosecuted on a number of occasions for his failure to render returns.

(b) Returns were eventually filed on behalf of the Applicant in respect of the relevant years, after the Respondent had raised assessments under section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("ITAA"); in general terms, the section 167 assessments were calculated on the basis that, of the gross fees received by the Applicant, 10% represented the taxable or profit factor.

(c) In April 1992, the Applicant filed income tax returns in respect of all of the relevant years. Each of those returns was prepared by the Accountant; each return usually contains the tax agent's certificate in the following terms (edited for anonymity):

"We [the Accountant] having charged a fee for preparing or assisting in the preparation of this return, hereby certify that the answers set forth above in the second column in this statement opposite to the questions set forth in the first column thereof, are true and correct in every particular."
One of those questions requires an answer as to "What books of account if any are kept by or on behalf of the taxpayer?; the answer was "Usual records, bank statements, etc." The Tribunal notes that it is surprised that the Accountant was prepared to issue certificates on this basis when quite plainly the records maintained by the Applicant were grossly inadequate, and surely not "usual".

(d) The Applicant, in respect of the 1987 year, filed a return dated 10 April 1992; that return (2T16-21) indicated, inter alia, that in respect of the 1987 year (and as appears from 2T 18):

( 1) contract fees received amounted to $439,557.48;

(2) expenditure in respect of contract work amounted to $110,820.70; and

(3)expenditure in respect of materials and purchases amounted to $346,733.40.

Subsequently, however, the Applicant filed an amended return of income for the 1987 year; that return (2T25-29) (which does not indicate the date in which it was signed) includes a profit and loss account (2T 27) on very different terms and in particular:

( 1) contract fees received (apart from interest received of $13,969.93) amounted to $1,224,208.93;

(2) expenditure in respect of contract work amounted to $599,153.88; and

(3) expenditure in respect of materials and purchases amounted to $530,170.64.

The differences between the two profit and loss statements are of so material a nature (some only of which have been set out in these Reasons) that the Tribunal cannot realistically rely on either of them. Presumably though, as the Applicant's Counsel urged, the later statement is, notwithstanding the paucity of the material from which it was prepared, to the be preferred to earlier statement. But there was no explanation for the startling differences in the most material amounts.

(e) In respect of the 1988 year there are again differences (but not as significant) between the profit and loss account filed in April 1992 (2T 43) and the amended profit and loss account filed in April 1993 (2T 62).

(f) In respect of the 1987 year, the Respondent prepared an analysis of allowable expenditure; that analysis which is set out at 2T13-14 is an exhaustive analysis of how the Commissioner came to allow an amount of $1,053,465.23 by way of expenditure in substitution of the higher amount claimed by the Respondent.

7. Mr Roberts contended that the Respondent had gone to some lengths to ensure that the Respondent had been treated fairly and indeed, in the opinion of the Tribunal, generously. Annexure D ("Annexure D") to the P Statement indicates the extent to which this is so. In respect of a number of relevant years, the Respondent accepted returns or substantial parts of them, in the form in which they were lodged, notwithstanding the Respondent's understandable and justifiable reservations, most particularly as to whether incomings as per bank statements constituted all of the relevant income, and whether payments extracted from cheque counterfoils (inadequately noted as set out previously) did not include private or domestic expenditure, or other expenditure not properly deductible by the Applicant in respect of his contracting business.

8. I turn now to deal with each relevant year in turn:

(a) the 1981 year

( 1) Following an exhaustive analysis during the audit (and see clause 1 of 1T4) the Respondent disallowed an amount of $18,939, in respect of expenses referable to the properties but included (and see Annexure D to the P Statement) by the Applicant in his own return in respect of materials and sub-contractors; this disallowance had the effect of reducing the 1981 year assessable income to nil.

(2) In the result, and in respect of the 1981 year, there was no loss to be carried forward.

(3) The Respondent otherwise (and except as set out in subclause (1)), accepted the Applicant's return.

(b) the 1984 year

The position in respect of the 1984 year is similar to that in respect of the 1981 year, except that the amount disallowed to the Applicant was $27,784.

(c) the 1992 year, the 1993 year, the 1985 year and the 1986 year

Excepting only that the Respondent disallowed any claims as to losses carried forward, the Respondent accepted the Applicant's returns.

(d) the 1987 year

( 1) In respect of the 1987 year, the Respondent in consequence of his analysis (previously referred to and see 2T13-14) reduced the deductions claimed from $1,224,208 to $1,053,464.

(2) Mr Lever-Naylor argued that in respect of the 1987 year there were two profit and loss statements prepared by the Applicant, that the later was to be preferred to the earlier, and that it should be preferred to the Respondent's analysis on the basis that they were all estimates. The Tribunal notes that Mr Lever-Naylor did not, in the context (or any other context) address the question of onus under section 14ZZK of TAA; in any event the Tribunal considers that the Respondent's analysis, which was plainly prepared after careful enquiries, is likely to be correct.

(3) Except as set out in subclause (1) and except that no loss carry forward was allowed the Respondent accepted the amended return.

(e) the 1988 year

( 1) In this instance, the Respondent substituted $934,971 (in accordance with the profit and loss statement furnished to the Finance Company or Bank and see 2T48) for the amount of $870,576.65 reflected by the Applicant in his amended profit and loss statement. The Tribunal accepts that the Respondent was, in the circumstances, correct to do so. The Tribunal has, as set out previously, refused to accept that the statement in which an amount of $934,791 was reflected was no more than an optimistic projection in which the Applicant played no part.

(2) Except as set out in subclause (1) and except that no loss carry forward was allowed, the Respondent accepted the Applicant's amended return.

(f) the 1989 year

In this instance the Respondent accepted the Applicant's return, excepting only that interest of $816 was included in accordance with figures furnished to the Finance Company or Bank; see 2T49; the Tribunal accepts that the Respondent was correct to do so.

9. The Applicant's evidence was in general terms unsatisfactory and cannot be accepted. It cannot be said that the Applicant made any real effort to discharge the onus on him under section 14ZZK of TAA. The evidence reveals that returns were produced only after prosecutions, an extensive audit and assessments under section 167 of ITAA. Returns and (where relevant) amended returns were prepared from records which were grossly unsatisfactory; in respect of the 1987 year in particular, the return and amended return are entirely irreconcilable. The evidence indicates in the clearest terms that the Applicant has repeatedly sought to avoid his tax obligations. The evidence by H, P and B (which was entirely credit-worthy) indicates that the Respondent was put to an enormous amount of effort in relation to the Applicant's affairs, and (as set out previously) in accepting much of what was returned (despite justifiable reservations) by the Applicant, they "leant over backwards" (as Mr Roberts contended) to treat him fairly and even generously.

10. In Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63, Latham CJ at pages 86 and following said (in relation to section 39 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1934) that:

"The latest amendments were made as a result of the consideration by the commissioner of reports made and accounts prepared by Smith, Johnson & Co., a firm of accountants employed by the taxpayer. Pars. 22 and 23 of the case show the course adopted by the commissioner and par. 24 shows that a precise allocation of income to the years in question is not possible. -- - They, and other facts stated, show that the taxpayer, having kept no proper record or books of account, has not provided information which enabled the commissioner to attribute to any particular year certain of the gains which he has made during the seven years 1921 to 1927 inclusive. - - - Sec.39 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1934 provides (inter alia) that the production of any notice or copy notice of assessment under the hand of the commissioner shall be conclusive evidence that the assessment has been duly made and that the amount and all the particulars of the assessment are correct, except in proceedings on appeal against the assessment, when it shall be prima facie evidence only. Isaacs J. said in Moreau v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1) that sec. 39 "throws the burden on the appellant does not rebut the presumption created by sec. 39 merely by showing that there is an error in it - and thereby "creating a blank" - a complete omission of the item in question - or that something else should be substituted for that item. The circumstance that the facts are (or were) peculiarly within the knowledge of one party is a relevant matter in considering the sufficiency of evidence to discharge a burden of proof. (See cases cited by Isaacs J. in Williamson v Ah On (2).) Obviously the facts in relation to his income are facts peculiarly within the knowledge of the taxpayer.
In the absence of some record in the mind or in the books of the taxpayer, it would often be quite impossible to make a correct assessment. The assessment would necessarily be a guess to some extent, and almost certainly inaccurate in fact. There is every reason to assume that the legislature did not intend to confer upon a potential taxpayer the valuable privilege of disqualifying himself in that capacity by the simple and relatively unskilled method of losing either his memory or his books.
The application of sec. 39 is not, in my opinion, excluded as soon as it is shown that an element in the assessment is a guess and that it is therefore very probably wrong. It is prima facie right - and remains right until the appellant shows that it is wrong. If it were necessary to decide the point I would, as at present advised, be prepared to hold that the taxpayer must, at least as a general rule, go further and show, not only negatively that the assessment is wrong, but also positively what correction should be made in order to make it right or more nearly right"
11. In McCauley v FC of T (1988) 88 ATC 4605 Lockhart J at page 4607 said:
"The taxpayer relies on the delay of some four years of the Commissioner in issuing the assessments after the returns were filed and the subsequent delay of almost five years in referring the matters to the Supreme Court of New South Wales as explaining the absence of records on his part. This is not a sound contention as there were hardly any records ever retained by the taxpayer relating to his betting activities. Moreover, the taxpayer must have known from 1976 onwards, after he became aware that his affairs were being investigated, that whatever records he had should be retained safe." "Ordinary prudence should have dictated that the taxpayer keep reasonable records of his betting activities. There is some evidence that certain records were kept by members of the syndicate other than the taxpayer, but none were tendered in evidence."
12. In Vale Press Pty Ltd v FC of T (No 2) [1994] FCA 1335; (1994) 124 ALR 210 the following extracts from the judgment of Hill J are of assistance:
"(1) That the burden of proof of showing the assessment was excessive lay upon the Applicant by force of s14ZZO(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth). That legal burden remained at all times with the Applicant and did not shift and that this burden would be the same whether the Commissioner had assessed under ss18(3A), 25(2A) or (as was the case here) s25AA of the Assessment Act. (2) That for the Applicant to discharge the burden imposed upon it of showing that the assessment was excessive, the Applicant must show not merely that the sale value calculated by the Commissioner was wrong and in consequence the sales tax assessed was too high, but also must show what the appropriate sale value was and in consequence what the appropriate sales tax to be assessed was. The Applicant properly accepted that the ultimate burden lay upon the Applicant to show the assessment was excessive. The matter really at issue between the parties depends, in my view, ultimately upon whether it was necessary for the Applicant, in discharging its burden under s14ZZO(b)(i) of showing the assessment to be excessive, to show that the proper sale value is a figure less than that adopted by the Commissioner so that there can be quantified a lesser sales tax liability than that adopted by the Commissioner in the assessment. It is now well settled that, in the context of income tax assessment made under s167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), a taxpayer will not succeed in showing the assessment to be excessive unless the taxpayer has shown not merely that the assessment proceeded on a wrong basis, but that the amount assessed as his taxable income in fact exceeded his real taxable income with the consequence that he has shown the extent of the excessiveness. "The view that for a taxpayer to succeed it was necessary to establish not only that the assessment was wrong but the extent of the excessiveness, originated from the decision of the High Court in Trautwein to which reference has already been made". The view originating in Trautwein that a taxpayer was required to show not merely that the assessment was wrong but the extent to which it was wrong, at least in a case involving a default assessment, thereafter prevailed: McEvoy v FCT (1950) 9 ATD 206 at 210-11; George v FCT [1952] HCA 21; (1952) 86 CLR 183 at 189 (per Kitto J) and more recently in Dalco's case".
13. In Denley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1986) 86 ATC 4423 Kelly J at pages 4426 and 4427 said:

"In George v FC of T [1952] HCA 21; (1952) 86 C.L.R. 183, the High Court said, at p.204:

"...unless the taxpayer discharges the burden laid upon him by s.190(b) of proving that this ascertainment or judgment is excessive, he cannot succeed and it can be no part of the duty of the commissioner to establish affirmatively what judgment he formed, much less the grounds of it, and even less still the truth of the facts affording the grounds. ...It is an error to treat the formation by the commissioner of a judgment as to the amount of the taxable income as if it were not the ascertainment of the taxable income which constitutes assessment or a necessary part of that process and as if it were but the fulfilment of a condition precedent to the power or authority to assess."
14. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco (1990) 90 ATC 4088, Brennan J (as he then was) said at p.4092:
"The ground of objection on which the taxpayer here relies is error in the formation of a judgment as to the amount on which tax ought to be levied. But mere error in the formation of that judgment by the Commissioner does not warrant the setting aside of the amount assessed. Given the validity of the exercise of the power to make an assessment under s.167(b), the ultimate question is whether the amount of the assessment is excessive. The amount of the assessment might not be excessive in fact, though the reasons which led to the assessment were erroneous. In George's Case the Full Court said, at p 201: "the law has always been taken to be that in an appeal from an assessment the burden lies upon the taxpayer of establishing affirmatively that the amount of taxable income for which he has been assessed exceeds the actual taxable income which he has derived during the year of income".
Kitto J., from whose judgment the appeal in George's Case was brought, said (at p 189):
"[Section] 190 (b) places the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive upon the appellant; and in order to carry that burden he must necessarily exclude by his proof all sources of income except those which he admits. His case must be that he did not derive from any source taxable income to the amount of the assessment.""
15. Although the cases cited refer to predecessor sections, the onus on the Applicant under section 14ZZK of the TAA is not materially different. In fact the position of the Applicant is, if anything, worse than some of the taxpayers referred to in such cases. In respect of the Applicant, the amended assessments cannot properly be characterised as estimates or guesses but rather as having been calculated in the light of exhaustive investigations, analyses, and in some cases, in the light of profit and loss statements referable to the Applicant himself. The Applicant bears the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive; section 14ZZK(b) effectively creates a rebuttable presumption that an assessment is not excessive; see McCormack v FCT (1979) 143 CLR 294.

16. (a) The Tribunal does not consider that the Applicant has made any real attempt to discharge the onus. Indeed the evidence, and his failure to keep proper records made it difficult, if not impossible, for him to do so.

(b) There is no argument by either party as to penalties, and indeed Mr Roberts advised the Tribunal (without dispute from Mr Lever-Naylor) that there was no issue between the parties as to penalties. It is thus unnecessary for the Tribunal to deal with this aspect; the Tribunal notes to the extent that it may be relevant, that if the penalties imposed had been an issue, it would not have considered them inappropriate.

17. Accordingly, the objection decisions under review are affirmed.
