Re Ernest Sydney Scott and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 135 (10 April 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ERNEST SYDNEY SCOTT

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. N86/115

AAT No. 3369

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRUBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Justice J.D. Davies (President)

Dr A.P. Renouf (Senior Member)

Dr H.D. Browne (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - discretion in s.7(2) to deem person retired on ground of physical or mental incapacity when person ceased to be an eligible employee, otherwise than by reason of death or on the ground of physical or mental incapacity - precondition that person unfit to perform duties by reason of physical or mental incapacity at that time - circumstances in which discretion may be exercised - whether applicant can be put back into position he would have been in had he retired on the ground of invalidity

Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth)

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) - ss.7, 150, 183

Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth)

Telecommunications Act 1975 (Cth) - s.56

Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (Cth)

Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (Cth)

Superannuation (Former Provident Account Contributors) Regulations

Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (No. VG.140 of 1986, 15 December 1986)

HEARING

CANBERRA

10:4:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

This is a review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, made on 4 March 1983 and subsequently confirmed by the Commissioner, refusing to exercise favourably to the applicant, Mr E.S. Scott, the discretion conferred by s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Act").

2. That provision reads :

"7.(2) Where a person has, before attaining his

maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible

employee otherwise than by reason of death or by

reason of his having been retired, or his services

having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of

physical or mental incapacity to perform his

duties, but the Commissioner is satisfied that, at

the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he

was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity,

unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner may

direct that the person shall, for the purposes of

this Act, be deemed to have been retired on the

ground of that physical or mental incapacity."

3. As was shown in Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia, delivered 15 December 1986), s.7(2) confers upon the Commissioner a power which is to be exercised in his discretion. It is a power which enables the Commissioner to treat a person, who has ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by death or by having been retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity, as a person deemed to have been so retired, that is to say, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform the duties of his office.

4. A person who ceases to be an eligible employee prior to his attaining the maximum retiring age may do so by reason of death, by virtue of his resignation, by dismissal on a number of grounds or by reason of his having been retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform the duties of office. Retirement on the ground of invalidity may be effected with or without the approval of the employee after the employee has been medically examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer or other appropriate person and when it is shown on medical grounds that the employee is unfit to perform the duties of office. See, eg, s.56(1)(c) of the Telecommunications Act 1975 (Cth). We need not set out details of the superannuation provisions, save to say that generally greater benefits are payable to employees who retire by reason of age or on the ground of invalidity than to employees who resign or are retired early on some other ground.

5. The applicant, Mr E.S. Scott, was born on 18 June 1923. He was employed by Telecom Australia and its predecessor for 30 years or more and became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 19 May 1960, when given the opportunity to become a contributor under the then Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth). Mr Scott would have been able to retire by reason of age on 18 June 1983 on attaining 60 years of age but in fact resigned on 3 October 1980. In his letter of resignation, Mr Scott specified the reason for his resignation as "medical grounds" but he did not undergo a medical examination or otherwise go through the process necessary for retirement on the ground of invalidity. His resignation was accepted when he informed a Personnel Officer in Telecom that he intended to obtain a Service pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth).

6. Had Mr Scott been retired on the ground of invalidity he would have been entitled to an invalidity pension and would also have had the option to commute that pension and to receive in lieu a lump sum being three times the sum of his contributions. That sum would have been $25,915.29. By reason of his resignation, Mr Scott received only a sum equal to the total of his contributions, that is, $8,638.43. See the Superannuation (Former Provident Account Contributors) Regulations, SR No.227 of 1977, which modified the operation of the Act, pursuant to s.183 thereof.

7. The discretion conferred by s.7(2) of the Act may be exercised whenever it appears subsequent to cessation of employment that, by reason of physical or mental problems, the employee could have been retired on the ground of invalidity and that, in all the circumstances of the case, justice would be done if the employee were treated as if he had been so retired. Such a circumstance may arise, for example, when subsequent events throw a different light upon a medical condition existing at the time of cessation of employment. The discretion ought not to be exercised so as to place the retired employee in a better position than other employees. Rather, it is a discretion which enables a person, who if the facts had been fully known and understood at the time of cessation of employment would have been retired on the ground of invalidity, to be treated as if retired, provided it is just to do so.

8. It is a pre-condition of the exercise of the discretion that the employee was, at the time of cessation of employment, by reason of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his duties. We are of the opinion that that condition is satisfied in Mr Scott's case.

9. Throughout the time of his employment with Telecom and its predecessor, Mr Scott was an electro-plater. He was a good tradesman, though towards the end of his employment he was slower in his work than he had been. He does not appear to have had any problems with the work itself or any problems with his superior officers. Mr Scott did, however, have some difficulty in communicating with others and we believe some limitation in his interpersonal relationships. Mr Scott is unable to write a letter. His letters are written for him by his wife, who sometimes also signs his name for him. Mr Scott has difficulty communicating on the telephone. It is not surprising to find that Mr Scott has had a history of anxiety and that his anxiety has reflected itself to some extent in his relationship with his fellow workers. The sick leave records record that on 20-21 December 1960 Mr Scott suffered from "neurasthenic", on 27 August 1969 from "nervous disorder", on 28 August 1970 from "nerves upset" and on 10 May 1972 from "neurasthenia". Such problems continued.

10. Mr Scott's general medical practitioner from October 1974 was Dr R.G. Williams. Mr Scott first presented to Dr Williams with a complaint of pain in the left heel. That was diagnosed as "plantar fasciitis". Over the years, Mr Scott complained of pain and swelling in both feet and said that his condition was aggravated by his working in rubber boots and by walking or standing for any length of time. Dr Williams is of the opinion that Mr Scott may have osteoarthritis in both feet but there is no x-ray evidence to support this diagnosis. A medical specialist, Dr D.C. Mackenzie, examined Mr Scott in 1985 and reported

"Present complaints are of pain in both feet and up

to both thighs. This can occur whether walking,

standing or even lying down. (He walks with the

use of a stick.) ...".

Dr Mackenzie found little physical reason for these complaints.

11. Whether the condition of Mr Scott's feet has been a purely organic one or whether there was a psychic or psychological contribution cannot be determined. We are satisfied that Mr Scott has had significant disability with his feet and that the disability has been considerably greater than Dr Mackenzie's report indicates. The condition of Mr Scott's feet has continued to deteriorate. As a result of his feet and his anxiety neurosis, he is now not active. He has not, for many years, driven a car. He does not do the shopping and he rarely leaves his home. We are satisfied that his disabilities have been real, though the extent to which they have an organic base is a matter of doubt.

12. Another condition suffered by Mr Scott has been hypertension and he has been treated for that since 1976.

13. Mr Scott had occasional time off work because of problems with his feet or because of hypertension. From 1 June 1979 to 18 June 1979, he was off work. Dr Williams gave a certificate, dated 1 June 1979, which reported that Mr Scott was suffering from "anxiety neurosis and depression which he states is aggravated by the noise of the fans at work". The fans were the large fans in the electro-plating works. Thereafter, Mr Scott increasingly suffered from anxiety and noise intolerance. He was upset also to some extent by the treatment of younger workers who tended to mock him, for the reason, he believed, that he did not drink and smoke as they did. On one occasion, his fellow workers dropped a drum loudly behind him to scare him. Mr Scott found increasing difficulty with travel. He had to give up driving his motor vehicle. He found the noise of the trains to be upsetting and found himself to be increasingly anxious and unsure about his travel.

14. In 1980, when Mr Scott was only 57 years of age, he reached the end of his effective working life. On 15 August 1980, Dr Williams gave the following report, as well as a certificate that Mr Scott would be unfit for work from 15 August 1980 until 26 August 1980 :

"This is to certify that Mr Ernest Scott 57 yrs

(DOB 30/6/23) of 2 Brennan St Yagoona has been

under my care since October 1974.

He suffers from anxiety state or neurosis with

noise intolerance which he blames on the effects

of his war service for four years in theatres of

war in New Guinea and New Britain during world war

2. He states that this neurotic state is now

getting worse and is aggravated by taunts of

younger members amongst his workmates, whom he

considers are misbehaving and drinking excessively

at work.

He also suffers from mild hypertension and is on

Aldomet and Chlotribe.

He also suffers from painful arthritis and

tendonitis of both feet which is aggravated by

standing. He may have some impaired arterial

circulation in his feet as well.

He appears to have reached the stage where he will

no longer be able to continue his present job and

should retire permanently from it because of his

health, and to have therefore reached the stage of

permanent unemployability."

Dr Williams gave evidence to the Tribunal confirming the opinion there expressed. Dr Williams said in evidence that Mr Scott had reached an age when he was unable to continue work and was permanently unfit for work, the main causes being anxiety neurosis and noise intolerance.

15. It is unfortunate that the above report by Dr Williams was not then communicated to Telecom and, further, that Mr Scott did not have a full discussion with superior officers or personnel officers at Telecom. We think that, as Dr Williams said in evidence, Mr Scott had come to a stage where he found his work intolerable and we would add to that that he found travel intolerable. He felt unable to present himself for work. He remained off work during August and September 1980 pursuant to certificates of Dr Williams which were forwarded to Telecom. He believed, correctly, that if he retired on the ground of invalidity he could obtain a lump sum payment under the Act and that, if he did so, and did not receive a pension, he could at the same time apply for and receive a Service pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth).

16. On 3 October 1980, Mr Scott forwarded a document of resignation on "medical grounds". We believe that his failure to ascertain the proper means of obtaining retirement on the ground of invalidity resulted from his medical problems including the anxiety and depression from which he was then suffering. He felt that he was unable to continue to work and unable to cope with the travel and conversations necessary to fully discuss the matter with Telecom.

17. Dr J.A. Gatenby, a psychiatrist, was called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent. Dr Gatenby examined Mr Scott in March 1985 and reported that

"... at the time of him taking leave in August

1980, Mr Scott was suffering from an anxiety

reaction, most likely associated with a grief

reaction following the loss of his mother."

Dr Gatenby concluded,

"I could not state now that this condition was of

sufficient severity to warrant his retirement in

1981 (sic) on the grounds of invalidity."

It appeared in cross-examination, however, that Dr Gatenby obtained an inadequate picture of Mr Scott's problems. We do not criticise Dr Gatenby for this, for, as we have said, we think that Mr Scott has a problem with communication and with relationships, and we would not expect this necessarily to become apparent on one examination. The view that Dr Gatenby expressed in his report and put in his oral evidence was that Mr Scott's problems had been of short duration and that in October 1980 the proper course would have been not to retire him on the ground of invalidity but to give him time off to see how he responded to rest. In Dr Gatenby's cross-examination it appeared that Mr Scott's problems were more long-standing than Dr Gatenby had understood and that the death of Mr Scott's mother may have occurred in 1976, after Mr Scott had first been treated by Dr Williams with an anti-depressant and, therefore, it was no immediate cause of Mr Scott's problems in 1980. We prefer Dr Williams' view as to Mr Scott's condition, for Dr Williams had the on-going treatment of him from October 1974.

18. We are satisfied that, had Mr Scott's condition been fully considered in October 1980, he would then have been retired on the ground that by reason of physical or mental incapacity, he was unfit to perform his duties. Mr R.G. Whithear, who appeared for the respondent, conceded in his final address that Mr Scott had been unfit to carry out his duties as an electro-plater. He submitted, however, that Mr Scott would not, in 1980, have been retired but would have been redeployed. In our opinion, having regard to Mr Scott's limitations and to his problems, particularly his personality or psychological problems, no other suitable employment would have been found for him and he could not have been redeployed.

19. It follows that the s.7(2) discretion may be exercised in Mr Scott's favour, if it is otherwise appropriate to do so. It is unnecessary to discuss further the concept of "duties" which was considered in Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (cited above).

20. Section 7(2) is cast in simple terms. If the discretion is exercised, an adjustment must be made back to the date of cessation of employment. That is our interpretation of the provision. Unless it is fair to treat the employee as having been retired on the ground of invalidity, as from the date of cessation of employment, the discretion ought not to be exercised. Section 150 provides the only basis for adjustment once the discretion has been exercised.

21. Mr R. Baer, who appeared on behalf of the applicant, said that all that Mr Scott wished was the difference between the $8,638.43 which he had obtained and the $25,915.29 which he would have obtained had he been retired on the ground of invalidity in October 1980 and then exercised the election as he intended to do. But neither the Commissioner nor the Tribunal can simply award a sum of money to Mr Scott. The only act now sought is the exercise of the discretion under s.7(2). If that discretion is exercised favourably to Mr Scott, Mr Scott will be entitled to receive in a lump sum the total of the pension payments from the date of cessation of employment to the date of exercise of the discretion. Those payments greatly exceed in total the lump sum of $25,915.29 which Mr Scott would have received had he exercised the option to commute in 1980, or were he now to exercise it.

22. In 1980, it would have been to Mr Scott's advantage to exercise the option for to do so then would have enabled him to receive a lump sum and also to apply for and obtain a Service pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth). At the present time, the legislation is different. Because repatriation pensions are now tested not only against income but also against assets, the receipt of either assets or income will affect the veteran's entitlement. It is not clear whether at the present time it would be to the benefit of Mr Scott to exercise an option to commute a superannuation pension. That is a matter which he would be entitled to consider and to make up his own mind about once the discretion under s.7(2) of the Act were exercised in his favour.

23. In the circumstances, it is no longer possible to put Mr Scott into the position he would have been in had he retired on the ground of invalidity. Since 1980 to date, he has been receiving a repatriation pension. No decision that the Commissioner could make and no order of the Tribunal can affect the sum that Mr Scott has received under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth) and the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (Cth). He has had that benefit and it is a benefit that cannot be adjusted by a decision taken under s.7(2) of the Act.

24. If the s.7(2) discretion is exercised favourably to Mr Scott, he will be entitled to insist on payment of arrears of superannuation pension from October 1980 to the present date and a continuing pension hereafter. In such event, he will also be entitled to retain, as a double benefit, the pension received from 1980 to date under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth) and the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (Cth).

25. In our opinion, the possibility of this double benefit makes it unjust to exercise favourably the s.7(2) discretion. It may be unfortunate that this is so, but of course one of the reasons for the early resignation was that Mr Scott wished to obtain the benefit of a pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth). He has had that benefit and, having done so, it is now impossible for the Commissioner or the Tribunal to reverse the situation.

26. For these reasons, the decision under review should be affirmed.
