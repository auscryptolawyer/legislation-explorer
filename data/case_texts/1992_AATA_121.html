Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 121; (1992) 92 ATC 196; (1992) 23 ATR 1132 (9 April 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. VT91/171-182

AAT No. 7891

Income Tax

(1992) 92 ATC 196

(1992) 23 ATR 1132

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mrs J.R. Dwyer(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - deductions - interest expenses in respect of mortgage on home - whether incurred in gaining or producing assessable income - whether of a private or domestic nature - motor car expenses - whether taxpayers were engaged in business as investors - whether expenses incurred in carrying on a business or in preliminary activities.

Income Tax - penalty tax - whether false or misleading statements were made - whether false statements were careless or reckless.

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.37

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.51(1), 223(1)

Defence Service Homes Act 1918 s.20(1)

Fletcher and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4950

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Carberry 88 ATC 5005

Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100

Case B11 70 ATC 46

Case M41 80 ATC 297

HEARING

MELBOURNE

9:4:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal varies the objection decisions under review to provide:

(i) that the amounts allowed to the

partnership in respect of motor vehicle expenses be

increased as follows:

Total Deduction

Year Allowed

1986 $385

1987 $690

(ii) that there be no penalty imposed under

s.223(1) of the Act in respect of the claimed deductions

home

(iii) that the penalty culpability component in

(iv) that the taxation returns of the individual

DECISION

This is an application to review a decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Albury, disallowing objections to assessments in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1983, 1984, 1985, 1986, 1987, 1988. The objections related to partnership deductions disallowed in the relevant years. They affected the assessments of both the husband and the wife in each year. There were therefore twelve assessments considered in the hearing of this matter.

2. The taxpayers were represented by their accountant. The husband gave evidence. The respondent ("the Commissioner") was represented by a departmental advocate. He called no oral evidence but he tendered three documents which were received as exhibits. The Tribunal also received in evidence the documents (the "T documents") lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") and one exhibit tendered on behalf of the applicants.

3. The issues arising at the hearing concern deductions disallowed by the Commissioner in respect of interest payments and motor vehicle expenses and the possible imposition of penalty tax under s.223(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Tax Act").

The deduction claimed in respect of interest payments

4. The facts on this issue are not in dispute. Prior to 1982 the taxpayers farmed a property in N.S.W. together with a relative. In 1982 the property was sold. The taxpayers received approximately $450,000 as their share of the proceeds of the sale. That money was invested in the State Bank on interest bearing deposit.

5. At about the same time as the taxpayers sold their interest in the farm they bought a home in a nearby town. Although they had more than enough money to pay for the home without borrowing money, they decided to take advantage of the husband's entitlement to a low interest housing loan from the Defence Service Homes Corporation ("the Corporation") under the Defence Service Homes Act 1918. The husband explained in evidence that the reason why they adopted this course was that the interest payable on a Defence Service housing loan in 1982 was about 8% whereas the interest on fixed deposits with the State Bank at that time was about 17.5%. A bridging loan of $25,000, at a considerably higher rate of interest, was obtained from the State Bank, until the Corporation loan became available.

6. In his opening the taxpayers' accountant said that the money borrowed from the Corporation was paid into the taxpayers' accounts with the State Bank and invested in income bearing deposits. The Tribunal suggested that this was unlikely in view of s.20(1) of the Defence Service Homes Act which reads as follows:

"Subject to this Act and to the directions of the

Minister as to matters of general policy, the Corporation

may, upon application in writing, make an advance to an

eligible person on the prescribed security, for the

purpose of enabling him-

(a) to erect a dwelling-house on a holding of the

applicant;

(b) to purchase land and erect thereon a dwelling-house;

(c) to purchase a dwelling-house, together with the land

on which it is erected;

(d) to complete a partially erected dwelling-house

owned by him;

(e) to enlarge a dwelling-house owned by him; or

(f) to discharge any mortgage, charge, or encumbrance

already existing on his holding."

7. The respondent had not obtained a title search of the taxpayers' residence to see whether there was a registered mortgage to the Corporation. After the Tribunal sought further evidence as to the use to which the taxpayers had put the loan from the Corporation, the respondent tendered a letter dated 25-February 1983 from the Corporation to the State Bank stating that the Corporation had "given" a mortgage on the taxpayers' home. The applicants' representative then obtained further instructions and conceded that the loan from the Corporation had in fact been used to discharge a mortgage by the Bank over the property and was secured by a mortgage.

8. The section of the Tax Act providing for deductibility of outgoings is s.51(1) which reads:

"51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which

they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable

income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or producing such

income, shall be allowable deductions except to the

extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or

of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in

relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

9. The applicant's representative submitted that the payments of interest on the bridging loan and the mortgage to the Corporation were outgoings incurred in gaining the assessable income derived from the State Bank deposits. He explained that if the taxpayers had not obtained the mortgages, they would have had to reduce the money on deposit, in order to purchase their home, and would therefore have derived less income.

10. The respondent's representative, while not challenging the evidence as to the taxpayers' reason for obtaining the mortgages, submitted there the character of the interest is determined by the use to which the principal sum was put. In this matter the principal sum was used to discharge a bridging mortgage on the taxpayers' home and was itself secured by a mortgage on their home. The interest was paid in order to comply with the taxpayers' obligations under the mortgages on their home.

11. The respondent's representative relied on Fletcher and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4950. At pp 4957 the High Court considered s.51(1). It said:

" The question whether an outgoing was, for the

purposes of s.51(1), wholly or partly 'incurred in gaining or

producing the assessable income' is a question of

characterisation. The relationship between the outgoing

and the assessable income must be such as to impart to

the outgoing the character of an outgoing of the relevant

kind. It has been pointed out on many occasions in the

cases that an outgoing will not properly be characterised

as having been incurred in gaining or producing assessable

income unless it was 'incidental and relevant to that end'.

It has also been said that the test of deductibility under

the first limb of s.51(1) is that:

'it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion

of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is

productive of the assessable income or, if none be

produced, would be expected to produce assessable

income'."

12. The applicants' representative relied on a passage in Fletcher's case, at p 4957, where the Court acknowledged that the motivation of the taxpayer in making the outgoing may be a relevant factor in characterisation of the outgoing. It said:

"At least in a case where the outgoing has been

voluntarily incurred, the end which the taxpayer

subjectively had in view in incurring it may, depending

upon the circumstances of the particular case, constitute

an element, and possibly the decisive element, in

characterisation of either the whole or part of the

outgoing for the purposes of the sub-section."

However at p 4958 the Court pointed out that, even if an outgoing is properly to be characterised as incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, it may still be "somehow excluded by the exception of outgoings of a capital, private or domestic nature". Furthermore the Court added:

"To the extent that the outgoings of interest incurred in

the borrowing can properly be characterised as of a kind

referred to in the first limb of s.51(1), they must draw

their character from the use of the borrowed funds."

13. The respondent's list of authorities contained a number of cases where taxpayers in a similar situation had been refused a deduction. One such case is Case B11 70 ATC 46 where the taxpayer borrowed money on mortgage to buy a new house so that he could let the former home rather than sell it. He claimed the interest paid on the mortgage, $1,536.00, as a deduction against the rental income of $1,965. In that matter, after carefully considering the reasons of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153, the Board of Review at p 49 acknowledged that the taxpayer's purpose in borrowing the money was not alien to the income producing activity and was "instrumental in or conducive to the production of the assessable income" but the taxpayer still failed. The Board said:

" 9. But it is only part of the truth to say that the

purpose of the borrowing was to enable the taxpayer to

retain ownership of A with a view to leasing it. The rest of

the truth is that the borrowing also enabled him to acquire

ownership of B with a view to occupying it as a family

residence. ...

10. As we see it, an outgoing incurred for interest

on borrowed money is related directly and only to the

principal in respect of which it is payable. Interest is in

the nature of payment for use of the principal. Interest

only comes into existence on account of the principal, and

if interest can be said to be 'incurred in gaining or

producing the assessable income' (sec.51) it can only be

so related through the principal on which it is paid.

Whether, in any particular case, interest is incurred in

gaining assessable income must logically depend on the

use to which the principal is put (see 12 TBRD Case M13

and 14 TBRD Case P23)."

This matter is not distinguishable in any relevant respect from Case B11 or a number of other similar cases.

14. The taxpayers' representative relied on Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Carberry 88 ATC 5005 as supporting his argument that the motive for the loan, being to leave the maximum amount of capital on high interest deposit, rather than the actual use to which the money was put, was relevant. He claimed that the fact that the money was secured by a mortgage on the taxpayers' home was not significant. However although Davies J. in Carberry's case agreed with the proposition that the security was not significant, in the following sentence he said, at p 5009:

"What has to be examined is the purpose for which the

moneys were borrowed and the use to which those

borrowed moneys were put."

His Honour repeated the comments of Brennan J. in Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100 at 4104 where His Honour said:

" Section 51 requires that a deductible expenditure be

incurred 'in' gaining assessable income, that is to say, it

must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that

income (Ronpibon Tin N.L and Tongkah Compound N.L. v. F.C.

of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p 56). An outgoing of interest

may be incidental and relevant to the gaining of

assessable income where the borrowed money is laid out

for the purpose of gaining that income (F.C. of T. v. Munro

[1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 at pp 170, 171, 197; Texas Co.

(Australasia) Ltd. v F.C. of T. [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 at p 468).

The laying out of the borrowed money for the purpose of

gaining assessable income furnishes the required

connection between the interest paid upon it by the

taxpayer and the income derived by him from its use."

15. The facts in Carberry's case were very different from those before me. Mr and Mrs Carberry sold a home and applied the proceeds to the purchase of a home and kindergarten business, both of which were on the one title. That purchase cost more than the proceeds of the sale of the home, so they borrowed the balance on the security of a mortgage over the new property. The partnership accounts showed the whole of the borrowing as referrable to the kindergarten business, on the basis that the proceeds of the sale of the original home were greater than the value attributable to the residence component of the new property. Davies J. said that the Tribunal had not made any error of law in accepting the suggested notional apportionment of the property so as to treat all the interest payable on the mortgage as payable in respect of the kindergarten business. The case gives no support to the submission that interest on a mortgage obtained to provide part of the purchase money of a residence is deductible.

6. One case which at first glance appears to provide some support for the applicants is Case M41 80 ATC 297. In that case part of the interest paid in respect of a loan secured by first mortgage over the taxpayer's home was held to be deductible. The distinction there however was that the taxpayer already owned his home, subject only to a much smaller mortgage, before he borrowed $120,000 from a trustee company to invest in an income producing project. The trustee company required that the loan be secured by first mortgage on a property. The property he offered was his home. Because the trustee company required a first mortgage, the taxpayer had to use part of the money to discharge his original smaller, $25,262, mortgage. The interest on that part of the loan was held not to be deductible but that on the balance was held to be deductible. The Tribunal said at p 299:

"In our opinion the simple fact is that $25,262 of the

total sum borrowed was in fact used to discharge a

private debt secured by a mortgage over a non-income

producing asset, and the interest on that amount falls

outside the positive limbs of sec.51(1), is an outgoing of a

private nature, and is non-deductible."

Precisely the same comments are apposite in this matter.

17. In this matter, although I accept that the taxpayers' motivation in obtaining the loan was to leave their own funds on higher interest bearing deposit, I am satisfied that the borrowed funds were in fact used as part payment of the purchase price of their home and were secured by mortgage on that home. Thus the deduction fails to come within s.51(1) both because of the use to which the borrowed funds were put and because the outgoing, being a payment of interest on a home mortgage was "of a private or domestic nature."

The deduction claimed in respect of motor vehicle expenses

18. The partnership claimed deductions in respect of motor vehicle expenses in the years 1983, 1984, 1985, 1986, 1987 and 1988. The respondent's Statement under s.37 of the AAT Act summarised the taxpayer's claim on this issue as follows:

"a. Taxpayer used his vehicles in relation to his

investment income, travelling to look at farms and other

potential purchases and in connection with the

management involvement in (the company).

b. In respect of his investment income, the taxpayer

undertook 6 trips each year @ 10 km/trip, that is, 60

km/year.

c. In respect of his potential purchases, the taxpayer

travelled 4,4,000 km in 1983; 3,500 km in 1984; 2,500 km

in 1985; and 10,000 km in 1986.

d. No farms or other businesses viewed during the trips

outlined above were purchased.

e. In respect of his investment in (the company), the

taxpayer travelled 3,418 km in 1986; and 5,236 km in

1987.

f. As well as claiming motor vehicle expenses, the

taxpayer also claimed travelling costs of $277 in 1984;

$193 in 1985; $205 in 1986; and $721 in 1988.

g. These were allowed in part as follows: $40 in 1984;

$40 in 1985; $205 in 1986 and $360 in 1988. The greater

amounts in 1986 and 1988 were allowed due to the (the

company) investment, which began in 1986.

h. The taxpayer has received no directors' fees,

dividends nor any other income from the company (the

company)."

19. The reasons for disallowing the claimed deduction were set out in the s.37 statement as follows:

"(i) Section 51(1) of the Income Tax

Assessment Act allows for a deduction in respect of

losses and outgoings incurred in the production of

assessable income, where those losses are not of a

capital, private or domestic nature. The primary purpose

of the travel was to view potential investments.

Expenditure of this nature is capital and not deductible

under either positive limb of Section 51(1) as it is not

incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable

income, or in the course of carrying on a business. Rather,

such expenditure is a preliminary or establishment cost.

(ii) Of the estimated 28,954 kilometres travelled in

total over all the years in dispute, 20,000 kilometres

were said to be in respect of viewing potential

investments. As travel in relation to viewing potential

investments is of a capital nature, the motor vehicle

expenses incidental to this travel are not allowable. In

addition, the taxpayer kept no records of his travel in any

of the years involved, and provided estimates only of his

motor vehicle expenses.

(iii) Of the remaining 8,954 kilometres travelled

throughout the disputed years, an estimated 8,654

kilometres relate to travel during 1986 and 1987 in

respect of (the company) - to Director's Meetings,

attending Stocktakes, maintenance supervision, and to

view similar stores. The remaining 300 kilometres

comprised the 60 kilometres travelled per year during

1983 to 1988 regarding the taxpayer's other investment

income.

(iv) The taxpayer has already been allowed $40

travel expenses in 1984, 1985, 1986 and 1988, as well as

an additional $165 in 1986 and $320 in 1988 towards the

travel for (the company), despite the fact that no

evidence has been given to prove that the activities of

(the company) represent the gaining of assessable

income, or the carrying on of a business to produce

assessable income."

20. During the hearing the respondent's representative provided a schedule of motor vehicle expenses allowed by the respondent. Both representatives assured me that there was no issue requiring decision as to the 1988 expenses claimed. At the conclusion of the evidence the respondent's representative stated that the Commissioner would increase the amounts allowed for 1986 and 1987. The amended schedule therefore read as follows:

Motor Vehicle Expenses Allowed

Year Amount Purpose

1983 $40 Visiting the bank

1984 $40 Visiting the bank

1985 $40 Visiting the bank

1986 $385 Visiting the bank plus

incidental food and

accommodation (relating to

(the company))

1987 $690 Visiting the bank and

travelling expenses in

connection with (the company)

21. In his opening address the taxpayer's representative submitted that the evidence would show that the taxpayers were at all relevant times carrying on the business of investing and that they invested in farming property, Bank deposits, and companies. He claimed that from the sale of the farm in 1982 until 1986 the taxpayers were looking for another farm to purchase in order to continue the farming aspect of their investment business. By 1986 they had given up hope of finding a suitable farm and therefore invested in the company. From that time on they claimed costs associated with that investment.

22. Prior to the hearing the husband had completed a Motor Vehicle Questionnaire sent by the respondent. In it he acknowledged that the claims made in the returns were for total motor vehicle expenses. In the questionnaire and in evidence the taxpayer explained that the farm had been sold in 1982 because of some conflict with his brother-in-law, with whom he and his wife shared the farm. He said he had been a successful farmer and was aged fifty-four when he sold the farm and did not intend to give up farming. He said he had inspected a number of farms, perhaps two or three a week and found nothing suitable for a number of years. Then from about 1984 when the drought broke and prices started to go up, he gradually lost interest and looked at alternative investments. He considered proposals such as a furniture store. In 1986 his accountant suggested joining with a group of other investors to buy some stores. A company was formed and two stores were purchased.

23. The taxpayer said that he had been a self-employed farmer for about twelve years before he sold the farm and he still considered himself to be self-employed once the farm was sold and he had invested the proceeds in the bank. He said he checked his monthly bank statements carefully and went to the bank to talk with the manager about interest rates or errors in accounts about every month or two.

24. The taxpayer said that after he bought the share in the company he helped with stocktakings in the two stores, even though they had a manager and other staff, especially in the first year. He said that on occasions he went with his accountant to look at stores in other towns to compare their stock and prices with those in the company's stores. He said he also attended meetings of the shareholders and the accountant, at which management decisions were made.

25. The respondent's first submission was that no motor vehicle expenses were "necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income". From 1982 to 1986 the taxpayers were not carrying on a business of primary production, they were simply investors, even if they hoped to resume farming in the future. Their investments did not necessarily require the incurring of the motor vehicle expenses claimed.

26. The Commissioner's representative relied on Softwood Pulp and Paper Ltd v F.C. of T. 76 ATC 4439, a decision of the Supreme Court of Victoria, as authority for the view that preliminary activities directed at deciding whether or not to commence a business could not be said to be incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income. I am satisfied on the evidence that the motor vehicle expenses claimed in the years 1983 to 1986, when the taxpayer decided not to buy another farm, were not incurred in carrying on a business but were as Menhennit J said in Softwood's case at p 4450:

"entirely preliminary and directed to deciding whether

or not an undertaking would be established to produce

assessable income."

27. When the husband gave evidence in regard to trips in respect of the business of the company he was very vague about what trips he had made to other stores or what he had done on those trips. The Commissioner therefore did not concede the entitlement claimed. His representative did, however, concede that there were expenses associated with two trips to each of the towns where the company had a store in 1986 and three trips to each of those stores in 1987, in addition to the expenses already allowed in regard to visits to the bank in those years.

28. The Commissioner conceded that the taxpayer was entitled to a deduction in respect of motor vehicle expenses associated with visits to the bank during the years 1983 to 1987 inclusive. The amount conceded was calculated on the basis of the appropriate rate per kilometre for the 60 km claimed by the taxpayer in the motor vehicle questionnaire in respect of visits to the bank.

29. The Commissioner's representative relied on s.190 of the Tax Act which provides:

"190. In proceedings under this Part on review before

the Tribunal or on appeal to a court-

.....

(b) the burden of proving that the assessment is

excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer."

In this matter I am not satisfied on the evidence that the

taxpayer's deduction in respect of motor vehicle

expenses should be any greater than the amounts now

conceded by the Commissioner.

Penalty tax

30. Part VII of the Tax Act includes s.223 which provides:

"223(1) Where-

(a) a taxpayer-

(i) makes a statement to a taxation officer, or to a

person other than a taxation officer for a purpose in

connection with the operation of this Act or the

regulations, that is false or misleading in a material

particular; or

(ii) omits from a statement made to a taxation

officer, or to a person other than a taxation officer for a

purpose in connection with the operation of this Act or

the regulations, any matter or thing without which the

statement is misleading in a material particular; and

(b) the tax properly payable by the taxpayer exceeds

the tax that would have been payable by the taxpayer if it

were assessed on the basis that the statement were not

false or misleading, as the case may be,

the taxpayer is liable to pay, by way of penalty, additional

tax equal to double the amount of the excess."

Section 227(1) provides that the Commissioner may remit the whole or any part of the additional tax payable under a provision in Part VII of the Tax Act.

31. On 15 February 1989 Income Tax Ruling IT2517 was issued. It deals with remission of additional tax imposed under s.223(1). The Commissioner's representative at the hearing submitted, on the basis of s.223 and Ruling IT 2517, that the additional tax imposed should be remitted from 200% to a 40% "culpability component" because both the claim in respect of interest and the claim in respect of motor vehicle expenses were made recklessly. The Commissioner's representative did not ask the taxpayer any questions designed to show that he had made the claims in respect of interest and motor vehicle expenses recklessly or to clarify how he had come to claim these deductions.

32. The taxpayers' representative elicited evidence from the husband that before deciding to borrow money on mortgage from the Defence Service Homes Corporation, he had discussed the proposal with his solicitor and his bank manager. It appeared that neither of them had alerted him to the fact that the interest payable in respect of the mortgages on his private home would not be allowable as a deduction against the income received from the bank deposits. From the enthusiastic and persistent way in which the taxpayers' accountant made his submissions on this point it appeared unlikely that he had ever advised the taxpayer that the deduction in respect of interest was not likely to be allowed.

33. Some support for the submissions made on behalf of the taxpayers is derived from the editorial comment in the CCH Australian Federal Tax Reporter at para 24-120, p 15331 where the following comment is made:

"(24-120) Security for the loan

The nature of the security (if any) given for the loan

is totally irrelevant in determining the deductibility of

interest: F.C. of T. v. Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153.

Interest on money borrowed for non-allowable

purposes on the security of assessable income-producing

assets is not deductible, Conversely, interest on money

borrowed for allowable purposes on the security of

private assets, such as a private residence, or exempt

income-producing assets is deductible."

34. In the partnership taxation returns the relevant "interest paid" deduction was correctly described as follows:

Year Ended $ Payee

30 June 1983 2,262.70 State Bank of New South Wales

30 June 1984 (return not included in T documents

1985 1,642.23 Department of Veterans' Affairs

1986 1,627.00 Department of Veterans' Affairs

1987 1,611.00 Department of Veterans' Affairs

1988 1,602.00 Department of Veterans' Affairs

35. In these circumstances I am satisfied that s.223(1) does not apply in relation to the claim to deduct interest payments under the two mortgages. No false or misleading statement was made, nor was any matter or thing omitted without which the statement was misleading in a material particular. In fact the disclosure that the interest, from at least 1985 onwards (the 1984 return not being available to me), was payable to the Department of Veterans' Affairs should, in view of the provisions of s.20(1) of the Defence Service Homes Act, have alerted officers of the Australian Taxation Office to the fact that it was probably paid in respect of a mortgage on the taxpayer's own residence. I am also satisfied that the reason why the deduction was claimed is that the taxpayers and their advisers considered they had an entitlement to the deduction.

Penalty tax in respect of the deduction claimed for motor vehicle expenses

36. The evidence as to motor vehicle expenses concerned three aspects of the claimed deduction, first the claim to total motor vehicle running expenses on the basis that both vehicles were required for carrying on a business, secondly the claim for specific travel which was claimed to be related to searching for new investments and thirdly the claim for travel related to the derivation of income from investments in the State Bank and the company.

37. The taxpayer's assertion that he was carrying on business as a farmer or investor between 1983 and 1986 cannot be substantiated, but I am not satisfied that he did not genuinely believe that he was a self-employed investor during those years. I consider that the statement is somewhere on the border between reckless and careless. I note that the Tax Ruling prescribes a range of 15-30 per cent culpability component for careless statements and 30-40 per cent for reckless statements. It appears that 30 per cent is the appropriate culpability component in this matter.

38. I consider that the claims for specific travel related to looking for new investments and for travel in regard to the company investment, of which no precise details were provided, also fall into the grey area between careless and reckless statements. A culpability component of 30 per cent appears appropriate in those cases too.

39. The Tribunal proposes to vary the objection decisions under review to provide:

(i) that the amounts allowed to the partnership in

respect of motor vehicle expenses be increased as follows:

Year Total Deduction Allowed

1986 $385

1987 $690

(ii) that there be no penalty imposed under s.223(1)

of the Act in respect of the claimed deductions for

interest payments on the mortgages on the taxpayers'

home

(iii) that the penalty culpability component in

respect of motor vehicle expenses claimed but not

substantiated be reduced from 40% to 30%

(iv) that the taxation returns of the individual

taxpayers be amended to reflect the varied distribution

from the partnership following these variations.
