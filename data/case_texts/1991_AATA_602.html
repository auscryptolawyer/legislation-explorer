QT90/150-87and Commissioner of Taxation [1991] AATA 602 (28 June 1991)

Last Updated: 7 July 2008

INCOME TAX - Allowable deductions - depreciation - investment allowance - fishing boat constructed for taxpayer - damaged during voyage shortly after construction - whether fishing boat used for purpose of producing assessable income - whether fishing boat installed ready for use for that purpose and held in reserve - whether taxpayer licensed to undertake fishing operations - whether issue of certificate relevant - meaning of installed considered - meaning of held in reserve considered - question of whether any requirement that property continue to be ready for use adjourned for further consideration - Decision set aside - decision substituted that fishing boat installed ready for use and held in reserve at the date the taxpayer took possession



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Sections 6, 8(1), 54, 54(1), 82AA(1), 82AB(1), 82AQ(3), 82AQ(3)(b)

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 - Section 37

S A FORGIE

Deputy President

REASONS FOR DECISION



By letter dated 24 November, 1988, the applicant requested the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") to refer to this Tribunal the Commissioner's decision notified on 27 September, 1988. That decision disallowed the applicant's objection dated 1 December, 1987 to the Commissioner's assessment issued on 5 October, 1987. The request referred to the years of income ending 30 June, 1981 to 1984. At the hearing, however, it was agreed that all matters for the financial years ending 30 June, 1981, 1982 and 1983 and some of those for the year ending 30 June, 1984, had been resolved. The only issue left for determination was whether or not the applicant is entitled to an investment allowance for the construction of a vessel and whether he was entitled to claim depreciation.



2. The relevant legislative provisions are found in sub-sections 54(1), 82AB(1) and 82AQ(3) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). Those sub-sections provide as follows:



"54(1) Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction."



"82AB(1) Subject to this Subdivision, where -



(a) on or after 1 January 1976, a taxpayer has incurred expenditure of a capital nature (in this section referred to as `eligible expenditure') in respect of the acquisition or construction by him or a new unit of sub-section 82AA(1) property;



(b) the eligible expenditure exceeded $500;



(c) the eligible expenditure was incurred -



(i) in respect of a unit of property acquired by the taxpayer under a contract entered into on or after 1 January 1976 and before 1 July 1985; or



(ii) in respect of a unit of property that was constructed by the taxpayer and the construction of which commenced on or after 1 January; 1976 and before 1 July 1985; and



(d) the unit of property was first used or installed ready for use before 1 July 1987,



there shall be allowed as a deduction from the taxpayer's assessable income of the first year of income during which that unit was either used for the purpose of producing assessable income, or installed ready for use for that purpose, an amount (in this section referred to as the `relevant amount') ascertained in accordance with the following provisions of this section."



"82AQ(3) In this Subdivision -



(a) a reference to the acquisition of property by a person is a reference to -



(i) the person becoming the owner of the property or taking the property on hire under a hire-purchase agreement; or



(ii) the construction of the property for the person by another person or other persons on premises of the first-mentioned person;



(b) a reference to property being installed ready for use is a reference to property being installed ready for use and held in reserve; and



(c) a reference to taking property on lease is a reference to taking property on lease or on hire otherwise than under a hire-purchase agreement."



3. In relation to the investment allowance, there was no dispute between the parties that the vessel, which is the subject of these proceedings, was a "unit of eligible property acquired or constructed by the taxpayer" within the meaning of sub-section 82AA(1). The only issue in dispute between the parties in relation to both the investment allowance and depreciation was whether the vessel had been either "used for the purpose of producing assessable income, or installed ready for use for that purpose" within the meaning of sub-section 54(1) and 82AB(1).



4. The documents lodged by the Commission pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") were admitted in evidence ("the T documents"). Also admitted were a photograph of the vessel after it had arrived at its home port, a surveyor's report on the vessel dated 20 October, 1983, a declaration dated 15 December, 1983 that the items noted by the surveyor had been completed, a record of payment of the registration fee for the vessel dated 30 December, 1983, a letter from the Queensland Fish Management Authority to the applicant dated 18 June, 1984, a certificate of renewal of a commercial fishing vessel licence issued on 9 August, 1985 pursuant to the Fishing Industry Organisation and Marketing Act 1982-1984 (Qld), a fishing boat licence issued to the applicant on 9 August, 1985 pursuant to the Fisheries Act 1952 (Cwth) and a letter from the Queensland Fish Management Authority to the applicant dated 12 September, 1984. The applicant gave oral evidence but no other witnesses were called.



5. After considering both the oral and written evidence in this case, I find that there is very little dispute between the parties as to the facts. Consequently, I will not set out the evidence in detail but will make reference to it in setting out my findings of fact which I now do.



6. The applicant had been employed for many years with a mining company but, because of the terms and conditions of his employment, he had time to undertake fishing on a commercial basis. As he stated in his letter to the Commissioner of 10 February, 1988, he started to fish on a commercial basis in May 1979 when he fished the estuaries, beaches and close coastal waters. It was a netting and line fishing operation using small craft, a Landrover four wheel drive vehicle, nets and line fishing equipment. For a number of reasons which are not relevant to this case, that operation was not very viable. He wanted to resign from his employment with the mining company and to work full time as a fisherman but decided that he could only do this by increasing his investment in the venture and moving into reef fishing. After looking for second-hand boats but finding them to be unsuitable for his proposed venture, the applicant commissioned a new boat to be designed and constructed. The vessel was designed to meet Marine Board specifications and approval and the design was submitted to the Australian Taxation Office ("the ATO") for approval of construction of the vessel as a commercial fishing vessel. That approval was given with exemption under the Sales Tax (Exemption and Classifications) Act 1935, item 119, First Schedule.



7. A boat builder was found to build the boat and he was located on the Gold Coast. The vessel which the applicant constructed was an aluminium vessel carrying three or four dories. The purpose of the vessel was to transport the dories to the fishing grounds and the actual fishing was then to be conducted from the dories on the reef. The dories would then transport the catch back to the vessel which would store the fish in metal storage compartments. Those compartments were specially constructed for ice storage so that the fish would be preserved but not frozen. The applicant had a market for such fish which were fresh but not frozen. Only a small amount of fishing was actually intended to be conducted from the vessel itself and that would be in the form of deep fishing and drop line fishing.



8. During its construction, the applicant was continually travelling from his home to the Gold Coast and was very involved in every stage of the boat's construction. He was personally involved in its sea trials which were conducted in the waters off the Gold Coast. The vessel was inspected by the Harbours and Marine Board of Queensland and a survey was conducted in October 1983. As a result of that survey the following matters were found to require adjustment:



"1. Bilge mudbox lids to be efficiently secured.



2. Hatch covers to be efficiently jointed. Note that the internal side of the strip should be welded on the deck hatches.



3. Efficient closing arrangements to be fitted to the for'd accommodation entrance in wheelhouse. (Exhibit 3)"



9. The applicant arranged for those matters detailed in the surveyor's report to be corrected and he signed a declaration "that items to be attended to before surveyor recommends issue of certificate of survey (items `A') have been satisfactorily carried out" (Exhibit 4). He made that declaration on 15 December, 1983.



10. He was unable to sail the vessel back to his home port immediately after these defects had been corrected because of inclement weather. From the time the vessel was launched, he lived on board and waited until Easter 1984. Despite his having waited for improved weather, the vessel had a fairly slow trip back to its home port as it had to take shelter for several days. The crew fished during the voyage but did so only for food for themselves. It was only able to travel at night when the weather was calmer. During its voyage, the boat hit something unknown in the water and the impact momentarily stopped the drivechain. It also caused a hairline crack in one of the plates. The hairline crack meant that the vessel began to take a very small amount of water into the rear compartment.



11. While still at sea, the applicant arranged for repairs to be conducted on the vessel when it arrived at its home port. He thought that they could be carried out if the vessel were in slippage for 48 hours. When he arrived home, he was told that, instead of cutting out the damaged section and welding it as he had planned, he had to have the whole plate removed. This could not be done in 48 hours and, in any event, he was unable to obtain the services of a welder who was approved by the Marine Board as none was located in the home port. Those repairs have still not been carried out although in the last few weeks a qualified welder has started business in the area. The applicant has continued to use the dories to fish even though the vessel has not been able to transport them. For the year ending 30 June, 1984, which is the year under consideration, the applicant's income from fishing was shown as $312 (T15, page 50). Originally, the vessel was moored in a river and the dories used to fish the surrounding creeks and estuaries but the vessel was then moved to the home port and the dories have been used to fish from there.



12. At the time he left the Gold Coast, the applicant thought that he had taken all steps required for the issue of a survey certificate. When he arrived at his home port, he found that the certificate had never been issued and has not since been issued to his knowledge.



13. Throughout the period during which the vessel was being constructed, the applicant continued to fish whenever time and conditions allowed. During this period, the fishing industry generally was undergoing major changes and there were restrictions placed on new commercial fishing vessels entering the industry. The applicant's previous involvement in the industry and the fact that he had previously obtained approval for the construction of the vessel meant that he was eligible to remain in industry and his application for a licence for the vessel was confirmed by the Queensland Fishing Management Authority on 18 June, 1984. The Queensland and Commonwealth commercial fishing vessel licences were subsequently issued on 12 July, 1984. Although the original licences were not in evidence, I have based this finding on the applicant's letter to the Commissioner at T20 of the T documents and on the basis of the licences at Exhibits 7 and 8. I particularly note that Exhibit 7 is a certificate of renewal of a commercial fishing vessel licence and that renewal was issued on 9 August, 1985.

14. Before he went to the Gold Coast to supervise the construction of the vessel, the applicant said that he had licences to fish. He was permitted to fish pursuant to section 35 of the Fishing Industry Organisation and Marketing Act 1982-1984 (Qld) and was also licensed to sell fish which he caught.



Submissions



15. The applicant's solicitor submitted that the applicant was a licensed fisherman and was able to undertake fishing operations when he undertook delivery of the vessel. When he sailed the vessel to its home port, that vessel was capable of going to sea and did go to sea. It carried dories which were capable of fishing and which have since been used for fishing out of the home port. Any fishing which the applicant has undertaken has been legal fishing with the appropriate licences. However, should it transpire that he has not had any of the appropriate licences, then that is a matter for the fishing authorities and does not affect the question of whether the vessel has been used or has been installed ready for use within the meaning of the Act. The vessel was capable of carrying out fishing operations and did in fact carry out such fishing operations.



16. The applicant's solicitor relied upon the case of W Smith v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 82 ATC 4,073 (Supreme Court of Queensland, Shepherdson J), the facts of which are summarised in the headnote to the report:



"In the 1977 tax year the taxpayer claimed an investment allowance deduction based on the acquisition of a new game fishing launch in May 1977. The taxpayer claimed that he had intended to make the vessel available for chartering during the three month game fishing season but he was primarily concerned that it would be used in Government contract work acting as a tender vessel for personnel and mother shipping during the remaining nine months as game fishing was not a financial proposition on its own. Unfortunately the vessel did not perform satisfactorily and as he felt that he could not commit himself to a long-term charter contract his hopes for work, apart from game fishing charters, were not fulfilled. Regardless of this, a witness, who was a senior investigation officer employed by the Department of Primary Industries, gave evidence that private boats were used only on an `ad hoc' basis and could expect only about four weeks work a year with work in respect of foreign fishing vessels becoming available only when those vessels were sighted."



Shepherdson J noted the Commission's argument and his view in the following passage at page 4,077 of the report.



"Secondly, Mr Morley submitted that for the purpose of sec.82AB the vessel was not `used' for the purpose of producing assessable income or installed ready for use for that purpose in the 1977 tax year. He based this submission on the first charter which was in July 1977. The vessel was ready for use in May 1977. Depreciation under sec.54 was claimed on the vessel as from that date and this claim has been accepted by the Commissioner in making the subject assessment. In the light of this fact I find this submission rather strange. In my view, once the vessel was delivered to the taxpayer and ready for use by him then from that date the provisions of sec.82AB(1) commenced to operate. I do not accept, so far as subsec.82AB(1) is concerned, that actual use is required before allowing the prescribed deduction. In my view the intention of the legislation is that once the unit is functionally ready for use then the taxpayer is to be allowed the deduction in accordance with the subsection. Accordingly I reject this second submission."



17. In his submission, the applicant's solicitor distinguished the decision of the No.2 Board of Review (Messrs Brady (Chairman), Stewart and Trowse (Members)) on 7 November, 1985 in Case S90 (85 ATC 656). He did so on the basis that, unlike the present case, the taxpayer amounted to no more than the preparation of the property for nursery operations in the future or to make it a more attractive setting for the construction of a proposed clinic and residence. In this case, however, the vessel was not in the preparatory stage but was in fact ready for use.



18. The Commissioner's representative referred to paragraph 82AQ(3)(b) of the Act which I have set out in paragraph 2 above. He submitted that there were no cases which directly assisted us with the interpretation of that paragraph of the Act but that two cases gave indirect assistance. The first was Case L52 which was a decision of the No.3 Board of Review (Mr Hogan (Chairman), Dr Gerber and Mr Beck (Members)) (79 ATC 384). In particular, he referred to a passage from the decision of Dr Gerber who was considering sub-section 54(1). For the sake of completeness, I will refer to a more extensive passage than that to which the Commissioner's representative referred. At page 394, Dr Gerber said:



"As I read the second limb, it is not an all embracing provision which permits an allowance, provided it is `plant' as defined and is held in cold storage. It appears to me to have a far more restrictive meaning, being limited to plant which requires (i) to be installed; (ii) `ready for use'; and (iii) for the purpose of producing assessable income during that year. An example which readily springs to mind is the installation of auxiliary generating equipment in an existing business, held in reserve in the event of a breakdown of the existing power supply. It is to my mind a misuse of language to treat as synonymous the acquisition of a typewriter, of which no more can be said than that it is intended to be used for the purpose of producing income at some future as yet undefined time, with installation. If that had been what the section intended to convey, it would have been easy to state that depreciation of plant `used or acquired by a taxpayer during the year of income shall be an allowable deduction'. The fact that the legislature has deliberately used the term `installed' has persuaded me that something more is required than mere acquisition. Furthermore, the fact that, during the year of income, the property must be `held in reserve' for the purpose of producing assessable income during that year makes the conclusion inescapable that property acquired in a year during which no business exists is disqualified from deduction by sec.54."



19. The Commissioner's representative referred also to Case S90 85 ATC 656 in which the taxpayer claimed depreciation and an investment allowance on certain farm equipment which he had purchased to work land he had purchased to operate as a plant nursery. The taxpayer had a dam and access road built and planted trees to combat soil erosion. However, he remained unaware for some considerable time that he needed, and did not have, Council approval to operate a plant nursery. No plants of a commercial nature were planted. Later, the taxpayer sought approval to build a medical clinic and residence at one end of the property. The Board considered whether the taxpayer had been carrying on the business of a plant nursery and found, at page 660 of its decision:



"18. In our opinion the evidence, and the authorities referred to, clearly establish that the taxpayer's activities, however vigorously pursued, did not amount, at best, to more than the preparation of the subject property for nursery operations at some future, but unspecified, time. Equally, however, it could also be said that the activities, to an extent unknown, were concerned with the general improvement of the property with a view to making it a more attractive setting in which to construct the proposed clinic and residence. Further, it could also be said that the taxpayer's activities, at least in part, were concerned with preparing the property so that it could be used in due course for nursery operations by his brother.



19. Whatever the true position might have been, and notwithstanding any intentions which the taxpayer might have had, the fact remains that the work undertaken on the property had not progressed beyond the preparatory stage in the relevant period or before the property was sold in 1981. The evidence indicates quite clearly that no commercial plants had been planted in that period and, it would seem, that none could sensibly be planted until the possibility of soil erosion had been removed and until the growth of the native plants had progressed sufficiently to provide an effective wind-break. We were not advised of the time that might be required for those matters to be resolved. It seems possible that some terracing was also required to be undertaken before planting could take place. In the light of the evidence, therefore, and having regard to the authorities, we are of the opinion that it could not truly be said that the taxpayer had commenced a business in fact which was, or could be, productive of assessable income or that his activities in fact carried on, were, or could be of themselves without more, productive of assessable income."



20. Moving to the next question, which was whether the plant could still be said to be used for the purpose of producing assessable income or was installed ready for use despite the fact that the taxpayer was not carrying on business of a plant nursery, the Board said:



"In reality, the evidence indicates that the property, and therefore the plant, was not used, and could not have been used, in the absence of effective soil erosion control and a wind-break, for the purpose of producing assessable income at any time subsequent to its acquisition and prior to the sale of the subject property in 1981. In the circumstances, it could not, in our opinion, be said in the alternative that the plant was installed ready for use for the relevant purpose in any one of the years in issue or in relation to any subsequent year.



21. Support for our conclusion in this matter is to be found, in our opinion, in the remarks of Brennan J in the Inglis case (supra) at p.4005, 80 ATC, concerning the claims for depreciation by Mrs Inglis. In that case, certain items of plant and machinery of a fixed nature had been used in the pastoral business previously carried on by Mrs Inglis in partnership and were suitable for use in such a business if it were again carried on. And it appeared to be accepted by his Honour (and the Court) that it was the intention of the partnership to recommence the pastoral business at some later time. However, despite that intention, his Honour, under what he described as the first base under sec.54(1) for allowing depreciation, disallowed the claim on the basis that the plant and machinery had not been used for the purposes of producing assessable income in the relevant year. Under what was described as the second base for allowing depreciation under sec.54(1), and which includes the phrase `installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by the taxpayer, his Honour also found against the taxpayer on the basis that the prior disposal of movable farm plant and machinery and the implementation of legal proceedings for specific performance of a prior contract for the sale of a major part of the pastoral property, were inconsistent with the application of the property to the future production of assessable income by the taxpayer.



22. As already indicated, the taxpayer in the instant case did not use the plant under consideration for the production of assessable income in the years in issue under the first base of sec.54(1). In so far as the second base for deductibility is concerned, the taxpayer did not so use the plant, nor could he so use it for the reasons mentioned earlier, at any time subsequent to the years in issue, which preceded the sale of the subject property in 1981. It seems to us, therefore, that, despite the stated intentions of the taxpayer, it was not possible for him to have used the plant at any time for the production of assessable income and that, to that extent, his intentions were inconsistent with, or, at least, were not supported by, the circumstances which existed in fact. Therefore, in our opinion, it is not possible on the basis of the reasoning adopted by Brennan J, to find that the subject plant was `installed ready for use for that purpose' or that it was `held in reserve' by the taxpayer." (page 661)



21. The Commissioner's representative argued that it followed from this that, despite the fact that the taxpayer intended to use the vessel to fish, he could not actually have done so. The taxpayer did not have a survey certificate for the vessel and did not have the appropriate fishing licences. In so far as the licences are concerned, the Commissioner's argument was not supported by any evidence to contradict the applicant's statement that he had all appropriate licences. With regard to the survey certificate, the Commissioner's representative submitted that a survey certificate was required by section 111 of the Queensland Marine Act 1958-1967. He persisted in that submission despite the applicant's evidence that the vessel could not be described as a "coaster". The representative went on to submit that, although the Commissioner taxes illegal income, it would not be within the spirit of the legislation to allow a deduction for an activity in respect of which formal legislative requirements had not been met. The taxpayer's solicitor submitted that a survey certificate was not required. In any event, there was nothing in the Act which prevented a deduction being made even if a certificate was required.



Consideration



22. I will consider first the arguments that the applicant did not have the appropriate fishing licences. As I have said, the Commissioner did not lead any evidence on this aspect. I also note that his representative did not direct me to the legislative provisions relating to fishing licences. The Commissioner was quite happy to agree with the proposition that income derived from illegal activities or from activities for which the necessary licences and permits have not been obtained is taxable and yet is not happy to allow a deduction for the production of that income. However to adopt that stance would seem to be to adopt an inconsistency and one which is not recognised by the Act. The term "assessable income" is defined in section 6 to mean "all the amounts which under the provisions of this Act are included in the assessable income". What is included in "assessable income" is dealt with generally in Division 2 of the Act. There is no reference to its being legally or illegally obtained. It follows that when the term "assessable income" is used in Division 3 relating to deductions without any qualification, it must also be irrelevant whether the assessable income is legal or illegal or, more appropriately in this case, obtained from activities for which all necessary permits and licences have been obtained or not.



23. However, it may be that the question whether the taxpayer has the appropriate licences is relevant in considering whether the vessel is "ready for use". In this case, there is no need to consider that question. The applicant has stated that he had the requisite licences and has tendered both Commonwealth and Queensland licences. As I have said, the Commissioner did not lead any evidence that the taxpayer did not have the requisite licences and, indeed, did not specify what licences were required. On the limited evidence before me and for the purposes of this case only, I am satisfied that the applicant had the necessary licences and permits to fish in the tax year ending 30 June, 1984.



24. The second argument to consider is whether the fact that a survey certificate for the vessel had not been received is relevant. The Commissioner pointed to section 111 of the Queensland Marine Acts 1958-1985(2) and particularly to sub-section 111(1) which provides:



"Except where this Act otherwise provides, no ship to which this section applies shall proceed to sea or ply on any voyage or excursion unless there is in force in respect of that ship a valid certificate of survey issued or deemed to be issued under this Part, or a permit issued under subsection two of this section, and applicable to the voyage or excursion on which the ship is proceeding or plying."



25. During the hearing, the parties' attention was directed to sub-section 111(4) of the Queensland Marine Acts 1958-1985(2) which provides:



"(4)(a) This section shall apply to coasters including passenger coasters and restricted limit ships.



(b) The Governor in Council may from time to time by Order in Council or regulation exempt from the provisions of this section all or any ships of any specified class or classes.



(c) The Board may from time to time grant, subject to such conditions as it thinks fit, to the master or owner of any ship to which this section applies and not carrying passengers, a permit exempting the ship from the provisions of this section and while such permit remains in force and the conditions, if any, subject to which the permit is granted are complied with in every respect, that ship shall be so exempted."



In response to my question, the applicant said that his vessel was not a "coaster" which he described as "coastal plying vessel that plying trade up and down the coast" (transcript page 18). Again, no evidence was led on behalf of the Commissioner to contradict the applicant's assertion and I was not directed to any legislative provision defining the term.



26. Sub-section 8(1) of the Queensland Marine Acts 1958-1985 provides the following definition:



"`Coaster' - A sea-going ship employed in trading in the jurisdiction or going between ports in the jurisdiction: The term includes passengers to sea and returning to the port of departure or employed for charter or letting out for hire or reward".



A "ship" is defined in the same sub-section as



"Every vessel used in navigation not ordinarily propelled by oars only"



and "trade" as



"used in relation to any vessel, includes plying for hire or reward or plying for the conveyance of passengers or of cargo or of both passengers and cargo".



27. On the evidence before me, the applicant had a ship but did not ply for hire either cargo or passengers. It follows that he could not be said to be employed in trading and, therefore, could not be a coaster within the meaning of sub-section 8(1). It also follows that the vessel did not require a survey certificate pursuant to sub-section 111(1) and it was not in breach of that section when it went to sea.



28. Consequently, on the evidence I have been given, I am satisfied that there were no legal impediments to the applicant's taking the vessel to sea. He did so and, in terms of commonsense, I can draw no conclusion other than that the vessel was ready to be used at least from the time he commenced the journey to his home port at Easter 1984. At that time, he was carrying the dories to their home port, he was equipped to fish and equipped to store the fish.



29. To say that the vessel was ready to be used is not to say that it comes within the terms of sub-sections 54(1) or 82AB(1). In order to come within those provisions, various elements must be established. The sub-sections provide for two different scenarios. In either case, the vessel must be owned by the taxpayer and I am satisfied that this is so in this case. In the first scenario, the taxpayer must establish that the vessel has been used during the year of income for the purpose of producing assessable income. In the second, he must establish that it has been installed ready for use for the purpose of producing assessable income and is, during the year of income, held in reserve by him.



30. Common to each scenario is the requirement that there be a purpose of producing assessable income. Case L52 considered whether the taxpayer must actually be conducting a business and, in the passage which I have quoted in paragraph 18 above, seemed to imply that his conducting a business is a pre-requisite to the operation of the sub-sections. It does so by reference to the words "installed" and "held in reserve". It seems to me that the words, "for the purpose of producing assessable income" should not be limited by reference to the words applicable to the second scenario. The words are used in both the first and the second.



31. The words as they were used in relation to the first scenario, were considered by Mr Beddoe, Senior Member, in Case X46 90 ATC 378. Mr Beddoe considered a claim for depreciation for a cotton picker, which had been purchased by a syndicate not then engaged in growing cotton. He dealt with a submission that revolved around whether the taxpayer was carrying on a business of growing cotton in paragraphs 13-17 (pages 380-381):



"13. The respondent's case, also very ably argued, erred in its emphasis on whether or not the applicant was carrying on business of growing cotton. Section 54 does not pose such a test. It looks to use for the purpose of producing assessable income, using words different in effect to the positive limbs of ss.51(1). It is now well established in relation to ss.51(1) that where no assessable income has been derived in the subject year of income the outgoings must be relevantly connected to operations which are expected to produce assessable income in the future. In Ronpibon Tin N.L. and Tongkah Compound N.L. v. FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 the Court said at page 57:



`In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income.'



The relevant connection depends upon the `essential character' of the outgoings in question - not whether the outgoings were incurred for the purpose of producing assessable income (Lunney v. FCT (1958) 100 Clr 478 at 497).



14. In A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd v. FCT [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175, Mason J said at page 197:



`It is inconceivable that Parliament intended to confine deductions to losses and outgoings incurred in connection with the production of income in the year in question and to exclude losses and outgoings incurred in connection the production of income in preceding or succeeding years.'



15. Unlike s.51, and in stark contrast, s.54 refers to use for the purpose of producing assessable income. It does not in any way suggest that the assessable income must be derived in the year of income and in the light of the decisions of the High Court in relation to the operation of ss.51(1) there is no basis for implying such a limitation on the operation of s.54. Depreciation is a concept depending upon there being capital expenditure which is to be matched with income over the effective life of the plant. It is neither a loss nor outgoing incurred in the year of income but rather an allowable deduction generated by force of the Act in relation to plant falling within the terms of s.54.



16. For the section to allow a deduction for depreciation the plant must have been used by the taxpayer during the year of income for the purpose of producing assessable income or been installed ready for use for that purpose. It is of course the latter concept that is relevant in this case. This latter part of ss.54(1) requires that:



(a) the plant is owned by the taxpayer;

(b) the plant is installed;

(c) the plant is ready for use;

(d) the use is for the purpose of producing assessable income; and

(e) the plant is held in reserve.



To the extent that the decision of Taxation Board of Review No 3 in Case L52 79 ATC 384 suggests a different set of criteria, I respectfully disagree with the decision. As I have already explained it cannot be consistent with the Act to infer that Parliament intended that the purpose of producing assessable income was restricted to assessable income actually produced in the year of income.



17. It is beyond argument, on the evidence, that the plant was owned by the applicant and that it was ready for use for the purpose of producing assessable income. The evidence before the Tribunal does not admit of any other finding."



32. I agree with Mr Beddoe that there is no requirement that the plant be used to actually produce income in the first scenario. Merely, that it be used for that purpose. It is a question of fact whether or not it has been so used. Whether or not the efforts directed towards that purpose actually bear fruit is not a relevant consideration.



33. Mr Beddoe does not specifically deal with the second scenario. That second scenario does not require use of the plant but that it be installed ready for use for the purpose of producing assessable income and be held in reserve. There is nothing in the provisions relating to the second scenario which requires that the purpose be interpreted differently from the way in which it is interpreted in the first. There is nothing in the provisions which requires that assessable income actually be produced. There is nothing in the provisions which require that the taxpayer be conducting a business. Again, it is a question of fact whether the is installed ready for use and held in reserve for that purpose. Of course, in reaching a conclusion on the facts, it will be relevant to consider whether there has been any business or any business to be operated in the future. If there is a business in operation and the plant is relevant to the operations of that business, then that will be very relevant in finding whether it was installed ready for use for the purpose of producing assessable income (i.e. from that business).



34. However, the fact that there is no business or operation producing assessable income to which the plant has any relevance is not of itself fatal to the taxpayer's case. The absence of the business or operation will simply be one of the factors taken into account to determine whether the plant is installed for use for the purpose of producing assessable income. It will then be relevant to consider matters such as whether the proposal to conduct the business or operation to produce assessable income is definite or nebulous, practical or impractical or possible or impossible. It seems to me that this approach is consistent with the Board's decision in S90 and that of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Inglis v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4,001, to which reference is made in the Board's decision.



35. Applying the principles in these cases to the present case, I am satisfied that the taxpayer actually had been conducting fishing operations on a commercial basis since 1979 and a substantial time before he commissioned the construction of the vessel specifically for fishing operations. It was still an on-going operation in 1984 even though the assessable income which it produced was the insignificant amount of $312. Taking all of these matters into account, I am satisfied that if I find that the vessel was either used or installed ready for use and held in reserve, then the purpose for which it was so used or installed was for the purpose of producing assessable income.



36. I will look then to the first scenario, as I have called it, and decide whether the vessel has been used for the purpose of producing assessable income. I have described the vessel in paragraph 7 above. It was used on the voyage from the Gold Coast to the home port in the sense that it carried the dories back to the home port. The dories were not launched to catch fish on the journey. During its journey from the Gold Coast to the home port, fish were caught from the vessel itself in order to feed the crew but not for sale. There is no evidence of any attempt during its voyage to catch fish for sale. During the return voyage, the vessel was used in the sense that it transported the dories back to the home port and, therefore, to a place proximate to the fishing grounds. The intention was that it would be used for fishing on its return and, therefore, for the production of assessable income. While it may be said that it had to get to that home port in order to be able to be used to produce assessable income, it cannot be said that its voyage to the home port was itself for the purpose of producing assessable income. It follows that the vessel has not been used for the purpose of producing assessable income before it reached its home port.



37. Turning to the second scenario, I have considered whether the vessel can be said to have been installed ready for use for the purpose of producing assessable income and held in reserve. I have considered that question from the time that the vessel was constructed on the Gold Coast. Dr Gerber said in Case L52 that the word "installed" meant something more than "mere acquisition". It was not enough that the typewriter was acquired. He made a link between the need for it to be "held in reserve" and the need to be carrying on a business in that year. This view would appear to be in direct contrast with that of Shepherdson J, who was also faced with a situation in which a vessel had not been used for charter purposes in the financial year for which depreciation had been claimed but had been so used in the following year. Shepherdson J said that the vessel was installed ready for use as soon as it was delivered to the taxpayer and was functionally ready. In considering this case on appeal, the Full Court of the Federal Court did not consider this aspect of the judgement (W Smith v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 82 ATC 4,240, Bowen C.J., Fisher and Fitzgerald JJ.)



38. The Shorter Oxford Dictionary defines "instal", in so far as it is relevant as:



"2. To place (an apparatus, a system of lighting, heating or the like) in position for service or use 1867".



The related word, "instillation", is defined, again in so far as it is relevant, as:



"2. The action of setting up or fixing in position for service or use (machinery, apparatus, etc); a mechanical apparatus set up or put in position for use; spec. used to include all the necessary plant, materials, and work required to equip rooms or buildings with electric light 1882".



39. The word "installation" was considered by the Court of Appeal in Engineering Industry Training Board v. Foster Wheeler John Brown Boilers Ltd [1970] 2 All ER 616 (Ld Denning MR, Lord Wilberforce and Phillimore J). The question before the Court of Appeal was whether certain work amounted to the "installation" of plant. The applicants placed 200 miles of piping into a framework which had been erected by another firm. Those pipes constituted the boiler. They also put in furnaces, stairs and lifts. That other firm returned and put the cladding on the boiler so completing the construction of a thermal power station. The Court of Appeal concluded that the appellants were engaged in construction and not with installation. In the case of his judgement, Ld Wilberforce said at page 620-621:



"Now `installation' is a metaphorical word. It is not a word of any great precision. It was well put in the Court of Appeal in Manitoba in City of Winnipeg v. Brian Investments Ltd (1953) 1 DLR 270 by Coyne J when he said:



`"Installed" is not a word of art nor a word of precision. Indefiniteness gives it, as it gives any word, a chameleon-like character so that associate words show through and give their colour and meaning to it.'



It conveys putting in place something already made so that it can be used. There may be an element of assembly required; but basically a thing installed is ready to work when it is put in its place and, if necessary, connected up. Here I think it would be a complete misdescription to say that the boiler is installed in the framework or in this building. It is the other way round; the framework is there to hold and cover the boiler. They form one unit which is a thermal power station. There is not one thing installed in another; the boiler has no previous existence. It exists before the appellants start work on it, no doubt in a designer's mind and probably on some plan or specification; and the steam drum, which is a very large component, is premanufactured and brought to the site; but what is then done by the appellants is the construction - and I use the word deliberately - on the spot out of manufactured elements - one million feet, if my arithmetic is right, of tubes or piping, plus a vast assembly of other connections: burners, conductors, etc; and one would be surprised if there were not modifications made as the work goes on. Installation seems to me to be very far from the reality of the facts, and I cannot agree with the Divisional Court [1970] 1 All ER 490 in the finding that it is. I think it is truly construction."



40. The views expressed by Ld Wilberforce are consistent with those of Shepherdson J. Applying the principles in those cases, the words "installed ready for use" must mean that the plant or articles must be already made so that they may be used forthwith i.e. they are functioning. Looking, then to the facts of the case before me, I consider that it is similar to that before Shepherdson J. The vessel was "installed ready for use" as soon as the applicant took delivery of it from the boat builders. It was then launched and functionally ready for use without further ado. It was "installed" in the sense that it was in the sea (the medium in which it was to be used) and it matters not that it had not yet reached the particular sea at the home port from which the taxpayer had intended to operate it. He could have used it immediately. The fact that it had but a short life is irrelevant.



41. The final issue for consideration is whether the vessel has been "held in reserve". In Case L52, Dr Gerber and Mr Hogan both said that property cannot be said to be held in reserve for a year during which no business is carried on. Mr Beddoe also took a similar view in QT87/1560 when he considered the meaning of the words at paragraphs 19-21 when he said:



"19. Whether the cotton picker was held in reserve is more difficult to answer. Something being held in reserve usually connotes that its use is contingent upon some future event. That future event may be the mechanical failure of other plant, the advent of famine, fire, flood or pestilence or the example given by Dr Gerber in Case L52 in relation to provision of an auxiliary power supply. On the other hand, that future event may be an upturn in market conditions to be taken advantage of by use of plant installed ready for use but held in reserve. The concept is not a narrow one and would have many examples in productive industries.



20. However, the concept is not so wide as to embrace income producing operations which may be undertaken at some future time. Certainly, on the evidence, the cotton picker was acquired for future expected operations and its use was contingent upon those operations resulting in a cotton crop. However, that contingency is not one of a kind embraced by the words `held in reserve'. The sense in which those words are used in the section is to set aside for future use, upon the happening of some contingency occurring, in the taxpayer's existing income producing activities. In other words to keep back or save for future use in those present income producing operations.



21. The sense in which the word is used in ss.54(1) does not therefore include plant being held in reserve for income producing operations yet to be entered upon by the taxpayer. To adopt such a view would be to give the second limb of the subsection a far wider field of operation than the first limb and could result in absurdity. On that basis I have come to the conclusion that the cotton picker was not held in reserve and was not therefore within the terms of the second limb of ss.54(1)."



42. Contrary to these two cases, I am unable to find anything in the sub-sections which places upon the words the limitations found in these cases. However, I need not decide that point finally for I have already found that the taxpayer is conducting a business which did produce assessable income. I am concerned only with the meaning of the words, "held in reserve".



43. The Shorter Oxford Dictionary defines "held" as "kept in, restrained". Under the heading "hold" in Words and Phrases Legally Defined, 3rd edition, the words "held" and "hold" have been considered:



"`What is the meaning of the word "held" [in s40 of the Companies Act 1867 (repealed; see now Companies Act 1985, s519(2))]. I think that the word "held" there has no specific technical meaning. I think it is sufficient that the shares have been registered in the contributory's name at the period mentioned, and that if they have been registered within the meaning of the two Acts of Parliament [the Companies Act 1862, and the Companies Act 1867 (both repealed)], which I am entitled to read together, it must be said that the shares are "held" by him for that period.' Re Wala Wynaad Indian Gold Mining Co (1882) 21 Ch D849 at 852, per Chitty J."



44. The Shorter Oxford Dictionary defines the word, "reserve" in its first meaning as "something stored up, kept back, or relied upon, for future use or advantage; a store or stock; an extra quantity 1658". In Words and Phrases Legally Defined, 3rd edition, reference is made to a passage from the judgement of McTiernan J. when he said in Jones v Commonwealth (No 2) [1965] HCA 6; (1964-1965) 112 CLR 206 at 221:



"To reserve a thing, real or personal, is to set it aside, to appropriate it, or to give it up to some special purpose".



45. Applying these concepts, the words, "held in reserve", used in sub-sections 54(1) and 82AB(1) mean that it is enough that the vessel is, during the year, kept or set aside specifically for the purpose of being used in the future for the purpose of producing assessable income. On the evidence before me, the vessel was to be used to take the dories to the fishing grounds when it returned to the home port and to be used to its full capacity. It was built for that purpose and it was always intended for that purpose. Even before it was actually used for that purpose, it was clearly earmarked for, and set aside for, that special purpose. This is so, even though the vessel had not actually reached its home port. It follows that I find that it was, from the date he took possession of the vessel, held in reserve by the taxpayer as well as having been installed ready for use for the purpose of producing assessable income.



46. On the evidence before me, I do not have the precise date on which the taxpayer took possession of the vessel. That may be a matter on which the parties may agree. Should they fail to agree, further evidence can be led.



47. I have already found that the vessel was damaged on its maiden voyage but have made no finding as to when that damage occurred. The parties did not make any submission as to whether it was relevant to consider whether the vessel remained ready for use for the continued application of sub-section 54(1) and 82AB(1) or whether it was sufficient that the sub-sections were satisfied at the outset. Depending upon the answer to that, there could be further questions that need to be addressed regarding the dories and whether they should be considered separately from the vessel. I express no view on any of these matters but, unless the parties can reach agreement, seek further submissions from them.



48. For the reasons which I have given, I



1. Set aside the decision under review.



2. Substitute a decision that the vessel was, at the date the taxpayer took possession of the vessel, installed ready for use and held in reserve within the meaning of sub-sections 54(1) and 82AB(1).



3. Adjourn further consideration to a date to be fixed.
