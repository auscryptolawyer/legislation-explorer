TT86/560-561 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 30; (1987) 18 ATR 3186; 87 ATC 255 (4 February 1987)

Last Updated: 9 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Premium on policy of personal life assurance - Policy effected by taxpayer against contingency of liability of guarantee - Guarantee for reward

Income Tax Assessment Act (Clth) (1936) Section 51

D P Smith v F C of T 81 ATC 4114

Income Tax - Amended assessment - Whether to correct mistake of law - Onus of proof - Weight of evidence

Income Tax Assessment Act (Clth) (1936) S170, S190

McAndrew v F C of T [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263

Jones and Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298

Income Tax - Onus of proof - Amended assessment - Whether to correct mistake of law - Weight of evidence

Income Tax Assessment Act (Clth) (1936) S170, S190

McAndrew v F C of T [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263

Jones and Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298

Re: TT86/560-561

Applicant

And: Commissioner of Taxation

Respondent

No: TT86/560-561

AAT Decision No 3184

Date: 4 February 1987

P M Roach (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No TT86/560-561

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: TT86/560-561

Applicant

And: Commissioner of Taxation

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr P M Roach (Senior Member

  1. The questions to be determined upon these references are whether the Applicant is eligible pursuant to Section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") for income tax deductions in the years of income ended 30 June 1984 and 30 June 1985 in sums of $12,750 for premiums paid in relation to a policy of personal life assurance; and, if not, whether the action of the Commissioner in issuing an amended assessment in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1934, with a view to denying the Applicant the deduction, sought in that year was within power.
  2. R and J are both chartered accountants. Their interest in matters commercial was both professional and entrepreneurial. They came into association with a particularly successful car salesman of limited financial capacity (B) and a successful car dealer (G). They formed a partnership and acquired the business of a car dealership. As a matter of professional judgment, R and J did not favour the continued use of a partnership structure and they arranged that the business should be reorganised. At that point G retired from the enterprise.

3. A body corporate (CCO) was formed. R, J and B became directors of CCO. CCO became trustee of a unit trust (UT). That was on 1 July 1981. Units in UT were held by other companies. One group of units was held in the interests of the family of R by a company (RCO). It held that interest as trustee pursuant to a discretionary trust. Initially the principal beneficiaries of that Trust were the wife of R and their then and future children. Later by Deed the terms of the trust were amended to establish R as a beneficiary. CCO carried on the dealership activities on freehold premises owned by another trust established for the benefit of the same families. First one finance company (FCO) and later another (NCO) provided finance on "floor plan" for the dealership. Westpac acted as bankers to the dealership.

4. The combined initiatives of B in selling and of R and J in matters of finance and the like caused the business to flourish and expand. Then the health of B started to deteriorate and in November 1983 B disclosed to R and J that he was terminally ill. B was concerned in the interests of his family to protect his position and R and J were similarly concerned to protect the positions of their families. They were particularly concerned at the prospect that the services and skills of B would be lost to the partnership. In the months following, those serious problems were carefully considered. It was in that period that approaches were made to R and J on behalf of a corporate representative of a life assurance office (LAO) seeking to persuade R and J to take out increased life assurance. One of the individuals representing that corporate agency had had correspondence with the Commissioner. The correspondence in no way identified R, J or any related entity of either of them.

5. On 23 March 1984 the LAO representative had written to the Commissioner in the following terms:

"Personal guarantees, backed by permanent life assurance contracts as collateral

I am writing to seek clarification on a point which has caused me concern and I sincerely hope you will be able to assist me.

I have been confronted with a situation where Directors of a Proprietary Limited Co, are required to provide Personal Guarantees (in addition to a guarantee to be provided by the Company) in order to avail of overdraft and/or credit facilities to maintain the business cash flow and meet its commitments.

To provide such guarantees, the Directors are desireous of effecting permanent life assurance contracts (earning annual bonuses) to the extent of such guarantees, as collateral security in the event of premature death or permanent disablement.

In this circumstance, I seek you advice as to whether

(a) the Company could pay the equivalent amount of such insurance premiums to the directors, as a fee to procure such personal guarantees and if so, to claim the cost as expenses under Section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, and

(b) the Directors, having paid the premiums for the life assurance policies from the fees paid to them, could claim these costs as expenses involved in providing the requisite Personal Guarantees, under Section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, in their Personal Returns.

Alternatively, could the Company pay the premiums directly to the Life Office concerned, to effect the policies on the lives of the Directors who provide the Personal Guarantees, and claim the expenses directly under Section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

Finally, if either alternative is acceptable as an expense under Section 51(1) what would he the taxation implications in the event of such life assurance proceeds being paid to the insured, in the event of a claim.

I await your advice with interest, and thanking you in anticipation."

5. By letter of 18 May 1984 the Commissioner replied:

"INCOME TAX

Reference is made to your letter of 23 March 1984 in connection with personal guarantees backed by life assurance contracts as collateral security and to subsequent discussions.

You will appreciate that the Commissioner of Taxation cannot be bound by the opinions expresssed by departmental officers as to the manner in which the income tax laws will be applied to projected transactions. When the time comes to make an assessment, the Commissioner must apply the law then in force to the facts as he then finds them.

Subject to this reservation and on the basis of the information supplied by you, the following opinions are expressed:

(a) the fees paid to the directors to procure the personal guarantees on moneys borrowed by the company for use in its business may be claimed as a deduction under section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The fees are assessable income of the director under section 25; and

(b) the director, having paid the premiums for a life insurance policy for the amount of his guarantee as part o f his agreement with the company regarding the personal guarantee may claim the cost of the policy under section 51 as an expense incurred by him in earning the fees referred to in (a) above.

The above described deductions would apply only so long as the personal guarantee was in force and only to so much of the life insurance as equalled the liability under the guarantee.

In the alternative proposed, it is considered that the company is not entitled to a deduction for life insurance premiums paid to the Life Office Taxation Ruling 155.

Finally and in relation to the first proposal only it is considered that the proceeds of any claim would be an affair of capital in the estate of the insured as would any payment by the insured under the guarantee."

R and J had been, and were to continue to be, guarantors of the dealership with contingent liabilities for vast amounts. Understandably and properly they were fearful as to the risk of those guarantees being called. They recognised that guarantees, like volcanoes and nuclear bombs, are not matters likely to cause concern while they are inert; but once put into effect are likely to have disastrous consequences for those concerned.

6. On 10 June 1984, B died. Before his death arrangements had been made for the retirement of B from the business and for R and J to indemnify him against any outstanding liability he might have. Thereafter R engaged in quite "friendly" correspondence with the dealership. So did J. The correspondence was friendly in that R wrote in a standard, prepared form supplied by the LAO representative and the dealership replied, also by a standard prepared form signed by J.

  1. On 15 June 1984 R wrote as follows:

"Re: Personal Guarantees

As you are aware, I have given several personal guarantees on behalf of the Company in order to secure its business borrowings.

When initial discussions were entered into in respect of the provisions of such personal guarantees, I indicated at that time that I would be seeking an indemnity from the Company. This indemnity was to protect my estate against any call-up of the guarantees in circumstances where the Company might otherwise be unable to meet repayment of the principal debt from its own resources.

I have also become somewhat concerned over the unofficial continuing obligation whereby, from time to time, I am called upon to provide additional personal guarantees in respect of different business borrowings undertaken by the Company.

In view of the potential liability faced by both myself and my estate, I must now advise that I do require a written indemnity from the Company in respect of all personal guarantees which I have executed. If such indemnity is not forthcoming, then I propose to issue notices of termination in respect of such personal guarantees and not to renew them as and when they fall due for any reason. In addition, I must unfortunately advise that I am unwilling to execute any future personal guarantees in order to secure business borrowings on behalf of the Company."

8. On 18 June 1984, CCO replied:

"Re: Personal Guarantees

It is with some concern that we have considered your recent correspondence relating to the abovementioned.

Whilst we fully appreciate the basis of the concerns which you have expressed, it is absolutely critical for the operations of the Company's business that the personal guarantees be available, both present and future. In any event, it would be extremely difficult if not impossible to provide the written form of indemnity which you have requested.

In view of these observations, the Company would be agreeable to payment to you, of an amount to be mutually agreed upon, on a continuing basis so long as the personal guarantees which you have given remain current. However, it must be understood that in considerations of this payment, you will not serve notices of termination when the personal guarantees fall due for renewal and you will also need to agree to execute, as and when required by the Company, personal guarantees to secure its future business borrowings.

We trust that this nature of arrangement will meet with your approval and we request that you advise us of an appropriate meeting time so that specific points can be detailed and agreed upon."

9. On 20 June 1984 a meeting was held of Directors of CCO (now comprising R, J and an executor of B). The principal business of the meeting was to deal with "guarantor fee". Minutes of that meeting (adopted 20 December 1984 and which I accept as being in substance correct) recorded the following:

"GUARANTOR FEE:

The Chairman informed the Meeting that discussions had been concluded with J and R in respect of their obligations pursuant to existing and future personal guarantees to be given on behalf of the Company, such discussions having been prompted in consequence of correspondence between the parties up to the date of this Meeting.

The Chairman further informed the Meeting that the said R and J have advised of their desire to obtain a written indemnity from the Company in respect of existing guarantees and of their lack of desire to enter into any future personal guarantees in order to secure business borrowings of the Company. In view of the critical necessity for the provision of such guarantees, as and when required, the Company considers that a fee of $13,000.00 p.a. should be paid to the said J and R by way of consideration for their agreement to maintain existing personal guarantees and to provide future personal guarantees for and on behalf of the Company as and when required in order to secure its business borrowings.

The Chairman further informed the Meeting that payment of the said fee, which is to be on a continuing basis, would be subject to and conditional upon the said J and R proposing and maintaining a whole-of-life policy in order to provide both death coverage and an increasing cash value amount.

It was resolved that the Company agree to the payment of a continuing fee of $13,000.00 p.a. to the said J and R in accordance with the provisions of the Agreement made between the parties of even date herewith a copy of which was presented to the Meeting."

10. In that year and the following year it is agreed that the Company duly paid R $13,000 and R in turn effected a policy of whole-of-life insurance on his own life with LAO. The sum insured was $500,000 payable (together with bonus additions) on the death of the life insured. The policy was to commence 9 July 1984. The annual premium was $12,750. It is agreed that that premium was paid in June 1984 and June 1985. The policy issued under the provisions of the Life Insurance Act and pursuant to the provisions of that Act and otherwise was capable of being surrendered, assigned or converted to a paid-up policy by R (as the life insured and owner of the policy).

11. By his income tax return prepared on 22 December 1984 to 30 June 1984 and lodged forthwith with the Commissioner, under Item 20 "any other income" R disclosed assessable income of $13,000 described as "personal guarantee (CCO)". Under Item 28, "other expenditure incurred in earning your income", R claimed a deduction of $12,750 describing the item as "premium payable in respect of personal guarantee insurance (LAO) Policy No ..." Comparable entries in the return of income to 30 June 1985, prepared 23 December 1985 and lodged promptly thereafter, read as follows: "personal guarantee fee ex (CCO) $13,000" and "premium payable on (sic) respect of personal guarantee insurance LAO Policy No ... $12,750". On 10 May 1985 an original assessment issued in relation to the 1984 year and the claimed deduction now in dispute was allowed. A further amended assessment unrelated to the issues before me issued 5 July 1985. Then on 24 February 1986 the Commissioner issued an original assessment in relation to the 1984 year and an amended assessment in relation to the 1985 year and gave notice "premium on personal guarantee insurance disallowed".

12. Before that action was taken the Commissioner had written to the LAO representatives on 2 October 1985 advising:

"INCOME TAX

You wrote to this Office on 23 March 1984 seeking advice in connection with personal guarantees backed by life assurance contracts as collateral security.

It is regretted that the reply sent to you on 18 May 1984 contained incorrect information.

The correct position is set out in Taxation Rulings IT 155 and IT 2147, copies of which are enclosed for your information."

13. On 20 March 1986 R objected to the assessments so raised. His objections were disallowed on 6 May 1986 and two days' later he requested reference of" his objections to a Board of Review. The references were effected on 29 May 1986. Upon the passage of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act on 1 July 1986 responsibility for the determination of those objections passed to this Tribunal. The matters came on for hearing and were heard in November 1986.

  1. The Taxation Rulings referred to in that letter are not reproduced in these reasons for decision, however I do note that the letter issued on 29 March 1985, before the issue of the relevant amended assessment (July 1985) and before the letter of October 1985.
  2. The questions for determination in these references are whether R is entitled to deductions for the premiums expended; and, if not, whether it was within power for the Commissioner to issue the amended assessment issued to disallow the deduction of premium previously allowed in relation to the 1984 year of income.

16. As to the first question I have no hesitation in holding that the applicant was not entitled to deductions in relation to the premiums so paid. In my view, the expense was not incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income constituted by the guarantee fee; the expenditure was not incurred in carrying on any business, and further, even if those problems had been overcome, in the circumstances of the applicant the outgoings were of capital or of a capital, private or domestic nature. The bargain whereby the applicant became entitled to the guarantee fee was something of an isolated nature. It related to the giving of a guarantee. Of its nature for this taxpayer it was an isolated act. He was not a financier. Further, even if the bargain be construed as obliging the applicant to enter into the particular policy (or a similar policy) and to incur expense such as he did, in my view the expense was not incurred "in the course of" gaining the assessable income.

17. But the more basic reason for so holding is based upon a consideration of the nature of the premium outlaid. The premium was paid by the applicant to effect insurance on his own life. In that sense it had a private character. Further, if death had resulted during the currency of the policy and even if it had been converted to a paid-up policy, the proceeds of the policy would not constitute assessable income. If the policy had been assigned for value or surrendered, the consideration would not constitute assessable income. The policy was not an asset of any business or of any income-earning enterprise or scheme undertaken by the applicant. It is true that the applicant was prompted (inter alia) by commercial and tax considerations to effect the policy: the commercial consideration being that in the event of his death, and to a lesser extent upon assignment of the policy for value or its surrender, moneys could have been available to the applicant or his executors (as the case might be) to assist in meeting any claim which might have arisen on the guarantee, the giving of which had generated the guarantee fee. But moneys so available would no more have been available for that purpose than they would have been to meet any other liability which might have existed at that time. The tax consideration was a belief that advantages could be had if the costs of the policy were met from a "guarantee fee" paid by the company - reducing the taxable income of the company - and the additional income to R was offset by the entitlement of R to claim a deduction in relation to the premium. But those circumstances cannot alter the private character of the insurance. I hold that R was not entitled to any deduction in relation to the premiums paid.

18. I now proceed to address the question whether it was within power for the Commissioner to issue the amended assessment when he did. The power of the Commissioner to issue amended assessments is not unlimited and it is common ground in this reference that the Commissioner could only have issued the amended assessment when he did if he did so to correct "an error in calculation or a mistake of fact". As nothing suggests that the amended assessment issued to correct "an error in calculation", hereafter I will only consider "a mistake of fact". The concept "mistake of fact" is used in contrast to the concept of a "mistake of law". Although there can be mixed questions of law and fact, ordinarily the concepts are mutually exclusive. Much effort has been given by many Courts over many years to efforts to clearly identify and define the distinction between a mistake of fact and a mistake of law and I do not propose to review the authorities in detail. I prefer in the context of this reference to ask the question: Given that an error was made in the original assessment in allowing the claimed deduction for the premium, was the error occasioned by a misunderstanding of, a mistake as to the facts giving rise to the claim for a deduction? Or, was it occasioned by the mistaken idea that such a claim as was made by the return was as a matter of law allowable as a deduction?

19. Resolution of the issue so posed calls for a finding of fact to be made by this Tribunal as to what happened when the original assessment was raised. As to that it seems to be the better view that the applicant bears the onus of proof under s190 (McAndrew v F C of T [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263). That onus seems to lie on the applicant notwithstanding that direct evidence of the assessment process and of the error made in the course of it could only be available from officers of the Commissioner. That is so even though the Commissioner is under no obligation to call them as witnesses and despite the fact that their evidence could not ordinarily be challenged by the applicant in cross-examination if he chose to call them.

20. If there is no evidence as to the making of the error or if such evidence as there is, fails to persuade on the balance of probabilities, the applicant fails. However, if there is some evidence supportive of the applicant's case, it is open to the Tribunal as a tribunal of fact to reach a conclusion as to the facts which may be supportive of the applicant.

21. In this instance the applicant has produced some relevant evidence. As appears from paragraphs 5 and 6 of these reasons, he has shown that in May 1984 the Commissioner held the views expressed in his letter of 18 May 1984 in response to the letter of 23 March 1984. The evidence has also shown that by 2nd October 1985 at the latest the Commissioner held the view that the information contained in his letter of 18 May 1984 was "incorrect". It appears from the fact of Taxation Ruling IT 2147 that the view was held from 29 March 1985. I find, as a matter of fact, that when the Commissioner, on 10 May 1985 issued his original assessment, it was already known that the earlier correspondence was incorrect but for the moment I pass over the question as to whether that changed understanding of the law had come to be known to the officer responsible for the issue of the original assessment.

22. There is evidence to support a finding that at least up to 29 March 1985, if the claim in the return had been recognised as being a claim such as was described in the correspondence with the LAO representatives, it would have been allowed because of the view of the law then held. To determine whether it would have been so recognised, one must have regard to the return as it was presented to the Commissioner. What was disclosed was consistent with the representations of the LAO representatives, but that did not necessarily ensure that that was clearly indicated to the Commissioner.

23. Such information as there was came in two parts: Disclosure under the Item "any other income" of $13,000 as assessable income described as "personal guarantee fee (CCO) Pty Ltd"; and under the general Item "other deductions", and more particularly under the Item "other expenditure in earning your income", a deduction of $12,750 claimed, described as "premium payable in respect of personal guarantee insurance (LAO) policy no ..." The two items were not stated as being inter-related and I find nothing in the return suggesting that they were. If the assessor had not observed any inter-relationship between them, it might not be appropriate to conclude that he was erroneously applying the view of the law expressed in the earlier correspondence. It might be more appropriate to conclude that he altogether failed to advert to the fact of the claim (a mistake of fact); or he might have intended to disallow the claim, but omitted to do so (a mistake of fact); or he might have given instructions to others to disallow the claim, only to later find that his instructions were not carried out (a mistake of fact). However, no officer of the Commissioner gave any evidence to that effect or, indeed, any evidence at all. I think it appropriate to assume in evaluating the available evidence that the claim to the deduction was observed and that it was decided that it should be allowed. But for the explicit reference to "personal guarantee insurance" that might have been because it was thought that the claim related, for example, to a premium on a policy of loss of income insurance (D P Smith v F C of T 81 ATC 4,114). However, no witness suggests that any such thing happened, and for my part, I am at a loss to understand how a deduction for a premium on "personal guarantee insurance" could reasonably have been thought to be allowable to this taxpayer as a deduction.

  1. The arguments on each side are quite substantial. For the Commissioner it can be said that, at least at the highest level in the office of the Commissioner, by the time of assessment any mistaken perceptions as to the relevant law had been corrected. On the other hand, for the applicant it can be said that it is rather more likely than not that those mistaken perceptions still remained in the mind of the assessor, despite the issue of the Ruling IT 2147 on 29 May 1985.
  2. It was open to the Commissioner to advance evidence to the Tribunal in support of his contentions; and it would have been difficult for the Applicant to have advanced any more evidence than he did. I have had regard to the principles expounded in Jones and Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298. It is open to me to conclude that the unexplained failure of a party to call witnesses who might reasonably have expected to he called by that party is because the evidence of those witnesses would not have assisted his case. Taking the view, as I do, that it was more reasonable to expect the Commissioner to call his own officers than for the taxpayer to do so, if even he could ascertain who they were, I think the appropriate finding is that the issue of the amended assessment for the year ended 30 June 1984 which issued on 24 February 1986, contomperaneously with the issue of an original assessment disallowing in the later year the same claimed deduction, was issued to correct a mistake of law, and not a mistake of fact or an error in calculation.

26. For these reasons I would uphold the objection of the applicant and set aside the amended assessment of 5 July 1985 in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1984; but I would affirm the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection before me in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1985.
