REASONS FOR DECISION



Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President)

  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection dated 20 June 1996 against a notice of assessment in respect of the year ended 30 June 1991. The applicants are trustees of a trust entitled Estate Mortgage Fighting Fund Trust. It derived assessable income in the year ended 30 June 1991 consisting of bank interest. It also received contributions from beneficiaries. These contributions, of course, are not assessable as income because of the principle of mutuality. So far as this decision is concerned, the only relevant item of income is bank interest. The Trustees sought deductions for certain expenses and sought to have the income of the Trust treated as if the Beneficiaries were presently entitled to that income. The decision under review rejected both claims.

2. Two questions arise in considering this decision. The first is whether mailing and other expenses are allowable deductions under the then section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The second question to be decided is whether the beneficiaries of the fund are presently entitled to the income, either in equity or because of the special provisions of the then section 95A(2) and, therefore, whether the trust should be taxed under the provisions of the then section 97. If it is found that the beneficiaries are not presently entitled, then the income of the trust is taxable under section 98.

3. The Estate Mortgage Fighting Fund Trust (the Trust) was established by a deed of trust dated 2 August 1990. The settlor, Mr Hari Bhagat, had lost a considerable sum of money through the collapse of the value of units in various Estate Mortgage trusts. He said in his witness statement that he decided to settle a trust to recover his losses and for that purpose instructed solicitors to prepare a trust deed. The purpose of the trust was to institute and pursue any claims and proceedings against Burns Philp Trustee Company Limited, Estate Mortgage Managers Limited and others for the recovery of monies lost by beneficiaries under the various Estate Mortgage trusts. Upon execution of the deed, Mr Bhagat transferred to the trustees the sum of $5,000. There were approximately 18,025 beneficiaries of Estate Mortgage Trusts throughout the whole of Australia. In order to finance the intended litigation, contributions were sought from those who had been affected, like Mr Bhagat, by the collapse of Estate Mortgage.

4. In order to canvass contributions to the trust, Mr Bhagat wrote to every one of these beneficiaries requesting contributions to the fund and advising beneficiaries of progress made from time to time in the trust's activities and in the progress of various pieces of litigation. In addition to the cost of mailing, the trustees also incurred administration costs consisting of accountancy fees, advertising, bank charges, bank handling and processing fess, courier fees, depreciation, legal costs, office expenses, postage, printing and stationery, telephone, and travelling expenses. Of these amounts, the sums charged to bank charges and bank handling and processing fees may be said to have been incurred in the course of earning bank interest. Those amounts, therefore, attract considerations different from those relating to all the other items.

8. Apart from the question of deductibility of mailing and other expenses, the main issues to be decided are whether each beneficiary of the trust estate was presently entitled to the income of the trust estate and, if not, whether each beneficiary had a vested and indefeasible interest in any of the income of the trust estate.

9. Both parties requested that the matter be decided on the papers without an oral hearing. Written submissions were made and this decision is given upon the basis of those submissions and on the basis of those facts which I have elicited from the records and which appear to me to be relevant to the issues. In the absence of direct evidence and oral argument, I have had to make certain assumptions, particularly as to the section 51 items and the matters in dispute. Relying on section 33(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, I decided to adopt this procedure rather than require the parties to conduct a formal hearing, apparently against their wishes.

10. Communications were mailed by the Trustees to those persons known to have been beneficiaries under the various Estate Mortgage Trusts. The purpose of the communications was firstly, to inform the addressees of steps that had been taken in various pieces of litigation and secondly, to solicit contributions from those addressees. The Trustees sought to have the cost of these mailings treated as a deduction against the trust's assessable income, pursuant to section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. I include in the item "mailing costs" those expenses described in the revenue account as accountancy fees, advertising, courier, mailing expenses, office expenses, postage, printing and stationery, telephone and travelling expenses. So far as I understand the documents that have been placed before me, all these items are intimately involved in the mailing expenses and may be characterised for taxation purposes in the same way. No claims appear to have been made for deductibility of legal costs. No explanation was given as to the item of $129 for depreciation. The applicants have, therefore, not discharged their onus of proof in relation to this item. It is necessary to consider the terms of section 51(1) (as it then was) in considering the remaining items.

11. That section was in the following terms:

"51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."



12. The trust estate was not carrying on a business. The allowability of mailing costs as deductions, therefore, must be justified under the first limb of the subsection. It cannot be said that the first purpose of the communications, namely to advise addressees of progress in the litigation, can be said to be a cost incurred in gaining the trust's assessable income, namely its bank interest. The only basis upon which the deduction can be claimed is if it can be shown that the second purpose of the costs incurred, soliciting contributions, can be characterised as a cost incurred in gaining the assessable income.

13. In their written submission, the applicants turned to some of the tests of deductibility propounded by Jacobs J in Cliffs International Inc v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 8; (1979) 142 CLR 140 at 173. The applicants sought to show that the mailing expenses were of a revenue and not a capital nature because the capital had a depreciating character and a limited life. The sole purpose under clause 1.1(b) of the Deed was to provide funds to institute continue and defend legal proceedings. Pursuant to clause 8.2, once these proceedings were settled, the trust was to be determined. It was submitted, therefore, that the trust fund was intended to be and, in fact, was both of a depreciating nature and of a limited life. Both the corpus and income of the fund were intended to be expended upon litigation.

14. The Trustees also submitted that another test referred to Cliffs International, namely the existence of an obligation to make the payments, was present in the instant circumstances. It was submitted that there was an obligation imposed upon the Trustees by sub-clauses 3.1(b), (l) and (m) to undertake these mail-outs. It was submitted that the only way in which the Trustees could fulfil the obligations imposed by these sub-clauses was by mail. I cannot agree that clause 3 imposes any obligations at all. It simply empowers the Trustees to have recourse to certain facilities in order to enable them to carry out their principal obligations imposed by the operative parts of the Deed.

15. The Trustees went on to submit that another test set out in Cliffs International, namely the recurrence of payments, was met in the present case. It was submitted that in order to maintain the fund, it was necessary to solicit contributions on a regular basis and to give beneficiaries of the trust regular updates on the progress of the proceedings.

16. Even if the Trustees were to convince me that these submissions should be upheld, it seems to me, the expenses involved basically are not relevant or working expenses in gaining or producing the bank interest. To the extent that they were for the purpose of soliciting contributions to the trust fund, they were capital in nature. The "character of the advantage sought" was an accretion to the capital of the trust. The moneys expended on mail-outs were not expended in gaining or producing the assessable bank interest. They were expended in securing an advantage or an "enduring benefit", namely an increase in the capital of the trust. In my view, there is simply not a sufficient nexus between the outgoing and the income in order to sustain deductibility of this item under section 51. For the same reasons, all other expenses shown in the revenue account fall outside the terms of section 51, except for the items relating to bank charges and bank handling and processing fees.

17. The Trustees then sought to show that the trust income should be taxed pursuant to section 97 and should be included in the taxable income of beneficiaries in accordance with their entitlements because, it was submitted, all those who had contributed to the fund were presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate.

18. Clause 2.3 provides that the Trustees are to stand possessed of the fund in trust for the beneficiaries in proportion to their respective contributions to the trust fund. The income, however, is to be accumulated pursuant to clause 3.2.

19. Notwithstanding this obligation to accumulate the income, it was submitted by the Trustees that the beneficiaries were presently entitled because the income was applied for the benefit of the beneficiaries. The object of the trust was to pursue litigation commenced by or against certain entities named in clause 1.2 of the Deed. The purpose of the litigation was to pursue and defend the rights of beneficiaries, as against the named entities, for the benefit of the beneficiaries. Expenditure of the income upon these objects, it was submitted, was expenditure for the benefit of beneficiaries, as discussed in Sacks and others v Gridiger and others 90 ATC 4299 at 4302.

20. In my view, this submission cannot succeed. All outgoings by the Trustees incurred in carrying out their trust obligations are, in the sense submitted by the applicants, on behalf of beneficiaries. It could not be said, however, that whatever is spent is an application of income for the benefit of any one or more specified beneficiaries. The parties benefited in Sacks were individual family members, easily identified and specifically connected with the purpose of the relevant payments. The payments in the present case (namely the costs of administration and so on) are general payments, which could not be said to be an application of income for any ascertainable beneficiary.

21. This has particular importance when one has regard to clause 3.2. To accede to the submission by the Trustees, one would have to accept that they were constantly in breach of their trust obligations to accumulate the income. If they applied the income for the benefit of beneficiaries, then they would clearly be in breach of trust. I am not prepared to assume, on the evidence before me, that there were such breaches. It is not to the point to say that clause 2.3 notionally divides the income in proportion to respective contributions. While there is an obligation to accumulate that income (or, more precisely, the net income after expenditure permitted by the terms of the Trust) then no person is presently entitled to that net income or any proportion of it.

22. In short, there was not an application of net income of a nature contemplated by the statute. It could not be said of any one of the thousands of beneficiaries that any particular person had "a right to have the present enjoyment of the income". The term "presently entitled" was discussed by Wilcox and Lee JJ in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Harmer 90 ATC 4,672 at 4,681 in these words:

"The term "presently entitled" has appeared in the Act and in earlier legislation for many years although in different contexts. As it appeared in sec 31(1) and 31(2)(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922, there was debate as to whether the expression "presently entitled" could only be satisfied if there was an actual receipt of income. (See F.C. of T. V. Higgins (supra); Howey v F.C. of T. [1930] HCA 45; (1930) 44 C.L.R. 289; Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Ltd. V F.C. of T. [1932] HCA 25; (1932) 48 C.L.R. 26 per Gavan Duffy C.J. and Starke J. at pp. 35-36, per Rick J. at pp. 39-40).

In sec. 97 and 98 of the Act, which deal with the matters formerly governed by sec.31(1) and 31(2) of the preceding Act, there is no reference to actual receipt of income. At one time it was suggested that the statement therein that a beneficiary's present entitlement to a share of income of a trust estate is to be included in the beneficiary's assessable income had no application to income actually received and to which sec. 26(b) of the Act applied directly. (See Union Fidelity Trustee Co. of Australia Ltd. & Anor v F.C. of T. 69 ATC 4084; [1969] HCA 36; (1969) 119 C.L.R. 177). However, the Act was amended in 1979 by the insertion of sec. 95A and substitution of a new sec. 26(b) to make it clear that a beneficiary was to be taken to have continued to be presently entitled to income notwithstanding that the income had been paid to, or applied for, the benefit of the beneficiary.

The object of sec. 97 is to fix the beneficiary of a trust with liability to pay tax and to include the share of the net income of the trust estate to which the beneficiary is entitled with other income of the beneficiary for the assessment of that liability. Section 97 provides an exception for a beneficiary who is presently entitled to a share of the income of a trust estate but is under a legal disability. Such a share of the income of a trust estate is assessable in the hands of the trustee who becomes liable to pay tax in respect of that share pursuant to sec. 98 of the Act.

The meaning of the words "presently entitled" has been taken to accord with the general principles of entitlement applied in law and equity. In Executor Trustee, Gavan Duffy C.J. and Starke J. equated the term to "entitlement to an estate or interest" (p. 36). Dixon J. considered that the ambit of the term could be tested by asking whether the beneficiaries were able to make immediate demand for their share of the net income of the estate (p.41).

To meet the purpose of the Act, it is clear that the present entitlement and interest in the trust estate possessed by a beneficiary must be more than an interest in a trust recognised in equity and that it must be an interest coupled with a right directly related to the income of the trust estate, the right being at least a right to have the present enjoyment of the income and not merely a right to be considered in the eventual distribution of income, or accumulated income (See Gartside v I.R. Commrs [1967] UKHL 6; (1968) A.C. 553.)"



23. The Trustees in the alternative relied upon the provisions of section 95A(2) and alleged that the various beneficiaries who had contributed to their own fund had a vested and indefeasible interest in any of the income of the trust estate. In my view, this submission is simply contrary to the provisions of the Deed. Clause 7.2 in particular precludes any beneficiary from entitlement to a transfer of any of the trust property. Furthermore, no individual beneficiary has a right to determine the Trust and to call for a transfer of any interest in which he or she is said to have vested and indefeasible rights. Clause 8.3, in fact, provides for termination of the Trust upon a two-thirds majority vote of the beneficiaries. Thus, the Trust could be terminated contrary to the wishes of one third of the beneficiaries. Their interest in maintaining the Trust and in continuing to derive the income in which they are said to have a vested interest could thus be defeated. In Walsh Bay Development Pty Limited and another v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 95 ATC 4378 at 4388, Beaumont and Sackville JJ discussed the distinction between a vested but defeasible interest and an indefeasible interest.

24. The interests of the beneficiaries are determined by the joint operation of clauses 2.3, 8.2, 8.5(b), 3.2, 4.5 and 7.2. The interests of beneficiaries under this trust are defeasible; that is to say, they may be defeated by the operation of a subsequent or mixed condition.

25. In my view, it cannot be said that each beneficiary of the trust estate is presently entitled to the income of the trust estate, either in equity or by virtue of the deeming provisions of subsection 95A(2). It follows that the trust income is liable to be taxed under section 98.

26. Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed except in so far as it purported to disallow a deduction relating to bank charges, bank handling and processing fees. This will be reflected in my formal decision.
