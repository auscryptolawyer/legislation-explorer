TT86/59 and Commissioner of Taxation [1986] AATA 430; 87 ATC 162 (19 December 1986)

Last Updated: 5 August 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Assessable income - profit on sale of real estate Intention at acquisition - Whether profit assessable - burden of proof

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) S 26(a), 190

Admin Exploration Proprietary Limited (in liq) v FC of T 72 ATC 4253 at 4259

No. TT86/59

AAT Decision No 3116

19 December 1986

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicants

And:

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. The Applicants in these references, which were heard together by consent, are a married couple. The wife ("W") was most severely injured in a car accident in 1969, since which date she has been very handicapped physically and she has had only the mental age of a child of about 10 years. Her husband ("H") was a tradesman. He has cared for his wife since her accident and he has modified his employment to enable him to do so. Despite her disabilities they were able to travel on vacation to distant places within Australia. Because of her mental condition he has borne responsibility for all decisions, whether taken in their joint or separate interests, and the Commissioner and the Applicants have agreed in these references that any finding as to the state of mind of the husband is also to be attributed to W. She did not give evidence.

2. Such monies as were available for investment, including compensation received in relation to the injuries suffered by W, were initially invested in fixed interest investments. In 1978 H arranged for them to invest in real estate for the first time. He arranged the purchase for them jointly of a property in Surfers Paradise, Queensland, his intention being that they would let the property. He also foresaw the possibility that they might retire there in due course. (At the date of hearing H was aged 64 years).

3. The property so purchased was a 2-bedroom residential flat set in a new, high-rise building. It was fully furnished and fitted with air-conditioning. It was serviced by lifts and presented no access problems for W. It was fully furnished in a manner suited to short-term holiday lettings and for about two years was so let. On occasion H and W occupied the premises themselves. The unchallenged evidence of H was that the body corporate responsible for the management of the building was controlled, or at least dominated, by flat owners who resided in the building and who, in the words of H, were "generally against tenants". H said he discussed that problem with a real estate agent who advised that they should sell the flat and buy a house. They accepted the advice. H's evidence was that they purchased the house with the same intentions as they had purchased the flat.

4. The house they purchased was on the Gold Coast Highway, at Surfers Paradise. It was sited on a corner and it was in an area which was being re-developed. It was a single-storey three bedroom residence without steps or stairs. It was not modern. It was essentially unfurnished and "totally inadequately" furnished with a view to any occupation by the Applicants or by any short-term vacation tenants. After purchase it was let. In the five months following purchase rental income amounted to $1,291, but losses and outgoings were claimed at $6,370. The major factor occasioning the loss was an outgoing described as "interest and fees $5,369". There was no comparable outgoing in the previous year in relation to the residential flat. There had been no mortgage on the residential flat, but as the purchase price of the residence ($175,000) substantially exceeded the sale price of the flat ($104,000), funds had to be borrowed to finance the purchase of the residence. Included in the purchase of the residence were floor coverings; a stove; and hot water cylinder. In their returns of income the Applicants claimed depreciation on those items on the basis that they had cost $1,500, $300 and $200 respectively when they were purchased in August 1980. That claim was not challenged.

5. Settlement of the purchase of the residence was effected on or about 9 August 1980. Shortly thereafter the Applicants contracted for the sale of that property at $250,000 and settlement pursuant to that contract was effected on or about 9 February, 1981. A profit of $60,545 on re-sale was calculated and the Commissioner added $30,272 to the incomes returned by each of the Applicants. Additional tax for incorrect returns was levied but, save for a contention that the profit did not constitute assessable income, was not challenged.

6. The Commissioner contended that the profit constituted assessable income by reason of section 26(a) and section 26AAA of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). Section 26(a) provided that the assessable income of the taxpayer shall include "profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit making by sale....". The Applicants contended that I should be persuaded on the balance of probabilities that the residence was not acquired for any such purpose. Section 26AAA provides that the taxable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising on the sale of property purchased within 12 months preceding the date of sale. The Applicants conceded that, but for a statutory exception to be mentioned, the profit would fall to be assessed pursuant to section 26AAA. The exception they rely on is sub-section 5 of that section which provides that:

"Sub-section (2) does not apply in relation to a sale by a taxpayer of property if -

(a) ....

(b) Section 54 applied in relation to the property and, as a result of the sale, section 59 applies in relation to the property; or

(c) ...."

In short they claimed that because section 54 entitled them to claim depreciation in relation to floor coverings, stove and hot water cylinder and because section 59 obliged them to bring to account upon the sale the depreciation allowable pursuant to section 54 that the profit on the sale of the total property was not assessable pursuant to section 26AAA of the Act.

7. I first consider section 26(a). The Applicants bear the onus of proof (section 190). That burden is not heavy. It only requires that the Tribunal be persuaded on the balance of probabilities. That standard will be satisfied if the Tribunal is persuaded that it is more probable than not that the property was not purchased with a dominant intention of profit-making by sale. It will be so satisfied if it is persuaded of the existence of an inconsistent intention or the absence of the "intention" stipulated for by the section. As Mason, J. said in Admin Exploration Proprietary Limited (in liq) v FC of T 72 ATC 4253 at 4259:

"The profit-making by sale was not the sole or dominant purpose for acquisition in a given case may be demonstrated by showing that the dominant purpose actuating the acquisition was another and inconsistent purpose, as for example, retention as an investment. So also the taxpayer may show that, although profit-making by sale was a purpose, or a consideration, which actuated the acquisition, it was not the dominant purpose, either because another and inconsistent purpose actuated the acquisition in a equal degree or because before and at the time of acquisition the taxpayer held no firm view as to what he would do with the property and the prospect of its sale at a profit was recognised as no more than a possibility to be later considered in common with other possibilities which at or before the time of acquisition appeared no less remote."

8. In the circumstances of this case after consideration of all of the evidence I am not persuaded that H did not have the dominant intention of profit-making by sale at the time of purchase. I have particularly taken into account the markedly different character of the new premises; the substantial borrowings necessary to fund the purchase; the unprofitable nature of the investment in the absence of any substantial increase in the value of the property; the unsuitability of the property for use by the Applicants; the circumstance that it was known to be on a corner site within an area undergoing redevelopment; the short period during which the property was held; the evidence of H that, when the opportunity to sell arose quite soon after purchase, he was not surprised as the opportunity was "consistent with what I and expected" and his evidence that he had "no alternative really other than to sell because values would depreciate if the premises were retained" as the only private house in an area which had otherwise been wholly redeveloped.

9. Having reached that conclusion it is not necessary to consider the argument addressed to me in relation to section 26AAA of the Act.

10. I would uphold the determination of the Commissioner upon each objection.
