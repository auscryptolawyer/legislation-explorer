Re John James Memorial Hospital Ltd and Commissioner of Act Revenue Collections [1989] AATA 6; 20 ATR 3208 (18 January 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: JOHN JAMES MEMORIAL HOSPITAL LTD

And: COMMISSIONER FOR ACT REVENUE COLLECTIONS

No. A88/15

AAT No. 4872

ACT Payroll Tax

[1989] AATA 6; 20 ATR 3208

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

ACT Payroll Tax - liability to payroll tax - whether hospital charitable organisation - whether carried on for charitable purpose - whether carried on for the purpose of securing pecuniary benefit to its members

Payroll Tax Ordinance 1987, ss.3, 6, 9(b)

Morice v. Bishop of Durham [1804] EngR 179; (1805) 9 Ves 399, 405: [1805] EWHC Ch J80; 10 Ves 522

Income Tax Special Purposes Commissioners v. Pemsel [1891] UKHL 1; (1891) AC 531

Re Resch's Will Trust (1969) 1 AC 514 (PC)

Joseph Rowntree Memorial Trust Housing Association Ltd. and ors. v. Attorney-General (1983) 1 All ER 288

Conoley v Quick (1902) 2 Ch 642

Rex v The Special Commissioners (ex parte Headmasters' Conference) (1925) 10 TC 73

Girls Public Day School Trust v Minister of Community and Country Planning (1951) 1 Ch 400

HEARING

CANBERRA

18:1:1989

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision of the respondent that the applicant is liable to pay payroll tax under the Payroll Tax Ordinance 1987.

DECISION

It is a trite observation, but worth repeating, that the concept of the word "charity" in law is not that which it has in popular parlance. Chapter 1 of Tudor on Charities (7th ed.) commences with the following passage:

"Sir William Grant M.R., said of the word 'charity'

that in its widest sense it denotes 'all the good

affections that men ought to bear towards each

other; in its most restricted and common sense it

denotes relief of the poor' (Morice v. Bishop of

Durham [1804] EngR 179; (1805) 9 Ves 399,405: [1805] EWHC Ch J80; 10 Ves 522). Lord

Macnaghten said of 'charity', 'of all words in the

English language bearing a popular as well as a

legal signification I am not sure that there is one

which more unmistakably has a technical meaning in

the strictest sense of the term, that is a meaning

clear and distinct, peculiar to the law as

understood and administered in this country, and

not depending upon or coterminous with the popular

or vulgar use of the word' (Income Tax Special

Purposes Commissioners v. Pemsel [1891] UKHL 1; (1891) AC 531,

581, 582). It is a word which is 'wide, elastic'.

It 'has a much wider meaning in law than it has in

popular speech'."

2. In this case the problem arises not simply in the context of the general conception of charity, although that is involved in it. It arises because the applicant has been assessed on the footing that it is liable for payroll tax under the Payroll Tax Ordinance 1987 ("the Ordinance"). It is common ground that it is so liable under it unless it can be found to fall within the exemption provision contained in s.9(b) of the Ordinance, which with other relevant provisions is as follows:

"6.(1) Subject to this Ordinance, tax is

payable in respect of all wages that are paid

or payable by an employer on or after the

commencement day."

"9. Section 6 does not apply to wages paid or

payable

(a) ...

(b) by a charitable organisation

(c)-(g) ..."

"charitable organisation" is defined in s.3 of the

Ordinance as meaning:

"an organisation, society, institution or body

carried on for a ... charitable purpose, other

than one carried on for the purpose of

securing pecuniary benefit to its members."

3. The history of how it came about that the liability of the applicant to payroll tax came under consideration is not of importance. It is set out in the statement provided pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. Of interest, though in no way determinative, is the course of reasoning adopted. The decision maker, contrary to advice that had been obtained, was of the view that the applicant "did not provide a pecuniary benefit to its members, although it does provide benefits of other kinds such as facilities for undertaking the practice of medicine". It was however considered that the applicant was not a charitable organisation, that is to say an "organisation carried on for a charitable purpose", according to general principles, and on this footing the challenged decision was made. The first two findings of fact made by the decision maker as set out in the s.37 statement may be conveniently set out by way of introduction:

"5. (a) The Hospital is a company limited by

guarantee. Clauses 3 and 7 of its

Memorandum of Association preclude the

Hospital from distributing income or

property, including any surplus of assets

upon it being wound up, to its members.

(b) The Hospital was established with a number

of objects (clause 2 of its Memorandum of

Association), the first of which was to

establish a private hospital in Canberra.

Other objects allow the Hospital to operate

the running of a private hospital in a

business-like manner, to make provision for

medical skills and treatment not otherwise

obtainable in the Australian Capital

Territory, the encouragement of the private

practice of medical science in the

Territory, the encouragement of the

publication of scientific papers by medical

practitioners and the dealing in plant,

machinery and goods."

4. Evidence both oral and on affidavit was given by Dr. P.D. Hughes, who had been one of the promoters of the applicant, and who is and has, save between 1972 and 1978, been a director of the applicant since its incorporation in October 1966. He was called on behalf of the applicant. Oral evidence was given by Dr. K.G. Doust, who had been from 1975 to 1980 the medical director of the Health Insurance Commission in Canberra and from 1980 to 1985 the chairman of the then Capital Territory Health Commission. He was called on behalf of the respondent. As the respondent's submission said, the facts are not substantially in dispute. Both Mr. A. B. Shand, Q.C., for the applicant and Mr. A.H. Slater, for the respondent, made both oral and written submissions. Helpfully, both submissions indicated the findings of fact that the Tribunal was invited to make. Perhaps unusually, most of the findings so proposed can and should in most respects be adopted by the Tribunal as its own, subject only to the deletion of some mild hyperbole; to the making of some corrections that appeared to me to be necessary, principally relating to the original loan funding of the applicant (see paragraph 5(e) and (f) below); and to the making of a finding in relation to the extent of remuneration of the members (see paragraph 6(d) below). As the respondent's submission said however, the facts are not substantially in dispute. In the first place some salient facts about the history of the hospital and its membership should be found. They are set out in the affidavit of Dr. Hughes and are as follows:

"3. In 1965 there was only one Hospital in

Canberra, a Public Hospital then known as

Canberra Community Hospital and subsequently

renamed Royal Canberra Hospital, on the Acton

Peninsula. Several doctors became aware that

many patients would like to have the option of

private hospital accommodation. There had

been a private hospital in Canberra in the

1930's and 40's, and this closed due to

economic non-viability in 1949 after public

hospital accommodation was made free to all by

the Chifley Labor Government.

In 1965 I attended a meeting of doctors

interested in encouraging the re-establishment

of a private hospital in Canberra. This

group initially approached an Order of Nuns,

the Little Company of Mary, who were in

possession of a block of ground in Barton for

the purpose of a private hospital. The then

Provincial of the Little Company of Mary,

Sister Mark, when approached by the group of

doctors who encouraged her to proceed with

building a private hospital, informed them

that her Order was negotiating with the

Government for finance to build a 200 bed

public hospital in Canberra, and was no longer

planning to build a private hospital.

4. The group of doctors including myself then

approached private hospital owners in Sydney,

but could find no-one interested in building a

private hospital in Canberra. The doctors

then decided to try to finance, build and run

a private hospital. At a meeting of eleven

doctors including myself in December, 1965

(when Canberra had a population of 80,000)

there was unanimous agreement that if such a

hospital was built and run by doctors, it

should be non-profit, so there could be no

conflict of interest in admitting patients to

the private hospital. This principle was in

the instructions given to the solicitors

drawing up the memorandum and articles of the

Company. Membership of the company entailed

lending money to the Company for fifteen years

at 4% interest, and guaranteeing the

borrowings of the Company. Associate

members, who have one vote were required to

lend $1,000.00 and full members, who have 3

votes were required to lend $6,000.00. A

lease of land was sought from the National

Capital Development Commission and architects

engaged to help plan the hospital. Apart

from a loan of $68,000.00 from Members, a

borrowing of $225,000 from the AMP Society was

necessary to fund the first stage of the

Hospital, and that loan had to be jointly and

severally guaranteed by the initial members.

The initial Hospital consisted of 24 beds, an

operating theatre, x-ray department including

major fluoroscopy unit, pharmacy and Pathology

Department. At the time the Hospital executed

the contract for the Hospital to be built the

hospital had sixteen members and today has

ninety two members.

5. From the commencement of operation the demand

for beds in the Hospital has been sustained by

patients preferring private hospital

accommodation. By and large, the patients

have the choice of single room or shared

accommodation and almost invariably they are

provided with what they want and it has always

been hospital policy to provide patients with

individual personal care and attention. The

whole operation of the Hospital is less

institutionalized with relaxed visiting hours,

more acceptable meal times and greater patient

comfort.

6. Fees charged by the Hospital have been set at

a level to maintain economic viability and

provide a small operating surplus, the latter

to be used for the purchase of new equipment,

refurbishment and expansion of the Hospital.

The Hospital has been gradually expanded and

now has ninety one beds including three

operating theatres and a day surgery unit.

In 1984 a second generation Computerised

Tomography (CT) Unit was installed and

following the commencement of operation of

this unit patients were referred from the

public hospitals for a period of eighteen

months to take the benefit provided by this

unit. In addition a Nuclear Medicine Unit

was introduced. The Hospital also includes a

cardiac rehabilitation and stress testing unit

which treats approximately 1700 patients per

year. The Hospital now ha(s) the best

diagnostic facilities in Australia for a

hospital of its size, whether public or

private hospitals.

7. The Hospital is predominantly surgical,

performing more than 8,000 operations each

year. The surgery performed includes a wide

range of major surgery, performed by

specialist surgeons in Neurosurgery,

Orthopaedics, Opthalmology, Ear Nose and

Throat, Urology, Gynaecology, Oral Surgery and

Plastic Surgery. These operations include

knee and hip joint replacement, laminectomy,

spinal fusion, correction of scoliosis,

hysterectomy, prostatectomy, total cystectomy,

nephrectomy and nephroureterectomy, colon

resection. Major surgery represents 57% of

bed-days and Advanced Surgery represents 14%

of bed-days.

8. The three public hospitals perform

approximately 20,000 operations per year,

compared to the 8,000 at the hospital, so that

functioning of the Hospital takes a lot of

pressure off the public sector. If the

Hospital did not exist the waiting lists at

the public hospitals would be much longer than

they now are.

9. ...(T)he In Vitro Fertilisation Unit (IVF) ...

not only saves patients having to go to

Melbourne or Sydney but is provided at a

financial loss to the hospital; many of the

procedures must be conducted on a Sunday, with

extra cost to the Hospital, but the Hospital

does not charge higher operating theatre fees

on those occasions. The Hospital also

provides facilities for patients of competent

doctors who do not have access to public

hospitals on the grounds of age.

10. The Hospital facilities have always been

available to non-members as well as members.

During 1987, for instance, 32% of doctors and

dentists using the hospital were non-members.

This would be representative of the proportion

of non-members using the hospital over the

years.

11. The Memorandum and Articles of the Hospital

prevent the distribution of any surplus funds

to members either during the operation of the

Hospital or on its eventual sale if this

should occur."

5. I find the following facts, in the terms in which they were expressly conceded by the respondent (see (a) and (b) below) or, substantially, in the terms sought by the applicant (see (c) to (k) below):

(a) The constitution of the applicant forbids

distribution of profits by the applicant to its

members, and that prohibition has been observed.

(b) The applicant conducts a hospital the facilities of

which are available for a not unreasonable charge

to any patient whose doctor can secure a bed and

the use of the hospital's facilities.

(c) The primary purpose for which the hospital was

established was the provision of facilities for

remedying the health of sick patients.

(d) For such purpose it was necessary to raise capital

in order to provide such facilities and to enable

hospital services at most moderate rates to such

patients.

(e) The most advantageous method of raising such

capital was by way of loans at nominal interest

rates over long periods (15 years).

(f) This method of loan raising was achieved by

obtaining such loan money from persons with an

interest in the operation of the hospital, i.e.

doctors interested in using the facilities.

Initially there was also a loan from an insurance

company.

(g) After successful establishment of the hospital, the

objectives of expansion of facilities required a

continuous process of loan raising from members,

including extension of existing loans and obtaining

additional loans at similar interest rates. Such

concessional loan capital could only be expected

from persons directly interested in using the

facilities.

(h) To the extent that the granting of preferential use

of facilities has occurred, this has served a

two-fold purpose, namely -

(i) ensuring that services of doctors would remain

readily available to the hospital and its

patients upon the basis that doctors could

make long term arrangements to use the

facilities;

(ii) the provision of an incentive to doctors to

become members and in the process to provide

loan capital.

(i) The Hospital is still in the process of

development.

(j) The fact that the bulk of patients dealt with at

the hospital comprise patients of members is the

result and not the purpose of the operation of the

hospital.

(k) There is no evidence that the establishment of the

hospital was in any way intended or had the purpose

of increasing the income of members.

6. I also find the following further facts, based on the respondent's suggested expression of them:

(a) There is a clear policy of giving to the

members of the applicant preferential access

to the facilities of the applicant, to the

point where the members for practical purposes

substantially monopolise the facilities (for

details see paragraph 11 of the respondent's

submission).

(b) The right to such preferential access is a

matter of value to the members, who are

prepared to make a substantial financial

outlay to obtain it: namely by way of a

fifteen year loan at merely nominal interest

and the giving of substantial guarantees to

the creditors of the applicant (for details

see paragraph 12 of the respondent's

submission).

(c) The benefit of membership is that it

substantially increases the patient throughput

of member doctors. Because nearly all

patients at the applicant are privately

insured, and none are "hospital patients" in

respect of which reduced fees must be

accepted, the actual increase in the earnings

of the member practitioners by reason of

membership and its privileges is increased to

a greater extent than the increase in patient

throughput (for details see paragraph 13 of

the respondent's submission).

On this point there was a significant difference in

the claims of the parties as to the facts which

should be found. The applicant's submission

claimed that there was insufficient evidence to

establish that the hospital had been conducted so

as to provide a greater income for member

practitioners than would have been received by them

had they not been members. The evidence was said

to go no further than to establish that at the

hospital, as a general rule, patients paid higher

fees to doctors than may have been paid to them by

public hospital patients. While elements of

estimation are involved, I consider that on balance

the finding sought by the respondent should be

made. The finding should however in my opinion

be limited to one of substantial increase of

patient throughput rather than to one that holds

that such throughput is doubled. That finding is

enough for the purposes of the respondent's

argument.

(d) A specialist's main income comes from hospital

patients, and the members of the applicant

hospital were and are exclusively specialists.

7. There is no doubt that a gift for the purposes of a hospital is prima facie a good charitable gift. In Re Resch's Will Trust (1969) 1 AC 514 (PC) esp. at 540 Lord Wilberforce, delivering the judgment of their Lordships said:

"...the provision of medical care for the sick is,

in modern times, accepted as a public benefit

suitable to attract the privileges given to

charitable institutions. This has been recognised

in the High Court in Australia in Taylor v Taylor

(1910) 10 CLR 218, 227 per Griffith CJ and

Kytherian Association of Queensland v Sklavos

[1958] HCA 47; (1958) 101 CLR 56: in England in In Re Smith, decd.

(1962) 1 WLR 763; (1962) 2 All ER 563, CA"

8. Their Lordships continued, relevantly for present purposes:

"In spite of this general proposition, there may be

certain hospitals, or categories of hospitals,

which are not charitable institutions (see In re

Smith, decd.). Disqualifying indicia may be

either that the hospital is carried on

commercially, i.e. with a view to making profits

for private individuals, or that the benefits it

provides are not for the public, or a sufficiently

large class of the public to satisfy the necessary

tests of public character."

See also Joseph Rowntree Memorial Trust Housing Association Ltd. v. Attorney-General (1983) 1 All ER 288 at 298.

9. There is equally no doubt in my opinion that the applicant as a hospital prima facie satisfies the test of public character that is fundamental to its being found to be charitable in the legal sense. It provides benefits for the public, or in the alternative for a sufficiently large class of the public, to satisfy the test referred to. The respondent however contends that the applicant and its members, or perhaps one should say through its members, had such a collateral purpose of providing a personal benefit to the members of the applicant as either

(a) to disqualify it after all as a charity within the

legal meaning of that term, (what I shall call "the

primary argument"), or

(b) to invoke the disqualifying condition contained in

the definition of "charitable organisation"

contained in s.3 of the Ordinance, (what I shall

call "the secondary argument").

10. As to whether the applicant satisfies the general conception of charity, Mr. Slater for the respondent referred to the statement in In Delaney; Conoley v Quick (1902) 2 Ch 642 at 649 that "Charity is necessarily altruistic", and to Rex v The Special Commissioners (ex parte Headmasters' Conference) (1925) 10 TC 73, for a proposition that a gift which promotes a charitable purpose, but also advances the private purposes of individuals, does not satisfy the requirement of charity. I agree however with Mr. Shand's submission that the latter decision, which involved a provision of the UK Income Tax Acts referring to a "...body of persons or trust established for charitable purposes only", rested on the proposition that at least some of the objectives of the institution permitted expenditure on non-charitable purposes.

11. In a submission which had strong implications for the secondary argument as well as for the primary, Mr. Slater submitted that it was not necessary that the fatal pecuniary benefit flow from the structure of the putatively charitable organisation by way of dividend, distribution of profits or otherwise. It was sufficient if the organisation was the vehicle whereby a private profit was made. Here, the affairs of the applicant as a hospital were conducted in a manner calculated to enhance the income of its practitioner members. Such conduct, it was said, precluded the applicant from having the legal status of a charity.

12. As to the secondary argument, Mr. Slater argued that if the Tribunal were against him on the primary argument, the exclusion provision still precluded the treatment of the applicant as a charitable organisation as defined in the Ordinance. The reference to the words "the purpose" in the definition was not to be construed as "the sole purpose". The word "the" was used, it was said, in its indefinite or generic sense. The words "carried on for the purpose of securing pecuniary benefit to its members" were said clearly to contemplate that the pecuniary benefit may be secured outside the structure of the organisation in question, by collateral arrangements. On the correctness or otherwise of this submission the determination of the case in my view depends.

The Primary Argument

13. In my opinion the applicant is an institution or body carried on for a charitable purpose. As such it fulfils the requirements of the general law as to what is "charitable". In so concluding I rely upon Resch's Case and upon the authorities about to be mentioned in relation to the secondary argument, for that argument is very closely related to that part of the primary argument which depends upon the proposition that collateral private profit is enough to deny the attribution of charitable purpose to the applicant. On one view it is no more than a statutory expression of that issue.

14. The respondent's written submission in relation to the primary argument also placed some emphasis on a requirement of altruism, referring to Re Delaney and to the Headmasters Conference case. The following example was given: "(I)f a corporation were formed to own, improve and maintain land for use as school premises, open to all, and were precluded from distributing assets or profits to its members or applying assets to any other purposes, and the corporation in fact so acted, it would be prima facie a charitable organisation. But if the facts disclosed that the use of the premises was wholly or substantially limited to pupils of approved teachers, that the approved teachers were all members, and that the member teachers earned a handsome remuneration by way of substantial charges to the pupils attending classes at the school premises, then the necessary element of altruism in the establishment and conduct of the school would be absent, and it would be proper to say, in the words of Lord Wilberforce in Resch's Case that the corporation was carried on 'with a view to making profits for private individuals'. In such circumstances the corporation could not be a charitable organisation". It seems to me however that when a requirement of altruism is ever of importance, it will be so in cases falling outside the specific classes of activity long held to be charitable, such as the provision of schools and hospitals. I am not at all sure that a school being conducted along the lines suggested would fail the test of charity.

The Secondary Argument

15. In my opinion the applicant in turn fulfils the requirement of the statutory definition of "charitable organisation" that it be carried on "for a ... charitable purpose". No authority was provided to me for the proposition that the statutory expression contemplates that the pecuniary benefit may be secured outside the structure of the hospital by the derivation of collateral benefits. I agree with the submission put for the applicant that such a proposition in fact runs counter to the authorities previously referred to, including Joseph Rowntree Memorial Trust Housing Association Ltd and ors. at 299 where Peter Gibson J. said:

"I have already discussed the cases which show that

the charging of an economic consideration for a

charitable service that is provided does not make

the purpose in providing the service

non-charitable, provided of course, that no profits

accrue to the provider of the service."

This passage in my opinion needs to be borne in mind when considering the passage on 298 of His Honour's judgment stating:

"It is of course crucial ... that the services

provided by the gift are not provided for the

private profit of the individuals providing the

services."

16. The passages referred to, so far from disentitling an organisation to the appellation "charitable" where persons providing services to it receive a professional reward for their services, instead make it clear on the contrary that the derivation of a profit by some person as a mere incident of the effecting of a charitable purpose does not disqualify. Immediately following the first passage cited above, Peter Gibson J., speaking of the particular housing schemes there in question, which did arguably offer a prospect of profit in certain circumstances to participating tenants, said at 299:

"It is true that a tenant under the schemes may

recover more than he or she has put in, but that is

at most incidental to the charitable purpose. It

is not a primary objective. The profit (if it be

right to call the increased value of the equity a

profit as distinct from a mere increase avoiding

the effects of inflation, as was intended) is not a

profit at the expense of the charity, and indeed it

might be thought improper, if there be a profit,

that it should accrue to the charity which has

provided no capital and not to the tenant which has

provided most if not all the capital. Again, I

cannot see that this objection defeats the

charitable character of the schemes."

17. Similar statements as to the unimportance of the making of a profit of a subsidiary kind are contained in Girls Public Day School Trust v Minister of Community and Country Planning (1951) 1 Ch 400 at 406.

18. All of these authorities, while dealing with the general concept of charity, are in my opinion directly relevant to an understanding of the statutory expression.

19. Further factors which I have decided do not detract from the conclusion that the applicant is not carried on for the purpose of securing pecuniary benefit to its members are:

(i) that the importance to applicants for membership

of that membership is shown by their preparedness

to invest at nominal rates of interest;

(ii) the significant proportion borne by the facilities

offered by the applicant to other hospital

facilities available in the ACT;

(iii) the charging of commercial rates for services

provided.

20. Ultimately the critical question is whether the collateral derivation of pecuniary benefit by doctors who happen to be members of the applicant, a derivation offering no benefit to the applicant itself in the course of effecting what I consider to be, from its point of view, a charitable purpose, should lead to a conclusion that it itself is being carried on for the purpose of securing pecuniary benefit to its members. I do not think that it should. Purpose should not be confused with collateral effect. It is inevitable that pecuniary benefit flows to those doctors who use the hospital's facilities and charge their patients for their services to them. Unless those services were provided by doctors who eschewed payment, like old-fashioned honoraries (now long gone) at old-fashioned public hospitals for the indigent sick, how could it be otherwise? But that in my opinion is irrelevant. If of course a hospital were run for the purpose of accruing profit for itself for distribution to its owner or shareholder members, that would be another matter. But that is not this case.

21. In my opinion the decision under review, whereby the applicant was found liable to pay payroll tax under the Ordinance, should be set aside.
