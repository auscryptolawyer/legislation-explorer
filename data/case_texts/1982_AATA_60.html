Re Imre Hegedues and Commissioner of Superannuation [1982] AATA 60 (16 April 1982)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: IMRE HEGEDUES

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A81/26

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr Ewart Smith (Senior Member)

Sir Ernest Coates (Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

CATCHWORDS

superannuation _ determination of invalidity retirement benefits _ benefit classification certificate _ whether conditions specified were 'relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act' _ physical and mental conditions specified _ whether incapacity caused by or connected with a condition specified in certificate _ whether document issued in 1977 purporting to be the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force has any independent operation _ meaning of 'incapacity' and 'connected with'

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16. 66, 154, 184

Superannuation Act 1978, s.77

HEARING

CANBERRA

16:4:1982

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decisions under review and in substitution therefor decides that the benefit entitlement of the applicant under the Superannuation Act 1976 upon his retirement on the ground of invalidity as from 13 August 1979 is to be determined in accordance with sub-section 66(1) of that Act.

DECISION

1. This is an application for review by this Tribunal of the decisions of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") made on 2 April 1980 and confirmed by the Commissioner on reconsideration under s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976:

(i) to issue under sub-section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions 'History of ulcerative Colitis with Ileostomy' and 'History of Anxiety State'; and

(ii) to be of the opinion under paragraph (c) of sub-section 66(2) of the Act that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition (or conditions) specified in the benefit classification certificate or by (a) physical or mental(condition or) conditions connected with such (a condition or) conditions.

Certain words (shown in brackets above for convenience of reference) appeared in the delegate's decision (ii) but were not included in the Commissioner's formal decision under s.154(4) (Exhibit T2, p.3). However, we do not think anything turns on the omission of these words in the latter decision; clearly the Commissioner's decision was intended to confirm the decisions as taken by his delegate on 2 April 1980.

Outline of facts

2. The facts, as they appeared from the documents furnished in pursuance of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and the oral evidence referred to below, are outlined in chronological order in paragraphs 3-21.

3. The applicant, who was born on 7 July 1938, commenced employment in the Australian Public Service in February 1975 and underwent a medical examination on 23 March 1975 for the purposes of permanent appointment to that Service and of s.5(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act"). In relation to this examination, the applicant, in completing his statement of personal medical history in the Medical Report Form, answered 'Yes' to each of the following:

'Have you now or have you previously had any of the following conditions?

1(c) Anxiety state

3(f) Disease of the bowels".

4. The applicant gave details of absences from work due to sickness over the previous two years in the following terms 'Operation & frequent absences to 1975' and gave details of surgical treatment or operations 'ileostomy 24/6/73' (questions 12 and 13). In response to question 14, 'Are you in good health now?', the applicant answered 'Yes' and to question 15, 'When did you last receive medical or surgical treatment and for what condition?', he answered 'Continuing treatment for Colitis'.

5. The examining Commonwealth Medical Officer commented on the positive answers to 1(c) and 3(f) as follows:

1(c) 'This is related to the colitis and is not a cause of trouble now.'

3(f) 'Under care of Mr Cuthbertson & Professor Hughes. Operation at Royal Melbourne Hospital. Had colitis for about 5 years altogether.'

6. The Commonwealth Medical Officer recorded in his clinical notes (Exhibit T2, p.20) comments as follows:

'Had operation for colitis Mr Cuthbertson at Royal Melbourne Hospital 29/6/73. Still has ileostomy v. likely to continue.* He mentions operation and frequent absences to 1975. Also mentions anxiety state related to his colitis and ileostomy but not having symptoms now. Has been an athlete and still competes. Dr McInnes agrees.'

(* This last word was not entirely clear as written and 'continue' should possibly read 'be an issue'.)

His clinical notes also contained a transcript of a report from Mr Cuthbertson dated 11 April 1975, which stated:

'On 22.6.73 he underwent subtotal colectomy and ileostomy at the Royal Melbourne Hospital. He had an uneventful convalescence and has remained well since operation. He is now in excellent health without any disability. He manages the ileostomy well and should present no difficulties with his employment. I would be happy to discuss his future in more detail if you think it is necessary.'

7. The Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended acceptance of the applicant for appointment under Provident Account conditions, and stated under 'REASONS FOR RECOMMENDATION AND REMARKS' simply '(1) Has ileostomy' (Exhibit T2, p19). The recommendation was dated 21 April 1975.

8. The Delegate of the Superannuation Board (the Public Service Inspector), on the same date, determined under s.5(1) of the superseded Act that the applicant's health and physical fitness 'justify his acceptance as a contributor to the Provident Account' (Exhibit T2, p15).

9. The applicant thereupon became an 'employee' for the purposes of the superseded Act and, on the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976, an 'eligible employee' for the purposes of that Act by reason of paragraph (a) of the definition of that term in s.3(1) of that Act. He continued as a 'permanent' officer of the Public Service until his ultimate retirement on 13 August 1979. On 8 June 1976, following a second 'clean-up' operation in September 1975 (referred to below as the proctectomy), the applicant wrote to the Superannuation Board seeking transfer from the Provident Account to the Superannuation Fund under s.79(2) of the superseded Act (Exhibit T2, pp27-28). He explained in making this request that the 1975 operation was a 'corrective' operation and since ileostomy was the 'criteria' used to place him in the Provident Account, he wished to be transferred to the Superannuation Fund. At this stage, the new (1976) Superannuation Act was on the point of coming into operation and no such requests were being processed. What the Commissioner did was to treat the letter of 8 June 1976 as a request (presumably under s.16(6)), to revoke the Benefit Classification Certificate deemed to be in force under the provisions of the 1976 Act (see Commissioner's letter of 25 January 1977 _ Exhibit T2, p29). For this purpose, a medical examination was required and was arranged.

10. Following this medical examination (as a result of which the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer expressed the opinion that the Benefit Classification Certificate should be revoked but the Adviser to the Commissioner (Dr Hackett) considered that the applicant did not meet the general medical standard), the Commissioner's delegate directed that a Benefit Classification Certificate remain in force in respect of the applicant and he was advised of this by letter in March 1977, with which was enclosed 'a copy of the Certificate' ("the 1977 Certificate"), dated 28 March 1977 (Exhibit T2, pp35-36). The only condition stated in this 'copy' was 'History of Ulcerative Colitis with Ileostomy'.

11. By letter of 20 April 1977 the applicant 'appealed' under s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 against the delegate's decision and supplied a supporting letter from Mr Cuthbertson (Exhibit T2, p39). However, reconsideration of the case by the Commissioner had not taken place before the Act was amended in 1978 by Act No. 17 of 1978 (Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978), which, amongst other things, repealed s.184 and substituted a new s.184 and provided (s.77(2)) that the repealed s.184(2) 'shall be deemed never to have applied to or in relation to existing contributors who did not cease to be eligible employees before the commencement of this section', i.e. before 24 April 1978.

12. On 30 June 1978, the Commissioner 'determined' in the light of the foregoing that his delegate's decision under the repealed s.184(2) and the applicant's request for reconsideration of that decision were no longer effective. We will return later to consider the submission made by Mr F.T. Brennan, Counsel for the applicant, in relation to the 'certificate' issued by the delegate on 28 March 1977.

13. In July 1978 the applicant was advised in writing of the Commissioner's determination (Exhibit T2, p43) and of the provisions of the new s.184. This section, amongst other things, enabled a benefit classification certificate to be issued upon written application. No such application was, in the event, made by the applicant.

14. In August 1976 the applicant had had a fall at his place of employment as a result of which he suffered injury to his back, in respect of which he made a claim for compensation under the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971. He was off work for some three months as a result of this injury.

15. In 1979, the applicant underwent a further medical examination 'for continued employment or retirement on invalidity grounds' pursuant to a request by the Department in which he was employed (the Department of Transport) dated 9 August 1979. He was then a Clerical Assistant Grade 3. The Medical Report Form used for this purpose (Exhibit T2, p54) showed the purpose of the examination as assessment for 'fitness for continued duty as requested by the Commissioner for Compensation'. The result of this examination was that the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer on 30 November 1979 (Exhibit T2, p55) considered that the applicant was suffering from 'Personality Disorder', that this condition was 100% the cause of his incapacity and that he was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity. The International Code Disease Number (ICD) was shown as '301.0'.

16. Following receipt of this report, the delegate of the Public Service Board on 5 February 1980 retired the applicant under s.67 of the Public Service Act as from 13 August 1979 (Exhibit T2, p56).

17. On 2 April 1980 the delegate of the Commissioner expressed the opinion under s.184(7) of the Superannuation Act 1976 that the conditions 'History of Ulcerative Colitis with Ileostomy' and 'History of Anxiety State' were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act in the case of the applicant and were the conditions by reason of which the (former) Superannuation Board was not satisfied under s.5(1) of the superseded Act that his health or physical fitness were such as to justify his acceptance as a contributor under Part III of the Act (i.e. to the Superannuation Fund as then established _ Exhibit T2, p57). On the same date, a benefit classification certificate ("the 1980 Certificate") was issued specifying 'History of ulcerative colitis with Ileostomy' and 'History of Anxiety State' as the relevant conditions (Exhibit T2, p59).

18. The Commissioner's delegate, also on 2 April 1980, made a decision under s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 that he was of the opinion that the incapacity of the applicant which was the ground for his retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate (i.e. the conditions referred to in paragraph 17) or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions. In forming that opinion the delegate stated that he had had regard to the condition 'Personality Disorder 100%' stated to be the condition contributing to the incapacity in the medical officer's report (Exhibit T2, pp60-61).

19. By letter of 7 May 1980, the applicant was advised of the decisions made as to his entitlements and of the issue of the benefit classification certificate dated 2 April 1980, a copy of which was attached (Exhibit T2, pp63-65). On 28 May 1980 the applicant 'appealed' against the delegate's decision to reject his claim for pension benefits and submitted medical reports in support of his appeal from Dr Brendan J. Dooley and Dr Leon Fennessy (Exhibits T2, pp67-71 and T2, pp72-74).

20. The Acting Commissioner, having received this 'appeal' _ in effect a request for reconsideration by the Commissioner under s.154 of the Act of his delegate's decision _ sought the opinion of the Senior Medical Officer of the Department of Health (Dr H.S. Turner), who advised on 11 November 1980 (Exhibit T2, p89) as follows:

'I consider that the incapacity which was the grounds for the retirement of Mr Hegedues i.e. 'personality disorder' was substantially contributed to by the conditions specified in the BCC i.e.

(1) History of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy. This disease, with its chronicity, relapses and remissions is usually associated in all sufferers with some degree of depression and anxiety.

(2) Anxiety state is undoubtedly connected with 'personality disorder'.'

21. On 23 February 1981 the Commissioner confirmed his delegate's decisions.

22. The Tribunal received in evidence, without objection, the documents furnished by the respondent in pursuant of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act as Exhibits T1 to T5, and heard oral evidence of Mr A.M. Cuthbertson (the surgeon who carried out the two bowel operations on the applicant), Dr Leon Fennessey (a psychiatrist whom the applicant has consulted since 30 May 1977), Dr M.H. Gilfedder (a general practitioner who treated the applicant since February 1972 and who referred him to Mr Cuthbertson) and Mr M.J.P. Fogarty (an orthopaedic surgeon who had treated the applicant since September 1976), all of whom were called by Mr Brennan on the applicant's behalf, and Dr B.J. Hackett (Senior Medical Officer in the Department of Health) who was called by Mr W.R. Wilde for the respondent.

23. Apart from what might be referred to as the merits of the case, Mr Brennan submitted an argument as to the legal power to issue the benefit classification certificate on which the Commissioner and his delegate relied. It will be convenient to deal with this submission at the threshold.

The 1977 Certificate

24. Mr Brennan's submission was that the Commissioner and his delegate ought to have addressed themselves to the 1977 certificate, which specified, as noted above, only 'History of Ulcerative Colitis with Ileostomy' and not to the subsequent 1980 certificate, which included also 'History of Anxiety State'.

25. To understand the submission it is necessary to refer shortly to the legislative history of the provisions relating to benefit classification certificates.

26. From the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976 (1 July 1976) until the enactment of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 the position was as follows. Section 16 of the Superannuation Act 1976 made provision for a person entering the Public Service after the commencement of the Act as a permanent officer (and hence an 'eligible employee') to undergo a medical examination under s.16 and if, as a result of that examination, the Commissioner was of the opinion that the person was not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the medical report, to continue to be an eligible employee until he attained his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner was required by s.16(4) to issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, specifying the relevant condition or conditions. Under s.16(6) provision was made for a person in respect of whom a benefit classification certificate was in force to request the Commissioner to revoke or vary the certificate and subsequent sub-sections provided for consequential procedures and revocation or variation of the certificate in appropriate cases. However, so far as concerns persons already in the Public Service as at 1 July 1976, who had already had the comparable medical examination, s.184 made special provisions. Sub-section (1) provided that, subject to sub-section (2), s.16 did not apply to existing contributors. Sub-section (2) of s.184 read as follows:

'(2) For the purposes of this Act (including sub-sections 16(6), (7) and (8)), there shall be deemed to be in force, on the commencing day, in respect of an eligible employee _

(a) who is an existing contributor and who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act;

(b) whose period of contributory service on the commencing day would have been less than 20 years if he had ceased to be an eligible employee on that day; and

(c) who, at the commencement of this Act, had not attained his maximum retiring age,

a benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner under sub-section 16(4) specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act.'

27. There was accordingly no provision for the issue of a certificate in any physical form in the case of existing contributors, except that a certificate was deemed to be in force for the purposes of the Act (including s.16(6), (7) and (8)) in respect of a former contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act in the circumstances set out in s.184(2). Thus such a contributor was enabled to request, as the applicant was treated by the Commissioner as having done (see paragraph 9 above), that his 'deemed' certificate be revoked under s.16(6).

28. The position was changed by the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 (Act No. 17 of 1978), s.77 of which repealed and replaced the original s.184. Section 16 was also amended by s.26 of that amending Act, but the amendments are not of significance for present purposes.

29. The new s.184 made no provision for there to be deemed to be benefit classification certificates in force in relation to existing contributors (i.e. persons who were contributors for the purposes of the superseded Act immediately before 1 July 1976) who were contributors to the Provident Account under the superseded Act. It provided for certificates to be issued in fact but that they were to be deemed to have been issued under s.16(4) and, in the case of a certificate issued under s.184(2), to have been in force in respect of the person concerned immediately before his death or retirement. It will be useful to set out the relevant provisions of the new s.184. Leaving aside provisions dealing with failure to furnish information, or furnishing false information, of which there was no allegation in the present case, the new section reads as follows:

'184(1) This section applies to a person who is, or at any time has been, an existing contributor other than _

(a) an existing contributor who has previously ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity, being an existing contributor to whom, upon his so ceasing, invalidity benefit was payable in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) an existing contributor who has previously ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity.

(2) Subject to sub-section (4), where _

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceased to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to _

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement.

(3) Subject to sub-section (4), the Commissioner may, if he thinks fit and shall, upon application in writing being made to him by an eligible employee who is a person to whom this section applies and who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account, issue in respect of the eligible employee a benefit classification certificate in which the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act is or are specified and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4).

(4) The Commissioner shall not, under sub-section (2) or (3), issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of a person if there is a benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the person at that time.

....

(7) In this section _

(a) a reference to a contributor to the Fund shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the existing Fund under Part III of the superseded Act;

(b) a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act; and

(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purpose of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which _

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund; or

(ii) the person was not, by virtue of a provision of the superseded Act (other than sub-section 5(1) or 79(2) of the superseded Act), accepted as a contributor to the Fund,

...."

30. Section 77 of the 1978 amending Act also contained several substantive sub-sections providing for transitional matters. Sub-section (2) is relevant for present purposes. It reads:

'(2) Subject to sub-section (3) and (4) of this section, sub-section 184(2) of the Principal Act shall be deemed never to have applied to or in relation to existing contributors who did not cease to be eligible employees before the commencement of this section.'

The applicant, of course, had not ceased to be an eligible employee before s.77 commenced (i.e. on 24 April 1978). (Sub-sections (3) and (4) are concerned with the situation where a 'deemed' certificate was revoked or varied under s.16(8) before the commencement of s.77).

31. Returning to Mr Brennan's submission, he argued that the original s.184 can only be made comprehensible if it is implied that in some circumstances a 'deemed certificate' will in fact be issued and once issued will have a force of its own rather than a 'deemed force' pursuant to the section. He submitted that for the Commissioner to exercise his powers under s.16(8) to revoke or vary the certificate (by deleting references to a particular condition), or refusing the request to revoke or vary it, there must be a certificate in existence, i.e. in physical form. On this basis, he said, there was no reason why the Commissioner should not have power to issue a physical certificate at that time. He submitted that 'where a person pursuant to a statute is given power to vary or revoke a certificate, where the certificate is deemed to have been in force from an earlier date and where that person does in fact issue the certificate as he purported to do on this occasion on 23 March 1977, then that must clearly be a valid exercise of power. Once the power has been exercised then the physical document is in existence and has a force of its own'. Mr Brennan then submitted that the provision (s.77(2) of the 1978 Amending Act) deeming the previous 'deeming' provided by the original s.184(2) never to have applied did not affect the validity of the certificate so issued. He said 'that certificate enjoys validity even though the later deeming provision says that the previous deeming should no longer continue in law'.

32. Mr Brennan then submitted that as the 1977 certificate remained in force after the 1978 amendments the Commissioner, by reason of the provision of the new s.184(4), had no power to issue the 1980 certificate; there was a certificate in force in respect of the applicant at the time and s.184(4) expressly prevented the issue of another.

33. Mr Brennan recognized that his submission was in some conflict with the view taken by this Tribunal (constituted by Mr A.N. Hall, Senior Member, Dr M. Glick and Sir Ernest Coates, Members) in Re Blakemore and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALD 124, but pointed out that the question was not fully argued in that case, the applicant being unrepresented.

34. In Blakemore's Case the same question of the effect of a 'certificate' issued in May 1977 in circumstances similar to those of the present case arose. The Tribunal in that case (at pp127-128) said:

'Having regard to the fact that under s.184(2) of the 1976 Act as originally enacted the benefit classification certificate referred to in the section was 'deemed to be in force' by virtue of that sub-section, we think it is clear firstly, that the operation of the section did not depend upon the Commissioner issuing a physical document. The certificate was brought into notional existence by force of the section itself. Furthermore, on the findings we have made as to the physical and mental conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor to the fund, that certificate as deemed to be in force by virtue of s.184, must be taken, at the date of commencement of the 1976 Act, as having specified:

(1) History of phychosis

(2) History of myopia.

Had nothing occurred in relation to the 'deemed' certificate prior to the repeal and substitution of s.184 by Act No 17 of 1978, it is equally clear that the 'deemed' certificate would be taken never to have existed (see s.77(2) of the 1978 Act). But what is the position when the Commissioner has professedly considered an application to vary or revoke the certificate, has stated that the certificate must 'remain in force' (see letter to Mr Blakemore dated 11 May 1977 _ Exhibit T4), and has issued a document in the form of a benefit classification certificate stating that after considering the report of the medical examination in relation to the applicant's request for revocation or variation of the certificate, he is of the opinion that the applicant is unlikely by reason of or for a reason connected with his history of anxiety state to continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age?

Mr Wilde submitted that:-

(i) there was no authority under s.184 or s.16 of the Act as it then stood for the Commissioner to issue the document dated 11 May 1977; and

(ii) in any event, the evidence before the Tribunal established that, due to the mistakes in Dr Genn's medical report dated 7 April 1977, the Commissioner had never applied his mind to the question whether the 'deemed' certificate should b varied by deleting the reference to myopia. The Commissioner could not be found, therefore, to have varied the certificate within the meaning of s.77(4) of the 1978 Act.

Dealing firstly with this later submission, we agree as we earlier indicated that the 'deemed' certificate brought into force at the date of commencement of the 1976 Act by the original s.184(2) must be taken to have specified history of myopia. It is significant in our view that neither the certificate itself nor the covering letter dated 11 May 1977 referred to any variation of the deemed certificate by deleting the reference to myopia. We have considered the possibility that, having regard to the provisions of s.16(8) of the 1976 Act, the Commissioner must be taken, as a matter of necessary implication, to have varied the deemed certificate by deleting myopia, but in the light of the evidence which satisfies us that the Commissioner did not apply his mind to that condition, we agree with Mr Wilde's submission that such an inference is not open to us. We therefore find as a fact that the 'deemed' certificate was not varied by the Commissioner on 11 May 1977 and that, accordingly, s.77(4) of the 1978 Act did not have any application to the applicant.

As to the certificate itself, we can find no provision in the 1976 Act as it stood on 11 May 1977, which either required or authorised the issue of a benefit classification certificate consequent upon the Commissioner's consideration of an application to vary a 'deemed' certificate. We note, of course, that the document was in fact served upon the applicant and that s.16(12) of the 1976 Act provides as follows:-

'(12) A copy of a benefit classification certificate, and a copy of any revocation or variation of such a certificate, and notice of refusal by the Commissioner to a request to revoke or vary such a certificate, shall be served on the person to whom the certificate relates or, if the person has died, on the personal representatives of the person or such other person or persons as the Commissioner, in his discretion, determines.'

This sub-section, however, is not one of the sub-sections of s.16 which, by force of the original s.184(2) of the 1976 Act, applied to an existing contributor such as Mr Blakemore. As a consequence, there was no specific obligation under the Act as it then stood, for the Commissioner to notify the applicant as to the result of his consideration of the application for revocation or variation, nor was there any special requirement as to the form of any notification that the Commissioner might see fit to give.

In the circumstances, we have concluded that the document dated 11 May 1977, despite its form, does not have any legal effect as a benefit classification certificate for the purposes of the Act. As neither the saving provision in s.77(4) of the 1978 Act nor the provisions of s.184(4) of the 1976 Act as amended applies, we are of the opinion that the Commissioner was entitled to exercise the powers conferred by s.184(2) of the 1976 Act as it stood when the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of his retirement on the ground of invalidity."

35. With respect, we agree with the view expressed in Blakemore's case. We do not think that the fact that the Commissioner, for convenience, issued a document in the form of a benefit classification certificate, or a copy of such a certificate, gave, or could give, any greater validity than if he had merely recorded his decision on the matter and notified the applicant by letter of his decision. We do not think that there is any implication to be drawn from the provisions to which Mr Brennan referred that the Commissioner has a power to issue a certificate that has validity extending beyond the provisions of the section that deems a certificate to be in existence. When, therefore, by virtue of s.77(2) of the 1978 amending Act, the original s.184(2) was deemed 'never to have applied' to or in relation to the applicant, there was thereafter no certificate in force within the meaning of the new s.184(4) precluding the Commissioner from issuing the 1980 certificate: all that there was was a piece of paper in the form of a certificate, recording a decision that in any event had become of no legal effect by virtue of s.77(2). There should not, in our view, be ascribed to s.77 an intention to give independent and continuing legal force to a document that had been brought into existence for administrative convenience when the statutory provision to which the document was a mere accessory had been not only repealed but expunged retrospectively. We do not think that Lawlor's Case (1978) 1 ALD 167, to which Mr Brennan referred, supports the contrary view. We note also that s.77(4) contemplates the issue of a certificate in place of a 'deemed' certificate that had been varied before s.77 came into operation.

The 1980 Certificate

36. It is now necessary to consider the alternative submission made on behalf of the applicant in respect of the decision to issue the 1980 certificate. This was that the Commissioner and his delegate were in error in including 'History of anxiety state' in the certificate because, having regard to the Commonwealth Medical Officer reports, the only condition which could have been included was 'History of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy'.

37. It is necessary to keep in mind, in considering this submission, that what the Act required and authorised the Commissioner to include in a benefit classification certificate under s.184(2) were the physical or mental conditions that were "relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act" in relation to the person whom the certificate related. The relevant conditions (so far as applicable to the present case) were defined in s.184(7)(c) as:

'the physical or mental ... conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, ... were the physical or mental ... conditions of the person by reason of which _

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the (Superannuation) Fund;'.

38. What has to be examined, therefore, is not what physical or mental conditions the applicant was in fact suffering from at the time he underwent the medical examination for the purposes of s.5 of the superseded Act, but (in the absence of any withholding of any information or furnishing of false information, which was not alleged in this case) what were the conditions that caused the Superannuation Board (through its delegate) not to be satisfied that his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. For this purpose, it seems to us clear that what must be examined is the evidence that was available to the Board or its delegate at the time when the decision was made to exclude him from that Fund, and not evidence that became available only subsequently and which could not have affected the decision that was taken at that time. In other words, what has to be considered is what was before the Board or its delegate when the decision was taken in 1975, not what has happened or been learnt since which, if it had been available to the Board or its delegate, would have been taken into account. We note, and with respect agree with, the view of s.184(7)(c)(i) expressed by this Tribunal constituted by Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member) and Mr V.J. Skermer and Dr M. Glick (Members) in Re Richardson and Commissioner for Superannuation ((No. V80/62) decision given 5 June 1981 as yet unreported) at paragraph 16 of the Reasons for Decision:

'This definition thus requires a reference not to a condition of the person that is merely proved to have existed and to have been a condition to which reference might properly have been made, but rather to a condition that has actually been taken into account by the Superannuation Board for the purposes of coming to a conclusion as to the health and physical fitness of the person.'

39. There is no question and it was not challenged that 'History of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy' was such a condition and was properly included in the 1980 certificate. But can the same be said of 'History of anxiety state'?

40. At the time the relevant decision of the Board's delegate was taken, the evidence disclosed that he had before him:

. the original medical report (Exhibit T2, pp15-19)

. a report from Mr Cuthbertson attached 'History of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy' was such a condition and was properly included in the 1980 certificate. But can the same be said of 'History of anxiety state'?

40. At the time the relevant decision of the Board's delegate was taken, the evidence disclosed that he had before him:

. the original medical report (Exhibit T2, pp15-19)

. a report from Mr Cuthbertson attached to the medical report. See Medicl Officer's comment opposite '8' at Exhibit T2, p.22.)

There is no evidence before the Tribunal that the delegate had before him the actual clinical card completed by the examining medical officer (reproduced at Exhibit T2, p20) and it is unlikely that in the ordinary course of events he would have had the card. However, even if he did, we do not think, in the light of Mr Cuthbertson's report, that the delegate would have viewed the matter differently. It could fairly be inferred that the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer and the Board's delegate had in mind also the Medical Standards for neuro-psycopathies applicable in 1975 in respect of applicants for entry to the Public Service and the Superannuation Fund (Exhibit T2, pp.24-25).

41. Before examining this material, it should perhaps be observed that the context in which the medical report and the decision on it was taken was that assignment to the Provident Account was the less favourable course so far as retirement benefits were concerned under the superseded Act but it was not so critical to identify precisely all the grounds for refusing to allow access to the Superannuation Fund as it is under s.184 of the 1976 Act. Dr Hackett, a senior Commonwealth Medical Officer, supported this observation in his evidence. There were then no benefit classification certificates or other such documents in which the precise grounds for that refusal were required by law to be specified.

42. It should also be observed that, while we have rejected the submission that the 1977 certificate continued to have legal force after the 1978 amending Act, we are of the view that in this case the second medical examination and report, leading up to the issue of that 'certificate', and what was consequentially included in that 'certificate' (i.e. only 'History of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy'), has some relevance in considering what condition or conditions could properly have been taken to have been included in the 'deemed' certificate under s.184(2) in its original form. This may appear to be at variance with the view taken in Blakemore's Case (1980) 3 ALD at p128, in the first paragraph under the heading 'Estoppel', but as was stated in that paragraph the certificate in that case 'was clearly vitiated by mistake'. We see no evidence of any mistake or clerical error in the present case, but rather the continuation of a view that we think was originally taken.

58. Bearing in mind the considerations to which we have referred in paragraph 38, and in the light of the evidence to which we have referred above, we are of the view that the facts justified the 1980 certificate specifying only the condition 'History of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy' and we so find.

59. We think, with respect, that the position was well stated by this Tribunal, constituted by Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member), Mr V.J. Skermer and Dr A.H. Marsh (Members), in Re Bailey and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 666 as follows:

'In a case such as the present, where there is no direct evidence to establish the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, it appears to us that the proper test to apply when considering the material before the Board when it reached its decision is _ what condition or conditions must have influenced the Board to accept the person into the Provident Account rather than the Fund. Unless, on a fair consideration of that material, the Tribunal can feel satisfied that a particular physical or mental condition was, in fact, a reason which influenced the Board's decision, the Tribunal would not, in our view, be justified in finding as a fact that the condition should be taken as being specified in the benefit classification certificate which is deemed, by s.184(2) of the Act, to be in force in respect of the applicant.'

60. On this basis, we have concluded that, on the evidence available to the Board's delegate on 21 April 1975, the anxiety state referred to in the applicant's answers on the Medical Report was not in fact a reason which influenced the delegate's decision. We rather infer from the evidence that the delegate would, on the evidence before him at the time the 1975 decision was made to exclude the applicant from the Superannuation Fund, have acted on the Medical Officer's recommendation and reason as stated by him. The fact that the Delegate's decision was made immediately (i.e. on the same date as the Medical Officer's recommendation _ see paragraph 8 above) may be seen as tending to support this view, in that it was a clear case, on the ground stated by the Medical Officer, for acceptance only to the Provident Account: it was not necessary for the delegate to investigate further and we are of the view, on the balance of probabilities, that he simply accepted the Medical Officer's recommendation and stated reason. As already mentioned, it is nothing to the point that the Board or its delegate may, or would, have taken a different view if events subsequent to the 1976 Act coming into operation had been known to the Board or the delegate.

The s.66(2)(c) decision

61. We now turn to consider the Commissioner's decision under s.66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976 on the basis that the benefit classification certificate included only the condition 'History of Ulcerative Colitis with Ileostomy'. Section 66(2) so far as material reads as follows:

'(2) Subject to sub-sections (3), (3A) and (4) of this section and section 79, where _

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled _

(d) where the period of contributory service of the person is not less than 8 years _

.........

(e) where the period of contributory service of the person is less than 8 years _ to a lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 73(2) and, where the person has paid supplementary contributions, an additional lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 73(3).'

62. As noted in paragraph 15 above, the Commonwealth Medical Officer's report on the applicant's examination for retirement on invalidity grounds stated that the applicant was suffering from 'Personality Disorder' and that this condition was 100 percent the cause of his incapacity. We agree with Mr Wilde that the applicant's retirement was due predominantly to the paranoid personality disorder rather than to the effects of the back injury sustained by the applicant in August 1976, although the latter may have been a contributory factor. We think that Dr Fennessy's evidence supports this.

63. There is a question whether the incapacity for the purposes of s.66(2)(c) is the particular physical or mental condition that results in the decision to retire the person concerned or the effects of that condition upon the person in relation to his employment. Mr Wilde submitted in this case that it was the latter and that consequently in the applicant's case it was the effects of his personality disorder, and not the personality disorder itself, that was the incapacity. Mr Wilde relied on Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation reported in (1980) 3 ALN p.N1 (Note 1). The Tribunal in that case, constituted by Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member), Sir Ernest Coates and Dr A.H. Marsh (Members) said at paragraph 22 of the Reasons for Decision:

'The phrase 'incapacity which was the ground for ... retirement' is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. It would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between 'incapacity' and a 'condition' is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions.'

64. Mr Brennen did not in his submissions focus specifically on this distinction and in his opening submission in relation to the decision under s.66(2) identified the incapacity for which the applicant was retired as 'that of paranoid personality'. Dr Turner, the Senior Commonwealth Medical Officer who advised the Commissioner in relation to the latter's reconsideration of the decisions of his delegate, clearly drew no distinction between the applicant's incapacity and the condition from which it flowed, referring in her report to 'the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement i.e. 'personality disorder'' (see paragraph 20 above).

65. In some cases a clear distinction can be drawn between the incapacity and the condition 'causing' it. To take an extreme case, the incapacity of an employee who had had to have both eyes removed because of cancer could be seen to be blindness and the cause of the blindness (or incapacity) to be cancer. In other cases, the incapacity and the condition that caused the incapacity may be seen rather to merge, and the distinction between them is difficult to draw. It might be observed that this difficulty was noted in Burr's Case, at paragraph 27 of the Reasons for Decision, where the Tribunal dealt shortly with the position 'if it should be thought that, contrary to what we have said in paragraph 22 above, coronary sclerosis should have been identified as the incapacity which was the ground for retirement'. In that event, the Tribunal said, the position was even clearer, and the decision would have been the same.

66. The question is, however, not of merely academic importance, as a different result may well be obtained in applying s.66(2)(c) in a particular case depending on what is regarded as the incapacity.

67. Accepting the distinction referred to in Burr's Case (see paragraph 63 above) and Mr Wilde's submission that in this case the relevant incapacity was the various effects of the personality disorder, it is clear enough that those effects were caused or were at least substantially contributed to by the personality disorder and that that disorder was not a condition specified in the 1980 benefit classification certificate. It is also clear on this basis that the other condition that may have contributed to the incapacity (the 1976 back injury and problems flowing from that injury) is not relevant for present purposes. What has to be examined then is whether the personality disorder is connected with the only condition we regard as properly included in that certificate, namely, 'history of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy'.

68. If on the other hand the incapacity and personality disorder are regarded as being inseparable, different questions must be considered. These are whether the incapacity, i.e. the personality disorder, was caused or substantially contributed to by:

(a) the 'history of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy' (being the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate); or

(b) some other physical or mental condition 'connected with' the 'history of ulcerative colitis with ileostomy'.

69. Having examined the questions stated in paragraphs 67 and 68 in the light of the evidence, we think that the result is the same in the present case whether the matter is approached on the basis indicated in paragraph 67 or on the basis indicated in paragraph 68, though we are disposed to think that the latter basis is the appropriate basis in this case. It is convenient to consider the questions stated in paragraph 68 first.

70. As already mentioned (see paragraphs 20 and 64 above), Dr Turner, in her report dated 11 November 1980 (Exhibit T2, p.89) regarded the personality disorder as being the incapacity. She took the view that this disorder 'was substantially contributed to' by both the ulcerative colitis with ileostomy and anxiety state. Her stated grounds were that ulcerative colitis, 'with its chronicity, relapses and remissions is usually associated in all sufferers with some degree of depression and anxiety' and that 'Anxiety state is undoubtedly connected withh 'personality disorder'".

71. The specialist medical evidence before the Tribunal does not, in our opinion, support the views expressed in that report.

72. Mr Cuthbertson, a surgeon of high standing, when asked as to the causes of ulcerative colitis, answered:

'I think I know as much as anybody else about the etiology of ulcerative colitis, which is very little. There has been an enormous amount of work done on it but it is really unknown what is the cause of the disease.'

He went on to say:

'Emotional aspects were thought at one stage to be the prime cause of ulcerative colitis but I think most authorities would now believe that much of the emotional trouble results from the colitis and when you treat somebody for ulcerative colitis frequently what is apparent as an emotional illness disappears. I do not think too many believe nowadays it is the primary causative factor.'

73. When asked whether the applicant gave any indication of any emotional trouble other than that caused by the colitis, Mr Cuthbertson answered: 'Not that I can remember, no.' He said that the applicant was very well adjusted to his illness. He pointed to the problem of 'psychological adjustment to what is a very radical change in existence' but said that 'we thought he had adjusted very well to that'.

74. Dr Fennessy, a specialist psychiatrist, said of ulcerative colitis:

'I do not think anybody knows exactly what is the cause of it. It is a condition that occurs in many patients that do appear to be (sic) pronounced psychological factors. Nobody knows for sure whether that is the cause of it and I do not think these conditions are present in every case of ulcerative colitis.'

75. He was then asked two further relevant questions. The questions and his answers were as follows:

'You would be unable to say whether in this particular case it caused the ulcerative colitis, the psychological factors?-I could not say that, no.

If it did not cause it, what effect would the underlying personality disorder have on the ability to cope with the ulcerative colitis?-It is difficult to say. As I said, he went straight to a surgeon after suffering from it a couple of years, took the surgeon's advice and coped with it quickly and satisfactorily that way. He had an ileostomy and subsequently a proctectomy.'

76. Dr Fennessy was asked to explain the connection, if any, between paranoid personality disorder and state of anxiety. He answered:

'There is no direct connection between the two of them. They are different manifestations of an inability to cope with underlying _ with the various stresses and strains which are placed upon him. Anxiety state is a relatively common, neurotic, relatively mild condition in which various symptoms such as the ones I detailed about the palpitations, the pain over the heart, dry mouth, feeling of heat, the pulsations in the body, the dizziness, headaches and so forth _ that is one. We all have some anxiety in mild ways at various times, under various pressures, if things go adversely for us but a paranoid personality is a completely different thing. It is a basic gross manifestation of his inability to cope in a major way, not a psychotic way, not losing complete touch, such as to develop schizophrenia or psychotic or paranoid psychosis, but a way in which these people who are like Mr Hegedues, who are basically hostile, aggressive, resentful, discontented, argumentative, jealous, suspicious and completely self-centred....'

77. Dr Fennessy stated in evidence that the applicant's paranoid personality disorder had super-added symptoms of anxiety and depression. He said he believed the latter symptoms were 'a direct sequel of the accident he sustained at work in '76'.

78. He was further questioned on the connection of the anxiety and depression with the colitis or ileostomy and stated that the anxiety and depression 'developed only after the injury to the back'. He added:

'I had never been able to obtain from him any history of anxiety symptoms prior to that time. The aggression, unpleasantness and the hostility and jealousy, but not symptoms of anxiety.'

79. In cross-examination Dr Fennessy conceded that the personality disorder might have been an underlying thread in relation to the ulcerative colitis but not in relation to the applicant's reaction to his back injury. And he said: 'I do not think there is any relation between the ulcerative colitis and the back injuries and the anxieties'.

80. Dr Fennessy was then asked whether the onset of the ulcerative colitis, if not directly caused by the psychological problems but exacerbated by it, could be regarded as being a triggering mechanism on an underlying paranoid personality disorder and that the manifestations of this were exacerbated because of the underlying disorder. He answered: 'It is a possibility but we do not know, really, so much about ulcerative colitis. Everybody that suffers from ulcerative colitis has not got a paranoid personality disorder.' He added:

'I do not think you can say that the ulcerative colitis is definitely a manifestation of the personality disorder. There is no proof of that. No-one can be sure.'

81. Dr Fennessy expressed the view, though he had only been treating the applicant since 1977, that his paranoid personality long ante-dated the 1976 accident at work. He mentioned incidents as long ago as 1961. He also said that the applicant 'did not have any obvious anxiety about the colitis, as far as I could ascertain .... the only anxiety appeared to be about the possibility of becoming impotent if he had the proctectomy done'. He added that 'he does not seem to have exhibited any anxiety after the operation'.

82. In the context of this evidence, and we regard Dr Fennessy's evidence as having the greatest weight in respect of psychological and psychiatric matters, we accept that the applicant had a long-standing personality disorder that ante-dated his ulcerative colitis and that became progressively more obvious over more recent years. We do not, however, think that on the evidence the personality disorder was 'caused by' or was 'substantially contributed to by' the ulcerative colitis with ileostomy.

83. Turning to the second question posed in paragraph 68, it seems to us that the only possible relevant condition suggested by any of the evidence is anxiety state. If the applicant was suffering from anxiety state and that 'caused' or 'substantially contributed to' the paranoid personality and the anxiety state was 'connected with' the ulcerative colitis with ileostomy then, on the view we have taken on what was properly to be included in the 1980 certificate, the applicant's entitlements would fall to be determined under s.66(2). But the expert medical evidence did not, in our view, show that the personality disorder was caused or substantially contributed to by any anxiety state that was so connected. As noted above, Dr Turner stated in her report that anxiety state was 'undoubtedly connected with personality disorder' but we do not think this statement was supported by the expert evidence at the hearing and any anxiety state that might be said to be related to that disorder arose from the 1976 back injury and its consequences and was not 'connected with' the applicant's ulcerative colitis or ileostomy. We refer here to Dr Fennessy's evidence which we think must be preferred to that of Dr Gilfedder, the applicant's treating general practitioner, who stated in evidence that he thought that the applicant had 'always had anxiety', but did not in fact refer him to a psychiatrist until May 1977 _ a date subsequent to the back injury he sustained at work.

84. Our consideration of the matter on the basis set out in paragraph 68 accordingly leads us to the conclusion that a negative answer must be given to each of the questions in that paragraph.

85. Turning now to the basis set out in paragraph 67, only one question needs to be considered on the view we have taken, namely, whether the applicant's personality disorder was 'connected with' the ulcerative colitis with ileostomy.

86. Mr Wilde referred to the views expressed by the Tribunal on the meaning of 'connected with' in s.66(2) in Re Vaughan and Commissioner for Superannuation ((1979) 2 ALN p.366 (Note 36)) and Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation ((1980) 3 ALN p.N1 (Note 1)). What emerges from those cases and from Re Brown and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation ((1981) 3 ALD 185) is that there must at least be a real or demonstrable connection. We do not think that it is enough merely that the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate and the condition that caused the incapacity resulting in the retirement are sometimes or, perhaps, not uncommonly associated and do not understand any decision of the Tribunal to have so decided.

87. The word 'connected' and its cognate expressions have been considered by the Courts in many cases in different contexts. We do not, however, think that they throw more light on the expression 'connected with' in s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act than is provided by the following meanings given in the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary:

''connect' : 1. Trans. To join, fasten or link together. Const. to, with.

2. To associate in occurrence, action or idea.

....

4. intrans. To become united or joined.'

''connection' : 2. Relation between things one of which is bound up with, or involved in, another.'

Considered in this light, we think that two conditions must be linked together on some sustainable medical basis before they can properly be regarded as 'connected' for the purposes of s.66(2).

88. Mr Wilde submitted that the scheme embodied in the Superannuation legislation was not only concerned with direct causative factors. He put the position, as he saw it, in the following terms:

'The submission is that if the legislature intended that there be a direct causal connection there then the words caused or substantially contributed to would have been repeated, as the words connected (with) are used.

This is entirely consistent with the scheme of the legislation. The previous superseded Act was concerned with identifying conditions which might give rise to later retirement and similarly the present 1976 legislation was also concerned with identifying these risk factors which might give rise to early retirement.

In identifying these risk factors the Commissioner only has to be satisfied as to the likelihood that a person might retire because of one of these conditions. What the whole scheme is concerned about, as I said, is identifying these risk factors which could possibly lead to later retirement, so that you would have a situation sometimes where there may well be a causal connection, that being that a particular condition has led to another condition and that condition has been the condition which has caused the retirement, but the scheme is also concerned with identifying and subsdequently placing on benefit classification certificates conditions which are symptoms of or manifestations of conditions which already exist so that we are takling about, if you like, a reverse connection.

If an underlying condition was not evident, or was not known at a particular time when the person came into the fund, but there were symptoms which manifested that condition and that condition was later discovered and that later condition became the condition which casued or substantially contributed to the retirement, then it is submitted that the fact that the condition which was put on the certificate in the first instance did not cause that later condition is not a bar to applying the connection principle in section 66(2).' direct e linked together on some sustainable medical basis before they can properly be regarded as 'connected' for the purposes of s.66(2).

89. Mr Wilde went on to submit that in this particular case the personality disorder may well have caused the ulcerative colitis and if it did then the ulcerative colitis was a manifestation of an underlying condition, and if the applicant was subsequently retired because of the personality disorder or because of another condition which has been grossly exacerbated by the underlying disorder, then there was still a conneaction in the relevant sense with s.66(2).

90. He submitted, in relation to the 'reverse connection argument', which he said had not been put before, that it was fundamental to the scheme of the legislation, and consistent with the words of the legislation, in that it does not have to be a causal conneaction in one direction - the connectio can be both ways. Dealing with the ulcerataive colitis aspect, he submitted that ulcerative colitis was associated with the personality disorder and the emotional vulnerability and all the features referred to in Dr Fennessy's report in that it was that which caused or exacerbated the ulcerative colitis and that but for the personality disorder that condition would not have existed or would not have existed in the severity that it did.

91. Mr Wilde submitted further that 'the scheme of the Act is that the benefit classification certificate can be issued and it can have the manifestation of the condition in question of which the etiology is unknown, and if the person subsequently retires and there is an association with that disease, and it is a known association and it is a recognized association, which is the case in this particular case with ulcerative colitis, then the Commissioner is able to draw that connection'.

92. The Tribunal accepts the medical evidence, particularly that of Dr Fennessy, as establishing that the applicant certainly had the makings of a paranoid personality disorder before 1975. And, of course, it was this disorder which resulted in the incapacity on the ground of which he was retired. But can it be said on the evidence that there was a connection of the kind required by s.66(2) between the ulcerative colitis with ileostomy and the paranoid personality? Certainly the evidence does not go so far as to suggest that all persons who have ulcerative colitis, or even most who have ulcerative colitis, have paranoid personalities. The most that could be said to come out of the evidence was that it was not uncommon that emotional aspects were associated with ulcerative colitis, that often these disappeared when the colitis was treated and that indeed this seemed to be the situation in the applicant's case until his injury at work in 1976 when anxiety related to that injury manifested itself.

93. We do not think that the applicant's personality disorder was recognized by the Commonwealth Medical Officers or the Board's delegate when he entered the Public Service in 1975. The ulcerative colitis with ileostomy was made known to them and this, in our view, was the reason for restricting him to the Provident Account. To now say that the ulcerative colitis was a manifestation of the personality disorder is, in our view, contrary to the best medical evidence before us (that of Dr Fennessy and Mr Cuthbertson). The reverse proposition, i.e. that the personality disorder stemmed from the ulcerative colitis, is contrary to the evidence, and so is the proposition that the ulcerative colitis and ileostomy exacerbated or brought to the surface his personality problem. What exacerbated it was, on the evidence of both Dr Fennessy and Dr Fogarty, the back injury he suffered at work in September 1976 and his consequent frustration. We are not satisfied on the evidence that there was any real or demonstrable connection between the ulcerative colitis with ileostomy and the personality disorder which led to his retirement and we find that there was no such connection. At most there was a possibility of a connection. That is not in our view sufficient for the purposes of s.66(2).

94. It follows that our consideration of the matter on the basis set out in paragraph 67 leads us also to give a negative answer to the question put in that paragraph.

95. The position in this case is then that, whichever approach is followed, the decision that was taken in respect of the applicant under s.66(2) is not, in our view, supported by the evidence before us.

96. There are a few final comments that should be made. Clearly enough, with hindsight, the applicant had at least an incipient personality disorder when he sought to join the Public Service which, if recognized, should have resulted, if not in his rejection as a permanent officer, then at least in his being restricted to the Provident Account. But it was not recognized on the evidence available to the persons making the decision, and there was no suggestion that the applicant withheld relevant information. If a decision could be made afresh later, in the light of subsequent evidence and developments, then no doubt, looking back, one can point to danger signals in accepting the applicant at all. But s.184(7) is concerned with the reasons of the Board (or its delegate) as at the time of his acceptance in 1975, not 1977 or 1980, and while one might speculate in the light of subsequent events that the Board or its delegate should have recognized the personality problem, there is, in our view, no evidence that this was the case.

Conclusion

97. In the result, we have concluded that the decisions under review should be set aside, and that the applicant's invalidity benefit under the Superannuation Act 1976 should be determined in accordance with s.66(1) of that Act.
