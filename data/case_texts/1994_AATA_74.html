Jean Madeline Black and Commissioner of Superannuation [1994] AATA 74 (25 March 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

JEAN MADELINE BLACK v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. N93/161

AAT No. 9392

Number of pages - 31

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

M. T. LEWIS (Senior Member), N. J. ATTWOOD (Member) AND I. R. WAY (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Original Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") substituted with BCC specifying "anxiety state" - history of anxiety state - Applicant denied psychiatric symptoms in Medical Report Form and at examination before original BCC issued - whether Applicant gave false or misleading information - whether correct answers would have led to diagnosis and specification of anxiety state in BCC - Applicant's actual knowledge and state of mind at time of medical examination - Applicant lacking insight into her condition - what is reasonable for this Applicant to have answered - whether retirement incapacity caused or substantially contributed to by that condition or a connected condition - whether Commonwealth should pay costs of witness essential to Tribunal's exercise of review function.

Words And Phrases - knowledge this Applicant "could reasonably be expected to have" - what Applicant "could reasonably be expected to regard as relevant to answering the question" - "actual knowledge and state of mind" - "disabilities" - "anxiety state" - "mental condition".

HEARING

SYDNEY, 22 and 29 October 1993

25:3:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Matthew Smith

Solicitors for the Applicant: Vardanega Roberts

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr Gageler

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside, and the matter is remitted to the Respondent with the following Directions -

(i) The original Benefit Classification Certificate specifying

the condition "Underweight" only is correct.

(ii) The said original Benefit Classification Certificate is not

to be revoked and no new certificate is to be issued; and

(iii) Pursuant to section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976,

the Commissioner is not to be of the opinion that the incapacity

which was the ground of the Applicant's retirement was caused, or

substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition

or conditions specified in the said original Benefit

Classification Certificate issued pursuant to section 16(4) or by

a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such

a condition or such conditions.

DECISION

M. T. LEWIS, N. J. ATTWOOD AND I. R. WAY On 15 March 1993 Jean Madeleine Black ("the Applicant") lodged an application to this Tribunal to review a decision of a Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Respondent") made on 23 March 1987, which was reconsidered on 19 February 1993 and varied. The original decision revoked the Benefit Classification Certificate ("the BCC") in force at the time and a new BCC was issued in respect of the Applicant specifying the conditions "History of Anxiety State, Obsessional Personality Disorder, and Underweight", and determining that the new BCC was deemed to have been issued on 19 April 1982. Furthermore, it was determined that pursuant to section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") the incapacity which was the ground for the Applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by the physical or mental condition specified in the BCC or by a condition or conditions connected with such conditions. On 19 February 1993 a Delegate of the Respondent varied the decision of 23 March 1987 by substituting a decision under subsection 16AC of the Act to issue a BCC specifying the conditions "Underweight, and History of Anxiety State" and affirmed the decision of the Delegate of 23 March 1987 insofar as it related to subsection 66(2) of the Act.

2. At the hearing the Tribunal had before it the documents provided by the Respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The following were accepted by the Tribunal at the hearing as exhibits on behalf of the Applicant -

. Copies of HCF Medical Benefit Claims for periods 1971-72,

1972-73 and 1973-74 in the name of John Holliday Black, Contributor

(exhibit A)

. Note from Dr N.C. Corlette dated 10 September 1956 (exhibit B)

. Letter from Dr. Peter Corlette dated 22 October 1993 (exhibit C)

. Copy of Medical Report Form - Permanent Appointment. Part 1

(exhibit D)

The following were accepted by the Tribunal at the hearing as exhibits on behalf of the Respondent -

. Copy of clinical notes of Dr. Peter Dawson (exhibit 1)

. ACT Health Record - J.M. Black (exhibit 2)

. List of dates and events headed "Mrs. Jean M. Black" as part of

oral evidence of Dr. H.V. Veness (exhibit 3)

. Handwritten notes of Dr. Veness headed "Jean Black" (exhibit 4).

. Document headed "13. Central Nervous System". "GMS - 1977 to 1985"

(exhibit 5).

3. The Applicant gave oral evidence at the hearing. Oral evidence was given on behalf of the Applicant by Dr. Hugh Victor Veness. The Respondent called Dr. Geoffrey David Davis, Dr. Peter Edgar Dawson, and Dr. Brian Charles Bromhead to give oral evidence.

4. In Re Meilak v Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] FCA 96; (1991) 22 ALD 32 it was held by Sheppard, Jenkinson and Ryan JJ. that the amendment to the Superannuation Act 1976 by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No.40 of 1990), which inserted subsection 16AC into the Principal Act, was to apply "to answers given by contributors no matter that they had been furnished under earlier legislation that did not require such a standard".

5. Section 16AC. of the Act provides -

(1) In this section:

"condition" means a physical or mental condition;

"relevant person" means:

(a) ..... ; or

(b) ..... ; or

(c) a person:

(i) who has ceased, because of death or retirement on the

ground of invalidity, to be an eligible employee before attaining his

or her maximum retiring age; and

(ii) whose period of contributory service is less than 20

years.

(2) Subsections (4) to (8), inclusive, apply where the Commissioner

is satisfied, in respect of a person who is a relevant person:

(a) that:

(i) at or in connection with a medical examination that the

person was required under subsection 16(2) or (6) or 16AB(3) to

undergo; or

(ii) .....

the person failed to answer properly a question asked of him or

her or gave false or misleading information; and

(b) that, if the person had answered the question properly or had

not given that false or misleading information:

(i) where there is in force, or there was in force immediately

before the person's retirement or death, a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the person -a condition or conditions of

the person not specified in the certificate would be or would have

been so specified; or

(ii) where subparagraph (i) does not apply - there would be in

force, or there would have been in force immediately before the

person's retirement or death, a benefit classification certificate in

respect of the person specifying a condition or conditions of the

person.

(3) Subsections (4) to (8), inclusive, also apply where the

Commissioner is satisfied, in respect of a person who is a relevant

person:

(a) that, in connection with an application under subsection

16AB(2) for the revocation of a certificate issued in respect of the

person, a person (in this section called the "non-discloser"), being:

(i) the relevant person; or

(ii) the applicant; or

(iii) a person acting on the applicant's behalf; or

(iv) a person on whose behalf the applicant was acting;

failed to answer properly a question asked of him or her or

gave false or misleading information; and

(b) that, if the non-discloser had answered the question properly

or had not given that false or misleading information:

(i) where there is in force, or there was in force immediately

before the relevant person's retirement or death, a benefit

classification certificate in respect of the relevant person not

specified in the certificate would be or would have been so

specified; or

(ii) where subparagraph (i) does not apply - there would be in

force, or there would have been in force immediately before the

relevant person's retirement or death, a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the relevant person specifying a condition

or conditions of the relevant person.

(4) If subparagraph (2)(b)(i) or (3)(b) (i) applies, the Commissioner

shall revoke the certificate and issue in substitution for it a new

benefit classification certificate in which the condition or

conditions referred to in that subparagraph is or are specified

either in addition to, or instead of, the condition, or some or all

of the conditions, specified in the revoked certificate.

(5) If subparagraph (2)(b)(ii) or (3)(b)(ii) applies, the

Commissioner shall issue in respect of the relevant person a

benefit classification certificate specifying the condition or

conditions referred to in that subparagraph.

(6) Except for the purposes of this section, a certificate revoked

under subsection (4) is taken never to have been issued.

(7) A certificate issued under subsection (4) in substitution for a

revoked certificate is taken to have been issued on the day on

which, and under the provision under which, the revoked certificate

was issued.

(8) A certificate issued under subsection (5) shall be taken to have

been issued on the day, and under the provision, that the

Commissioner determines to be the day on which, and the provision

under which, a benefit classification certificate would have been

issued, or would have been taken to have been issued, as the case

required, in respect of the relevant person if the relevant person,

or the non-discloser, as the case may be, had answered the question

properly, or had not given the false or misleading information, as

the case may be.

(9) For the purposes of this section, a person answers a question

properly

if, and only if, he or she gives in answer to the question all the

information that he or she could reasonably be expected to give, on

the basis of:

(a) his or her knowledge about the relevant matters; and

(b) the knowledge that, having regard to his or her knowledge

about the relevant matters, he or she could reasonably be expected to

have about those matters;

if he or she answered the question fully and truthfully on the basis

of the knowledge referred to in paragraphs (a) and (b).

(10) In subsection (9):

"relevant matters" in relation to a question asked of a person,

means:

(a) in any case - the matters that the person could reasonably be

expected to regard as relevant to answering the question; and

(b) if the question is asked of the person:

(i) at or in connection with a medical examination that the

person was required under subsection 16(2) or (6) or 16AB(3) to

undergo; or

(ii) in connection with a request by the person under

subsection 16(6); or

(iii) .......

the person's medical history and past and present state of physical

and mental health.

Issues before the Tribunal

6. Counsel for both parties submitted that the Tribunal could only affirm the decision under review if it is positively satisfied on the balance of probabilities as to each of the following three issues.

(i) Whether, pursuant to section 16AC(2)(a) of the Act, the

Applicant failed to answer properly questions asked of her, or gave

false or misleading information in the 1981 Medical Report Form

(T14/100-103) or at her medical examination on 22 January 1981,

concerning whether she then had a "history of anxiety state".

(ii) Whether, pursuant to section 16AC(2)(b) of the Act, if she had

answered the questions properly or had not given false or misleading

information, the condition "history of anxiety state" would have been

specified in her benefit classification certificate.

(iii) Whether section 66(2) applies to the Applicant's entitlements

under the Act, because her 1985 retirement incapacity was caused or

substantially contributed to by that condition or a connected

condition.

The Tribunal agrees with this submission and recognises the issue in (i) as the critical threshold issue. The central issue was whether the applicant "failed properly to answer" a question contained in the 1981 Medical Report Form (T14/100-103) within the meaning of subsection 16AC of the Act.

The Evidence

7. The Applicant was born on 23 July 1929. She attended school until the age of 14 years and then did a business college course before commencing work with a private insurance company and later with the New South Wales Public Service. From 1948 she undertook a dressmaking course which she finished prior to her marriage on 31 March 1951. Her husband was an accountant in the State Public Service but soon after they married he joined the Royal Australian Air Force when he was stationed at many places throughout Australia, and in Singapore and Penang. She went with her husband on his postings and finally in about 1970 he was posted to Canberra.

8. In 1971 the Applicant commenced employment as a clerical assistant with the Department of Navy in Canberra for a few months. After a brief interval she then obtained work in the Department of Customs and Excise for about four years. She said she left that job because she was physically exhausted after delivering telex messages all day. She said she resigned after being refused leave without pay. In cross-examination she referred to being physically exhausted as a result of the thyrotoxicosis driving her into more and more physical activity until she became physically exhausted and needed a rest. She said in reference to the report of Dr. Truman, consultant psychiatrist (see T.22. p.127), who reported her saying, in respect of her leaving work at that time, that she was "worked into the ground", "cracked up and left" - that she used 'cracked up' loosely, meaning physically rather than mentally 'cracking up'. Again, with reference to her alleged statement to Dr. Veness that she resigned because of pressure of work, she said this related to physical pressure. She also explained that at the time Dr. Veness was given this information, he was providing treatment for her husband and she merely attended a consultation with her husband as the patient.

9. The Applicant made about five attempts to rejoin the Public Service between 1976 and 1980 by taking the entrance examinations, and when she failed to gain entry as a clerk she decided to retrain as a typist. She was then offered a position as a typist with the Department of Defence.

10. The Applicant became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the 1976 Act on 10 November 1980. She was medically examined by Dr Bromhead, CMO, in January 1981, and he recommended that the Applicant did not meet the General Medical Standard (GMS) because she was underweight. Nevertheless it was recommended by a Senior Medical Officer (SMO) that the Applicant was fit for permanent appointment. On 19 April 1982 a BCC was issued by the Respondent pursuant to sub-section 16(4) of the Act specifying the condition "underweight".

11. The Applicant took sick leave from 15 September 1982 until 17 January 1983. On the day before she went on sick leave she attended her local doctor, Dr P Dawson, suffering from dermatitis of both hands which were bleeding and weeping. By the time Dr. Dawson reported to the Department of Defence on 27 January 1983 (T.19) he considered that the dermatitis on the Applicant's hands was a stress related condition. He also referred to the period from 1978 to 1980 when the Applicant was not working, during which time he said she had many symptoms "due to anxiety". At the time of her returning to work on 17 January 1983 he thought work "might be occupational therapy for her", but by the time he wrote the report on 27 January 1983 he considered that the position to which she was transferred after she returned to work would also cause some stress, and he recommended that she cease work for the sake of her health. He opined that "it is not likely that she will find a job that gives her pleasure without anxiety".

12. During her period of sick leave she was examined by Dr Noel Corlette, consultant physician, who reported on 21 October 1982 (T.17) that he had advised the Applicant to take three months leave from work because she had been under stress at work due to temperamental incompatibility with another woman with whom she was working. Dr Corlette considered that the Applicant was in no condition to continue working at that time and he considered that her thyroid condition could be activated if her stress condition was to continue. She was also examined by Dr. Shephard, Dermatologist, on 17 September 1982 (T.20). After excluding an allergic basis for the dermatitis he concluded that the condition may be due to contact with a substance in the workplace, but it could well be that her skin problem was "constitutional" on the basis of her being under considerable stress.

13. The Applicant was medically examined on 1 February 1983 by a CMO to determine her fitness for work. The CMO noted that she was suffering from dermatitis and anxiety and recommended redeployment to less stressful duties (T.20). On 22 April 1983 the Applicant again took sick leave. She remained off work until she was medically examined on 7 January 1985 by a CMO (T.28) and again redeployment was recommended, but on 24 January 1985 an SMO recommended the Applicant's retirement on the grounds of invalidity due to "anxiety state" and "excessive compulsive neurosis".

14. On 25 June 1985 a decision was made by the Department of Defence to retire the Applicant on grounds of invalidity with effect from 24 January 1985. In turn this action led to the determination of the delegate on 23 March 1987 which was the primary decision now being reviewed by this Tribunal.

15. The Applicant's evidence was that during the 1950's she was plagued by mouth ulcers and as a result of a referral to a specialist (Dr Corlette) she was treated with vitamin B12 injections. She first went to Dr Dawson's practice in 1972, when she commenced seeing Dr Davis, as she felt there was something physically wrong with her and she said "I just wasn't myself". She said that Dr. Davis considered the problem was menopausal and recommended prescription drugs. After she was in Canberra for about eighteen months she felt that her condition was deteriorating and so she went back to see Dr Corlette. He treated her for an overactive thyroid and she continued to see him monthly. She described the treatment as "a chemical" that acts on the thyroid. She said -

"they are not a drug, they do not act on your nerves or anything

like that. They are just chemical".

At this time she also recommenced the vitamin B12 injections after a period of cessation. The Applicant said that she continued seeing Dr. Corlette regularly from 1972 for treatment and supervision of her thyroid condition.

16. The Applicant said that she also continued to see Dr. Dawson regularly, including the period between 1976 and 1980 when she was not working, regarding her thyroid condition, to implement the treatment recommended by Dr. Corlette. About 1974 she recalled Dr. Dawson recommending her referral to a psychiatrist. Her evidence was -

"I don't really know how it came about, he just said, "Would you

like to go and see a psychiatrist", and I said, "No, I don't need

to see a psychiatrist. I'm perfectly all right. I know who I am

and where I'm going. I don't need to see a psychiatrist", and I

felt I was well in control of my own life, apart from the

physical problems which I have no control over".

She said that she then discussed Dr. Dawson's suggestion with Dr. Corlette, who responded that she did not need to see a psychiatrist, and her problem arose from her thyroid condition. She said that at no time before her medical examination in 1981 had she been told by any doctor that she had anything wrong with her mentally although she admitted in cross-examination that she understood Dr. Dawson to consider she was "in a nervous condition" and was treating her for this (transcript p.36), but that the Applicant herself did not believe she had a nervous condition - she thought it was an overactive thyroid. The Applicant was asked -

"But you agreed that many doctors, in particular Dr. Dawson, Dr.

Davis, Dr. Truman, have told you that you do have problems?"

The Applicant replied -

"Yes, and I don't, because I have this over-active thyroid and

they're misinterpreting, they don't know enough about over-active

thyroids I don't think. I don't think they understand. Even in

America they're only just coming to terms with it" (transcript

p.38).

17. The Applicant was referred in cross-examination to the clinical notes of Dr. Davis and Dr. Dawson (exhibit 1). On 17 April 1970 she first attended the practice, at which time it was recorded "marked depression and insomnia" and Tryptanol was prescribed. The Applicant said that she did not tell the doctor that she was depressed. She said she has never been depressed. She claims that she said "I don't know what's wrong with me ......I'm just not myself, there's something physically wrong with me..." (transcript pp.40-41). She said she did not know that Tryptanol was a drug commonly prescribed for depression, and as it made her feel much worse she did not take it. She said that she kept telling the doctor that she did not want drugs, but she wanted to find out what was wrong with her.

18. In January 1971 Dr. Davis again recorded "now markedly nervous, depressed, apprehensive. Resume Tryptanol 10 mg. b.d.". Again the Applicant denied ever being depressed, but she said the drugs (Tryptanol) were making her feel physically worse. She described herself as a very active person who looks for things to do. She said that Dr. Davis kept prescribing Tryptanol and she continued not taking it. She said she had the scripts dispensed and they remained unopened in the cupboard until, when she moved from Canberra to Port Macquarie, she threw them out. She said -

"...I wouldn't take them because I won't take drugs, no one is

going to get me on to drugs, I don't need drugs" (transcript

p.43).

19. On 29 April 1971 Dr. Davis recorded "Valium 2 mgm. b.d. in place of Tryptanol for anxiety state". The Applicant said she did not recall Dr. Davis prescribing Valium, but she reiterated that she kept telling Dr. Davis that she would not take drugs. She said her mother had been treated with Valium for many years without benefit. She then said -

"I don't like people getting on to drugs, they just put you on to

drugs and they don't find out what the real cause of your problem

is" (transcript p.44).

However, she admitted that she knew Valium was prescribed for people "to calm them down". She said she did not remember whether or not she had a discussion with Dr. Davis about her having an anxiety state (transcript p.45). In respect of an entry dated 24 June 1971 in Dr. Davis's notes which read -

"Increased Valium to 1 t.d.s. of own accord. Needs 2 at night at

times. Suggest 1 b.d., 2 nocte. Periods now regular"

she said it was not correct and "I wouldn't take the tablets..." (transcript p.46). The Applicant then said that she was taking the drugs "in the very beginning till I found out they were having such a dreadful affect on me ....". On 14 August 1971 Dr. Davis recorded "....anxiety and depression. Resumed Tryptanol 10 mgm. t.d.s. with little effect. Continue same". The clinical notes then indicate that for the rest of the year she was prescribed a regime of Premarin (which the Tribunal understands to be a hormonal medication) and Valium. On 29 November 1971 it was recorded "Cut down Valium herself to 2 mg. b.d. Has begun work with Customs and Excise". Then on 22 February 1972 there was an entry "also advised cut down Valium to 2 mg. daily then cease. Continue Premarin for approx. 6/12 longer". In response to all these entries the Applicant insisted in cross-examination that she was not taking the drugs.

20. The Applicant also agreed in cross-examination that she had been suffering from palpitations when she first went to Dr. Davis and she could not recall when they ceased. The clinical notes of Dr. Davis indicate on 30 May 1972 "palpitations have ceased". The Tribunal notes that at T.5, which was completed in 1972, the Applicant acknowledged that she had received treatment for menopause. In cross-examination she said "I don't know what is related to menopause and what isn't" (transcript p.67).

21. A further entry in Dr. Davis's clinical notes on 13 September 1974 read "she has tension" and again Valium was prescribed. At the same time an entry was made "no signs thyrotoxicosis". On 7 May 1975 she was prescribed Mogadon (which the Tribunal understands to be a sedative). There was no indication in respect of that entry of the symptoms about which she had complained and which had led to this prescription. On 7 August 1975 Dr. Davis recorded

"Worried because people at work state 'she doesn't need to work'

and 'she doesn't need this money, she is filthy rich etc'.

Letter given explaining therapeutic necessity of occupation".

In cross-examination the Applicant said in respect of this entry "it wasn't worrying me" and she was not suffering any stress at work at that stage. In respect of "therapeutic necessity of occupation" she said she needed to be occupied all the time because of her over-active thyroid.

22. The applicant transferred to the care of Dr. Dawson, in the same medical practice, when Dr. Davis was not available. The first entry in Dr. Dawson's clinical notes was on 20 June 1978 when he noted "wakes 3 a.m. wanting to cut throat". He prescribed Inderal. The next entry bears an unclear date, and states "Seems much better than Tues. when ++ anxiety and depression. Continue Inderal. Feels heavy on this - did not start Tryptanol. Paroxysmal auricular tachycardia a possibility but anxiety state more likely. On 29 June 1978 Dr. Dawson wrote -

"stopped Inderal - eyes swollen and weepy.....Dr. Hill treating

her with psychotherapy at Narrabundah Health Centre. Feels she

can talk to Dr. Hill who has been very helpful. Very frightened

person ---->tachycardia .... not as depressed".

In his oral evidence Dr. Dawson said that 'Stop Inderal. Eyes swollen and weepy' could have been hay fever or an allergy to Inderal. He also advised in his oral evidence that a thyroid function test was subsequently normal, and therefore he considered a diagnosis of 'anxiety state' more likely to be the cause of her rapid heart rate and her nervous state . Dr. Dawson felt sure that he would have discussed this diagnosis with her (transcript p.136).

23. On 13 September 1978 Dr. Dawson recorded -

"Should see psychiatrist. Typical mania/depressive state".

When questioned about these 1978 entries the Applicant could not recall Dr. Dawson referring to 'anxiety state', and she said he kept referring to 'nerves' (transcript p.58). Again she insisted that Dr. Dawson was trying to treat her for 'nerves' when her problem was thyroid. In relation to her consultations with Dr. Hill, a general practitioner, the Applicant said "he tricked me into going by wanting to test my heart". In respect of the psychotherapy the Applicant said that Dr. Hill explained that he did not cure people with tablets, he cured people by talking to them. She then said that in her case she needed "a chemical to kill my thyroid off and all the talking in the world won't do that". The Applicant also said that she told Dr. Corlette that she was recommended to see a psychiatrist and she claims that he said "you don't need a psychiatrist, there's nothing wrong with you..." The Tribunal notes that a report from Dr. Corlette (T.12) dated 27 March 1979 to Dr. Peter Dawson states

"As you say she does not wish to see a Psychiatrist and I feel

sure will not go into hospital and be treated by one. I feel myself

she is not really a psychiatric problem but rather one of what one

might call 'family incompatibility". I do not believe her Thyroid

function is abnormal but she is undoubtedly going through a

menopausal situation.

I discussed their problems with both she and her husband and sent

them home with Mrs. to take Serepax 15 mg t.i.d. and both to be more

tolerant of each other and let her daughter's husband look after his

wife etc. and not to take too much notice of their attitude towards

her at this stage...."

Dr. Dawson said, in evidence, that he did not agree with Dr. Corlette on this point.

24. The Applicant's evidence was that Dr. Dawson was always telling her that she needed to see a psychiatrist and "I was always fighting him". On 2 March 1979 Dr. Dawson wrote -

"Admission arranged under Hughson. Refused to see Psych. or go

to M Ward."

The Applicant said that Dr. Dawson wanted her to go to M Ward for "electric shocks to your brain and I said, no...." (transcript p.62). On 7 April 1989 Dr. Dawson recorded -

"Dr. Corlette gave Serepax 15 mg. t.i.d. and one week ago added

Nomeocazole on clinical grounds......"

The Applicant said in cross-examination that she was prescribed Serepax because she could not sleep because her husband snored and kept her awake. She claims that she did not take the Serepax. Dr. Dawson in his oral evidence said that Serepax was prescribed for excessive anxiety and agitation.

25. The Applicant provided a record which her husband had kept of medical benefits claims for the period 1971 to 1974 (exhibit A). This confirms that the Applicant attended her general practitioner on a number of occasions in 1971 and 1972 for menopausal symptoms and from July 1972 she attended her general practitioner and Dr Corlette for thyroid treatment. In June 1973 she had a consultation with her general practitioner for "hormone imbalance". No reference was made on that record for any consultation for the Applicant in respect of a nervous condition although note was made on a number of occasions for the attendance of another member of the family for "nervous tension". In cross-examination the Applicant said that her husband entered 'menopause' on this record because that is what the doctor told her was her problem and she reported this to her husband.

26. The Applicant had also been seeing Dr. Shephard, dermatologist, for treatment of her skin lesions. On 2 March 1981 Dr. Dawson recorded "Shephard suggests marriage guidance and ANU Stress Clinic. The Applicant's interpretation of this to the Tribunal was that it was "all just casual conversation because I used to tell him my husband's working all the time and I'm on my own all the time...." She also said that she refused to go to the ANU Stress Clinic because she felt she could manage her own marriage.

27. The Applicant said that when she attended for the medical examination by the CMO in 1981 she was given a form and asked to complete it. No assistance was provided to her in completing the form. She recalled telling the examining doctor that she had an overactive thyroid but she did not recall providing any other information to him.

28. Again in cross-examination the Applicant was referred to a letter written on her behalf by Dr. Dawson dated 27 January 1983 to the Compensation Section, Department of Defence (T.19) which stated -

"She did not work from 1978 to 1980 during which time she had

many symptoms due to anxiety. I thought returning to work

17.1.83 might be occupational therapy for her".

She said that Dr. Dawson considered her to be "a nervous wreck or nervous" and that -

"He kept pushing me into going to work all the time which I

didn't want to do and he called my husband there and said: 'I

think she should go to work' and I didn't want to go to work and

they more or less pushed me into it and I went along with them

and thought: Well, well, if it is going to cure me, if it's

going to be the answer, I'll do it" (transcript p.63).

In relation to Dr.Dawson's comments that she had symptoms of anxiety during the period between 1978 and 1980 the Applicant interpreted this to be referring to problems she was having with her teenage children which she discussed with him.

29. In cross-examination the Applicant was referred to a letter signed by her to the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 15 May 1987 (T.50) which she said was drafted and read to her by her husband. The letter referred to her claim for compensation in March 1983 for 'stress', and this term was changed subsequently to 'anxiety state' at the instigation of the 'compensation clerk'. The letter stated that prior to 1983 she suffered from thyrotoxicosis and was not aware of having 'anxiety state'. The letter then concluded that if the Medical Report Form had included 'stress' she would "most certainly" have answered "in the affirmative". Of this letter she said in cross-examination "I really don't understand it, it's all so complicated". In the circumstances the Tribunal gives no weight to this letter. The Applicant's response to this letter in cross-examination was consistent with her general demeanour and limited comprehension reflected throughout the hearing.

30. After incisive cross-examination the Applicant could not explain why she admitted in her 1972 CMO questionnaire that she had had sun spots removed, and in the 1981 CMO questionnaire she had failed to note this procedure. In response to the same questionnaires regarding 'anxiety state' she said she did not recall Dr. Dawson telling her she had an anxiety state. When it was put to her that she perceived that she had a nervous condition, she said -

"Nervous condition, yes, and he wouldn't talk to me, I'd ask him

questions and he wouldn't answer".

It was then put to the Applicant -

"Before 1981 you had been told by Dr. Dawson that you were

suffering from a nervous condition? -

The Applicant replied -

"That was his assumption. He had said it, yes. ...... I'm still

trying to tell you that I wasn't happy with his diagnosis of even

nervous condition or anything. I knew there was something physically

wrong with me, and it was just putting me like I was......He's

obviously misunderstood anxiety state for thyroid toxic (sic) - he's

not experienced enough to know a thyroid toxicosis (sic) patient,

that's what I feel........I had Dr. Corlette and that's what I based

the whole of my life on.

31. The Applicant was referred to question 13(b) (T.14) which asked -

"Are you aware of any circumstances regarding your health which

will interfere with the satisfactory discharge of the duties of

the position for which you are now applying?"

She replied -

"No, I thought if the doctor thought I was well enough to go to

work I must have been well enough to go to work. He was the one

that sent me to work" (transcript p.81).

32. The Applicant agreed that in respect of question 2(h) on the 1981 questionnaire in respect of 'frequent palpitations' she should have answered "yes" when indeed she answered "no". She attempted to explain the discrepancy in a manner that could only be described as incoherent (transcript p.82).

33. The Applicant advised that Dr. Corlette is now deceased. In a letter from Dr. Peter Corlette (exhibit C), son of the deceased Dr. Noel Corlette, he reported that his late father was "exceptionally well qualified to deal with thyroid disease". He was the senior thyroid surgeon at University College Hospital, London, from 1935 to 1939. On his return to Australia he was a senior surgeon at Royal South Sydney Hospital and Balmain Hospital, and established the first fully integrated thyroid clinic in New South Wales. On this evidence the Tribunal finds that the late Dr. Noel Corlette was an expert in the treatment of thyroid disorders and he was well qualified to diagnose and treat the Applicant for thyrotoxicosis, including being able to differentiate diagnostically between thyrotoxicosis and anxiety state.

34. Oral evidence was given by Dr. Geoffrey Davis. He was the Applicant's treating general medical practitioner from early 1970 until mid 1978. He said that he did not continue providing the Vitamin B12 injections for the Applicant. He said that he diagnosed her condition of thyrotoxicosis early in 1971. He referred her to Dr. Noel Corlette who confirmed the diagnosis and initiated treatment. He said that the Applicant was markedly better as a result of treatment with Neomercazole. She had a brief relapse of her symptoms when treatment with thyroxin was introduced and until this treatment was withdrawn. He explained that there is no cure for the condition thyrotoxicosis, but Neomercazole keeps it under control.

35. Dr. Davis recalled the Applicant's insomnia being discussed. He prescribed Tryptanol, an antidepressant drug, on his first consultation with the Applicant because depression appeared to be her only symptom other than insomnia at that time. He considered that she needed Tryptanol rather than vitamin B12. He understood that she ceased taking Tryptanol in April 1971. Dr. Davis also said that the Applicant was taking small doses of Valium for "quite a while". He thought at that time he treated her she was actually taking the prescribed medication, but he understood from Dr. Dawson that she did not always take the medication that Dr. Dawson prescribed. He said that he prescribed Valium because he thought she was "a very nervous type of person and had probably been that way for all of her life". He said he thought she was suffering from an element of anxiety as well as thyrotoxicosis. He did not recall ever using the term 'anxiety state' in his discussions with her because she was the sort of person who was "terrified if you mentioned that she had anything wrong with her", although in talking with her husband about the Applicant's condition he thought he may have used the term 'anxiety state'.

36. Dr. Davis said that his role in the Applicant's treatment for thyrotoxicosis was that he implemented the treatment which Dr. Corlette suggested. Dr. Davis was not aware of any opinion of Dr. Corlette about the Applicant's mental state. Dr. Davis thought that the Applicant's palpitations were due to her thyroid condition. He also considered that thyrotoxicosis can cause emotional problems similar to an anxiety state including fatigue, increased anxiety, depression and sleeping difficulties. He was aware that the Applicant suffered from such symptoms, and he attributed them to her thyrotoxicosis and told her so. He considered that the Applicant would have been aware that she had suffered from depression. Dr. Davis said in relation to the Applicant's 'nervousness' that -

"Mrs. Black's whole lifestyle, as long as I knew her and probably

long before I knew her was that nervousness was accepted as

normal for her".

37. Oral evidence was also given by Dr. Peter Dawson, a general medical practitioner who had treated the Applicant from June 1978 until 1983. He recalled the Applicant's demeanour at the times of his consultations when she was agitated and got upset about minor matters. He recalled that she worked cleaning her house until midnight because "everything had to be perfect". He interpreted his reference in June 1978 "wakes 3.00 a.m. wanting to cut throat" to be depression. She refused referral to a psychiatrist.

38. Dr. Dawson said that in January 1981 he had thyroid function tests performed which were negative, because he thought she was somewhat nervous and agitated. By 19 January 1981, at the time of her next consultation, he advised her of the normal thyroid function test. Dr. Dawson interpreted his clinical notes to say that on 17 March 1981 he prescribed anti-depressant tablets. The Applicant had been to the stress clinic and was told "Incompatibility with husband is the problem". Dr. Dawson considered that in January 1981, when the Applicant was asked "are you now suffering, or have you ever suffered from depression?" she could reasonably say that she had been depressed on many occasions, but she would not be qualified to answer a question regarding the diagnosis of depression. Dr. Dawson also considered that the Applicant would have to answer "yes" to the question "are you now suffering or have you ever suffered from difficulty in sleeping"? However, he qualified his answer by saying that "everyone had to put "yes" for that". He considered that the Applicant could answer "yes" to having a nervous condition, but she may not consider it a disability. He also considered that although the Applicant suffered from 'fatigue', however, she would not know that it was 'nervous fatigue'. Dr. Dawson noted that in the period 7 November 1980 when the Applicant started work as a typist until January 1981 she had not mentioned any nervous problems to him, and therefore it was reasonable that she answered "no" to the question "are you aware of any circumstances regarding your health which will interfere with the satisfactory discharge of the duties of the position for which you are applying?"

39. In cross-examination Dr. Dawson said that in January 1981 he did not think that the Applicant was not capable of working as a typist on medical grounds. He agreed that it was only after the Applicant developed difficulties in the Department of Defence job that he was firmly of the opinion that she had an anxiety state. He also noted that the Applicant "blamed everything on her thyroid problem" (transcript p.148).

40. Dr. Hugh Veness, the Applicant's treating psychiatrist, reported to the Respondent on 11 March 1992 (T.64) and attended the hearing, having been called as a witness by the Applicant, to give oral evidence to the Tribunal. Dr. Veness first saw the Applicant in July 1984 when her husband was at that time his patient and when, because of the nature of associated family problems, Dr. Veness saw the Applicant as well as other members of the family. Hence, at that stage the Applicant was not receiving psychiatric treatment from him.

41. Dr. Veness first saw the Applicant on her own behalf on 21 November 1984 only for the purpose of making an assessment and report for the Director of Grievance and Appeals Bureau, in the office of the Public Service Board (T.27) when she was on sick leave from the Department of Defence and before her retirement on invalidity grounds. Dr. Veness reported at that time that the Applicant retired from her job in 1976 because of "pressure of work". He opined -

"She has always been a nervous, shy and retiring person, she has

an obsessive compulsive personality and was always exceptionally

clean and fastidious. Her mother was the same. She also has a

number of phobic symptoms including fears of breaking glass, of

heights, of aeroplanes and of lifts..........Mrs. Black has a severe

neurosis, characterized by obsessive compulsive symptoms and by

multiple phobias. She is a tense anxious and worried woman who, I am

sure, would rapidly break down under the stress of a full time job,

even one with diminished responsibilities...."

42. Dr. Veness told the Tribunal that the Applicant "did not take kindly" to seeing a psychiatrist on her own behalf. She did not believe that she was psychiatrically ill and she was adamant about not taking any medication, particularly psychotropic medication (transcript p.93). He agreed that although she became his 'patient', he did not think she viewed the consultations in that way.

43. In his report dated 11 March 1992, and after receiving unspecified material from the Respondent regarding the Applicant's health prior to his first seeing her, Dr. Veness wrote -

"I was aware of much of her previous history as a result of

consultations with her and other members of her family but it was

revealing to read of the interpretations that her physicians

placed upon Mrs. Black's symptoms and signs and her own

understanding of them".

In considering various records and reports of Doctors Dawson, Corlette and Shephard, Dr. Veness concluded (T.64 p.214) -

"....whilst these doctors do see her as a woman suffering from

anxiety and neurotic problems, they achieved no success in

convincing her of this".

Dr. Veness reported that the Applicant -

".... did not see herself as obsessive, dominating and verbally

aggressive but rather as energetic and "over active"....... In the

initial stages, Mrs. Black had absolutely no insight into the nature

and severity of her psychiatric problems. On 24th August 1984, I made

the following notes:

'Long session - wife beginning to accept the need to look at

herself - acknowledges she is under pressure, resentful,

fastidious.'

Mrs. Black's denial of the severity of her problem continued despite

this seeming progress........ On 21st September I had a separate

interview with Mrs. Black alone for the purposes of compiling my

report dated 21st November 1984. Whilst she acknowledged she had

always been a nervous, shy and retiring person, she was certainly not

aware that this was an illness. Nor did she realise her phobias

could be classified as a psychiatric illness. She interpreted her

obsessional nature as conscientiousness..... After studying all the

documents, those supplied by you and those I had on hand, and after

recollecting the situation with the Blacks in 1984, I come to the

definite conclusion that Mrs. Black did not know that she was

suffering from an obsessive compulsive disorder and an anxiety state

at the time she had her entry medical examination on 22nd January,

1981. Indeed, there is ample evidence both before that, and

subsequently, that one of the biggest problems her doctors had ......

had to deal with was her denial that there was anything wrong with

her. I am using the word denial in a technical sense - it means she

denied to herself that she had emotional and psychiatric problems.

It is an unconscious, not a conscious denial. For this reason she

was always very reluctant to agree to see a psychiatrist and it was

only because of extensive family difficulties and my family therapy

approach that she agreed to join her husband in therapy after he had

been first referred to me. ....... She is quite right when she

states she had no knowledge that she had a psychiatric disorder when

she filled in her medical form. Similarly, any anxiety symptoms that

she experienced prior to that date were interpreted as symptoms of

thyrotoxicosis and this belief was fortified by the fact that she was

treated for thyrotoxicosis and not for anxiety many years ago...."

Dr. Veness concluded that the Applicant probably did suffer from anxiety or an obsessive personality disorder in the period prior to 10 November 1980 and that she was likely not to have had any knowledge of the nature and the severity of the condition. He considered that the Applicant was unlikely, at 10 November 1980, to have regarded herself as suffering from "nervous fatigue". He noted that her anxiety state had been diagnosed and treated as if it were thyrotoxicosis, and she did not at that stage believe that she suffered from such a condition. Dr. Veness was also of the opinion that work related stress aggravated the Applicant's anxiety state and her obsessive compulsive neurosis. In his oral evidence he said that the Applicant's opinion that she did not have a psychiatric condition up until 1981 was a reasonably held opinion of hers (transcript p.104). Indeed, he said that at no stage has she accepted that she has suffered from a psychiatric condition as such (transcript p.110).

44. In answer to a question from the Tribunal Dr. Veness explained that in the Applicant's case she had virtually no insight into her behaviour or her functioning. He was confident in saying that her denial to which he referred was an unconscious mechanism over which she has no control. He considered that her use of denial is integral to her personality. The Tribunal finds, from Dr. Veness's evidence, particularly that at p.119, that the Applicant would have believed that she had honestly answered to the questionnaire at her medical examination in respect of those symptoms which were, objectively, psychiatric symptoms.

45. In his oral evidence Dr. Veness said that the symptoms of an overfunctioning thyroid were very similar to the symptoms of an anxiety state - including "rapid pulse, tremor, excessive sweating, intolerance to heat, loss of weight and sometimes disturbed sleep and a general feeling of being overwrought or pepped up" (transcript p.96).

46. Dr. Veness's evidence was to the effect that, given the Applicant's good response in 1972 to treatment with Neomercazole, which is specifically used for thyrotoxicosis, it was likely that this was the correct diagnosis then notwithstanding the normal thyroid function test results at that time.

47. At the hearing Dr. Veness was asked to consider the clinical notes of Dr. Davis and Dr. Dawson up to 1981, and on the basis of the information in those clinical notes and attached specialist reports whether he could make a definite psychiatric diagnosis. On that information he said he was not able to make a psychiatric diagnosis, although neither would he have been able to rule out a psychiatric disorder (transcript p.104).

48. Dr. Brian Bromhead, retired general medical practitioner, was also called by the Respondent to give evidence. He was a Commonwealth Medical Officer from May 1978 until May 1989, and was made Senior Medical Officer about 1983. He conducted the medical examination of Mrs. Black on 22 January 1981 and reported on that examination (T.14). He said that he had thyroid function tests performed as part of the medical examination, which were normal.

49. In relation to the questionnaire completed by the Applicant, Dr. Bromhead said that if she had answered "yes" to the question about 'frequent palpitations' he would have questioned her to establish their significance. In particular he would have attempted to consider whether they were related to heart disease, thyrotoxicosis or anxiety. He would also have sought a specialist opinion and information from her treating doctor. However, he agreed in cross-examination that if she told him she had palpitations in the course of her thyroid disease he did not think he would have investigated the palpitations further. In respect of questions 1(a) to 1(d) inclusive - nervous fatigue or neurasthenia, mental or nervous conditions, anxiety state, depression or difficulty in sleeping - if the Applicant had answered 'yes' to any of those questions Dr. Bromhead said he would have referred her to a psychiatrist and sought further information from her treating doctor. He explained that it was their policy that psychiatric disorders were investigated critically and the Commonwealth Medical Officers were encouraged to follow up any indication of psychiatric disorders. If he obtained a psychiatric opinion at that stage that the Applicant was suffering from or had in the past suffered from an anxiety state he would have recommended that she be issued with a benefit classification certificate for a history of anxiety state.

Reviewed last in 1978". It was then entered separately that thyroid function was normal, apparently as a result of test results being returned to Dr. Bromhead. Not unreasonably, Dr. Bromhead did not recall any details of the examination. Despite the Applicant's acknowledgment of thyrotoxicosis there is no evidence that Dr. Bromhead attempted to check with the Applicant in interview about symptoms which, on the evidence of Dr. Veness, may have accompanied that condition, for example palpitations, anxiety, rapid pulse rate.

52. On the face of it there is some ambiguity in the medical information before Dr. Bromhead which, in the Tribunal's opinion, warranted his clarification. However, from Dr. Bromhead's oral evidence, the Tribunal concludes that he did not see it to be his role to 'go behind' the Applicant's responses to particular questions, and in so doing to suggest to persons that they should have responded "yes" to a question where already s/he had written "no" (transcript p.162).

Analysis of the Evidence and the Issues.

55. The primary question for the Tribunal is whether the Applicant failed to answer properly questions asked of her, or gave false or misleading information at the medical examination on 22 January 1981 concerning whether she then had a 'history of anxiety state'".

56. The Respondent submitted that the Applicant gave incorrect answers to questions 1(a) to (d), 2(h); 9(a), 12(a), 12(b) and 13(b) when completing the Personal Medical History Form in January 1981, and that the evidence established that she should have answered 'yes' to those questions .

57. It was submitted on behalf of the Applicant that section 16AC(9), which refers to knowledge that the Applicant 'could reasonably be expected to have' and 'could reasonably be expected to regard as relevant to answering the question', requires the Tribunal to expect the Applicant to "answer the questions truthfully according to his actual knowledge and state of mind" (Re Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229 at 237 and Re Meilak at p35). In considering the provisions of sub-section 16AC(9), the Tribunal should consider what knowledge this Applicant could reasonably have been expected to possess at the time of the examination, (taking into account her particular state of mind at the time she completed the questionnaire in 1981) and then on the basis of that, the Tribunal should consider what information the Applicant should have provided. In Re Fry (at 480) it was held that the extent of the Applicant's obligation to provide information depends on the questions asked of him/her.

58. The questions asked of the Applicant which are at issue are -

"Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered from any of the

following disabilities -

1. (a) Nervous fatigue or neurasthenia

(b) Mental or nervous conditions

(c) Anxiety state

(d) Depression or difficulty in sleeping

...............

2. (h) Frequent palpitations

9. (a) Skin eruption or rash

12. (a) Have you had any operations, surgical treatment or

accidents?

(b) Have you had any complaints, illness or injury not

mentioned above?

13. (b) Are you aware of any circumstances regarding your health

which will interfere with the satisfactory discharge of

the duties of the position for which you are now

applying?

(Tribunal's emphasis).

59. It was submitted on behalf of the Applicant that she was asked to answer 'yes' or 'no' to whether she had the 'disabilities' as listed. It was submitted that the concept of 'disabilities' or 'conditions' has the connotation of a medical diagnosis having been made, and it was not merely a question of whether or not the Applicant experienced 'symptoms' as listed. The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary defines 'disability' as "want of ability; inability, incapacity ..." The question of 'disability' was considered by the Tribunal Re Meilak when Deputy President Thompson, with Members Webster and Woodard wrote -

"If the question asked about the conditions specified in Boxes 1

to 30 had been intended to elicit whether the employee was suffering

from, or had ever suffered from, any of those conditions, however

trifling or temporary it may have been, the appropriate word to have

used in the question would have been "conditions" and not

"disabilities". The word "disabilities" was presumably chosen

deliberately to denote a disabling condition of some substantial

magnitude. Certainly an employee completing the form would have been

entitled to believe that it did so".

Although this refers to a different form (one used in October 1974, predating the form used in the matter before this Tribunal) nonetheless it asked the same umbrella question about 'disabilities' as the form at T.14. With respect, we agree with the Tribunal Re Meilak (AAT 8354). We also note that the form used by the Respondent (T.14) has been criticised in previous decisions (see Re Fry; Re Watts and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD 267; Re McLean and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT 9123, 15 November 1993)).

60. We distinguish the matter before us from Re Watts - in that matter it was held that when the Applicant was in doubt it was her duty to disclose the facts about which she was in doubt. In the matter before this Tribunal we find that the Applicant was in no doubt as to the facts as she saw them, notwithstanding that many others may not have agreed with her.

61. We also distinguish the matter before us from Re Bainrot and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT 6420, 20 November 1990). In that matter it was found that the Applicant had failed to make the required disclosure about her involvement in a car accident. The Applicant failed to make the disclosure in response to a question which asked specifically whether she had ever had any accidents.

62. The Applicant in the matter before this Tribunal was also specifically asked whether she now suffered, or had ever suffered from particular disabilities. However, whether or not she did in fact suffer from those disabilities, she did not believe that she did. In Re Bainrot the Applicant would have known that she had been involved in a car accident. Although it might have been debatable as to whether or not it was knowledge she could reasonably have been expected to regard as relevant to answering the question, there was no doubt, as there is here, as to whether or not it was knowledge she could reasonably be expected to possess.

63. In Re Rohde and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 18 ALD 791 the Tribunal, relying on a decision of the Full Federal Court Re McMullen v Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 61ALR 189, that an 'anxiety state' need not necessarily be a medical condition, said -

"We would agree that the words "physical or mental condition" in

the Act need not necessarily be a condition which requires medical

treatment, however, the word "condition" in connection with the word

"mental" must mean something more than feelings which commonly occur

to all people at some time in their lives, those feelings being

encompassed by words such as unhappy, depressed, stressed, uptight,

tense, sad, in conjunction with temporary common effects of

sleeplessness, lethargy and gastric or gastroenteric disturbances.

There must be some dividing line, albeit on occasions blurred, when

emotions, feelings and symptoms are such that by virtue of their

chronicity, severity or longevity are beyond average of (sic)

ordinary experience and such that they can properly be regarded as a

mental condition. As the Tribunal in Re RM and Commissioner for

Superannuation (1984) 6 ALD 272 at 294 said: "it is a matter of

common experience that personality characteristics vary considerably

between individuals. In one sense, it is clearly correct, as Dr.

Merrifield says, that paranoid personality is one of the normal

variants of personality. Many people probably display such

characteristics at some stage of their lives without being

characterised either as mentally ill or as suffering from a 'mental

condition'. But it appears to us, having regard to Dr. Merrifield's

evidence and to the publication to which we have referred, that it is

ultimately a question of degree".

.......

Looking at the words "physical or mental condition" in the context of

the Act and the role of a BCC, and addressing ourselves particularly

to mental conditions as diagnosed on examination, we consider that

the meaning of "anxiety state" should be that espoused by the

specialty of psychiatry, which is a medical model also used by

general medical practitioners, although probably applied by them in a

less refined way than psychiatrists".

64. It was submitted for the Applicant, relying on Re Rohde, that a past experience of upset mental state did not call for a 'yes' response if it was a condition which would appear part of the Applicant's normal personality and lacking a psychiatric or morbid quality. It was submitted that a 'yes' response to questions 1(a) to (d) would reasonably be read as enquiring as to the suffering of a disabling mental illness based on a medical diagnosis.

65. It was submitted for the Applicant that prior to 1970 she had not experienced any complaints which might have been diagnosed as mental illness. She was then 40 years old, and she saw herself as a shy, tense person, who was susceptible to stress. This self-image, it was submitted, should not require a self-diagnosis of mental illness. Rather, it would reasonably lead her to attribute stress-reactions to her personality and external causes. Prior to 1981 no doctor had reached a diagnosis of a mental illness which would appropriately be labelled as a 'disability' falling within the descriptions in questions 1(a) to (d). Dr. Dawson agreed that in March 1979 his suspicions of a mental illness were conditional on obtaining a psychiatric opinion (transcript p.145) and, at least prior to January 1981, he deferred to the contrary opinion of Dr. Corlette (transcript p.147). Doctors Davis and Corlette had reached a firm diagnosis of thyrotoxicosis, without psychiatric disorder.

66. On the basis of evidence from the Applicant and Dr. Davis the Tribunal finds that although the Applicant was receiving treatment with psychotropic drugs from Dr. Davis she was not aware that her symptoms or treatment related to an anxiety state, and no such diagnosis had been made by January 1981. The Tribunal finds it reasonable that the Applicant attributed her symptoms of palpitations, fatigue, anxiety, depression and insomnia to thyrotoxicosis and/or menopause.

67. The Tribunal finds, on the balance of probabilities, that the Applicant did not see question 2(h) regarding 'frequent palpitations' as one requiring an answer separate from her acknowledgment of having suffered thyroid disease when she understood that palpitations arose out of her thyrotoxicosis, and that this was a view that was reasonable for the Applicant to hold. It was an understanding confirmed by Dr. Davis (transcript p.132). The Respondent's submissions in respect of palpitations fail to consider these issues, and treat 'palpitations' as if it was a disease entity. In this Applicant's case the disease entity was thyrotoxicosis.

68. If we are wrong on that issue, and the Applicant failed to answer properly question 2(h), then, in applying subsection 16AC(2)(b) of the Act, we consider, on the evidence of Dr. Bromhead, that if the Applicant had advised of her history of palpitations, which at one time had been 'frequent palpitations', on the balance of probabilities we find that it is not likely that this would have been investigated further and that the Applicant is most unlikely to have been referred to a psychiatrist on that issue, and as a consequence, to have been diagnosed as having a history of anxiety state. The Tribunal considers that in January 1981, the Applicant's history of palpitations would most likely have been considered to be part of her thyrotoxicosis, and, therefore, the CMO and the Commissioner would have been unlikely to form the opinion, under section 16(4), that there was a "real risk" that the Applicant would be forced to retire before she reached the age of 65 because of an anxiety state or some connected condition.

69. In respect of the Applicant's response to the questions about skin eruption or rash, and surgical treatment, while the Tribunal finds that the Applicant's answers to these questions should have been 'yes', a response in those terms in January 1981 would not have led to a diagnosis of her stress-related skin disorder which did not occur until the time when she ceased employment in September 1982. The non-disclosure of her basal cell carcinoma for which she had surgical treatment, in the Tribunal's view, did not go to whether she had a history of anxiety state.

70. In considering the facts and the case law the Tribunal is of the view that the Applicant, with her knowledge and personality characteristics, and with her reliance on the opinion of Dr. Corlette in the presence of a conflict of medical opinion on the issue of her psychiatric health, could not reasonably be expected to have answered 'yes' to questions 1(a) to (d). The test of what she could reasonably be expected to have known is not a completely objective test, but one anchored in the Applicant's own perceptions at the time she completed the questionnaire. The Tribunal must consider what is 'reasonable' for this applicant to have answered.

71. We reject the submission of the Respondent that, on the basis of Dr Dawson's tentative diagnosis and his attempt to have her hospitalised and treated, the Applicant should have disclosed that she had been diagnosed as suffering from a nervous condition. When Dr. Dawson was in conflict with Dr. Corlette, and when she trusted Dr. Corlette, she simply believed Dr. Dawson was wrong and frequently told him so. Since she did not accept or agree with that diagnosis, of course she would not acknowledge it as indicative of her actually suffering from the 'disability' 'anxiety state' or 'nervous or mental condition'.

72. On the evidence before the Tribunal we find that the Applicant ceased work in 1976 largely because of what she saw to be physical exhaustion and symptoms which she associated with her thyrotoxicosis. These symptoms included ones of a psychological nature, including anxiety, which the Applicant at no time accepted were of psychological origin. Since the time of the diagnosis of thyrotoxicosis we find that the Applicant has attributed her psychological symptoms to that condition, and moreover she has not, at least until after commencing psychiatric treatment with Dr. Veness in 1984, acknowledged that she has had any psychological or psychiatric condition. Even now, the Tribunal doubts that the Applicant has much insight into her behaviour and its origins.

73. The Tribunal interprets Dr. Veness's evidence as indicating that if the information available in the clinical notes of the Applicant's treating doctors had been referred to Dr. Veness in 1981 there would have been sufficient information to warrant referral for psychiatric consultation and assessment, but on the clinical notes alone he could not have diagnosed a psychiatric illness then.

74. We find that the Applicant from time to time before 1981 has been suffering from thyrotoxicosis and anxiety state, either separately or concurrently. However, even when some of her doctors have been of the opinion that she was suffering from an anxiety condition, she rejected this possibility on the basis of her treating physician, Dr. Corlette, reinforcing with her that she was not suffering from a psychiatric illness. We find, on the evidence of Dr. Veness, that the Applicant has used the psychological defence mechanism of denial in respect of all aspects of her psychological symptoms, and that this is an unconscious mechanism over which she has no control. The Tribunal considers that the Respondent's submissions lack an understanding of the evidence about unconscious defence mechanisms. We find that her denial of suffering from disabilities (including nervous fatigue, mental or nervous conditions, anxiety state, or depression) was not a conscious process, and that this denial mechanism does not, therefore, interfere with our acceptance of the applicant as a credible witness.

75. The Respondent submitted that "the Applicant did not present in her evidence in all respects as a person who was genuinely seeking to recollect the truth". The Respondent referred to the Applicant's evidence as "lacking any internal coherence or inherent credibility". The Tribunal agrees that much of the Applicant's evidence appeared to be an attempt to justify her position. She was defensive and much of her evidence lacked coherence. However in our view, these manifestations are not, in this case, indicative of a lack of credibility. Rather, the Applicant is a person with significant personality limitations and the manner in which she gave her evidence over several hours is consistent with the overall assessment made of her by Dr. Veness. We do not accept that her demeanour in giving evidence was indicative of a deliberate attempt to misrepresent herself to the Tribunal.

76. While the Tribunal accepts that the Applicant's anxiety state contributed substantially to her incapacity for work, we conclude that the Applicant did not fail to answer properly questions asked of her, and that she did not give false or misleading information at the medical examination on 22 January 1981 concerning whether she then had a 'history of anxiety state'.

77. As it appears, on one analysis, that the Respondent was in a very disadvantaged position, we have asked ourselves whether there was any way in which the Applicant's psychological symptoms could have been disclosed and investigated at the medical examination. As noted already, the Medical Report Form draws criticism and in itself has disadvantaged the Respondent. Beyond this, on the evidence of Dr. Bromhead. there is no apparent assistance or guidance given to the Applicant in completing or discussing the form or the answers to questions. On the disclosure of information about the history of thyrotoxicosis Dr. Bromhead may reasonably have perceived the possible inconsistency between this and the negative answers to at least some of the questions 1(a) to (d). It is not unreasonable that the examining CMO might bring his/her own professional knowledge about physical and mental disorders to bear in relation to the medical history provided by the person through the Medical Report Form. After all, the CMO is the expert, and many persons whom CMOs examine, including this Applicant, have little specific knowledge or understanding and indeed much confusion of diseases and symptoms.

78. The decision under review is set aside, and the matter is remitted to the Respondent with the directions that -

(i) The original Benefit Classification Certificate specifying

the condition "Underweight" only is correct.

(ii) The said original Benefit Classification Certificate is not to

be revoked and no new certificate is to be issued; and

(iii) Pursuant to section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976, the

Commissioner is not to be of the opinion that the incapacity which

was the ground of the Applicant's retirement was caused, or

substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or

conditions specified in the said original Benefit Classification

Certificate issued pursuant to section 16(4) or by a physical or

mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or

such conditions.

79. At the conclusion of the oral evidence of Dr. Veness Counsel for the Applicant sought costs pursuant to section 67(3) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 in respect of the expenses of calling Dr. Veness as a witness. At the request of the Applicant the Tribunal had issued a summons for Dr. Veness to attend the hearing to give evidence. In support of the application in respect of costs the Applicant submitted that Dr. Veness was the only psychiatrist whose opinions were sought by the Respondent at the time the delegate made the reconsideration decision. Neither party in the review before this Tribunal sought to qualify and lead any other psychiatric evidence, and it was alleged on behalf of the Applicant that the Respondent intended to call Dr. Veness if the Applicant did not do so. On the basis of both parties apparently agreeing that Dr. Veness was an essential witness, it was considered by the Applicant to be preferable for the Applicant to call Dr. Veness as he was her treating psychiatrist and had previously given a written report in her favour (T.64). It was submitted for the Applicant that she has not received any financial assistance in bringing her case to the Tribunal for review. It was submitted, but no evidence was provided to this effect, that the Applicant and her husband live on a DFRDB and superannuation pension of $39,400 per annum after tax, from which they meet a mortgage repayment of $14,170 per annum. They apparently have savings of approximately $6,000.

80. It was submitted on behalf of the Applicant that given these submissions and the nature of the issues to be decided, the Tribunal would not adequately have exercised its function of review if it did not give consideration to the evidence of Dr. Veness (Re Sullivan and Secretary, Department of Transport (No.3) (1979) 2 ALD 502).

81. While written submissions were made to the Tribunal on behalf of the Applicant at the end of the hearing, which included submissions in respect of costs, and these were served on the Respondent, the Respondent made no submissions in reply in respect of this issue. The Tribunal assumes, therefore, that the Respondent does not oppose the order sought by the Applicant.

82. In the decision of the Tribunal of Davies J (then Deputy President) with Howell and Thompson (Members) in Re Sullivan it was stated -

"Plainly, should an applicant desire that a witness be summoned

and that his fees and allowances be paid by the Commonwealth on

this basis, it is desirable that, at the time the Tribunal or the

Registrar is asked to summon the witness, it is brought to notice

that an application will be made for such payment..."

It is unfortunate that the Applicant did not proceed in this way, and did not approach the Tribunal until after the evidence was given. However, as was the case in the Tribunal's Order for the costs of Dr. Evans to be paid by the Commonwealth in Re Sullivan, the oral evidence of Dr. Veness in this matter, likewise, was essential to the Tribunal in making its decision. In Re Sullivan at 507 the Tribunal annunciated the general principal in the jurisdiction that "the ordinary rule that the fees and allowances of a witness summoned at the request of a party other than the decision-maker should be paid by that party .... applies". In that matter the Tribunal also said, at 506 -

"a witness may be called to prove a matter which, during the

hearing, the decision-maker should have admitted ....... the

Tribunal's discretion should not be exercised arbitrarily .....

if an order is to be made for the payment of witnesses' fees and

expenses by the Commonwealth of Australia, there must be a sound

reason for it".

83. In Re Ricci and Director-General of Social Services (1982) 4 ALN 44 the Tribunal made a distinction between persons who were not able to pay his/her witness's fees, and persons who were unwilling to do so. In that case it was established that the Applicant was unwilling to pay even though he had "a substantial part of a lump sum compensation award that had been paid to him". An Order under subsection 67(3) was refused.

84. In considering principles outlined in Re Sullivan and the decision of the Tribunal Re Ricci we consider that the Applicant's financial position is a relevant consideration. If we accept the submissions made about the particular financial circumstances of the Applicant we find that the weekly income of the Applicant and her spouse after tax is $757, and that they pay $272 per week in mortgage repayments for their house. The Tribunal is of the view that the housing loan they have made to their son is a philanthropic gesture on their part, but it is not something which the Tribunal can take into account in considering their financial position. Even without the loan to their son and their $6000 savings, the Tribunal considers that the disposable weekly income of the Applicant and her spouse is such that there are no grounds of financial hardship for making the Order.

85. The making of an Order pursuant to subsection 67(3) is discretionary. Having considered the decisions of the Tribunal that are relevant to this subsection, and the particular facts in this case, the Tribunal decides that in spite of the importance of the evidence of Dr. Veness to the Tribunal in making its decision the financial circumstances of the Applicant are not such as to warrant the Tribunal exercising its discretion. To do so would, in our view, undermine the general principal that each party is to be responsible for the payment of its own costs. It is unfortunate that the Applicant, in acknowledging the Tribunal's decision Re Sullivan, did not see fit to raise the issue with the Tribunal at the time the summons on Dr. Veness was issued. That would have given the Tribunal an opportunity to consider the issue before the Applicant incurred the costs, and it would have given the parties an opportunity to negotiate informally, given the special nature of the evidence of Dr. Veness, in case the Respondent may have agreed to call Dr. Veness as its witness, or at least to agree to meet his costs. However, that is an exercise which in no way can be influenced by the Tribunal.
