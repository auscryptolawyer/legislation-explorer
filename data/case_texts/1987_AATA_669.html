NT85/16183-6 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 669; (1987) 18 ATR 3637; 87 ATC 749 (10 June 1987)

Last Updated: 21 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - allowable deductions s51(1) - interest paid on various loans used to re-finance a previous loan being the principal in a 'borrowing agreement' - borrower's default - applicant not in the business of money lender - objective purpose of re-financing; Ure, Magna Alloys - character of expenditure; Lunney.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1939 s. 51(1)

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; 38 CLR 153

Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100

Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation SO ATC 4542

REASONS FOR DECISION

10 June 1987 Mr B.J. McMahon (Senior Member)

  1. This is a review of a decision by the respondent to disallow objections to assessments for income tax in four fiscal years. The applicant had claimed as deductions one half of amounts of interest paid jointly by herself and her husband in each of those years in the following circumstances.
  2. The applicant's local bank manager introduced her to a borrower who was then in desperate need of $30,000. The applicant and her husband jointly agreed to advance that sum to the borrower on condition that $60,000 was repaid 4 months from the date of the loan. This, of course, represents a simple interest return at the rate of 300% per annum. As security for repayment of the loan, the borrower gave a Bill of Sale over certain plant and equipment and signed two collateral promissory notes for $30,000 each. The loan was made on 19 April 1974 and was to be repaid on 19 August in the same year.
  3. The principal sum advanced was made up as to $2,000 from the funds of the applicant and her husband, and as to $28,000 from funds which they borrowed from a finance company. General Credits Limited, on the security of a second mortgage over their home. Interest was payable under the second mortgage at the rate of 15% per annum. The mortgage was to be repaid in 1 year.
  4. The term of 4 months expired but the borrower did not repay the loan, nor did she pay any part of the agreed amount of interest. In fact, it was established that she did not even own the plant and equipment, the subject of the Bill of Sale and that the representations she made in order to get the loan were false. After complaints were made to the police, the borrower was subsequently convicted and imprisoned. Judgement was entered up against her in the Supreme Court of New South Wales for $60,000 plus costs on or about 1 December 1975. Interest continues to run on that judgement at the rate of 10% per annum. The borrower has not paid any part of the judgement sum or any part of the interest on that amount.
  5. In April 1975 it became necessary to repay the loan from General Credits Limited. The applicant and her husband approached the Commercial Bank of Australia Limited (which then held the first mortgage on their home) to provide the necessary funds. It was arranged that the existing first mortgage of $10,458 should be repaid by the applicant and her husband from their own funds. A fresh borrowing of $28,700 was then made. By written direction to the bank, that sum was applied in repayment of the loan from General Credits Limited.
  6. Having regard to the consequences of the initial introduction of the borrower by the bank, it was not surprising that relations between the applicant and her husband on the one hand and the bank on the other hand, deteriorated. By March 1976 the applicant had decided to change banks. Arrangements were made with CBC Savings Bank Limited whereby funds were advanced in order to pay out the monies owing to Commercial Bank of Australia Limited. On or about 19 March 1976 the applicant and her husband borrowed $28,000 from CBC Savings Bank Limited on the security of a first mortgage over their home, and directed the bank to pay the proceeds to Commercial Bank of Australia Limited in discharge of their obligation to that bank.
  7. Some four and one half years later in November 1980, the applicant and her husband decided to carry out extensions to their house. To finance these works and to pay out the obligation to CBC Savings Bank Limited, arrangements were made with National Australia Bank (which had merged with the CBC Bank group) to borrow the sum of $50,000. Repayment of that amount was secured by a first mortgage over the home and the sum raised was used partly to pay out the loan due to CBC Savings Bank Limited and partly to finance the extensions.
  8. In 1985 the borrower became a bankrupt. It was by then clear that there was no possibility of recovering any sum on account of principal or interest from the original transaction.
  9. There was no issue that interest paid to General Credits Limited on the original advance of $28,000 would have been deductible as the sum borrowed was outlaid for the purpose of producing income, even though in fact no income resulted. The amounts of interest in question in the present application are those amounts paid by the applicant to the three hanks.

10. The applicant referred to the three hank loans as re-financing arrangements. Her argument was that if the original loan from General Credits Limited had been for a term of 10 years, there would have been no question that any interest the applicant continued to pay would have been deductible. As it was only for a 1 year term, it was argued that the re-financing arrangements should be treated no differently from the original loan. Apart from the additional sum borrowed for home extensions, it was said that there was no purpose in the re-financing except a purpose referable to the original loan. Interest was claimed only in respect of that part of the original principal that would have still been outstanding. No part of the interest referable to the home extensions was claimed. No authority was cited to support this approach as a valid framework within which to analyse the deductibility of the amounts in question. It was simply asserted as a matter of common sense that the subsequent borrowings should be impressed with the same character as the initial borrowing from the finance company as they all derived from the one transaction which was intended to be of an income productive nature.

11. The applicant's claims are made under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act which is in the following terms:

"51(1) [Deductions for losses and outgoings] All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. It was agreed that the applicant did not carry on the business of a money lender. This was the only occasion on which monies had been advanced in such a fashion. It was further agreed that the loss of capital and expectations of interest would not be deductible as the applicant could not support a claim under the second limb of the above section. The question then to be decided is whether the amounts paid by way of interest to the three banks were outgoings which were incurred in gaining assessable income, or whether they were outgoings of a capital, private or domestic nature.
  2. The issue will be resolved by establishing the objective purpose of the expenditure. Generally speaking, if interest is to be deductible under the first limb of section 51(1), the taxpayer must show that the interest services a borrowing undertaken for the purpose of making an outlay which is for the purpose of deriving assessable income and that that money is in fact so outlaid (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; 38 CLR 153).The applicant must therefore show whether the function of the three borrowings which were serviced by her payments of interest were to support the process of income derivation.

14. In her mind the three bank loans were mere substitutes for an original loan entered into for this purpose. No doubt from the point of view of personal finances, that would be a satisfactory way of considering the transactions. One can understand that she may have thought: "If it had not been for the original loan from the finance company which we used for productive purposes, we would not owe this amount to our present bank. I am only paying interest to the bank instead of to the original finance company. In my mind they are all the same." However, when applying section 51, the purpose of the borrowings is not to be established by reference only to the subjective intention of the borrower. It is to be established objectively. As was said by Brennan J. in Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100 at 4104:

"Section 51 requires that a deductible expenditure be incurred "in" gaining assessable income, that is to say, it must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that income (Ronpibon Tin N.L. & Tongkah Compound N.L. v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p56). An outgoing of interest may be incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income where the borrowed money is laid out for the purpose of gaining that income (F.C. of T. v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 C.L.R. 153 at pp. 170, 171, 197; Texas Co. (Australasia) Ltd. v F.C. of T. [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 C.L.R. 382 at p.468). The laying out of the borrowed money for the purpose of gaining assessable income furnishes the required connection between the interest paid upon it by the taxpayer and the income derived by him from its use."

15. In Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4542 the same Judge at page 4550 had referred to the way in which the "required connection" was to be established. He said:

"However, the evidentiary effect of a taxpayer's state of mind is to be distinguished from the objective purpose of expenditure incurred."

16. In Ure (supra) he reverted to this test saying:

"The purposes for which money is laid out is an issue of fact, turning upon the objective circumstances which human experience would judge to be relevant to the issue."

  1. In order to find a purpose which will connect the payment of interest to the three banks with the process of income derivation, we must therefore look at the objective facts. In my view, they clearly establish that the purpose of borrowing from each of the three banks was in order to discharge a capital obligation to the previous lender. The very fact that a written direction was given to a lending bank to apply the proceeds to discharge a liability to a preceeding bank, is sufficient corroboration of that objective purpose. However the applicant visualised each loan in her own mind, there is no doubt that the proceeds were not laid out for income derivation purposes. They were, at her express written direction, applied in discharge of a capital obligation.
  2. One can also have regard to "the essential character of the expenditure itself" (Lunney v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 100 CLR 478 at 497). To use Brennan J's words, "human experience" would judge capital borrowings from Bank B in order to pay out Bank A where the monies are not subsequently laid out for an income producing purpose to be simply that, namely capital sums. The interest payments in question would therefore bear the essential character of capital payments or even, as the amounts involved were secured by mortgages on the applicant's house, of private or domestic payments. In either event their character would be such as not to qualify under the first limb of section 51 or be excluded by the exceptions contained in that section.
  3. It is unreal to regard these borrowings as substitute borrowings for one that was initially laid out for a relevant purpose. The character of the payments cannot be supported by such an interpretation. The last loan (diluted in purpose as it was by an increased amount for home extensions) was made some six and one half years after the original finance company loan. During that time the proceeds of the original finance company loan had been outlaid and lost. No income was produced or could have been produced directly and proximately from the proceeds of any of the bank loans upon which the applicant relies. If the applicant's view of the situation were correct, there would be no end to her entitlement. If, for example, the present bank loan were increased or paid out and replaced by a larger loan in order to finance further improvements, the applicant, in logic, would have to claim that her entitlement to deduct at least part of the interest continued. This absurd result illustrates the impossibility of regarding the present borrowing as anything other than what it is. It is not the original loan from the finance company with a change of name and terms. It is a new bank loan made for different purposes. Whilst it cannot be denied that the sums paid for interest were to service the three borrowings, there can be no doubt in my mind that the borrowings were not laid out for the purposes of producing income and that therefore the interest payments cannot be regarded as falling within the terms of section 51.
  4. Accordingly the objection decisions under review are affirmed.
