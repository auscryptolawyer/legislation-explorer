Trimnell and Commissioner for Superannuation [1992] AATA 762 (28 August 1992)

Last Updated: 1 August 2008





ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - discretion in s.7(2) to deem person retired on ground of physical or mental incapacity when person ceased to be an eligible employee, otherwise than by reason of death or on the ground of physical or mental incapacity - precondition that person unfit to perform duties by reason of physical or mental incapacity at that time - what were "his duties" - circumstances in which discretion may be exercised - decision under review set aside.



Superannuation Act 1976 ss.7(2), s.7(1) & (2)

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.37

The Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act No. 130 of 1991 s.6(2), s.7(2)



Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings 6 AAR 152

Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation 8 AAR 20

Re De Vries and Commissioner for Superannuation 12 AAR 380

Re Elliot and Commissioner for Superannuation Decision No. 5642 (unreported)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. V91/413

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: THOMAS JONES TRIMNELL



Applicant



And: COMMISSION FOR

SUPERANNUATION



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



Deputy President B.M. Forrest

Mr G. Woodard, Member

Dr A. Shanahan, Member



This is an application by Thomas Jones Trimnell for review of a decision made on 21 December 1990 by a delegate of the respondent Commissioner to refuse to direct under sub-s. 7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 'the Act' that the applicant should for the purposes of the Act be deemed to have retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.



The material lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 "the T documents" included additional information obtained from the applicant's personnel file from the Department of Social Security "the department". Oral evidence was given by the applicant, his wife, Mr Guy, Regional Manager of the department at Hamilton, Mr Maloney, formerly a deputy regional manager of the department, Dr Bekhor, a dermatologist and Drs Stephens, Kornan and Taylor, psychiatrists.



The applicant who is 58 years of age, was born on 23 August 1935. He became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 14 July 1959 when he commenced work as a clerk in the department at Hamilton. In the late 1960's he became a field officer, responsible for investigating the circumstances and eligibility of claimants for benefits. Until regional offices were established in Horsham and Warrnambool in the mid 1970's, and field officers appointed at those offices some 1-2 years later, the applicant was the sole field officer of the department in the Western and Wimmera regions of Victoria. He found that his workload increased significantly in 1974 because of changed requirements in investigating claimants and remained high notwithstanding the opening of the Horsham and Warrnambool offices and the appointment of an additional field officer at Hamilton in 1976. About that time the applicant had a significant personality clash with Mr Cannon (Manager of the Hamilton office from 1974 to 1978) which caused the applicant a good deal of distress. His work "took a tumble" which he attributed to a combination of increased workload, the demands of Mr Cannon and domestic upheaval, having separated from his wife. He felt inadequate and became frustrated in trying to keep up his workload in what he perceived to be a lack of support from management. His work performance declined dramatically. He knew he was not coping. Some days he sat in his car, unable to face interviews he was required to conduct.



Mr Cannon made a report to the Inspector of Personnel in May 1978 to the effect that the applicant's work performance and attitude to his work and colleagues left a lot to be desired and recommending that the applicant be counselled. The applicant attended a meeting with senior personnel and was shown Mr Cannon's letter. He agreed that his work performance was waning and incidents did occur in the workplace which transferred over to his personal life. Mr Cannon left Hamilton in November 1978 and his departure eased the applicant's situation to some extent. In 1979 the applicant took the advice of a work colleague and sought medical advice. He attended the Hamilton Medical Group practice and was placed on anti depressant medication. He spent from February 1979 to May 1980 on almost continuous sick leave. He was then a Field officer Grade 3 responsible for the efficient functioning of the Field Officer sub-section including arranging the workload of the other field officers.



A Commonwealth medical officer, Dr Rowland who examined the applicant in July 1979 to determine his fitness for continued duty considered that he was unfit for continued employment and recommended invalidity retirement. In his opinion, 'depression' was the major factor in the applicant's incapacity for work. A senior medical officer, Dr Hackett disagreed with Dr Rowland's recommendation and the applicant was referred to consultant psychiatrist Dr Freed who examined the applicant on 24 March 1980. He wrote:



"the abovenamed appears to have been suffering from a personality disorder involving heightened perceptions of interpersonal hurt with quite marked and well documented interpersonal difficulties. Accordingly, resumption of previous job responsibilities would not appear feasible. Alternate employment in the Public Service involving re-deployment could be contemplated provided the abovenamed was prepared to accept psychiatric treatment and provided recommendations then could be obtained from the treating psychiatrist regarding possible effects upon his health of continued employment in any sort of work capacity with the Public Service.



In the interim retirement from his position with superannuation may need to be supported."



On the basis of Dr Freed's report Dr Hackett reported (28 March 1980):



"We now have attached a report on this man from Dr Freed, a consultant psychiatrist. His recommendations are consistent with our impression, that there is a possibility of this man being found suitable alternative employment. This would need to be with significantly reduced responsibility and probably reduced status.



This recommendation may well be made difficult to implement if he continues to live in Hamilton and considering the time he has already been away from work. However, neither of these factors are relevant to his invalidity which I do not think at this time is sufficient to justify retirement."



Within the department discussions about the applicant's future were apparently in train in late 1979. Mr Lodge (Regional Manager) expressed the view in October 1979 that the applicant:



"is incapable of performing the duties of any Clerical Assistant position in this office efficiently."



On 14 May 1980 the applicant at the direction of the Personnel officer returned to work in the Hamilton office on duties which equated with that of a Clerical Assistant Grade 3 in the unemployment and sickness benefit section. These duties, normally done by junior staff, were basic clerical duties in checking statement of earnings forms to determine ongoing eligibility for unemployment and sickness benefits. These duties were arranged following the recommendation of the senior medical officer and Dr Freed for less stressful employment. The applicant was paid at his substantive salary level, that of a Clerical Assistant Grade 6 the appropriate field officer classification.



The applicant gave further evidence that he was concerned about his health and that he had difficulty adjusting to this environment but coped reasonably well for the first few weeks during which his work performance was satisfactory. He acknowledged the work was not difficult yet felt he was not coping and requested permission to take eight weeks accumulated recreation leave. He was allowed three weeks leave. On 25 September, Dr McVicker of the Hamilton Medical Group who the applicant had consulted for treatment of a skin complaint on his face and back provided a referral to a dermatologist, Dr Stahle. The applicant saw a locum of Dr Stahle, Dr Fergin. On review a week later Dr Bekhor also a locum of Dr Stahle reported "his rash has settled fairly well". It was the applicant's evidence that Dr Bekhor told him his skin complaint was work related, a remark which prompted his resignation. Next day (9 October 1980) the applicant told Mr Maloney (Acting Manager) that he had decided to resign as he could not see any future for himself in the department. According to department records (T42/119), the applicant had been absent on sick leave from 22 September to 2 October for 'nerves and stress disorder' and from 3 October to 9 October for 'nerves, anxiety depression, skin disorder' although the applicant recalls only two days off in that period. In his letter of resignation the applicant wrote:



"After due consideration as regards my health, I hereby tender my resignation from the Department of Social Security as a Field Officer Grade 3.



I was put off work in February 1979 on the advice of Doctors and had to return to work in May 1980. As my health has again failed requiring further medical treatment I feel I have no alternative but to resign, as the Department has shown no interest in considering me for Superannuation."



The resignation was accepted. By reason of his resignation the applicant received a refund of his contributions.



After resigning the applicant did various part-time casual work, as a gardener 2-3 mornings a week for 7 months, a painter for about a year, a drive-in theatre attendant one evening per week for two years, and a courier, three hours per day from 1982 to 1988.



The applicant's solicitors in a letter dated 12 March 1986 made application to the respondent pursuant to s.7(2) of the Act that the applicant be deemed to have been retired on the ground of physical and mental incapacity to perform his duties.



Section 7(2) of the Act has been amended by the substitution of 'Superannuation Board' for 'Commissioner' and the addition of the words "invalidity because of" after "on the ground of" and further amended by s.6 of The Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act No. 130 of 1991 by omitting the words "unfit to perform his duties" and substituting "totally and permanently incapacitated within the meaning of Part IVA". The Amending Act further provides (s.6(2)) that s.7(2) of the Act as in force prior to commencement of the Amending Act (2 September 1991) continues to apply in respect of a person who ceased to be an eligible employee before commencement.



Section 7(1) and (2) of the Act prior to amendment and relevantly for present purposes reads:



"7(1) A reference in this Act to the retirement of an eligible employee on the ground of invalidity shall be read as a reference to his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.



7(2) Where a person has, before attaining his maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the Commissioner is satisfied that, at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner may direct that the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have been retired on the ground of that physical or mental incapacity."



In considering whether or not the applicant ought to be deemed to have retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties the primary issue in this application is whether the expression "his duties" referred to his work as a field officer or that of a clerical assistant, at the date he resigned.



The Full Federal Court, in Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings 6 AAR 152 and differently constituted, in Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation 8 AAR 20 has considered the proper construction of s.7. In Hastings, the Court held that the phrase "his duties" referred only to the duties of the employee in his employment, in that case as a senior health inspector, at the time his services were terminated and did not encompass the full range of duties to which the employee might have been transferred or redeployed. In that case it was common ground that when he ceased to be an eligible employee, on the date his service was terminated, the employee was not physically able to perform the duties of a senior health inspector.



In rejecting the submission of the Commissioner that "his duties" referred not only to the duties of a senior health inspector, but the full range of available duties which the employee could reasonably have been required to perform through redeployment or transfer so that an employee can be said to be incapacitated only in a case where no suitable work can be found, the Court in Hastings said at p.156:



"Counsel for the respondent argues that the term "his duties" ought to be construed literally, to refer to the duties of the position held by the person at the relevant date, that is, retirement. There is no warrant, he says, for any extended meaning and, particularly, for one which would require the Commissioner to investigate the duties of some alternative position which might have been, but was not, allocated to the employee by the employer. We agree. Had it been the intention of Parliament to have the Commissioner embark upon such an inquiry, this intention could easily have been stated. Normally the words "his duties", used in reference to an employee, would be understood as referring to the duties of the position which that employee held at the relevant time. We see no warrant for interpreting them in s.7 in any other way."



In Re De Vries and Commissioner for Superannuation 12 AAR 380 the applicant, a meat inspector, had been placed on light duties following a laminectomy and continuing back problems. The Tribunal found that he was not precluded from being held to be unfit to perform his duties where he could perform only a portion of the clerical duties to which he was redeployed from his position as a meat inspector. In De Vries, it was accepted that in the period immediately before he ceased work "his duties" were that of a clerical assistant; the issue there being the applicant's capacity to perform those duties not, as in the present case, what were his duties.



In Re Elliot and Commissioner for Superannuation Decision No. 5642 (unreported) the Tribunal discussed the meaning of "his duties" in the context of an applicant who was able to perform the duties of her office as had been modified for her. The Tribunal said in its reasons for decision para. 54:



"Thus if the employee, by reason of medically advised restrictions, is allowed to perform a modified version of the duties in the duty statement, it is this modified version that is intended by the phrase "his duties". The employee must demonstrate an inability to carry out the modified duties before the prerequisite to the exercise of discretion has been established."



Again in Re Elliott the question of what were her duties at the date of the applicant's deemed resignation was not the central issue. At that time, she was a clerical assistant of the same pay rate as her previous telephonist grade and able to perform the duties as modified for her. The Tribunal found in those circumstances that the applicant was not unfit to perform her duties for the purpose of s.7(2) unless incapable of performing all the duties she was required to perform at the time of her deemed resignation.



Counsel for the applicant, Mr McInnis submitted that "his duties" should refer to the duties of a clerical assistant grade 6 which was the applicant's level as a field officer or a modified version of that position. Further the department did not reclassify the applicant or offer him alternate duties; it was simply an informal arrangement whereby he was temporarily engaged in an unofficial capacity as a clerical assistant grade 3.

As an alternative argument Mr McInnis submitted that the evidence established that the applicant was unable to cope with the lesser position of clerical assistant grade 3.



Mr Whithear for the Commissioner contended that at the time the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee, he was not by reason of physical or mental capacity unfit to perform "his duties" which he submitted were those of a clerical assistant grade 3.



We accept that the work as a field officer was entirely different to that of a clerical officer grade 3. It could not be said that the position of clerical officer was a modification of a field officer position. The nature of the work and level of responsibilities appropriate to a clerical officer grade 3 were quite different - as was made clear from the evidence of the applicant, Mr Guy and Mr Maloney. The clerical officer grade 3 position was as Mr Maloney described it "a very basic clerical function".



The applicant had not been redeployed. He returned to work in May 1980 without any prior consultation with him about the nature of his duties, other than a discussion with the Manager on the morning he returned to work. He was, as Mr Maloney said in evidence, filling in for someone else on long service leave on a temporary basis. According to My Guy, there was no vacancy for a clerical officer position at Hamilton.



That the applicant performed duties equal to those of a clerical assistant grade 3 when he returned to work in May 1980 is not in issue. Clearly he did. Whether that was the position he held at the date he resigned is another issue.



Intra-department correspondence revealed uncertainty about the applicant's position. Clarification was sought as to his future position whether he be redeployed, and whether vacancies existed to which he might be transferred. In practical terms, there appeared to be little opportunity for redeployment elsewhere. The matter appeared not to have been resolved.



A memorandum dated 19 September 1980 from personnel to the Hamilton regional manager read:



"Further to our discussion on 18/9/80 Mr. Trimnell will occupy a Field Officer Grade 2 position on a trial basis until 31/10/80.



2. In this regard at the cessation of the trial period would you please forward your comments as to his performance as a Field Officer Grade 2 and your recommendation as to whether he should become the nominal occupant of the position."



The decision to return the applicant to a field officer grade 2 position for a trial period was not divulged to the applicant. It appears to have been made following a favourable assessment of his work as a clerical officer but in disregard of the restrictions in May on his return to work. In the absence of any explanation, it is difficult to understand the department's head office decision to return the applicant to field officer duties without medical advice to do so, especially in view of previous medical advice.



While there was no evidence as to the implementation of the instruction it should also be borne in mind that the applicant was, according to department records, on sick leave from 22 September 1980 until he resigned. Had he not been on sick leave, it seems reasonable to infer that in the terms of the memorandum, he would have been some way through the trial period as a field officer. In view of the above, and notwithstanding a degree of inconclusiveness about the evidence, it does not seem to us that the applicant held the position of a clerical officer at the date he resigned.



We are therefore not satisfied that the words "his duties" can properly refer to the position of a clerical officer as at the date the applicant resigned. In our opinion, applying what was said in Hastings, his duties at the relevant date were those of a field officer. The Commissioner in considering the applicant's request under s.7(2) proceeded on the basis, incorrectly in our view, that "his duties" were those of a clerical officer.



Having determined what his duties were, the Tribunal must now consider the applicant's capacity to perform those duties. Dr Bekhor saw the applicant initially on 8 October 1980. Dr Bekhor considered the applicant had a mild skin problem, low grade seborrhoeic dermatitis of the face and trunk which was constitutional in nature. At the time Dr Bekhor wrote "patient much improved, to continue with treatment". This consultation had followed an earlier consultation on 1 October with Dr Fergin who considered that the applicant had two different skin problems, one on his back and upper chest since childhood which remained a problem, and the other an eczematous rash on his back and face which erupted a month or so earlier. He treated the applicant with facial cream and medication.



Dr Bekhor on review in November 1987 observed that the condition was still present though much improved. He agreed under questioning by Mr McInnis that the condition can flare up with stress; that the symptoms could be a manifestation of stress at the time and that the flare up could be employment related. In a report to the applicant's solicitors dated 13 November 1987 (T34\103) Dr Bekhor wrote:



"Clearly, I do not remember the fine details of a consultation carried out 7 years ago. I do know for certain that I would never tell a patient with this condition that it was primarily caused by work nor would I tell him even if I felt the condition to be aggravated by work that he should stop work because of it. I discussed this with Mr Trimnell today. He told me that "I didn't even remember your name or his (other locum)". He also agrees "at no stage did I ask you whether I should quit. I made the decision myself". In a situation like this, I would indicate to a patient that there exists a possibility that emotional stress may aggravate the problem. He would never have been told that the condition was primarily caused by stress, nor would I indicate that the condition of itself would justify ceasing work."



The Hamilton Medical group records the applicant attending on 25 January 1980 for a skin condition and again 25 September 1980, the latter being the occasion when he was referred by Dr McVicker to Dr Stahle. Dr Jamieson in report dated 3 July 1989 (T49\138) wrote:



"On examination Mr Trimnell had an acne form rash on the chest and back. There was no evidence of the rash on the mid-back reported by Dr Fergin. He also had Seborrhoeic Dermatitis of the face, and Dermatitis of the flexures of the elbows.



Mr Trimnell has a constitutional skin problem of acne and Seborrhoeic Dermatitis, plus a tendency under stress conditions to develop Lichen Simplex, (reported by Dr Fergin) and Flexural Eczema which is now present."



Dr Stephens examined the applicant in 1985 at the request of his solicitors. The history he took was that of a gradual onset of a chronic anxiety state with depressive features. In a report dated 16 November 1985 he expressed the opinion that the applicant was unable to work as a clerical officer whether in or outside the department for more than about 20 hours per week and then restricted to a menial job without responsibility. Since the time of writing that report he considers the applicant's health and fitness for work to be basically unchanged.



Dr Kornan examined the applicant in 1981 at the request of his solicitors. He was called by the Commissioner in these proceedings. In November 1981, Dr Kornan was of the opinion that the applicant had a wide ranging neurosis of moderate severity with features of anxiety depression and a secondary skin condition. In his report dated 12 November 1981 he wrote:



"I do not see him as being capable of working for a further two or three years.



On the other hand, I do see him working again at some stage although clearly he has been sensitized to working the with [sic] Department of Social Security. In general terms though he would be capable at some stage of doing any work within his basic capacities with the exception of perhaps working again for the Department of Social Security."



Asked in evidence to comment on the applicant's achievements in local government since his retirement from the department, Dr Kornan was in no doubt that the applicant had since demonstrated a good level of psychological functioning with the capacity to return to work with the department on clerical duties.



Dr Taylor who examined the applicant at the request of the Commissioner in July 1983 considered at the time that the applicant had no significant nervous problem. She noted the applicant was not taking any medication or receiving any treatment. She took a history that he was subject to bouts of depression until two years previously and noted that he felt "a bit tense" after his return to work in May 1980, but coped fairly well despite feeling somewhat out of place working with younger employees. In her opinion the applicant was not significantly incapacitated by his nervous symptoms, was capable of full-time employment, but considered he was unfit for investigative work.



There is no medical report contemporaneous with the applicant's decision to retire which would support that decision. We accept the applicant's evidence that he was told by Dr Bekhor that his skin condition may have been stress related. Equally we accept the evidence of Dr Bekhor that he did not give the applicant advice to retire. The medical reports as to the applicant's mental state at the time of resignation also suffer from a lack of contemporaneity. Dr Stephens upon whom the applicant relied was at the disadvantage of not having examined the applicant until five years after his resignation.



We accept the applicant as an honest witness. We are satisfied that the applicant because of mental incapacity is unfit to perform the duties of a field officer. There is ample medical evidence to that effect. In addition to the oral evidence of Drs Stephens, Taylor and Kornan, references are also made to the reports of Dr Hackett at T29\92 and Dr Freed at T45\125-127 and Dr Jamieson at T49\138-9. That the applicant could not perform a significant part of the duties of a field officer was not in issue. Mr Whithear conceded as much. The medical evidence established that the applicant suffered a stress reaction or anxiety depressive reaction to the work of an investigator which was responsible for a 15 month absence from work in 1979 - 1980 and resulting in an incapacity to perform that work which is permanent.



On 7 March 1979 the applicant sought and was granted permission from the department, to contest a by election for a seat on Hamilton City Council. The applicant was unsuccessful on that occasion and did not contest a local government election again until 1987 when he was elected to Council. He is presently in his third year as Mayor. He spends most mornings at the Town Hall on council work and in addition to chairing various committee and monthly council meetings he attends numerous functions a year in his capacity as Mayor. As such he has adopted a leadership role in municipal activities, a role he finds enjoyable and rewarding. Given the apparent ease with which the applicant has handled his role in local government, we consider it is reasonable to assume that he has considerable interpersonal skills and the necessary confidence to perform that role. It is true of course that his municipal activities developed some years after his resignation from the department, none the less they demonstrate considerable achievement on his part. Whether he has the capacity to perform a basic clerical function in the department is not a question we are required to decide in view of our earlier finding that when he resigned he held the position of a field officer.



We turn to consider the question whether the discretion vested in the respondent in s.7(2) of the Act should be exercised in favour of the applicant. In Hastings the Court said at 157-158:



"In our opinion, it is too simplistic to describe s7(2) as creating a power intended to be used for the benefit of persons who comply with its pre-conditions. Rather, we see it as a provision designed to avoid injustice in cases where an employee might be thought to be morally entitled to a pension, because effectively forced out of his or her employment for health reasons, but is not within s(1). Because the circumstances of individual cases must differ widely it is impossible to cast such a provision in a rigid form; a discretion must be vested in a suitable person, to be exercised upon the merits of the individual case. The industrial and medical history of the applicant for a pension is undoubtedly material to the decision to be made. Otherwise, as the relevant matters are unspecified in the legislation, it is for the Commissioner to determine what matters are to be taken into account and the weight to be given to them "unconfined except in so far as the subject matter and the scope and purpose" of s7(2) enables a court to say that "given reasons are definitely extraneous to any objects the legislature could have had in view": see the words of Dixon J in Water Conservation and Irrigation Commission (NSW) v Browning [1947] HCA 21; (1947) 74 CLR 492 at 505 adopted in R v Australian Broadcasting Tribunal; Ex parte 2HD Pty Ltd [1979] HCA 62; (1979) 144 CLR 45 at 49. The ultimate question for the Commissioner is whether it would be reasonable, having regard to the interests not only of the applicant for a pension but of other contributors to the superannuation fund, to treat the applicant as being entitled to the same benefits as if he or she had retired on the ground of invalidity."



The approach of the Court in Hastings that s.7(2) was designed to avoid injustice where an employee might be thought to be morally entitled to invalidity benefit was followed in Scott. On one view it is not easy to reconcile the applicant's decision to resign with the notion of being effectively forced out of his employment because of his health. Dr Bekhor did not recommend retirement and the day before the applicant resigned he reported the skin complaint to be improving.



On the other hand, too little attention seems to have been placed on the applicant's psychiatric state at the time of his resignation. Dr Freed's assessment of the applicant from his examination in March 1980 makes the point in his report dated 24 March 1980 that alternate employment in the public service could be contemplated provided the applicant accepted psychiatric treatment. There is no evidence that his recommendation of psychiatric treatment was communicated to the applicant's medical advisor or otherwise acted upon. This omission is considered significant in the light of the decision to return the applicant to field officer duties and in regard to the acceptance of his resignation without any further medical assessment of his anxiety state. Dr Freed's assessment, when asked in 1989 to make a retrospective assessment as at date of resignation, is necessarily inconclusive but he made the point that the possibility of his resignation being the consequence of psychological ill health cannot be disregarded with any degree of assurance. It is unfortunate that at the time he resigned the department did not have the applicant medically examined but accepted his resignation without any investigation as was warranted in the light of the applicant's previous medical history. The evidence suggests that at the time of his resignation, the applicant genuinely believed he was unable to continue working in the department because of his health.



Finally, the Tribunal must consider the question of special circumstances. As the Court pointed out in Scott the existence of special circumstances may justify a refusal to exercise a discretion given by s.7(2). In that case the applicant resigned his employment after many years on medical grounds and received a refund of his contributions. The Tribunal found that he satisfied s.7(2) but that it would be unjust to exercise discretion in his favour because of the possibility of a double benefit, he being entitled to retain a pension under the Veterans' Entitlements legislation. The Full Court, by a majority held that all the relevant considerations indicated that the discretion should have been exercised in the applicant's favour. We turn to those considerations.



In the present case, firstly, there is the finding of mental incapacity to perform his duties. Secondly, at the time the applicant ceased employment while it could not be said that he did not have some awareness of rights under the Act (as was found to be the case in Scott) as in 1979 he had discussions about retirement on invalidity grounds and signed a consent to be retired. Equally, we believe the applicant at the time he resigned felt he was unable to continue and considered his rights to obtain retirement on the ground of invalidity had been exhausted when retirement did not eventuate in 1979. This is indicated by the terms of his letter of resignation.



Thirdly, there is the question of whether there has been an unreasonable delay in seeking invalidity benefits. The applicant gave evidence that he first discussed compensation and superannuation claims with his solicitor in January 1981. A workers compensation claim was made in 1981 and medical reports were obtained for the purpose of that claim. Determinations in favour of the applicant were made on 6 March 1984 for aggravation of anxiety and depressive syndrome (T24/82) and on 21 March 1986 for aggravation of a pre-existing skin condition (T26/84). In evidence, the applicant said that he had no idea why a claim under s.7(2) was not made until 1986. The question of the applicant's entitlements was left in the hands of his solicitors and it was reasonable to do so. The material before the Tribunal would suggest that the superannuation issue was addressed upon termination of incapacity for work payments. Medical reports relevant to the workers compensation claim were made available to the Commissioner. Notwithstanding the delay there was no evidence to suggest that the respondent has suffered any prejudice by the delay and we are satisfied he has not.



In Hastings the Court said (at p. 158) that the interest of other contributors was also a factor to be considered. However in Scott the majority judgment did not agree with that. We make the observation that given the length of the applicant's service, being a contributor since 1959, we do not consider the interests of other contributors operate against the exercise of discretion in favour of the applicant.



On balance, we are inclined to the view that special circumstances do not exist as would justify a refusal to exercise the discretion in s.7(2) of the Act.



Accordingly, the decision under review is set aside with a direction that the applicant is to be deemed for the purposes of the Act to have been retired on the ground of mental incapacity.
