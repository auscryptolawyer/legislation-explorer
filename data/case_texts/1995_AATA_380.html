Russell Walter Fox and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 380 (19 December 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

RUSSELL WALTER FOX v. THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. N94/83

AAT No. 10609

Number of pages - 18

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.M. FORREST (Deputy President), B.C. LOCK (Member) and I.R. WAY (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Invalidity retirement - level of pension to which applicant entitled - contribution to pension at date of retirement - whether entitled to pension on salary applicable to rank of Lieutenant or Captain in Permanent Military Forces - meaning of 'salary' - whether temporary performance allowance 'salary' for superannuation purposes - decision affirmed.

HEARING

SYDNEY, 20-21 June and 20 October 1995

19:12:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Slattery

Canberra

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

B.M. FORREST, B.C. LOCK and I.R. WAY This is an application for review of a decision by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ('the respondent'), confirmed on reconsideration, that following the applicant's retirement on the grounds of invalidity, he became entitled to a pension under s.60N of the Superannuation Act 1922 ('the Act') based upon 8 units of pension calculated on the salary applicable to him in his rank of Lieutenant in the Permanent Military Forces ('PMF').

2. The Tribunal received in evidence the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1-T77), together with a set of legislative material (T1-T11) and the exhibits tendered in evidence.

3. The applicant objected to the decision under review on the ground that when he was retired from the Army he was a Captain and therefore his pension should be paid on the basis of 10 units of pension being applicable to the salary of a Captain.

4. When the matter came before the Tribunal counsel for the applicant indicated that he would not be pursuing the issue of whether the applicant was a Captain or a Lieutenant but would confine his argument to the meaning of 'salary' as defined in Part 1, s.4(1) of the Act. It was his submission that the extra pay which the applicant received as Captain was salary for superannuation purposes and entitled the applicant to pension based on 10 units of pension.

5. The respondent contends that for superannuation purposes the applicant was at all times a Lieutenant and his salary as a Lieutenant fixed the number of units of pension for which he was entitled to contribute and that he did indeed contribute for that number of units and no more. His pension was to be calculated on the basis of the number of units for which he was contributing.

6. The following facts are not in dispute:

(a) The applicant is aged 75 years (born 30 September 1920) and

is a former member of the PMF. He enrolled as a cadet in the Royal

Military College ('RMC'), Duntroon in February 1938 and graduated

on 17 August 1940 with the rank of Lieutenant. He was posted to

the Australian Staff Corps, a corps of the PMF on 18 August 1940.

(b) On graduation and posting as Lieutenant to the Australian Staff

Corps, the applicant became an 'employee' for the purposes of

s.60A of the Act. He was in receipt of pay at the rate of 292

pounds per annum and was required and in fact did contribute for

5 units of pension at age 20 rates.

(c) On 6 October 1939 a force was constituted under the Defence Act

for service locally and overseas called the second Australian

Imperial Force ('AIF'). The AIF became and remained until its

disbandment in 1947 a part of the Australian Military Forces

('AMF'). On 1 March 1942 the applicant became a temporary Captain

in the AMF.

(d) On 1 March 1942 the applicant volunteered for and was seconded

to the AIF. He had operational service on two occasions; in 1942 in

New Caledonia and in 1944 in Papua/New Guinea during the

Finchhaven Campaign. It was during this Campaign that the

applicant became ill with what was first thought to be scrub

typhus but which was later diagnosed as epilepsy. He was returned

to Australia and discharged from the Army on 23 March 1945. At all

times during his service with the AIF he held the rank of and

received the pay of a Captain. As from 23 March 1945 the applicant

received an invalidity pension under the Act in the sum of 208

pounds ($416) per annum.

(e) On discharge from the Army the applicant undertook studies in

law and became a successful barrister and Queens Counsel at the NSW

Bar. In 1977 he was appointed a Judge of the Federal Court of

Australia. He continued to receive his invalidity pension until

10 June 1960 when it was suspended on the grounds that his health

had been restored. The applicant appealed against this decision

but was unsuccessful.

(f) On 3 March 1986 the applicant wrote to the respondent advising

that he was contemplating retirement and enquiring about the

status of his invalidity pension. He was advised by letter dated

16 April 1986 that the legislation provided that his pension could

only be restored on cessation of employment and he was invited to

advise of that event. The applicant took issue with the respondent

on whether the legislation was being correctly interpreted. The

applicant maintained that his pension should be restored from the

time he turned 65 years of age while the respondent held the view

that the suspension should remain unless it could be demonstrated

that:

(i) the applicant was unfit for continued employment;

(ii) his incapacity was 10% or more in relation to civil

employment; or

(iii) his earnings from employment were less than two thirds of the

notional rate of his salary (s.50 of the Act).

In any event the applicant's pension was restored from 1 February

1989, the day following his retirement from the Federal Court.

(g) The applicant took the matter of the age 65 reinstatement to

the Administrative Appeals Tribunal and the matter was settled

between the parties. The respondent determined on 31 May 1991 that

the applicant's pension should be restored from 1 January 1978,

given that the medical evidence indicated his retiring condition

had worsened since January 1978. Arrears of pension for the period

12 January 1978 to 31 January 1989 were paid to the applicant.

(h) On receipt of this payment the applicant became aware for the

first time that his pension was based on him being a Lieutenant at

the time of his discharge. He complained that his correct rank was

Captain. The respondent sought advice from the Army office and

that advice appeared to confirm the applicant's claim.

Accordingly, on the basis that his units should have been 10 and

not 8, his pension was increased prospectively from 22 August 1991

and he was paid a lump sum of $60,158.64, representing arrears for

two periods, viz 23 March 1945 to 9 January 1960, when the pension

was suspended, and 1 January 1978 to 22 August 1991 when payment

at the increased rate commenced. The applicant then requested the

Commonwealth Ombudsman to assist him in an application to have his

arrears of pension paid at a rate which reflects today's values.

As a result, an investigation of the applicant's entitlements was

undertaken by the respondent. The outcome of those investigations

was a finding that the applicant was, for the purposes of the Act,

in receipt of the salary of a Lieutenant at the time of discharge

and was not, for the purpose of the Act, receiving the salary of a

Captain and that his pension should be calculated on 8 units of

pension and not 10.

(i) The decision of the delegate of the respondent on

reconsideration under sub-s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976

('the 1976 Act') was to confirm the decision, the effect of which

was, following the applicant's retirement on the ground of

invalidity from the PMF on 23 March 1945, he became entitled to a

pension under s.60N of the Act based upon the salary applicable to

him in his rank of Lieutenant in the PMF. The delegate on 14

January 1994 concluded:

'I am satisfied that:

- (the applicant) was permitted and required to contribute under

the Superannuation Act solely by virtue of his being a member of

the permanent military forces;

- at the time of his retirement, he held the rank of lieutenant in

the permanent military forces and captain in the Australian

Imperial Force;

- his nominal salary in the rank of lieutenant was 450 pounds per

annum;

- his salary as a captain in the Australian Imperial Force

was 511 pounds but, because of subsection 31(4) of the Defence

Act - whether or not the Australian Imperial Force was part of the

permanent military forces - such salary was not to be taken into

account for superannuation purposes;

- his nominal salary of 450 pounds corresponded to a unit

entitlement of 8 and hence, an annual pension, at commencement,

of 208 pounds.'

THE SUPERANNUATION ACT 1922

7. The Act is described as an act to provide superannuation benefits for persons employed by the Commonwealth and to make provision for the families of those persons. By Part IVA of the Act (sections 60A to 60T inserted in 1924 (No.45 of 1924)) members of the Military and Air Forces of the Commonwealth were included as persons employed by the Commonwealth. Although Part IVA of the Act was later repealed, payment to the applicant of his invalidity pension continued after that event because sub-s.8(1) of the Superannuation Act 1948 ('the 1948 Act') provided that pensions already granted under that Part were to continue to be paid. Sub-section 8(1) of the 1948 Act reads:

'8. (1.) Part IVA. of the Principal Act is repealed, but the

provisions of that Part shall, subject to the succeeding

sub-sections of this section, continue to apply -

(a) in relation to pensions and benefits payable under that

Part at the date of commencement of this section; and

...

as if that Part had not been repealed.

... '

As a member of the PMF the applicant was an 'employee' pursuant to s.60A of the Act, which reads:

'60A. In this Part, unless the contrary intention appears -

"officer" means a commissioned officer of the Permanent Air Force

and includes an Air Cadet but does not include a commissioned

warrant officer;

"employee" means a member of the Permanent Military Forces, and

includes a commissioned warrant officer, warrant officer or

non-commissioned officer or man of the Permanent Air Force, but

does not include any commissioned warrant officer, warrant officer

or non-commissioned officer holding an honorary commission.'

and Part IVA applied to him pursuant to s.60B:

'60B. This Part shall apply to and in respect of employees and

officers and not otherwise.'

The applicant became an employee and a contributor under the Act on 18 August 1940. As such s.60C(4) applies to him:

'60C. ...

(4). An employee, who is not, prior to the commencement of this

Part, a contributor under this Act, shall not be required or

permitted to contribute for units of pension under this Act,

except in pursuance of the provisions of this Part, nor shall

pension under this Act be payable to or in respect of that

employee, except in pursuance of those provisions.'

Contributions by an employee were required by s.60D, which reads:

'60D. Employees and officers shall, subject to this Part, be

liable and entitled to contribute in accordance with Part III.

of this Act for units of pension under this Act, and, for the

purpose of determining the rights and obligations of those

employees and officers in respect of contributions for units of

pension, Part III. shall, subject to this Part, apply in relation

to contributions by those employees and officers:'

Part III of the Act (ss.12 to 22) deals with contributions by employees, scale of units, scale of contributions by employees, contributions by the Commonwealth and General Provisions as to contributions.

8. Under s.16 of the Act the amount of contribution to be paid by an employee was based upon (a) the number of units of pension in respect of which the employee contributes; (b) the sex of the employee; and (c) the age of the employee at the time each new unit of pension was taken up.

9. The number of units of pension to which an employee was entitled and in the applicant's case required to contribute for, had a relation to the salary of the employee in accordance with the scale set down in s.13(1) of the Act as follows:

10. On becoming an employee on 18 August 1940 the applicant was in receipt of an annual salary of 292 pounds and was required and was contributing for 5 units of pension. Putting aside for one moment the argument between the parties as to what is the correct salary figure, when the applicant retired on the grounds of invalidity on 23 March 1945 the respondent's records indicate that the applicant was in receipt of an annual salary of 450 pounds and that he was entitled to and did contribute for 8 units equivalent to a pension of 208 pounds per annum. Between 18 August 1940 and 23 March 1945 the units contributed for were adjusted whenever an increment or other adjustment in the annual salary of the applicant moved beyond the limits set out in s.13(1) of the Act. While we are looking at the scale in s.13(1) it is worth noting that the applicant contends his annual salary was that of a Captain, namely 536 poundsand that therefore his unit entitlement was 10 and his pension should have commenced at 260 pounds per annum.

11. The scale indicates that if a contributor was on the bottom of the scale he/she received a pension of 50% of salary. However, s.13(2), 13(3) and 13(4) of the Act (as it was in 1940), which are set out hereunder indicate that in certain circumstances contributions were not compulsory. In particular once a contributor reached the age of 40 years his additional contributions were voluntary. This being the case not all employees would receive the 50% of salary referred to above but would only receive the pension equivalent of the units for which they had contributed.

'(2) An employee whose salary does not exceed Two hundred and

eight pounds per annum may elect to contribute additional units

or half units to make up a total number of two and a half, three

or four units.

(3) Where an employee enters the service after the commencement

of this Act, and at the time of such entry is not less than forty

years of age, the Board may, upon application by the employee,

reduce the number of units in respect of which he shall contribute

and any contributions paid by him, prior to such reduction, in

respect of units in excess of the reduced number shall be credited

as payments for paid-up pensions to be actuarially calculated, or

refunded to him, as the Board determines.

(4) If the salary of a contributor is increased, and by reason of

that increase, falls within a higher salary-group in column one of

the scale contained in sub-section (1.) of this section than the

provisions shall apply to the contributor:-

(a) if he has attained the age of forty years, or was at the

not exceeding the sum which will provide units of pension to the

salary-group; and

(b) in all other cases, he SHALL increase the amount of his

and any such increased contribution shall be payable as from the

These sections were amended in 1942 and now read:

(b) where an employee under the age of thirty years whose salary

(3.) Where an employee becomes a contributor after the

commencement of this Act, and at the time of becoming a

upon application by the employee, reduce the number of units in

respect of which he shall contribute and any contributions paid by

him, prior to such reduction, in respect of units in excess of the

reduced number shall be credited as payments for paid-up pensions

to be actuarially calculated, or refunded to him, as the Board

determines.

(4.) If the salary of a contributor is increased and, by reason of

following provisions shall apply:-

(a) If the contributor has attained the age of forty years and

is contributing for the number of units corresponding to the

column two of the scale contained in sub-section (1.) of this

salary falls, unless he elects, not later than six months after

the first payment of the increased salary -

(i) not to increase the amount of his contribution; or

(ii) to increase the amount of his contribution, but to such a sum

as will provide a number of units less than the number so

specified.

(b) If the contributor has attained the age of forty years and is

not contributing for the number of units corresponding to the

salary-group in which his salary fell immediately prior to the

increase, he shall increase the amount of his contribution so as

to add to the number of units for which he is contributing the

maximum number of additional units for which he could have

contributed if he had been a contributor to whom paragraph (a) of

this sub-section applies, unless he elects, not later than six

months after the first payment of the increased salary -

(I) not to increase the amount of his contribution; or

(ii) to increase the amount of his contribution, but so as to add

less than that maximum number of additional units.

(c) If the contributor is a contributor to whom the last preceding

paragraph applies and he does not elect under sub-paragraph (I) or

(ii) of that paragraph, he may elect to increase the amount of his

contribution to an amount which will provide units of pension not

exceeding the number specified in column two of the scale

contained in sub-section (1.) of this section opposite to the

salary-group within which his increased salary falls:

Provided that the election shall not have effect unless, within

the prescribed time, he satisfies the Board that he is not

suffering from any physical or mental defect likely to render him

incapable of performing his duties before attaining the maximum

age for retirement.

(d) In all other cases, the contributor shall increase the amount

of his contribution to such a sum as will provide units of pension

to the number specified in column two of the scale contained in

sub-section (1.) of this section opposite to the salary-group

within which his increased salary falls.'

The applicant submitted that the use of the word 'salary' in these sections must mean actual salary. The Tribunal does not accept this submission. The words of the original section state and the changes restate in clearer terms, the options of a contributor over the age of 40 years to accept or reject additional units of pension, requiring the contributor to make an election. They emphasise the actuarial nature of the Superannuation Scheme established under the Act and that not all contributors were required to make the contributions that their salary otherwise dictated. It follows that the number of units of pension for which a person was contributing has a special significance.

12. Once the number of units was established by reference to s.13(1) the amount to be contributed each fortnight for each of those units was obtained by reference to Schedules to the Act. In the applicant's case Schedule III applied in 1940 and Schedule VII applied from the increase in contributions on 12 November 1942 (T15/104). It was submitted by counsel for the respondent that these schedules were actuarially calculated and that they took into account not only the normal life expectancy of contributors but also the fact that some of the otherwise healthy contributors at commencement would not reach age retirement because of ill health and would be retired on the grounds of invalidity. We accept that submission.

13. Thus in 1942 an employee aged 20 years, contributing for units of pension based on a retiring age of 60 years, would pay 3/3d per fortnight for each new unit whereas an employee aged 50 years would pay 1-1-2 pounds per fortnight for each new unit. It can be seen therefore that to receive the full value of a unit from the fund the employee had to contribute the full share of the unit of pension value. This view is supported by the wording of s.29, which reads:

'29. Subject to this Act, a contributor shall, upon retirement, be

entitled to receive a pension according to the number of units FOR

WHICH HE WAS CONTRIBUTING AT THE TIME OF HIS RETIREMENT:

Provided that any contributor -

(a) who has attained the age of sixty years and elects, or is

called upon to retire before attaining the maximum age for

retirement; or

(b) the age for whose retirement is fixed by law at an earlier age

than sixty-five years, who retires on attaining the age so fixed,

shall as from the date of his retirement be entitled to a pension

WHICH IS THE ACTUARIAL EQUIVALENT OF THE CONTRIBUTIONS
MADE or to

be made BY HIM and of the share of pension payable by the

Commonwealth and accruing to him under this Act.'

(Tribunal emphasis)

and which makes provision for contributors over the age of 60 years and who elect or are required to retire before reaching the maximum retiring age, to receive the pension which is the actuarial equivalent of the contributions made by them.

14. The only exception to this occurs where (as in the case of the applicant) a contributor is retired on the grounds of invalidity. Section 60N reads:

'60N. Where a contributor under this Part is retired on the ground

of invalidity or physical or mental incapacity to perform his

duties, he shall -

(a) if the invalidity or incapacity is not due to wilful action on

his part for the purpose of obtaining pension, BE ENTITLED TO THE

FULL PENSION FOR WHICH HE WAS CONTRIBUTING at the time of his

retirement; and

(b) ... ' (Tribunal emphasis)

Section 30 of the Act makes similar provisions for contributors who are not members of the PMF. Thus in the case of retirement through invalidity a contributor may not have paid the unit value in full but would still receive the full pension for which they were contributing. We have however accepted the submission of the respondent that whatever shortfall in the fund may have occurred it has been accounted for in the Schedule of contributions for all contributors.

15. It was the applicant's submission that at the very core of the superannuation scheme created by the Act was the terms 'salary' of the employee and 'annual salary'. That salary determined the number of units of pension for which the employee was required to contribute. The following definitions appear in s.4(1) of the Act:

'4. (1.) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears -

...

"Contributor" means an employee who is or has been contributing

under this Act to the Fund;

...

"Salary" means salary or wages, and includes the value of

allowances such as allowances for rent, house allowed rent free,

light, fuel, rations, and fees allowed regularly as emoluments of

office, but does not include bonuses, overtime payments, or

allowances for forage, equipment, climatic disadvantages, cost of

living or travelling expenses:

"Service" means service under, or employment by, the Commonwealth

or an approved authority and, in relation to any qualifying period

of employment, includes -

(a) continuous service as a member of the Permanent Naval,

Military or Air Forces of the Commonwealth; and

(b) where an employee is transferred from the service of a State

or Territory to the service of the Commonwealth, such permanent

service of the employee under the State or Territory as is

continuous with his service under the Commonwealth;

... '

There is no dispute between the parties that the applicant was a contributor and that he had continuous service as a member of the PMF of the Commonwealth.

16. The parties were opposed as to the meaning to be given to the word 'salary'. The respondent's position is that the term salary means salary received on a permanent basis and that in the applicant's case this is salary as a Lieutenant in the PMF. The applicant, on the other hand, contends that the additional salary received as Captain was 'salary' within the terms of the definition. The applicant further contends that if he was required to contribute extra at the higher level of salary those extra contributions should have been paid by the Commonwealth because of the special arrangement made by the government of the day to pay the superannuation contributions of those contributors who enlisted in the military services for overseas service.

17. On 5 October 1939, the War Cabinet decided that, as in the case of officers of the Commonwealth Public Service, members of the PMF who joined the 2nd AIF would have their superannuation contributions paid by the Commonwealth during their period of service with the 2nd AIF (see Ex.4). Despite this decision anomalies arose when the decision was put into practice. The following anomalies became apparent by mid 1943:

'(1) Public Servants - All members who are appointed to the AIF,

RAAF or RAN are eligible for the full superannuation concession

even though they may not leave Australia, whereas many of those

who are in the Citizen Military Forces receive no such concession

and others are eligible only for limited benefits. Some of the

members of the Citizen Military Forces have served in battle areas

in New Guinea, and all are liable for service outside Australia in

a defined area.

(2) Permanent Military and Air Forces - If appointed to the AIF,

members of the Permanent Military Forces, with a few exceptions,

have their superannuation paid for the whole term of service in

the AIF, whereas members of the Permanent Air Force, although also

liable for overseas service, pay their own superannuation except

for any periods they are actually serving overseas. (Paragraph 7

of report).

...

With the change in the disposition of the forces which has since

occurred and the need to provide for the defence of the Australian

mainland, many AIF officers and men are retained in Australia.'

(Ex.A)

To overcome these the War Cabinet decided as follows:

'WAR CABINET MINUTE

SECRET MELBOURNE - 9TH JULY, 1943.

(2931) AGENDUM NO.281/1943 - SUPERANNUATION CONTRIBUTIONS -

COMMONWEALTH PUBLIC SERVANTS IN THE FORCES AND MEMBERS

OF THE PERMANENT MILITARY AND AIR FORCES

Having regard to the desirability of ensuring uniformity of

treatment wherever practicable, between members of the various

branches of the Forces, it was decided that the Commonwealth

would, as from the date of this decision, accept liability for

the superannuation contributions of all its employees, including

members of the Permanent Military and Air Forces, serving in the

Forces during the war period.

(signed)

Secretary

War Cabinet'

(Ex.A)

It is apparent therefore that on a combination of these decisions, as at 3 March 1942 the Commonwealth accepted liability for the applicant's superannuation contributions that fell due for payment during his war service. The records (T11, T12, T15, T16) show that the amount of contributions payable on behalf of the applicant were adjusted whenever his salary as a Lieutenant in the PMF varied between the scales in s.13(1) of the Act.

18. Dr Horner, a Fellow at the Strategic and Defence Study Centre, Australian National University, who has been a full-time military historian for 14 of the last 21 years provided a written report dated 24 September 1994 (Ex.B) and gave oral evidence to the Tribunal about some aspects of the historical background related to the claim by the applicant. Included in this evidence are details of the method of promotion of Army officers.

19. The method employed at the time of the applicant's military service and subsequent to the end of World War II is relevant to the deliberations of the Tribunal. It was Dr Horner's evidence that graduates of RMC Duntroon were appointed in the rank of Lieutenant to the Australian Staff Corps. The Australian Staff Corps was established as part of the PMF for the training and administration of the Citizen Military Forces ('CMF') in times of peace. It provided the professional officers who occupied certain of the senior commands and essential staff, regimental and instructional appointments.

20. The composition of the Staff Corps was determined by the Annual Establishment of the Staff Corps which was approved by the Minister for Defence and included in the Army's Annual Estimates. Substantive promotion within the Staff Corps was slow and subject to serving set periods in one substantive rank before promotion was allowed to the next higher rank. At the outbreak of World War II and throughout the war, for example, promotion from Lieutenant to Captain was allowed after eight years as a substantive Lieutenant and from Captain to Major after eight years service as a substantive Captain, provided that the officer had passed the relevant promotion examinations. In some cases during the war the requirements for passing promotion examinations were waived.

21. It was realised that were officers promoted to Colonel or higher ranks when the Army reverted to pre-war strength there would be too many senior officers. Therefore, after 2 November 1939, no substantive promotions to Colonel or higher ranks were made in the Staff Corps, despite the fact that such vacancies had occurred. It was also realised that if officers below the rank of Lieutenant-Colonel were to reach the normal age for promotion during the war they would be severely disadvantaged if they were not allowed to be promoted. Members of the Staff Corps became eligible to contribute for additional units of pension under the Commonwealth Superannuation Act on promotion to the next substantive rank, and they would have been disadvantaged if they could not take up these units at the normal age. As a result, Staff Corps officers up to the rank of Lieutenant-Colonel were promoted at the normal time intervals, although the examination requirement was waived because of war service. With the expansion of the Army during the war, Staff Corps officers were often posted to positions requiring a higher rank, and they usually received temporary promotion in the Staff Corps while they were holding these positions, irrespective of the actual provision in the annual estimates for the Corps.

22. The 2nd AIF was formed in October 1939 for deployment overseas and a number of Staff Corps officers were seconded for service with the AIF either with their Staff Corps rank or with a higher rank and in due course they qualified for higher temporary or substantive rank in respect of the appointments held in the AIF. The History of the Activities of the Office of Military Secretary during the War 1939-1945 (Ex.C) clearly explains the status of the Staff Corps officers in the AIF. This history states, at pp.70-71:

'They were consequently so appointed or promoted in the AIF and

not in the Aust Staff Corps ... Substantive or temporary rank

granted to Staff Corps officers either in the AIF or CMF did not

give such officers any claim whatever to equivalent rank in the

Aust Staff Corps. Such rank in the AIF or CMF or temporary rank

in the Aust Staff Corps did not permit them to contribute for

additional units of superannuation.'

23. Dr Horner, in his oral evidence, explained temporary rank as follows:

'A temporary rank may be given if an officer has not reached the

number of years service that are required for him to be made

substantive. Another reason for making an officer temporary is

that for some particular situation, he might be thought to be the

best officer to fill a particular position. But, in the total

army, he might not be the next officer on the list due for

promotion and so by making him temporary, it will not get the

seniority list out of sink (sic). So, you make him temporary and

then at a later stage when he has served the right number of years

and when you can work out what his correct seniority position will

be, he will then be made substantive on a particular day and

furthermore, not only on a particular day but with seniority after

somebody and seniority before somebody, so that he appears in the

right place on the list.'

(Tr. p.104)

24. Following the entry of Japan into the war in December 1941 the AIF progressively returned to Australia. For the purpose of the defence of Australia the CMF and the AIF were amalgamated to form the Australian Military Forces ('AMF'). It should be noted that Dr Horner explained that although the term AMF sometimes had been used prior to this to denote forces other than the AIF and the PMF, as from 2 October 1942, following the issue of an Army Headquarters General Routine Order (Ex.D), the term AMF was not used with this restricted meaning. For purposes of promotion and seniority the AMF was considered as one Force. About this time all the officers of the Staff Corps who had not already been seconded to the AIF or the CMF were appointed or posted to appropriate positions on the war establishments of the AIF or CMF. In effect the PMF ceased to function as a separate organisation. This did not mean however that the Staff Corps ceased to exist. A separate Staff Corps seniority list was maintained and a Staff Corps officer could have two substantive ranks - his 'war substantive' rank in the AIF or CMF, and his Staff Corps substantive rank. 'War substantive' rank was substantive rank granted during the war and subject to adjustment after the war.

25. After the war the Army progressively assigned substantive rank to Staff Corps officers in a way that was designed to maintain their seniority but also give some recognition to their war service. There was therefore a slight reduction in the time required for promotion to the next rank and in some cases a considerable reduction in the time required for promotion to the following rank. As Dr Horner pointed out in his report, the Army was remarkably consistent in its approach, as illustrated by his examination of the dates of promotion of the applicant and seven of his RMC contemporaries chosen at random (see Ex.B, para.25).

26. This examination showed, with respect to Staff Corps rank, with the exception of MacAdie and the applicant, that all the officers were promoted to substantive Captain in the Staff Corps on 14 April 1947, six years and eight months after graduating from RMC and were promoted to Major on 18 August 1959, 10 years after graduation. The promotion to Captain had been accelerated by one year and four months from the standard eight years in an attempt to shorten the promotion period in a way that preserved previous seniority. MacAdie was an exception because he had served as a Lieutenant-Colonel in the AIF since 1943 and, in view of this, his promotion to substantive Captain was accelerated by a further two and a half months ahead of his contemporaries. He was then immediately promoted to substantive Major. The other exception was the applicant who had left the Army before the date on which his contemporaries were promoted to substantive Captain and was not promoted to substantive Captain.

27. A total of 22 officers graduated from RMC with the applicant in August 1940. Of these, at the end of the war, one was a Lieutenant-Colonel, nine were Majors, eight were Captains and four were Lieutenants, one of whom had been killed in action. As all were still Staff Corps Lieutenants at the end of the war, save for the officer killed and the applicant who had left the service earlier, these were AIF or CMF ranks.

28. Dr Horner concluded from this historical background that:

(a) the applicant was a substantive Lieutenant in the Staff Corps

throughout the war and was never promoted to substantive Captain

in the Staff Corps;

(b) the applicant was promoted to temporary Captain in the AIF

and later to 'war substantive' Captain in the AIF, but this did

not affect his substantive rank in the Staff Corps;

(c) had the applicant served in the Army after the war he would,

in all probability, have been promoted to substantive Captain in

the Staff Corps on 14 April 1947;

(d) on retirement from the Army he would have been given the

retired rank of Captain in recognition of his service as a Captain

in the AIF; and

(e) although the AIF was a permanent military force the conditions

of service in the AIF were quite different from those in the

pre-war PMF (formed under Defence Act section 31) and the PMF

which continued to serve during the war. The Staff Corps remained

part of the PMF and members of it were only seconded to the AIF.

29. On this topic the Tribunal has considered carefully all the material contained in the T documents, the evidence of the applicant and of Dr Horner and the submissions of both counsel and finds that during the period March 1942 to March 1945 the applicant carried out the duties of Captain in the AIF. The Tribunal is also of the view that this period of duty as Captain in the AIF was not of a permanent nature and would end when the war ended, the AIF was disbanded and the members of the PMF seconded to the AIF resumed their ranks in the peace time army. The applicant was discharged before the war ended but we are satisfied on the evidence of Dr Horner that had he remained in the AIF he would have resumed his Permanent Army rank of Lieutenant when the AIF was disbanded, as did so many of his contemporaries.

30. Nevertheless, while he performed the duties of Captain he was paid an allowance equal to the difference in salary of a Captain and a Lieutenant in accordance with War Financial (Military Forces) Regulation 10, which reads:

'10. A member who is granted acting rank, classification or

appointment shall, while so acting, be entitled to pay and

allowances at the rates prescribed by these Regulations for the

rank, classification or appointment in which he is acting.'

and as substituted by S.R. 1944, No.111 20 July 1944:

'10. A member who is granted acting, provisional or temporary

rank, classification or appointment shall, while holding that

rank, classification or appointment, be entitled to pay, pay

allowances and other allowances at the rates prescribed by these

Regulations for the rank, classification or appointment in which

he is acting or which he holds provisionally or temporarily.'

31. The question and indeed the main issue in this matter from the applicant's point of view is whether or not this temporary performance allowance is salary for superannuation purposes under the Act. The words 'salary' and 'wages' have been considered at length in many cases before the Tribunal. In Re McLennan and Commissioner for Superannuation [1990] AATA 301; (1990) 12 AAR 586, the Tribunal (Deputy President Forgie, Senior Member Beddoe and Member Horrigan) considered at length whether compensation payments could be characterised as 'wages' or 'salary' for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 and as discussed in Re Cahill and the Commissioner of Superannuation (1988) 9 AAR 492; Eastman v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 15 FCR 139 and 6 AAR 518; Commissioner for Superannuation v Carpenter [1983] FCA 148; (1983) 77 FLR 224; Graham v Baker [1961] HCA 48; (1961) 106 CLR 340. Except for Graham's case the cases were concerned with entitlements under the Superannuation Act 1976. Graham's case considered whether sick leave was salary for superannuation purposes and decided that it was. Cahill's case was concerned with compensation payments and decided they were not salary for superannuation purposes.

32. It appears to the Tribunal that the definition of salary has evolved over the years and that the meaning to be given to the word 'salary' in the Act should be its ordinary meaning as understood, not only by the public at large, but by those members of the public who are directly affected by its meaning. This is particularly so when considering the definition in the Act because there are so many members of the public involved, ie the entire permanent membership of the Commonwealth Public Service and where the interpretation applied by the respondent has gone unchallenged for the entire life of the Act. In Re McLennan (supra) at 596 the Tribunal referred to London County Council v Henry Boot and Sons Ltd (1959) 3 ALL ER 636 in which Viscount Simonds, with whom Lords Radcliffe, Cohen and Keith of Avonholm agreed, said that there was:

'... no doubt that what the workman gets from the operation of the

holidays agreements is a benefit to him. It might, perhaps, be

loosely called part of his remuneration. But to me it seems an

essential element in a 'wage' that it should be paid to the

workman for work done by him, and it is just this element which is

lacking in the holiday credit. It is neither paid by the employer

to him nor related to the work which he does for the employer. I

would say emphatically that it would not occur to an employer,

being asked what wages he paid his workmen, or to a workman, being

asked what wages he was paid, to refer in his answer to the

holiday credits. They might, indeed, say that, in addition to his

wage, he got the benefit of the holiday credits. But that would no

more be a wage than would the benefit of a payment by the employer

to a pension, superannuation, or sick fund.'

(Tribunal emphasis)

That was said in 1959. Mr Slattery suggested that the respondent should look at what was happening in the real world. Indeed in the real world of 1995 the very items recorded by Viscount Simonds would be considered part of a salary package. But we are not dealing with 1995, we are dealing with 1945 and indeed the whole life of the Act from 1922-1976.

33. The Tribunal notes that Viscount Simonds emphasised the fact that the holiday credits were not paid directly to the employee. If, however, we could paraphrase what he said we would say emphatically that (between 1922 and 1976) it would not have occurred to any Commonwealth employers who were asked what salary they paid their employees, or to any employees, who were asked what salary they were paid, to refer in their answer to any allowance paid for temporary performance of a higher paid position. From an employee's point of view the reason is obvious - the extra contribution to superannuation. That contributions to superannuation were at that time (1939-1945) a matter of concern to all, contributors and government alike, is evidenced by the lengthy report from the Treasury Finance Committee on the subject (Attachment 1 to Ex.A) which led to consideration of the matter by the War Cabinet in 1943. Counsel for the applicant referred to an opinion of the Solicitor-General dated 8 February 1923 which indicates that from the commencement of the Act (November 1922) contributions were of concern to those who had to make them. The following is a copy of that opinion (see para.1314, pp.992-993 'Opinions of Attorneys-General of the Commonwealth of Australia, with opinions of Solicitors-General and the Attorney-General's Department'):

'SUPERANNUATION

Section 15 of the Act reads as follows:

'15. Where the salary of a contributor is or has been reduced

from one salary-group to another salary-group, the Board may, upon

application by the contributor, reduce the number of units in

respect of which he shall contribute to the number appropriate to

the salary-group to which his salary has been reduced, and any

contributions paid by him, prior to such reduction, in respect of

There was no evidence submitted to the Tribunal as to whether the Solicitor-General's opinion was accepted or otherwise. It may have been that the contributors found the provisions of s.15 less than appealing or that the contribution rates at the higher salaries were exorbitant. There is insufficient material before the Tribunal for us to draw any conclusion from this opinion.

34. Turning once again to the cases discussed in Re McLennan, Eastman's case concerned the question of 'Higher Duties Allowance', which is somewhat analogous to what the applicant was being paid when he was a Captain in the AIF. Eastman however was decided on the definition of salary in the 1976 Act and on the regulations under that Act in respect of 'Higher Duties Allowance'. Those regulations, were quite specific. We have not been directed to any similar provisions in the Act or in the regulations under the Act. In Eastman at 15 FCR 147 the Full Court said:

'The character of a higher-duties allowance is indicated by

reg 87(2) of the Public Service Regulations. The officer is to be

paid, in respect of the performance of higher duties, an allowance

at a rate equal to the difference between his rate of annual

salary and the rate of annual salary payable to an officer

occupying the higher office. But it does not necessarily follow

that a higher-duties allowance will always fall within the

ordinary concept of salary. In Commissioner for Government

Transport v Kesby (supra), Gibbs J (with the concurrence of

Menzies, Walsh and Mason JJ) said (at 388):

"The question what is the 'salary' in a particular case is purely

one of fact. It does not simply depend on the terminology used to

describe the amount payable. Thus in the present case it can

hardly be doubted that the increments and the margins form part of

the 'salary' of bus drivers. Similarly a payment referred to as 'a

shift penalty' or as 'overtime' could be said to constitute part

of the 'salary' of bus drivers if in fact it was an amount payable

to bus drivers generally as remuneration for the services that bus

drivers generally are regularly required to perform."

Whether the allowances paid to the applicant fall within the

ordinary concept of salary is thus a question of fact and degree.

We are inclined to think that they do not.'

Although the allowance paid to the applicant was paid on a more regular basis than that to Eastman, we are inclined to the view that without the specific regulations to include the allowance as salary, as is the case in the 1976 Act and regulations, such an allowance does not fall within the ordinary concept of salary. If it did why did Parliament see the need to include in the 1976 Act and regulations provisions which made higher duty allowance in certain circumstances salary for superannuation purposes. If the allowance fell within the ordinary concept of salary there would be no need to do so. We therefore reject the applicant's submission on this point and find that the allowance paid to the applicant while he was a Captain in the AIF was not salary within the definition in s.4 of the Act.

35. Counsel for the applicant and the respondent accused each other of putting the cart before the horse. The applicant submitted that the pension the applicant is entitled to receive is to be determined by the salary he was receiving because that salary determined the number of units of pension for which the applicant should have contributed and those contributions in the applicant's case should have been made by the government of the day. It was the applicant's submission that the contributions calculated by the respondent at T11, T12, T15 and T16 and paid by the Commonwealth to the fund on behalf of the applicant were incorrectly calculated in that they took into account movements in the salary of a Lieutenant and not of a Captain. It was the submission of the applicant that this mistake had been made and that s.80c., which reads:

'80c.-(1.) Where the Board is satisfied that any pension or

benefit has, whether before or after the commencement of this

section, become payable under this Act as the result of the

service of a contributor as a member of the forces, the

commonwealth shall pay to the fund the amount by which the

proportion of he pension or benefit equivalent to the

contributions made by the contributor is less than the amount

of the pension or benefit.'

enables the mistake to be corrected by the Commonwealth paying to the Fund that which has been short paid. We reject this submission. Section 80c. of the Act is a provision which seeks to account for an abnormal drain on the Fund by invalidity retirements or widow's benefits occasioned by illness or death as a result of service of a contributor as a member of the forces. Such extra charges on the Fund could not have been foreseen in the actuarial calculations referred to above when discussing the Schedules to the Act. Indeed at T26/142 it is indicated that as from 16 November 1945 and pursuant to s.80c. the whole of the pension payable to the applicant was payable from consolidated revenue.

36. As to the applicant's submission that the pension entitlement is to be determined by salary, the respondent contends that this is not what the section says. Section 60N says 'he shall ... be entitled to the full pension for which he was contributing'. This phrase appears in ss.60N and 30(1) (retirement on the grounds of invalidity) and the phrase 'a pension according to the number of units for which he was contributing' appears in s.16A(3) (age 60 retirement) and in s.29 (maximum age retirement) and in s.60(M) (maximum age retirement (Military and Air Forces). On the death of a male contributor pension is payable to his widow equal to 'one half of the pension for which he was contributing' (s.31). There is no reference in any of these entitling sections to salary. Even if we are wrong in our finding as to the salary question, the Act is quite clear in its definition of entitlement to pension. Indeed, given that the Act makes provision for contributors of 40 years of age and over to elect not to contribute for additional units of pension otherwise allowed by increases in their salary, the only consistent yardstick is the number of units of pension for which a contributor is contributing.

37. Both counsel referred the Tribunal to the regulations contained in the Australian Military Regulations 1927, the Military Finance Regulations 1935, and the War Financial (Military Forces) Regulations 1941. We have considered all of that material and the submissions of counsel, along with all of the evidence and other material placed before us and find that the applicant's pension should be calculated on the basis of the number of units for which he was contributing at the time of his retirement.

38. Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed.
