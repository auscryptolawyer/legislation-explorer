QT94/64 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 109; 95 ATC 286; (1995) 30 ATR 1308 (24 April 1995)

Last Updated: 11 February 2009



INCOME TAX - whether a one-off payment by service station operator to Petroleum Company under an agreement is an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature or whether it is pre-payment of expenditure of a revenue nature - consideration of the character of the advantage sought by the payment - whether advantage was establishing business or carrying on business - whether advantage was to use Petroleum Company’s marketing services in carrying on business for purpose of producing assessable income.



Decision - decision affirmed.



Dissenting Decision - decision set aside and matter remitted to respondent to allow appropriate deduction in accordance with section 82KZM and adopting an eligible service period of three years from 5 October, 1990.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Sections 51, 51(1), 82KZM, 169

REASONS FOR DECISION



The applicant taxpayer is a service station operator. On 5 October, 1990, it paid a lump sum of $55,000 to a petroleum company (“the Petroleum Company”) pursuant to an agreement between them. In 1993, the taxpayer’s tax agents requested a ruling pursuant to section 169 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) and an amendment of the taxpayer’s taxation return for the financial year ended 30 June, 1991 to allow a deduction in respect of the $55,000 paid to the Petroleum Company. The payment was made in the year ending 30 June, 1991 but, because of carry forward losses, the claim for deduction has to be determined in relation to the financial year ended 30 June, 1992.



2. On 14 December, 1993, the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation (“the Commissioner”) issued a notice of amended assessment for the year of income ending 30 June, 1992 but rejected in full the taxpayer’s claim for a deduction in the sum of $55,000. The taxpayer’s tax agents lodged an objection on its behalf and this was disallowed in full by the Commissioner on 5 January, 1994. By an application lodged in this Tribunal on 4 March, 1994 the taxpayer sought review of the Commissioner’s decision.



3. At the hearing, Mr Morris Q.C. of Counsel appeared for the taxpayer and Mr Bland of Counsel appeared for the Commissioner. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the T documents”) were admitted in evidence together with a statement made by a director of, and shareholder in, the taxpayer (“the taxpayer’s director”) and a letter from the taxpayer’s solicitors dated 27 January, 1995 to the Tribunal enclosing a copy of a letter from the taxpayer’s accountants to the Commissioner. Oral evidence was given by the taxpayer’s director in support of the taxpayer’s case.



THE ISSUE



4. The issue for consideration in this case is whether a one-off payment made by the taxpayer pursuant to an agreement with the Petroleum Company is an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature for the purposes of sub-section 51(1) of the Act or whether it is a pre-payment of expenditure of a revenue nature.



BACKGROUND



5. The evidence on many of the matters forming the background to this case was not contradicted. With that in mind, I have made a number of findings of fact which I will now set out.



6. On the basis of the director’s evidence in his written statement and oral evidence, which is not contradicted in this regard, I find that, before commencing business at the service station it currently operates (“the second service station”), the taxpayer had operated another service station (“the first service station”). It had purchased the business of operating the first service station in approximately November, 1987 when there were still some six years left on the lease of the premises. The purchase price had been approximately $40,600 and included an amount to purchase the unexpired term of the lease.



7. Approximately two months after the taxpayer had purchased the business of the first service station, the Petroleum Company introduced a marketing programme and approached the director to participate in it. The director had various discussions with a representative from the Petroleum Company regarding the way in which the programme worked and the taxpayer ultimately entered an agreement with the Petroleum Company. That agreement (“the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement”) required the taxpayer to pay the Petroleum Company a single fee of $25,000. Later in these reasons, I will set out the evidence which the director gave as to the substance of his discussions with the representative.



8. Late in 1989, the taxpayer transferred to the second service station, which it still operates, and ceased business at the first service station. It considered the move worthwhile as the second service station has a much better exposure and a significantly higher turnover than the first. At the same time as it entered the lease for the second service station, the taxpayer also entered a new Petroleum Company Programme Co-operation Agreement. The taxpayer paid a single once only fee of $55,000.



9. I find that that there were three agreements entered into between the taxpayer and the Petroleum Company in relation to the taxpayer’s taking over the business of operating the second service station. They were what I have called the “Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement”, the “Lease” and the “Reseller Agreement” (T documents, pages 23-61, 62-87 and 88-102). I also find that there was a fourth agreement entered into and that was what I have called a “Meter Wholesale Agreement”. No copy of that is found in the documents I have been given but there is a reference to it in the Schedule to the Lease Agreement (page 18 of the lease and T documents, page 82).



10. The four agreements are collectively referred to as the “co-operation programme agreements” in the lease (T documents, page 65). The Lease recited that the taxpayer and the Petroleum Company had agreed to enter the other three agreements “... in relation to the conduct by the ... [taxpayer] of a service station ...” and that the Petroleum Company had agreed to provide the taxpayer with one copy of manuals, which I will call the “Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals” and which were said to contain detailed information in relation to the operation of a Petroleum Company service station as well as standards described as the “Co-operation Programme Standards” (T documents, page 65). The agreement then went on to make provision for such matters as the terms of the lease including its duration (three years), the rent payable ($42,600 for the first year, $48,600 for the second year and $53,400 for the third year), the taxpayer’s right to quiet enjoyment and the obligation upon the Petroleum Company to pay certain rates and taxes including State land tax and water and sewerage rates. The taxpayer also gave certain undertakings in relation to such matters as alterations to the site, damage to improvements, repairs, use of the site, payment of certain charges, insurance and access to the Petroleum Company. In addition, the taxpayer also agreed to comply with all co-operation programme agreements and the Co-operation Programme Standards specified in the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals as updated from time to time. (T documents, pages 67-74)



11. The Reseller Agreement commenced with a recital that the taxpayer proposed to carry on a service station business pursuant to a Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement and the other agreements comprising the co-operation programme agreements and that the taxpayer desired to obtain petroleum products from the Petroleum Company for that purpose. The agreement went on to provide that the Petroleum Company would sell to the taxpayer, and the taxpayer would purchase from the Petroleum Company, the taxpayer’s requirements of petrol, automotive distillate and automotive liquid petroleum gas for resale at or from the site. In addition, the Petroleum Company would also sell and the taxpayer would purchase the quantity of the Petroleum Company’s other products that complied with the Co-operation Programme Standards under the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement and that were sufficient to satisfy the customers’ expectations that the products would be available at the service station. The agreement went on to deal with matters such as delivery, price of the product re-sold and default. The Meter Wholesale Agreement was incorporated as part of the Reseller Agreement.



12. The Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement set out a number of matters in the recital clauses at the beginning of the document. Among the matters recited were:



“A. ... [‘The Petroleum Company’] has the right to the use within Australia of a system for the identification, lay-out and operation of service stations for the retailing of petroleum products and associated services, including know-how, the trade name and mark ...., a distinctive ... trade mark, distinctive ... signage and graphics, and the .... [sign] (hereinafter collectively called ... [‘the Petroleum Company Model’]).



B. ... [‘The Petroleum Company’] has developed considerable goodwill and reputation in the ... [‘Petroleum Company Model’] within Australia.



C. ... [‘The Petroleum Company’] and its dealers have been co-operating in order to develop and introduce at ... [‘the Petroleum Company’] service stations in Australia, a business package with the objective of ... [‘the Petroleum Company’] and its dealers achieving and maintaining market leadership in the petroleum retailing industry and comprising new and improved products, methods of marketing, merchandising and promotion, and new and improved means of customer service (hereinafter collectively called ... [‘the Co-operation Programme’].



D. Dealer wishes to be granted the right to carry on a service station business at the address specified in paragraph 3 of the Schedule (‘the Site’) selling petroleum products acquired from ... [‘the Petroleum Company’] and other associated products and services, to use the ... [‘ the Petroleum Company Model’] in the conduct of its business at the Site and to participate in the ... [‘the Co-operation Programme’] and ... [‘the Petroleum Company’] is willing to grant to Dealer the right to do so on the terms and conditions set out in this Agreement.



E. The parties propose to enter into immediately following execution hereof, certain other agreements described in paragraph 4 of the Schedule (hereinafter together with this Agreement collectively called ... [‘the Co-operation Programme Agreements’]) in relation to the dealer’s business at the Site.” (T documents, pages 27-28)



13. Under the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, the Petroleum Company gave the taxpayer, in carrying on its business at the second service station selling petroleum products acquired from the Petroleum Company together with other products and services approved by the Petroleum Company, the right to use the Petroleum Company Model in the conduct of the taxpayer’s business at the service station and to participate in the Co-operation Programme (clause 1, T documents, page 29). The Agreement commenced on 5 October, 1990 and remained in force for three years. Provision was made for renewal of the Agreement. It was also provided that the Agreement would end automatically if the Lease or the Reseller Agreement were to end. (clause 2, T documents, pages 29-30)



14. Under the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, the Petroleum Company agreed to provide, under the heading of “Training”,



“... from time to time as ... [the Petroleum Company] deems appropriate, provide assistance and training for Dealer and its employees in the efficient operation of a ... [Petroleum Company] service station in accordance with the ... [Co-operation Programme] including:-



(a) if Dealer is a new ... [Petroleum Company] dealer, a free ... [Petroleum Company Co-operation] pre-installation course, covering, amongst other things, customer relations, money management, merchandising and safety; and



(b) free accreditation training in accordance with ... [Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals].” (sub-clause 3(1), T documents, pages 30-31)



15. Under the heading “Co-operation Support”, the Petroleum Company would



“... in addition, from time to time as ... [the Petroleum Company] deems appropriate, provide further support to Dealer including:-



(a) advice and assistance in relation to Dealer’s business plans, financial affairs and accounting systems;



(b) obtaining national offers for Dealer and all other ... [Petroleum Company Co-operation] dealers through preferred suppliers;



(c) conducting incentive programs for ... [the Petroleum Company] dealers and their employees;



(d) advice based on extensive analysis and testing carried out with suppliers, in relation to allocation of shelf space;



(e) advice in relation to sales room and service station lay-out;



(f) point of sale material in ... [Petroleum Company] graphics;



(g) advice on merchandising;



(h) advice on signage;



(i) the planning and execution of marketing programs specifically directed to the benefit of ... [Petroleum Company] service station network.” (sub-clause 3, T documents, pages 31-32).



16. For its part, the taxpayer agreed to undertake certain courses of action. So, for example, after acknowledging that adherence of all Petroleum Company dealers to the Co-operation Programme Standards and to the policies and other requirements of the Co-operation Programme is essential for the success, goodwill and reputation of the Petroleum Company dealer network, the Petroleum Company Model and the Co-operation Programme, the taxpayer agreed to comply with those standards. The Co-operation Programme Standards are set out in the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals. Those manuals also set out information in relation to the operation of a Petroleum Company service station. The subjects covered include customer service, service station appearance, marketing, business and staff management, safety, operations and hygiene. (clause 4, T documents, page 33).



17. Under the heading, “Conduct of Business”, the taxpayer agreed to use its best endeavours to promote effectively and to maximise the sale of the Petroleum Company’s products and to comply with the other Co-operation Programme Agreements (clause 5, T documents, page 33).



18. In clause 6, which was headed “Co-operation Programme Expenses and Fee”, the taxpayer acknowledged that costs were incurred in establishing and maintaining the Co-operation Programme. It agreed, therefore, to pay an Accreditation Fee of $55,000 to the Petroleum Company (sub-clause 6(1) and the Schedule). Although an initial Accreditation Fee could be paid with the balance paid at a later time (see sub-clauses 6(2) and (3)), the taxpayer paid the one lump sum under sub-clause 6(1). Sub-clause 6(4) set out the way in which that Accreditation Fee would be used when it said:



“... [The Petroleum Company] shall use the initial Accreditation Fee payments under (2) by Dealer [taxpayer] and other ... [Petroleum Company] dealers in paying for ... [Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme] expenses, including but not limited to, the expenses of training, marketing advice and support, advertising and promotions, and general set-up and operating expenses. When received, all Accreditation Fees belong to ... [the Petroleum Company], and may be used at its discretion subject to the commitment described in the preceding sentence.” (T documents, pages 34-35)



19. Clause 7 was headed “Marketing and Sales Promotion”. It dealt with the Co-operation Programme Marketing Council (“the Council”) formed by the Petroleum Company and representatives of its dealers. That Council was established to develop an annual Co-operation Programme Marketing Programme and to budget for network related marketing and promotion. From 1 January, 1990, the Petroleum Company allocated from the rent paid by the taxpayer under the Lease Agreement one-tenth of a cent for each litre of petrol, automotive distillate and automotive liquefied petroleum gas the taxpayer purchased from the Petroleum Company for sale at or from the service station. The amount allocated from the taxpayer and from all other dealers in the Co-operation Programme in this way was matched by the Petroleum Company which used the total amount to fund the annual Co-operation Programme Marketing Programme developed by the Council. Each year, the taxpayer would be given a statement of the allocations, marketing fees and total expenditure on the marketing programme.



20. The taxpayer also acknowledged and recognised the Petroleum Company’s interest and right to the Petroleum Company Model and agreed not to infringe, use or imitate any part of that Model (including any manuals, operating guidelines, forms or other literature or advertising relating to the Model) without the written consent of the Petroleum Company. It would keep confidential the Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals and all other elements of the Petroleum Company Model not already in the public domain. The taxpayer could use the Petroleum Company's trade marks, brand names and other elements of the Petroleum Company Model only in accordance with the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement. It could use the Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals only in connection with the promotion, handling and sale of Petroleum Company products and other products or types of products and services approved by the Petroleum Company. These matters were dealt with in clause 8, which was headed “Trade Marks and Brand Names”. (T documents, pages 36-37)



21. Under the heading of “Assignment”, it was provided that neither party



“shall assign, charge or otherwise encumber any of its rights or obligations under this Agreement, or the other ... [‘co-operation programme agreements’], without the prior written consent of the other party. Where legislation requires it, that consent is not to be unreasonably withheld.”



Clause 14 goes on to provide that any assignment, charge or other encumbrance of either rights or obligations is void unless prior written consent is obtained. Where the dealer is a company or the trustee of a trust, provision is made for certain specified changes in the beneficial ownership of the trust or control of the company or entitlement to its dividends. Where those specified changes occur, the dealer must offer to the Petroleum Company to assign its rights under the co-operation programme agreements to a person nominated by the Petroleum Company. It must do so for a consideration equal to their then market value. If the dealer and the petroleum company cannot agree upon the market value it is to be determined by independent arbitration.



22. The Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement commenced on 5 October, 1990 and continued in force for three years. Subject to any exceptions provided for by the Petroleum Retail Marketing Franchise Act 1980, the Petroleum Company was to renew the agreement in accordance with it. Even if that legislation were to be repealed, the Petroleum Company was obliged to offer to renew the co-operation programme agreements for a further two terms, each of three years. (clause 2, T documents, pages 29-30) Sub-clause 2(3) makes it clear that the agreement terminates when either the Lease or Reseller Agreement terminates.



THE EVIDENCE



23. A director of the taxpayer gave oral evidence as to manner in which it came to enter the Co-operation Programme Agreement with the Petroleum Company in respect of the business the taxpayer conducted at its first service station. He said that a representative from the Petroleum Company had visited him at the first service station and explained that the concept involved a system of advertising and promotion for the Petroleum Company’s dealerships throughout Australia. This would replace the efforts made by individual dealers to advertise and to conduct their own promotions.



24. In his experience, the director said, the service station business is highly competitive. Other petroleum companies have developed very effective campaigns to attract customers to their dealerships. He also believed that there was not a great deal of “customer loyalty” among the general public and the dealership they choose is influenced by a number of factors. Those factors include price and promotions. If there is a “price war”, they will choose the dealership with the cheapest price but, if that is not a factor, the public is attracted by promotions such as “free give-aways”.



25. As he was aware of the highly competitive nature of the retail fuel industry, the director considered it imperative for the continuing profitability of the taxpayer that it enter the Petroleum Company’s Co-operation Programme system. He could envisage that, unless the taxpayer did so, revenue would be lost and the service station’s profitability would be reduced. As a member of the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, he expected that the taxpayer would, at the very least, maintain its existing market share and so maintain its then current levels of revenue and profitability. He was also optimistic that the taxpayer would produce increased revenue and profits as a result of the Petroleum Company’s promotions.



26. At the time the taxpayer took up the lease of the second service station, it also entered a new Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement. As the director had previous experience with the system at the first service station, he did not think it necessary to discuss the details with anyone at the Petroleum Company about the way in which it operated and what it involved.



27. As it turned out, the director felt that the Co-operation Programme was not as successful as he had hoped it would be. Despite that, he was convinced that it was necessary that the taxpayer remain a Co-operation Programme dealer when it acquired the second service station. This was based on the director’s view that it was necessary in order to maximise the potential revenue and profitability of the business. Indeed, the director considered that the higher exposure and greater turnover at the second service station meant that it was imperative to be associated with advertising and promotions which would enhance the taxpayer’s competitiveness against other petroleum companies.



28. The director said that the Petroleum Company provided on-going training for the staff, marketing advice as and when required, advertising and promotions. The Petroleum Company supplied copies of the Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals. Those manuals, which are updated regularly and remain the property of the Petroleum Company. No changes were made to the structure of lay-out of the service station and no plant, equipment or other fittings or fixtures were supplied to the taxpayer under the Co-operation Programme Agreement.



29. In relation to the advertising and promotions conducted under the Co-operation Programme Agreement, the director said that:



“The advertising and promotions provided by ... [the Petroleum Company] usually apply for quite a limited period of time - no more than two or three months. It is my experience that the promotional campaigns organised by each of the major oil companies for their dealerships tend to change very rapidly, as each of them struggles to maintain its competitiveness with the others. There is no way that the Taxpayer would be able to develop such rapidly changing advertising and promotional campaigns, without the assistance of ... [Petroleum Company] through the ... [Co-operation Programme model] system. It is my experience that, once a particular advertising or promotional campaign has expired, it has little or no on-going benefit for the Taxpayer’s business; such advertising and promotional campaigns only seem to influence potential customers whilst they are current, and as soon as a particular advertising or promotional campaign has finished motorists have a tendency to take their business to the Taxpayer’s competitors unless and until a fresh advertising or promotional campaign is undertaken.” (Exhibit 2, paragraph 18)



30. In his oral evidence, the director said that, if looked at overall, promotions normally operate on a “break-even” basis. This means that if the taxpayer is required to give away fuel, for example, it is usually reimbursed its value. In cross-examination, he was asked whether the effectiveness or otherwise of the marketing campaigns affected the taxpayer’s profitability. He said that some led to increased profits and, some led to the taxpayer's incurring a loss. The marketing campaigns were never judged by reference to individual dealers, however, but by their overall national effect.



31. The director said that the taxpayer would find it almost impossible to run its own marketing programme and promotions as it could not meet the costs of, for example, giving away free fuel for a two month period. It was important to work with the Petroleum Company or with any other petroleum company with whom a dealer might be associated. This meant that the taxpayer had to run promotions whether they were a success or a failure.



SUBMISSIONS



32. In the following paragraphs, I will set out only the broad framework of the submissions made on behalf of each party.



33. Mr Morris submitted, in essence, that the taxpayer paid the sum of $55,000 to the Petroleum Company in order to receive benefits of a revenue nature. That this was the intention of the taxpayer was clear from the evidence of its director. If the intention was to be determined by reference to the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement and not by reference to the evidence of the taxpayer’s director, it was apparent from sub-clause 6(4) that the sum was to be used for on-going operational expenses.



34. That this was the intention behind the payment was reinforced by the fact that the taxpayer has actually received the benefits it was promised under the agreement e.g. promotions and services. None of those benefits had any lasting quality. At the end of each promotion, for example, any advantage the taxpayer received during the course of the promotion disappeared. There was nothing of a capital nature left at the end of the day and nothing that the taxpayer could sell. It makes no difference to the character of the payment that the taxpayer did not receive the increased profitability it expected to receive.



35. On behalf of the Commissioner, Mr Bland submitted that the correct approach to the determination of the issue is to analyse the whole of the contractual arrangements between the parties in order to identify the direct or indirect advantage which the payment is intended to gain for the taxpayer. In other words, Mr Bland submitted, I must identify what the payment was really made for.



36. When the contractual arrangements are analysed, Mr Bland submitted, the advantage for which the taxpayer paid the sum of $55,000 was the right to use the Petroleum Company’s trade name and trade marks for nine years. That is to say, the taxpayer paid for the right to use the Petroleum Company’s intellectual property. That gave the taxpayer an advantage of a lasting character which will enure for the benefit of the taxpayer's business. Consequently, the payment is of a capital nature.



CONSIDERATION



37. Sub-section 51(1) of the Act provides that:



“All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”



38. The issue, as I have said, turns upon whether the payment of $55,000 is an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature within the meaning of sub-section 51(1). There is no suggestion by the Commissioner that the payment is properly characterised as an outgoing of a private or domestic nature or that it was paid in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income. I agree that the payment does not come within any of those categories.



39. The distinction between outgoings of a capital nature and outgoings of a revenue nature has been considered in a number of cases to which I was referred and I will refer to each of them. In general terms, the distinction was described by Dixon J, as he then was, in Sun Newspapers Ltd and Associated Newspapers Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 (Latham CJ, Dixon and McTiernan JJ) when he said:



“The distinction between expenditure and outgoings on revenue account and on capital account corresponds with the distinction between the business entity, structure, or organization set up or established for the earnings of profit and the process by which such an organization operates to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the difference between the outlay and returns representing profit or loss. The business structure or entity or organization may assume any of an almost infinite variety of shapes and it may be difficult to comprehend under one description all the forms in which it may be manifested. In a trade or pursuit where little or no plant is required, it may be represented by no more than the intangible elements constituting what is commonly called goodwill, that is, widespread or general reputation, habitual patronage by clients or customers and an organized method of serving their needs. At the other extreme it may consist in a great aggregate of buildings, machinery and plant all assembled and systematized as the material means by which an organized body of men produce and distribute commodities or perform services. But in spite of the entirely different forms, material and immaterial, in which it may be expressed, such sources of income contain or consist in what has been called a ‘profit-yielding subject,’ the phrase of Lord Blackburn in United Collieries Ltd v. Inland Revenue Commissioners (1930) SC 215, at p.220.” (pages 359-360)



40. While the general distinction may be easy to state, Dixon J went on to say, the distinction may be difficult to determine in a practical situation. He reviewed the cases which had attempted to find some test or standard to determine whether outgoings are of a capital or revenue nature. He concluded:



“There are, I think, three matters to be considered, (a) the character of the advantage sought, and in this its lasting qualities may play a part, (b) the manner in which it is to be used, relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and under the former head recurrence may play its part, and (c) the means adopted to obtain it; that is by providing a periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payment or by making a final provision or payment so as to secure future use or enjoyment.” (page 363)



41. In the case of Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 68; (1953) 89 CLR 428 (Williams ACJ, Webb, Fullagar, Kitto and Taylor JJ), Fullagar J succinctly stated the relevant questions to be:



“(1) What is the money really paid for? - and (2) Is what it is really paid for, in truth and in substance, a capital asset?” (page 454)



42. The Privy Council has considered a similar issue in relation to New Zealand legislative provisions in Europa Oil (N.Z.) Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioner [1976] 1 W.L.R. 464 (Viscount Dilhorne, Lord Diplock, Lord Edmund-Davies and Sir Garfield Barwick, Lord Wilberforce dissenting). Lord Diplock, who delivered the judgement of the majority, said that:



“... it is not the economic results sought to be obtained by making the expenditure that is determinative of whether the expenditure is deductible or not; it is the legal rights enforceable by the taxpayer that he acquires in return for making it. The difficulty to which the section gives rise is not one of interpretation of the words it uses, but of the application of those words to particular transactions which may be entered into in the course of business where those contractual arrangements are complicated and involve a multiplicity of parties.” (page 472)



43. In looking at those arrangements,



“... the court was not bound by the description, such as `price of goods,’ attached to it in the taxpayer’s own accounts or in a particular contract, if upon an analysis of the contractual arrangements taken as a whole under which the payment was made it appeared that its true legal character did not accord with that description.” (page 472)



44. In Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v South Australian Battery Makers Proprietary Limited [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 (Gibbs ACJ, Stephen, Jacobs, Murphy and Aickin JJ), Gibbs ACJ expanded upon the passage I have just quoted and said that:



“Their Lordships could not have meant to suggest that in every case the character of an outgoing must be determined by having regard only to the contractual or other legal rights that the taxpayer acquired in return for it. That would indeed have been inconsistent with the principle stated by Dixon J. in Hallstrom’s Case, and with cases too numerous to mention in which payments made ‘voluntarily and on the grounds of commercial expediency’ (to use the words of Viscount Cave L.C. in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v. Atherton [1926] AC 205, at p.212) have been held deductible as outgoings of a revenue kind although the taxpayer obtained no legally enforceable rights in return for them. However it is unnecessary for the decision of the present case to consider whether the second Europa Case ([1976] 1 WLR 464; [1976] 1 All ER 503) laid down a principle in terms too wide to be applied to s.51. ... If on the other hand that statement needs qualification so far as Australia is concerned, the decisions in the Europa Cases, and indeed the dissenting judgments in those cases, support the conclusion that it is the advantage which the expenditure was intended to gain, directly or indirectly, for the taxpayer that is relevant in determining the character of the expenditure, ...” (pages 659-660)



45. Hill J summarised the effect of the previous authority in the field when, in Goodman Fielder Wattie Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4438, he said:



“The judgment in Sun Newspapers case makes it clear that it is necessary to consider carefully the nature of the business which carried on, so as to be able to distinguish between recurrent expenditure, that is to say `expenditure which is made to meet continuous demand’ (per Rowlatt J in Ounsworth v Vickers Ltd [1915] 3 KB 267-273) and that expenditure which is made once and for all. A pharmaceutical company, the business of which includes continuing research and development as part of the continuous or constant demand for expenditure in its business, does not each time that expenditure is incurred make an outlay of capital or of capital nature. Its business, when properly analysed, includes its research and development, at least in the ordinary case. No doubt, there are matters of degree involved and in a particular case the research and development may be concentrated on a product so far removed from the day to day products of the taxpayer, that the expenditure cannot be properly seen as part of its working expenditure.” (page 4,449)



46. Shortly before the Battery Makers’ case was decided, the Full Court of the Federal Court had considered the issue and had said:



“In determining whether an outgoing is of a capital nature, it is necessary to determine what it is incurred for. Sometimes one consideration may point clearly in one direction, while the other, and vaguer indications, point in a contrary direction. It has been said ‘it is a common-sense appreciation of all guiding features which must provide the ultimate answer’ (B.P. Australia Limited v. F.C. of T. (1966) A.C. 224 at p.264).



A test frequently quoted is that of Lord Cave in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Limited v. Atherton (1926) A.C. 205. His Lordship (at pp.213-214) said:-



‘But when an expenditure is made, not only once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade, I think there is very good reason (in the absence of special circumstances leading to an appropriate conclusion) for treating such an expenditure as properly attributable not to revenue but to capital’.



This statement places emphasis on what is acquired as well as upon the character of the expenditure. It is a positive statement. It does not follow that the negative furnishes a test of what is not capital (see John Fairfax & Sons Limited v. F.C. of T. [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 C.L.R. 30 at p.36).” (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cliffs International Inc. (1977) 77 ATC 4, 564 at 4,573, Bowen CJ, Franki and Brennan JJ)



47. More recently, Lockhart J has considered whether a levy paid by a manufacturer of tobacco products to an industry association was deductible under sub-section 51(1). The expenditure by the manufacturer was to defeat the passage of the legislation which would curtail sales and advertising of tobacco products and probably result in a loss of its market share. The legislation did not pose a threat to the existence of its business or to any of its capital assets. The Commissioner argued that the levy paid was a payment of capital expenditure as the taxpayer was seeking to obtain an enduring advantage, in the sense of the right to advertise in the manner in which it chose or, alternatively, as the payment was made to preserve the taxpayer’s business.



48. After considering a number of cases including the Sun Newspapers case, to which I have referred, Lockhart J concluded:



“A company which manufactures and supplies tobacco products these days is under fire. Community attitudes towards smoking have changed considerably in recent times. Pressures are exerted upon legislatures, both Federal and State, to prohibit or reduce the sale and consumption of tobacco products. On the other hand, the market for the consumption of tobacco products is large. Pressure groups are at work. As legislatures react to public and scientific opinion there are repercussions on the business of the tobacco producers and suppliers. But this is today an ongoing part of the setting in which those companies carry on business. Expenditure of the kind with which this case is concerned is on revenue account. The capital of Rothmans’ business was in no way increased by the expenditures in question. The expenditure arose from Rothmans’ commercial activities in the course of carrying on its business. It was incidental to the carrying on of Rothmans’ business. There is a clear relation between the expenditure and the carrying on of that business. It was incurred in carrying on its business and was not of a capital nature.” (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Rothmans of Pall Mall (Australia) Ltd (1992) 92 ATC 4508 at 4,514)



49. Mr Morris drew a comparison between the current case and that of B.P. Australia Limited v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386 (Lord Reid, Lord Morris of Borth-y-Gest, Lord Pearce, Lord Upjohn and Lord Wilberforce). B.P. paid to certain service station owners lump sums to assist them to defray some of their expenditure or generally to assist them. In return, the service station owners promised to sell only B.P.’s brand of petrol for a set number of years.



50. Lord Pearce, who delivered the judgement of their Lordships, noted that, in 1951, there had been considerable changes in the way in which petrol was marketed in Australia. Apart from the war years, various brands of petrol were sold in competition with each other at each service station. Each producer owned particular tanks and pumps at each service station and let them to the retailer at a nominal figure. In 1951, the Shell Company suddenly announced its intention of securing economy of distribution by introducing the “solo site service station plan” whereby it would supply petrol to service stations on the basis that those station would deal exclusively with Shell. Within a few months of Shell’s introducing its plan, B.P. had been asked to remove its pumps from over a quarter of the service stations it had previously supplied. B.P. then embarked on the arrangements I have outlined in the previous paragraph.



51. Lord Pearce noted that considerable emphasis had been placed on the fact that B.P.’s whole existence was threatened by the

events that had occurred during 1951 and continued:



“But this is not a decisive factor. Whenever a business finds that its trade rivals are getting ahead of it, its existence is threatened. The seriousness of the situation has, however, this much relevance, that it provided ample justification for capital expenditure in the reorganization of its business structure if that should be necessary or desirable. It demolishes any argument that the occasion was too trivial or too ordinary to enable counter-measures in the means of marketing to assume the structural quality of capital expenditure. But it still leaves unanswered the question whether the steps which were taken to meet the crisis were in fact of a capital or of a revenue nature.” (pages 393-394)



52. He continued:



“Where a trader buys out a rival in order to secure his goodwill or to suppress it and so provide or maintain a clear field for his own enterprise over a substantial period, there is a definite prima facie towards a capital payment. But in the present case B.P. was not achieving a monopoly nor buying off competition nor obtaining any substantial area for its own domain. Although one retailer was tied to B.P., the retailer next door could still buy some other brand and the passing motorist could do likewise.” (page 395)



53. Lord Pearce considered this aspect further in relation to the advantage which B.P. sought to obtain when he said:



“The advantage which B.P. sought was to promote sales and obtain orders for petrol by up-to-date marketing methods, the only methods which could now prevail. Since orders were now and would in future be only obtainable from tied retailers, it must obtain ties with retailers. Its real object however was not the tie but the orders which would flow from the tie. To obtain ties it had to satisfy the appetite of the retailers by paying out sums for a period of years, whose amount was dependent on the estimated value of the retailer as a customer and the length of the period. The payment of such sums became part of the regular conduct of the business. It became one of the current necessities of the trade.” (page 398)



54. On this aspect, he later said:



“The benefit was to be used in the continuous and recurrent struggle to get orders and sell petrol. The agreements were not strictly `bundles of orders’ but they were the basis of them and made orders inevitable. The retailer was bound to sell none but B.P.’s petrol and to increase the sale of its products to the best of its ability. This means that in practice he was bound to give orders for petrol which B.P. was bound to supply. Although the price and time of delivery were not specified these would be implied by law as reasonable. No fresh consensus between the parties was necessary. All that was needed was that the retailer should specify from time to time what quantity he required. Thus the agreements merged in and became part of the ordinary process of selling. These facts point to the expenditure being a revenue item.” (page 405)



55. As in the other cases, Lord Pearce proceeded to look at the whole of the arrangements entered into and their purpose before concluding that, on balance, the relevant considerations tilted the scales in favour of the expenditure’s being revenue and not capital outgoings.



56. The contrary conclusion was reached, but in quite different circumstances, by Gummow J in W Whebe Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1993) 27 ATR 172. The question he had to decide was whether legal expenses incurred in respect of proceedings taken by the taxpayers in the Federal Court under the Petroleum Retail Marketing Franchise Act 1980 were in the nature of capital or revenue expenditure.



57. Gummow J found that the franchise agreements gave the taxpayers all the means necessary to carry on a business as a Caltex service station at two particular sites. At the time of the dispute between them and Caltex, the taxpayers had never carried on a business at either of the sites as anything other than as a Caltex service station. Gummow J concluded:



“In a sense, one might broadly have described the businesses of the taxpayers as that of service station operator. But the arrangements with Caltex in each case gave the taxpayer the vital means for the conduct of such a business. This was not merely the supply of product but the means of visible association with the goodwill and reputation of a major player in the petroleum industry. The business which existed under the aegis of the relevant agreements and the protection of the Franchise Act were two Caltex Service Stations and it was the severe threat to the continuation thereof which precipitated the litigation in this court.



Indeed, in the case of Chullora, what was at stake was the occupation of the site itself. Here Caltex had set about bringing to an end any business association between the taxpayer and that site on which its only operations were conducted. It is true that the Bankstown site itself was not in jeopardy and that arrangements later were made with BP Australia Ltd. But the business of the taxpayer as a Caltex Service Station was in jeopardy and the object of the expenditure here was, at the very least, to preserve that business and thereby avoid the need to replace it with another service station business.



Accordingly, the expenditures were of a capital nature, being in relation to the very business structures of the taxpayers.”(page 180)



58. In view of the principles in these cases, I must determine the character of the outgoing by determining the character of the advantage sought and I must do so in light of all relevant matters including the nature of the taxpayer’s business and the arrangements it has made with the Petroleum Company both generally and in relation to the lump sum.



59. With regard to the nature of the taxpayer’s business, I am satisfied that, generally described, it is to operate a service station. I am also satisfied that its business can be more particularly described as the operation of a Petroleum Company service station. My finding of fact is based on two aspects of the evidence. The first is the evidence of the director that the taxpayer has previously operated a Petroleum Company service station when it operated its first service station.



60. The second basis is formed by the contractual arrangements that have been entered into between the taxpayer and the Petroleum Company. The Lease Agreement specifically provides that the taxpayer will use the site leased to it as a Petroleum Company service station and not for any other purpose without the permission of the Petroleum Company. There is no evidence to suggest that the Petroleum Company has given any such permission.



61. When the four co-operation programme agreements are read together, they add weight to the finding that the taxpayer must sell only Petroleum Company products, or those approved by it, and so is a Petroleum Company service station. So, for example, the Reseller Agreement provides that the Petroleum Company will sell, and the taxpayer will buy, the taxpayer’s requirements of a broad range of petroleum products, including petrol, automotive distillate and automotive liquid petroleum gas for resale from the site. There is no allowance for the taxpayer to purchase those particular products from any other supplier. The Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement gives the taxpayer, in selling the Petroleum Company’s products from the site the taxpayer leases from it, the right to sell those products according to the Petroleum Company Model and using the Petroleum Company’s trade marks and brand names. It may not sell any other products using that Model. For its part, the taxpayer must conduct its business in a manner to protect and enforce that Model.



62. I will turn now the nature of the payment of $55,000. The director understood that the taxpayer paid it so that it could take advantage of the system of advertising and promotion in place for the Petroleum Company’s dealerships. This would replace the taxpayer’s individual efforts and maintain its profitability in a highly competitive industry. The director also said that the taxpayer would be provided with training of its staff in the standards required of a Co-operation Programme service station, on-going marketing advice and support and establishing the service station to operate as part of the Co-operation Programme service station network by providing manuals which the Petroleum Company would update from time to time.



63. I do not doubt that the director honestly stated his understanding of why the money was paid but I need to look also to the other evidence to determine whether there was any other reason for its payment. When I look at the four co-operation programme agreements, I find support for the director’s belief in, for example, sub-clause 6(2) but I also find that his belief is only one aspect of the whole picture.



64. An examination of the recital clauses in each of the agreements reveals that the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement is the first in time of the four agreements to be entered between the taxpayer and the Petroleum Company. Reference is made in paragraph E to the parties’ proposing “... to enter into immediately following execution ...” of that agreement certain other agreements (T documents, page 28). The Lease confirms that this must have been so for it refers in clause B in the recital that the Petroleum Company has agreed (and not that it will agree) to provide to the taxpayer one copy of the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Reference Manuals. Those manuals are supplied pursuant to the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement (T documents, page 65).



65. Returning to the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, the Petroleum Company has granted the taxpayer two rights. The first is to use the Petroleum Company Model in carrying on a service station business at the premises leased from the Petroleum Company and in selling the Petroleum Company’s products or those approved by it. The second right granted to the taxpayer is that it could participate in the Co-operation Programme. The rights were granted subject to the taxpayer’s entering the other agreements (clause 1, T documents, page 29). All three agreements in the T documents reveal that they may be terminated in the same way i.e. if there is a ground of termination as described in sub-section 16(2) of the Retail Marketing Franchise Act 1980.



66. Like the initial period of three years stated as the life of the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, the term of the lease of the site is also three years. Unlike the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, which contemplates extensions up to a further six years, there is no provision made in the lease for extensions of the initial term. I have, however, no reason to doubt that extensions would be the subject of negotiation between the Petroleum Company and the taxpayer.



67. The Reseller Agreement does not stipulate a period of years or months for which it is to remain in force. It limits its term by reference to the term of the lease and then goes on to state that “Otherwise this Agreement shall apply during the period of the ... [Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement]” (clause 2, T documents, page 92)



68. Paragraph 2(b) of the Lease provides that the Lease ends automatically should there be an end of either the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement or the Reseller Agreement in relation to the taxpayer’s business at the site (T documents, pages 66-67).



69. When I look at the overall operation of the contractual arrangements between the taxpayer and the Petroleum Company, I have concluded that the three co-operation agreements with which I have been provided are interlocking and interdependent. One cannot exist without the other. While I find that, I also find that the keystone in the interlocking arrangements between the taxpayer and the Petroleum Company is the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement. While that operates, the taxpayer can operate a Petroleum Company Service Station to sell products of, or approved by, the Petroleum Company at a Petroleum Company site, and be assured that it will be supplied with the Petroleum Company’s products. When it does not, the taxpayer has no such rights.



70. Certainly, the rights the taxpayer acquires under the arrangements are, initially, for a very short period. I find that it is for three years for that is the period of the lease and, without the lease (or possibly a similar lease of another site), the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement is at an end. If it is extended, it is important to note that the Petroleum Co-operation Programme Agreement contemplates that it too may be extended. It may be extended for two periods, each of three years, and no further amount beyond the initial $55,000 is payable. Assuming that the term of the lease is not extended, at the end of that term, the taxpayer has nothing as a result of paying the $55,000. It no longer has the right to use the Petroleum Company’s trade marks, Model or to purchase petroleum products from it. It cannot even keep the manuals but has to hand them back to the Petroleum Company. If I was to look only to these matters, I would be inclined to state that the payment is in the nature of revenue even though the payment was made in a lump sum which, in some circumstances, could give it the air of a capital payment.



71. I must, however, look at all of the circumstances and, when I look at the contractual arrangements to which I have referred, I find that the taxpayer could not have operated the particular business at the second service station had it not paid the $55,000. Had it not paid the sum and entered the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement, it would not have been able to enter the Lease or the Reseller Agreement. That is to say, it could not operate as a Petroleum Company service station. As that was its business, it could not operate the particular business at all. The payment, therefore, had to be made to enter the agreement and to establish the business. The character of the advantage sought was not the right simply to use Petroleum Company’s marketing services in carrying on its business but rather the right to establish business at all as a Petroleum Company service station. That right, together with the other rights and obligations acquired under the co-operation programme agreements are regarded as having some enduring value, if only for so long as the agreements remain on foot, for clause 14 of the Petroleum Company Co-operation Agreement contemplates that the rights and obligations may be assigned, charged or encumbered or that they may be transferred for value (see paragraph 21 above).



72. Certainly the sum was not described this way in the agreement. It was described as an “accreditation fee” and it was said to be paid for a number of services. Its description in that way does not sway me from my conclusion, based as it is on an analysis of the agreements, as to why the $55,000 was paid. I am strengthened in my conclusion by the evidence of the director as to the minimal services, by way of advice, he actually received from the Petroleum Company and by the separate allocation of an amount from the rental payments for a marketing and promotion programme. Had the $55,000 been paid for the services and marketing advice, advertising and promotion programmes referred to in clause 6, it is difficult to understand why a separate amount need be allocated for network related marketing and promotion under clause 7.



73. Taking all of the matters into account, it follows that the payment of $55,000 was an outgoing of a capital nature and I affirm the decision under review.

REASONS FOR DECISION



Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Mr J D Horrigan (Member)



We respectfully agree with paragraphs 1 to 68 of the learned Deputy President’s reasons for decision and adopt them as our own. However, we have come to a different conclusion and will state our reasons briefly, given that our view will not be determinative in making the decision of the Tribunal.



2. Although not entirely clear from the evidence we are prepared to infer that the applicant carries on business as a service station operator. To enable it to carry on business the applicant must acquire premises from which to carry on business and it is reasonable to assume that it does so by leasing service station premises from an owner, whether it be a petroleum company or some other person.



3. In the particular instance, the applicant entered into arrangements with Petroleum Company for the purpose of acquiring the lease of a service station, so that the applicant could continue to carry on business as a service station operator. The applicant, it seems, preferred to operate a Petroleum Company service station and it entered into the three agreements for that overall purpose (ie the operation of a Petroleum Company service station). The applicant was not offered any option in relation to the three agreements. All three were to be entered into to acquire the right to operate the service station.



4. It was the Lease Agreement which gave the applicant an interest in the premises including the right to occupy and carry on business in the premises owned by Petroleum Company. The Reseller Agreement gave the applicant the right to purchase petroleum products from Petroleum Company and to resell those products.



5. The Lease and Reseller Agreements, once entered into, gave to the applicant the structure by which it carried on business as a service station operator. Those two agreements created the structure.



6. On the other hand the Petroleum Company Co-operation Programme Agreement gave the applicant access to the marketing strategies of Petroleum Company. As already noted this agreement was a condition precedent to the Lease and Reseller Agreements. The condition precedent was to the effect that Petroleum Company would only enter into the Lease and Reseller Agreements if the applicant had entered into the Co-operation Programme Agreement.



7. The advantage sought and obtained under the Co-operation Programme Agreement was not access to the structural assets of the service station and to Petroleum Company products. The agreement makes it clear that Petroleum Company would provide access to assistance and training for the efficient operation of a Petroleum Company service station and participation in Petroleum Company marketing and merchandising programs. The agreement was concerned with the provision of services relevant to the day to day operation of the service station and not with establishing or preserving the business structure. The force of the terms of the agreement was to ensure that the service station’s day to day operations were run in such a manner as to be to the advantage of both the applicant and Petroleum Company. In a sense that could be construed as an enduring benefit to the applicant but not, in our understanding of the law, in the sense envisaged by Viscount Cave L.C. in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v Atherton [1926] AC 205 and the subsequent decisions of the High Court. We are satisfied that the $55,000 was paid for Petroleum Company's assistance in the day to day operation of the service station as a Petroleum Company service station. Petroleum Company agreed to provide its services in circumstances clearly connected with the day to day operations in the course of the applicant’s income producing activities. Petroleum Company adopted a role not dissimilar to that of a professional marketing consultant engaged to assist a business in the conduct of its income producing activities. The mere fact of the payment being made “up front” does not, in our view, alter the essential character of the outgoing. The character of the advantage sought was to use Petroleum Company’s marketing services in carrying on the applicant’s business for the purpose of gaining assessable income.



8. While we recognise the strength of the case for saying that the outgoing is of a capital nature we are satisfied, on balance, that the $55,000 was an outgoing on revenue account deductible under section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. We would therefore set aside the objection decision under review and remit the matter to the respondent to allow the appropriate deduction in accordance with section 82KZM of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 adopting an eligible service period of three years from 5 October 1990.
