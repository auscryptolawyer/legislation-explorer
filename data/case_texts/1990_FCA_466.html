Re Hanson Subdivisions Pty Limited v the Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 466; 90 Atc 5068/21 Atr 1117 (26 November 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: HANSON SUBDIVISIONS PTY LIMITED

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. G59 of 1990

FED No. 662

Administrative Law - Income Tax

90 ATC 5068/21 ATR 1117

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Davies J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Law - appeal from Administrative Appeals Tribunal - whether error of law.

Income Tax - purpose in purchasing land - purchaser a property developer - onus of proof.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) - ss.25(1), 26(a)

HEARING

SYDNEY

26:11:1990

Counsel for the applicant: Mr R.F. Edmonds

Solicitor for the applicant: Ms F.E. Giuseppi

Counsel for the respondent: Mr R.B. Wilson

Solicitor for the respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The application be dismissed with costs.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

This is an appeal from a decision of an Administrative Appeals Tribunal which upheld the view taken in an assessment issued in relation to the income of the applicant, Hanson Subdivisions Pty Limited, for the year ended 30 June 1982, that certain profits derived in earlier years from the sale of land in the Shire of Eurobodalla were assessable income. The issue came into the 1982 year by reason of carry-forward losses. As the appeal is brought under s.44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth), it is limited to questions of law.

2. The Tribunal found:-

"I have not been persuaded that the property was not

acquired for the dominant purpose of profit-making by

sale, nor for the carrying on or carrying out of a

profit-making undertaking or scheme, nor that, so far

as the land in question is concerned, that it was

acquired otherwise than in the course of the ordinary

trading activities of the company and later realised in

the course of those activities. To that extent the

application fails."

The Tribunal therefore had in mind the provisions of ss.25(1) and 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth)("the Act").

3. The applicant contended before the Tribunal that, in purchasing in early 1973 the property, which was rural land, the applicant had the purpose of selling off a number of 5 acre lots and of using the remainder of the property as a farm. Chapman v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 CLR 167 was relied upon.

4. The Tribunal turned its attention to what the applicant did with the land and to what the Managing Director of the applicant, Mr A.J. Hanson, had in mind at the time of the purchase. It has not been suggested in the present appeal that the Tribunal was incorrect in looking to see what were the factors which actuated Mr Hanson to arrange for the purchase of the property, to whether some purpose other than profit-making by sale was the predominating purpose. Nor is it in dispute that, in considering this matter, the Tribunal was entitled to engage in a wide survey. The challenge is brought on administrative law grounds including an alleged failure to take into account material considerations and the taking into account of immaterial considerations, matters with which I shall deal shortly. It is not alleged that the Tribunal came to a decision to which no reasonable decision-maker would have come.

5. Mr Hanson and the companies which he controlled were property developers. An employee of Mr Hanson's organisation, who had previously lived in the Eurobodalla Shire, thought that Mr Hanson would be interested in the subject property and brought his attention to it. The property was then a grazing property in a run down state running only 40 to 50 head of cattle. It was the subject of an application for the subdivision of the whole property into 25 acre lots. The property was presumably in an area which had sub-divisional potential. Mr Hanson spoke with an officer of the Shire Council and ascertained that the Council was not in favour of the subdivision applied for, although the Council thought that, because of the existing planning under the interim development order, it might have to approve it. The Council preferred a subdivision in which 20% of the land would be subdivided into 5 acre lots with the balance of the land retained for agricultural purposes. Mr Hanson then arranged for the purchase by the applicant, a company engaged in land development.

6. Mr Hanson gave this evidence as to his intentions at the time of purchase:-

"... I was quite happy to be prepared to farm the

balance of the land because I wanted to use that land

as my own farm. I saw the opportunity of having a

large farm in a very desirable area that could be

developed into a prime area, probably the nicest area

in the whole of the South Coast, that farm would then

become my own family farm. It would be there for the

use of my three boys if ever they wanted to go onto the

land which was a big possibility because it had been in

our family and that it was a unique opportunity for

myself and my wife to retire there at a later stage."

Mr Hanson did not give evidence that he became interested in the property because he was looking for a farm. The evidence rather suggests that the property was brought to his attention because he was a land developer.

7. After purchase, correspondence with the Eurobodalla Shire Council was undertaken. Over the next few years there were changes in the planning for the area. Some 5 acre lots were developed and sold off first. But, ultimately, the whole of the property was subdivided, mainly in 100 acre lots, and sold during the years of income ended 30 June 1979 to 1981.

8. The applicant did indeed enter upon primary production. It improved the land for grazing purposes and placed stock on the property and on other properties in the area. This primary production business was significant; but it was scarcely the use of the farm for the enjoyment of the family as Mr Hanson's evidence suggested was in mind at the time of purchase. Income from the sale of stock was returned in the 1975-1979 years of income. After a few years, when the primary production business was found to be unsuccessful and other companies in the group were suffering financial problems, the land was sold.

9. The above is intended as no more than a brief outline to explain the general circumstances which the Tribunal had to consider. On the evidence, it was open to the Tribunal to find either for or against the claim. A crucial question was what had actuated Mr Hanson, and thereby the company, in the purchase of the land. Was it, so far as the main part of the land was concerned, the potential which the land had for profit making by sale or was it the potential which the land had for farming purposes?

10. The first ground of challenge is that:

"3.(i) The Tribunal erred in failing to take

into account in reaching its decision the unchallenged

evidence of Mr Hanson that:

(a) the reason he did not proceed with the

subdivision of the South Coast property ("the

property") into lots of 25 acres as approved by Council

was because of his preference to use the land as a farm

and only subdivide a small amount off as 5 acre blocks;"

This ground refers to the following evidence given by Mr Hanson:-

"Can you tell the tribunal why you did not proceed with

the subdivision?---It was never my intention to proceed

with that subdivision as I had already agreed before I

purchased the land that, with the council officers,

that I would prefer to use the land as a farm and only

subdivide a small amount off as 5 acre blocks."

The Tribunal did not refer specifically to this evidence in its reasons for decision.

11. However, the 25 acre subdivision was approved by the Council only after Mr Hanson had agreed, prior to the purchase, that the subdivision would be a step towards obtaining the different planning proposal which the Council preferred and which Mr Hanson was prepared to accept, namely planning which permitted the subdivision of a part of the land into 5 acre lots, the balance to remain unsubdivided. The principal evidence on this matter was mentioned by the Tribunal in its decision and taken into account. It was not necessary for the Tribunal in its reasons to deal any further with the matter, for it had never been intended by Mr Hanson, or expected by the Council when it approved the subdivision, that the 25 acre subdivision would be acted on. That subdivision was simply a step taken to obtain a reconsideration of the existing zoning proposals under the interim development order. I cannot draw the conclusion that the Tribunal failed to take any matter into account in this respect.

12. The next matter alleged not to have been taken into account was:-

"(b) the reason which actuated the decision to

sell the farming portion of the property was the

economic and financial circumstances which the

Applicant found itself in at the time."

Although the Tribunal did not refer to all the evidence given as to the applicant's financial circumstances, it dealt with the grazing activities and the losses engendered by those activities. The Tribunal went on to say:-

"Following the failure of the grazing enterprise, as a

result of changes effective throughout the Shire, it

became possible to sub- divide land into units of 40

hectares minimum. The Applicant availed itself of the

opportunities presented."

Thus, the Tribunal accepted that the main part of the land was subdivided and sold after losses had been incurred and the grazing enterprise had failed. Although the Tribunal's reasons did not deal with every piece of relevant evidence, I cannot draw the conclusion that any material matter was ignored by the Tribunal. The Tribunal dealt with the substance of the point in a manner which was fair to the applicant.

13. It is alleged that the Tribunal took into account the following irrelevant considerations:-

"(a) That prior to contracting for the purchase of the

property no assessment of its farming potential had

been carried out, or that Mr Hanson had the experience

and qualifications to make such an assessment on behalf

of the Applicant.

(b) That no major work would have been necessary to

enable the Applicant to run cattle on the property as

they had been run shortly before its purchase.

(c) That no cattle were introduced to the property for

some sixteen months after the Applicant entered into

possession of the property.

(d) That no stock was introduced to the

property until about August, 1974 at the earliest."

14. None of the above matters was irrelevant. They were all matters which the Tribunal was bound to take into account as part of the factual matrix from which it had to decide what were the factors influencing Mr Hanson and the applicant in the purchase of the property and which, if any of those factors, was predominant. The weight to be attached to individual aspects of the evidence was, of course, a matter for the Tribunal.

15. The matters set out in paragraph (a) had considerable weight, for lack of investigation of the potential of the property for farming tended to show that the use of the property as a farm was not a matter predominantly actuating the acquisition. In deciding to aquire the property, Mr Hanson relied upon his own expertise, which was that of land developer. He did not make inquiries of any person expert in agriculture. The two uses to which the property was put were both money making purposes. They were subdivision and primary production. Yet, the latter use does not appear to have been the predominant matter in Mr Hanson's mind when he acquired the property for no investigation as to the feasibility of such a business was made prior to the purchase and the property was then carrying only 40 to 50 head of cattle and was in a run down condition.

16. The matters set out in paragraphs (b), (c) and (d) reflect the point that the evidence given by Mr Hanson that, prior to the purchase he had in mind a use of the property by himself, his wife and his children, was not developed or supported by the evidence as to what occurred after acquisition. There was no evidence of family use. The evidence showed that little was done for some time until the applicant commenced a business of primary production. An employee or employees occupied the homestead.

17. All these matters were relevant factors which could influence the decision of the Tribunal in one way or another.

18. It is also alleged that the Tribunal took into account the following irrelevant considerations:-

"(e) The description of the property as one

of the Applicant's `land development projects' in its

annual accounts.

(f) The statements appearing in the

Applicant's accounts with respect to `land development

projects'."

Again, it was not irrelevant that the land was purchased by a land development company or that that applicant included the whole property in its schedule of lands held for land development or that the applicant made no distinction in its books between that part of the land which is now said to have been purchased for subdivisional purposes and that part which is now said not to have been purchased for that purpose. The whole property was recorded as one item in the land development schedule. The Tribunal was bound to take these matters into account though the weight to be attached to them was a matter for the Tribunal. The use to which a taxpayer puts a property after acquisition is evidence of the use which the taxpayer had in mind at the time of acquisition.

19. Finally, counsel for the applicant challenged paragraph 15 of the Tribunal's reasons which reads:-

"It is not disputed that, in relation to many of its

undertakings, Sub-divisions did engage in a business of

dealing in land by buying and selling it, with or

without sub-division and with or without structural

development. When such an entity, whether a body

corporate or an individual person, derives profit from

the sale of any class of property in which it

ordinarily trades, it is more difficult for it to

persuade that the disputed profit is not a profit of

its trade or profit-making activities than it would be

if the profit was generated by dealing in a class of

asset in which it did not ordinarily trade. But it is

certainly not impossible for a taxpayer to establish

that profits which are so generated may have the

character of capital gains."

Counsel submitted that the Tribunal had imposed an excessive onus of proof on the applicant.

20. It is unfortunate that the Tribunal used the term "not impossible" and included both the first and second limbs of s.26(a) with issues of trading stock and trading arising under s.25(1). Nevertheless, I do not draw the conclusion that the Tribunal applied an incorrect onus of proof or failed to keep an open mind. The matters mentioned and others weighed in the balance adversely to the applicant's case. To this extent, the applicant's task of establishing that the property was not acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale or in the course of trading in land was more difficult than it would have been had the acquisition been made by a person or company which was not involved in and was unfamiliar with land development. This was all that the Tribunal had in mind by the observation.

21. The grounds of challenge to the Tribunal's decision therefore fail. The Tribunal has disclosed the substance of its reasons and those reasons deal fairly with the applicant's case in the sense that consideration was given to the substance of the matters for and against the applicant's claim and the decision was not one to which no reasonable decision-maker could have come. I see no reviewable error in the Tribunal's decision.

22. For these reasons, the appeal will be dismissed with costs.
