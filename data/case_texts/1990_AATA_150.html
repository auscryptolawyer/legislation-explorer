Re Taxation Appeals [1990] AATA 150 (14 June 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. NT86/5023

AAT No. 5967

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

K.L. Beddoe(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - assessable income - suspension from employment - reinstatement - subsequent payment of salary not paid during suspension - whether payment for services rendered - characterisation of payment according to statute under which paid.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : ss.25(1)

Public Service Act 1922 : ss. 55, 62, 63C.

Crimes Act 1914

C. of T. (NSW) v. Cam and Sons Ltd (1936) 4 ATD 32

F.C.T. v. French [1957] HCA 73; (1966) 11 ATD 288

The Adelaide Fruit and Produce Exchange Company Ltd v. DFCT (1932) 2 ATD 1

Scott v. C. of T. (NSW) (1935) 3 ATD 142

Scott v. F.C.T. [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 14 ATD 286

Commissioner of Taxes (Victoria) v. Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 3 ATD 330

F.C.T. v. Inkster 89 ATC 5142

HEARING

BRISBANE

14:6:1990

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that the objection decision under review be affirmed.

DECISION

The question for decision raised by this application is not without its difficulties. The question revolves around an amount of $52,905 paid to the applicant by the respondent; the respondent having been the applicant's employer at the relevant time. In a nutshell the question is whether that amount is assessable income within the terms of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The amount of $52,905 was assessed to the applicant as assessable income of the year of income ended 30 June 1983. During the hearing the applicant's representative advised the Tribunal that the amount in dispute was limited to $38,750.

2. The relevant statutory provisions are sub-section 25(1) and paragraph 26(e).

3. Exhibit b is a statement of agreed facts from which I make the following findings. The applicant was an employee of the Commonwealth Government on 1 February 1980 when he was suspended in that employment by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office purporting to act pursuant to section 62 of the Public Service Act 1922. A copy of the instrument effecting the suspension is Exhibit 1 in these proceedings. It reads as follows (edited to maintain confidentiality):

8. On 25 February 1983 the applicant resigned from the Public Service. At some date thereafter the Australian Taxation Office produced a group certificate in the name of the applicant and stating that he had been employed from 1 July 1982 to 25 February 1983. The certificate is dated 30 June 1983. The following details are included in the group certificate (a copy of which forms part of Exhibit B):

Taxable Earnings $52,905

Lump Sum payments

(A) assessable in full 7,039

(B) 5% assessable 5,525

(1) Super. (sic) 2,739

Amount of instalments deducted 26,938

9. Exhibit A includes the applicant's return of income for the year of income ended 30 June 1983. The return disclosed, inter alia, the details on the group certificate except that "Gross Income", which might be thought to correlate with Taxable Earnings, was shown as "NIL". The lump sum payments shown on the group certificate were disclosed as was a pension received from the Department of Social Security. Although no longer attached to the return there is no reason to suggest that the group certificate issued by the Australian Taxation Office was not included with the return.

10. Notwithstanding the statement of agreed facts further evidence was adduced for the respondent. An officer of the Australian Taxation Office gave evidence as to the departmental practice regarding suspension from duty. In essence the procedure of suspending an officer is adopted while there are outstanding allegations against an officer. By "allegations" I think the witness was referring to "charges" rather than allegations. The general practice is that a suspended officer is not paid salary although in some cases salary may be paid. It is also the general practice that when an officer is acquitted of a charge or the charge is withdrawn and the suspension lifted then the officer is paid salary (net of other salary income during suspension) for the period of suspension, as in the present case. The charges in this case concerned the taking of a bribe as an officer of the Commonwealth and were therefore of a serious nature. The charges under the Crimes Act 1914 of soliciting a bribe and conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth did not proceed, it was said, because the Australian Federal Police advised the Australian Taxation Office that there was insufficient evidence to found a conviction. That is the Taxation Office version of what happened to the charges. The applicant says that the charge of conspiracy to defraud was dismissed in the committal proceedings and the charge of soliciting a bribe resulted in a verdict of not guilty, the presiding judge having directed the jury that there was insufficient evidence to found a conviction.

11. Sub-sections 63C(6) and 63C(7) of the Public Service Act provide:

"(6) Where -

(a) An officer has been suspended from duty under section

63B;

(b) the amount of salary that would otherwise have been paid

to the officer in respect of the whole, or a part, of the

period of the suspension was not payable to him; and

(c) the suspension is required to be removed under

sub-section (3) or (4) or would have been required to be

so removed had it not previously been removed,

the Chief Officer shall, subject to sub-section (7), authorize

payment to him of an amount equal to the amount of salary

referred to in paragraph (b).

(7) Where the Chief Officer is satisfied that an officer to

whom sub-section (6) applies has engaged in employment outside

the Service during the whole, or a part, of the period of his

suspension, the amount payable to him under that sub-section

shall be reduced by an amount equal to the total of the

amounts of the earnings that the Chief Officer is satisfied

were received or are receivable by him in respect of that

employment or work."

(The Public Service Act was amended between the date of suspension and

the date of reinstatement. That is the reason for the disparity in

section numbers between the notices and these provisions.)

12. No other adjustment appears to be necessary by the employing department because the suspended officer retains his existing and accruing rights as to leave, superannuation, long service leave etc during the period of suspension (p 11 transcript).

13. The essence of the applicant's case is that the applicant performed no services for the Australian Taxation Office during the period of suspension so that the payment in dispute was not for services rendered. The income was not income from property nor was it income from personal exertion, no services having been rendered by the applicant during the period of suspension.

14. The applicant's representative relied upon a number of authorities both before the Tribunal and in a supplementary written submission filed by leave of the Tribunal. I will deal with those authorities seriatim.

15. To support the proposition that salary paid during a period when no service was rendered is not assessable income the applicant relies on dicta of C of T (NSW) v. Cam and Sons Ltd (1936) 4 ATD 32 at 34. There his Honour the Chief Justice (Jordan CJ) stated that the all important factor, in determining whether an employer of seamen was liable for wages tax, is the doing of the work and the contract of employment and the payment are relatively insignificant. That was said in the course of deciding what proportion of the wages paid to seamen operating within and without territorial waters was subject to New South Wales taxation. The Court did not have to decide whether the amounts represented assessable income per se. His Honour went on to say that in the case of an appointment to a sinecure the engagement and the payment may be the only significant factors. Clearly the Court's decision was only concerned with the source of wages paid to seamen for services performed within and outside New South Wales. The decision is of no assistance in the present case being a decision as to source of income rather than the character of the receipts. The same distinction applies to the decision in FCT v. French [1957] HCA 73; (1966) 11 ATD 288 where again the issue was the source of the income, not whether the amounts received were income.

16. The applicant's case also relies on the decision of Napier J in the Supreme Court of South Australia in The Adelaide Fruit and Produce Exchange Company Ltd v. DFCT (1932) 2 ATD 1. The taxpayer in that case conducted the Adelaide Fruit and Produce Markets deriving income from a number of separate sources in connection with the conduct of the markets. The question was whether an additional rate of tax payable in respect of property income was payable on stall hire charges and fees payable in respect of haulage trucks using the market premises. It will be apparent that the factual circumstances of the case are immediately distinguishable from the facts of the present application. The applicant relies on dicta of Napier J at page 4-5 which I will set out in its context:

"If the disputed items are not `earnings' it is not suggested

that they can be brought within any of the other categories;

and I think it follows that the real question in the case is

whether these items are `earnings' within the meaning of the

definitions. I think that the natural meaning of earnings in

this context is money gained as the result of labour, work or

services. I have no doubt that the meaning is capable of a

liberal application to cover earnings in the sense of the net

profit derived in the course of trade or for services rendered

by one who owns and uses property for that purpose. In many

cases it is, no doubt, a question of fact and degree. It was

conceded that the profits of a boarding house or a railway

might be earnings in this sense, but it was not suggested that

the profits from a block of flats could be so described even if

the rental should include payment for the use of lifts,

lighting or other services. It was argued, on the part of the

appellant, that the disputed items are earnings of the

business, in the sense that the money is paid for the right of

participation in the market, and is therefore gained as the

result of the work or service of `conducting the market'. It

was pointed out that no one would be willing to pay stallage or

truck fees if the company did not undertake to render these

services, but, I think that it is probable that the same

observation might apply with almost equal force, to the shop

rents just as it might apply in the case of office buildings or

flats where lifts or lighting are provided. I think that the

argument confuses causa since qua non with causa causans - a

motive or incentive with the consideration for the contract.

The first question is not - why does the stallholder take the

stand - but - what does he get for the stallage. In my view

the answer to that question must be that he gets the right to

occupy the space appropriated to him, and prima facie the

stallage is paid for the use of the property and not for the

services rendered."

It will be apparent from this extract of his Honour's judgment that the case is of no assistance.

17. The applicant argues that because no services were rendered to his employer during the course of the suspension he was, in effect, being paid for refraining from carrying on his employment. That does not sit well with the facts as found. The applicant was paid an amount in respect of net salary lost during the period of suspension after the suspension was lifted. He was not paid to not work, he was compensated for lost salary due to the suspension. If he had been dismissed from his employment he would not have been eligible for such compensation. In effect the applicant was compensated for wrongful suspension from his employment. The applicant's case relies on the decisions in Scott v. C. of T. (NSW) (1935) 3 ATD 142 and Scott v. F.C.T. [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 14 ATD 286. In the 1935 case the question was whether an amount recovered as compensation for early termination of appointment to a statutory office was assessable income. The Supreme Court of New South Wales held that the amount was not assessable income (Stephen J dissented in that he held that 5% of the amount was assessable as a retiring allowance). The leading judgment was that of Jordan C.J. and the most relevant point of that judgment is at page 145. I can find nothing in that judgment which supports the applicant's case. The present case does not involve the payment of damages as was the case in Scott.

18. In the 1966 case the question at issue was whether a gift made to a solicitor by a client of the solicitor was assessable income in the hands of the solicitor. The taxpayer succeeded on the basis that the gift was not given or received as a recompense for services rendered. In the course of his reasons Windeyer J said at page 293 that the relation between the gift and the taxpayer's activities (as the donor's solicitor) must be such that the receipt by the taxpayer is in a relevant sense a product of those activities. That, in my view is the essential point of distinction between Scott's case and the present case. Here the payment of salary for the period of suspension is a product of the applicant's employment under the Public Service Act.

19. The applicant also relies upon the judgments in Commissioner of Taxes (Victoria) v. Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 3 ATD 330. That case concerned the question as to whether compensation paid for termination of the taxpayer's employment was assessable income. The High Court decided that the compensation was assessable income. The ratio decidendi of the decision is to be found in the following extract of the joint judgment of Dixon and Evatt JJ at page 335:

"In the present case, the contract expressed the total of the

monthly payments as a lump sum. But the period being fixed,

the rate of payment being estimated and the intervals being

already established, the statement is no more than an

arithmetical equivalent. Indeed it is not the present value of

the future payments but merely their sum. In Van den Berghs

Ltd v. Clarke (1935) AC 431 at p 442, Lord Macmillan says:- `If

the Taxpayers were merely receiving in one sum down the

aggregate of profits which they would otherwise have received

over a series of years the lump sum might be regarded as of the

same nature as the ingredients of which it was composed.'

In the present case, there is no lump sum down; there are

monthly payments estimated to correspond in amount with the

monthly payment which, otherwise the taxpayer would have

received and the payments are to be made over the same period

of time. In these circumstances they must, in our opinion, be

regarded as of the same nature as the payments they replace.

They are of an income nature."

Rather than supporting the applicant's case the decision binds this Tribunal to find in favour of the respondent. The fact that the outstanding net salary was paid as one amount is of no consequence.

20. As was made clear by the Federal Court in F.C.T. v. Inkster 89 ATC 5142 the payment received should be characterised according to the provisions of the statute under which it was paid. In this case section 63C of the Public Service Act establishes that the amount paid to the applicant was the amount of salary which otherwise would have been paid to the applicant but for the suspension. That the payment was subject to deduction of an amount equal to the earnings of the applicant from employment outside the Public Service during the period of suspension does not alter the essential character of the payment. It was a payment of outstanding salary by the applicant's employer and was clearly of an income nature and therefore assessable income within the terms of sub-section 25(1) of the Act. I do not need to consider whether paragraph 26(2) would also apply to make the payment assessable in the hands of the applicant.

21. The Tribunal has no option, in view of the state of the authorities, but to affirm the objection decision under review.
