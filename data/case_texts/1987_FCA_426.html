Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Gregrhon Investments Pty Limited; Norman Cecil Clough and Nancye Jean Clough [1987] FCA 426 (26 November 1987)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: GREGRHON INVESTMENTS PTY. LIMITED; NORMAN CECIL CLOUGH and NANCYE JEAN

CLOUGH

Nos. G8-G10 of 1987

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fisher(1), Lockhart(2) and Spender(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Arrangement to avoid liability for tax - sale of all shares in company with current and accumulated profits - business of company sold - assets converted into cash - whether proceeds from sale of shares assessable in hands of former shareholders - assets of company paid to nominee of purchaser upon sale - complex series of transactions - whether sale void as against Commissioner.

Income Tax - Incidence of tax - situation remaining after "arrangement" avoided as against Commissioner - whether sale price assessable under s.44, s.47 or s.108 - distribution by company of assets comprising issued capital and profits - s.260 applies only "so far as". . . arrangement has required "purpose or effect".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.44, 47, 108, 260

HEARING

BRISBANE

ORDER

The appeals be allowed.

The question of additional tax be remitted to the Supreme Court of New South Wales (Administrative Law Division) for further consideration.

The respondents pay the costs of the appellant both on these appeals and at first instance.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

These are three appeals from decisions of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in its Administrative Law Division. Each decision related to an amended assessment of the Commissioner of Taxation in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1980. In each instance the Supreme Court allowed the appeal and directed that the amended assessment be set aside. The respondents to these appeals ("Mr Clough, Mrs Clough and Gregrhon") had appealed to the Supreme Court against amended assessments which increased their taxable incomes for the year of income by the following amounts:

Mr Clough $297,736-00

Mrs Clough $297,736-00

Gregrhon $148,766-00

3. The adjustment sheet attached to the amended assessment of the taxable income of Mrs Clough was in similar terms except that her taxable income as returned was increased from $20,670 to $318,406.

4. In the case of Gregrhon, its taxable income was in like manner increased from $4,876 to $153,642. The adjustment sheet stated that "in accordance with the rights attached to the 50 X class shares held by you $148,766 has been included in your assessable income". That sheet also indicated that the sufficient distribution for Division 7 purposes of Gregrhon for the year ended 30 June 1980 had been increased to $150,918.

5. As the various adjustment sheets disclosed, the amended assessments were issued to reflect the receipt by the taxpayers of their respective shares of profits arising out of the sale by them of shares in Detail Furniture Co. Pty Ltd ("Detail"). The Commissioner contended that s.260 of the Act has application to that transaction and avoided the agreement for sale and transfer of the taxpayers' shares in Detail. Thereafter, once the agreement is avoided, the proceeds of sale in the hands of the taxpayers represent assessable income. The trial judge did not accept this submission and set aside each of the amended assessments.

6. Detail was a company incorporated in 1964 which carried on business as a manufacturer of cabinets and furniture. Mr and Mrs Clough each held 2 ordinary $2 shares in Detail and were the only directors. Gregrhon, which was controlled by Mr and Mrs Clough, held 50 X class $2 shares in Detail. Mrs Clough and Gregrhon as partners in a business Detail Furniture Sales ("Detail Furniture") sold the products manufactured by Detail. Detail conducted a profitable business and in 1977 obtained a contract to renovate the Wentworth Hotel in Sydney. This contract produced a very considerable increase in gross profits. Towards the end of the 1979 calendar year this contract was nearing completion and Detail was enjoying very much higher operating profits. In the financial year ending 30 June 1979 sales of Detail totalled $659,589, gross profit on sales $238,001 and net profit $124,903. The operating statement of Detail prepared for the first 8 months of the next year (to 3 March 1980) disclosed sales for the period of $1,086,940 and gross profit of $698,104. The net operating profit for the period was $604,445. Mr Clough who was the person in charge of the operations of Detail, acknowledged in cross-examination that he had a "tax problem" in February 1980. Detail was liable to primary tax at the rate of 46 cents in the dollar on its net profit, and Division 7 undistributed profits tax to the extent that it failed to make a sufficient distribution of its after tax profits. The shareholders of Detail would be assessed to additional tax consequent to the extent Detail made a distribution to them.

7. It was not disputed that towards the end of the 1979 calendar year Mr Clough discussed this state of affairs with his accountant Mr Gelder, including the possible sale of Detail. Consideration of such a sale was prompted by the fact that at that time there were many potential purchasers seeking companies with current profits or accumulated profits or revenue. These purchasers regularly inserted advertisements in publications such as "The Chartered Accountant in Australia" and other professional journals. In 1979 Mr Gelder had been approached by a firm called Allied Professional & Company Services Pty Ltd which was interested in purchasing profitable companies. Subsequent to the discussion between Mr Clough and Mr Gelder and the consideration of various advertisements Mr Gelder approached a Mr Goldspink who was active in the business of acquiring profitable companies. In a letter dated 7 January 1980 Mr Goldspink confirmed an offer made in a conversation of that date to purchase the issued capital of Detail at a figure referred to in "a conversation". He included for completion a "Corporate Questionnaire" and requested it be returned together with a "Settlement" Balance Sheet and set of Memorandum and Articles of Association. The letter did not indicate whether the Settlement Balance Sheet required that the assets of Detail had been reduced to cash, though this was the normal requirement of a purchaser, a fact which was known to Mr Gelder at the time. The Balance Sheet to be filled in however certainly only identified the assets of Detail as being "Funds on Deposit" and "Inter Company Loans". In his initial discussions Mr Gelder offered to sell the shares of Detail to a purchaser nominated by Mr Goldspink on the basis that the net assets of Detail were $500,000. Mr Goldspink was to receive a fee of $33,000. The sale however was ultimately effected to a company Shareholder Pty. Limited ("Shareholder") on the basis that the value of these assets was $744,346 with Mr Goldspink's fee remaining at $33,000. These assets comprised mainly two capitalized revenue items, namely operating profits for the 8 months' period of $604,445 and unappropriated profits as at the commencement of that period of $127,131, totalling $731,576. This amount, together with paid up capital of $108 and capital profits reserves of $12,662 made up the sale price of $744,346 before the deduction of Mr Goldspink's fee.

Gregrhon was in this instance not a shareholder. Mr and Mrs Clough held a meeting of directors of Detail on 11 February 1980 with Mr Gelder present at which it was resolved as follows :

10. The evidence was that Trazian took over both the business of Detail and Detail Furniture on this day. However neither Mr or Mrs Clough nor Gregrhon provided at this time any funds to Trazian to enable it to make payment to Detail. This had to wait upon the sale by the three taxpayers of their shares in Detail. The matter of payment to Mrs Clough and Gregrhon for their interests in the Detail Furniture partnership seems thereafter to have been conveniently overlooked.

11. The trial judge accepted that the net value of the assets of Detail purchased by Trazian was $744,346, which figure was the exact amount eventually paid to Mr and Mrs Clough and Gregrhon on the sale of their shares to Shareholder prior to deduction of fees. The evidence did not disclose how the figures and book values listed in the minute "Purchase of Detail Furniture Co. Pty Limited" set out above, made up the stated amount of $744,346. It would appear to bear more relationship to the total of unappropriated profits, capital profits and current operating profits as at 4 March 1980. These circumstances tend to support the inference that there was at least an association between the sale of the business to Trazian and the sale of the Detail shares. On 4 March 1980 Trazian drew a cheque, in circumstances subsequently to be related, for $738,846 in favour of Detail, being the abovementioned value of the net assets less $5,500 which was at that date standing to the credit of Detail in its bank account. Nothing was done in respect of the purchase price payable by Trazian to Mrs Clough and Gregrhon in respect of the sale of Detail Furniture.

12. It appears that on or about 11 February 1980 Mr Gelder completed the Corporate Questionnaire required by Mr Goldspink and supplied an operating statement of Detail for the period 1 July 1979 to 3 March 1980. This operating statement showed the various figures abovementioned which together with the capital and capital reserves made up the sale price of $744,346 which amount was stated in the accompanying settlement Balance Sheet to be "Cash at Bank". This figure considerably exceeded the amount of the net assets of Detail, namely $500,000, as previously stated by Mr Gelder, but no arrangement was made for any increase in Mr Goldspink's fee.

13. On 3 or perhaps 4 March 1980 Mr Gelder received from Mr Goldspink a proforma agreement for sale and purchase of the shares in Detail. Shareholder was nominated as the purchaser and the evidence disclosed that it was a company controlled by Mr Wynyard, a brother of Mr Goldspink. Mr Clough was unaware who was to be the purchaser of the Detail shares until this day. Pursuant to the agreement Mr and Mrs Clough were to be paid $284,538 each and Gregrhon $142,270. Mr and Mrs Clough were required to pay the outstanding primary tax of Detail in respect of the year ending 30 June 1979 and the agreement expressly referred to the fact that no provision had been made for income tax in respect of any subsequent period. On settlement a cheque for the sum of $744,346 being the net assets of Detail was required to be paid to a company nominated by Shareholder, namely Lockwing Pty Ltd ("Lockwing").Another clause of the agreement stated that Shareholder warranted to the vendors that it would not directly or indirectly by any means cause Detail to give financial assistance in connection with the purchase of its shares. The trial judge accepted however that by requiring payment of the assets of Detail to Lockwing in the circumstances, Detail was in fact funding the purchase of its shares.

14. Settlement took place on 4 March 1980 at the Riverwood branch of the Rural Bank of New South Wales, the bank which held the accounts of Detail, Mr and Mrs Clough and Gregrhon. Mr Ray the manager of that branch was present. He had previously discussed the sale of Detail's shares and the arrangements for settlement with Mr and Mrs Clough and Mr Gelder. An office minute prepared by Mr Ray on that day disclosed that he said he was informed that the affairs of the Clough's and Detail were being restructured "mainly for taxation purposes, and because of the very substantial profits reflected by Detail Furniture Company Pty. Limited in recent times". The minute went on as follows:

"To facilitate the excercise (sic) Detail Furniture Pty.

Limited has been 'sold' and a new company (Trazian Pty.

Limited) formed which will trade as sole proprietor of

Detail Furniture Sales".

He became aware that another company Transia Pty. Ltd. was supplying the funds for bank cheques in favour of Mr and Mrs Clough and Gregrhon and was instructed by his bank's Hunter Street premises that these cheques were not to be released until the amount of $744,346 was paid to Lockwing.

15. The trial judge has set out in some detail the manner in which various cheques were drawn and payments were made and it is unnecessary for me to recapitulate. Suffice to say that events can be traced to have occurred in the following sequence. Mr and Mrs Clough as directors of Detail paid by bank marked cheque the sum of $744,346 to Lockwing being the cash assets of Detail as required by the agreement for sale and purchase of shares. Lockwing then paid this amount to Shareholder, which in its turn, after deducting $30,866 on account of Mr Goldspink's fee, paid $713,480 to Transia. Pty. Ltd. Transia Pty. Ltd. deducted its fee which comprised the balance of the agreed fee of $33,000 due to Mr Goldspink and paid $711,346 back to Shareholder. Shareholder paid this amount being the purchase price of the Detail shares, to Mr and Mrs Clough ($284,538 each) and Gregrhon ($142,270). These three taxpayers then paid their respective amounts to Trazian thereby putting in funds to enable Trazian to complete its purchase of the business of Detail. The taxpayers however were not involved in nor would they have been aware of the happenings between the time when they paid $744,346 to Lockwing and they received their payments from Shareholder.

16. On the basis of these facts the trial judge stated that after the completion of the sale of their shares in Detail Mr and Mrs Clough found themselves as sole directors and shareholders of Trazian, carrying on thereby the business previously carried on by Detail and Detail Furniture. Each of them had previously owed money to Detail, namely Mr Clough $153,484-98, Mrs Clough $125,433 and Gregrhon $143,000 which in consequence of the sale of the assets of Detail to Trazian they then owed to Trazian. The trial judge stated that in consequence of the funds which each had made available to Trazian (the proceeds of sale of their Detail shares) Mr and Mrs Clough became creditors of Trazian and Gregrhon's debt was "virtually discharged". However in making this finding the trial judge appears to have overlooked the amount of $191,840 "together with advances at book value" which Trazian owed Mrs Clough and Gregrhon as the purchase price of Detail Furniture's business. The crucial end result had, notwithstanding this oversight, been achieved. Mr and Mrs Clough retained control of the furniture manufacturing and sales businesses and had received as proceeds of sale of their shares in Detail the entire net operating profit and reserves of that company less Mr Goldspink's fee. Detail for its part was left without resources from which to pay its current year primary tax and any Division 7 undistributed profits tax.

17. The Commissioner's contention was that s.260 operates to strike down the sale of the three taxpayers' shares to Shareholder and renders the amount paid to Lockwing (less $108 issued capital) taxable in their hands. The taxpayers for their part contend that they were not involved in nor a party to any arrangement within s.260 as the only arrangement in which they were involved began and ended with the sale of their shares under the agreement. They claim that the acquisition of Detail's business by Trazian was not part of any arrangement in which Shareholder was involved and that the sale of their shares was merely an ordinary business or commercial dealing which produced for them a capital payment by way of the purchase price paid by Shareholder. After giving careful consideration to the views of the transaction alternatively advocated by the Commissioner and the taxpayers, the trial judge concluded that the three taxpayers were not parties to an arrangement within s.260 but merely transacted a bona fide sale of their shares no different from that upheld by the High Court in Slutzkin v. Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 140 CLR 314.

18. On the hearing of the appeal counsel for the Commissioner disputed the applicability of Slutzkins' Case, contending that there was a great deal more to the transactions in this matter than a mere realisation by the taxpayers of their shares. He referred to particular aspects of the matter in which the taxpayers were themselves or through their agents knowingly concerned. In these circumstances he said Slutzkin's Case and Hennessey v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4007 were clearly distinguishable upon their facts. Viewed objectively the transactions in this matter were not capable of explanation as comprising "an ordinary business dealing" but were necessarily labelled as a means of avoiding tax. In these circumstances the relevant authorities cited were the decisions in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1; Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258; Mayfield v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) [1961] HCA 58; (1961) 108 CLR 323 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ellers Motor Sales Pty. Ltd. [1972] HCA 17; (1972) 128 CLR 602, which were prior to Slutzkin and the subsequent decisions of the High Court in Gulland, Watson and Pincus [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 62 ALR 545.

19. There are a number of grounds upon which I accept the submissions of the Commissioner. In arranging for the "restructuring of their affairs" and the sale of shares in Detail, the taxpayers and their advisers placed crucial reliance upon the reasoning in Slutzkin's Case as did the trial judge and also the taxpayers on the Commissioner's appeal to this Court. However in my view, the facts, both in Slutzkin and in Hennessey, are very significantly different and neither case is conclusive or even persuasive authority in support of the taxpayers' contentions.

20. In Slutzkin's Case, Francis Richard Holdings Pty Ltd ("the target company") was at the relevant time not carrying on a business and had become redundant. It had no "tax problem" whether primary or undistributed profits tax and no necessity to make any distribution of its profits. The taxpayers wanted to dispose of their shares and realize the assets of the company in a manner which would not attract the tax which would be payable if the accumulated profits were distributed by way of dividend or on a liquidation. At the time of sale all the assets of the target company remained within its control in a bank account. The sale by the taxpayers of their shares in these circumstances the High Court found did not defeat or frustrate the provisions of the Act and was capable of explanation as an ordinary business dealing. It was nothing more than a mere realization by the taxpayers of their shares.

21. Likewise in Hennessey's Case the target company (W.I. Development Pty Ltd) was redundant in the sense that it no longer had a significant role to play as the holding company of a group of operating companies. It also had no "tax problem", over and above its obligation to pay tax on its current small interest income. It had over distributed its profits in past years and was under no obligation to make a sufficient distribution in the current year or of its current year's profits. At the time of settlement on the sale of its shares it had, in accordance with the terms of an agreement for sale, called up its most substantial asset, loan moneys, and sold such shares as were saleable. However the bank cheque for the target company's cash assets which its directors produced on settlement was made payable to itself, the target company. These assets therefore did not leave the control of the target company and remained available, at least to the knowledge of the taxpayers, to meet any liability for the target company's tax. Zelling J. on appeal from a Taxation Board of Review held that the transaction was a bona fide sale of shares and as such wholly explicable as an ordinary business transaction.

22. In the present matter the circumstances surrounding the sale of Detail's shares had a number of very significant differences. Mr Clough conceded that there were tax problems both for Detail and for its shareholders if a sufficient distribution of profits were made. The profits of the current year were, by past standards, exceptional and most substantial. He said that he and his wife "would be in difficulty in paying the tax in that period of time" particularly as they needed funds to complete a land development project. After taking advice and becoming aware that there were purchasers seeking to buy shares such as they held, they were introduced to a procedure whereby notwithstanding the sale, the business could be retained. Arrangements were made to incorporate a new company Trazian Pty Ltd, under their control which would acquire the business, the employees and the existing contracts of Detail. Notwithstanding the view of the trial judge, I can only see this transaction as inter-connected to the sale of Detail's shares. The timing of the sale together with financial arrangements in respect thereof, slipshod as they were and never satisfactorily explained or understood, confirms this view of the facts. The retention of the businesses was crucial, the fiscal arrangements were a mere formality. As I have already said the financial consequences of the sale by Mrs Clough and Gregrhon of their interests in the partnership Detail Furniture were completely ignored. Coincidentally the stated purchase price payable by Trazian was exactly the amount ultimately received by the taxpayers on the sale of their shares, less Mr Goldspink's commission. A further exceptional and distinguishing feature was the obligation imposed by the sale agreement on Mr and Mrs Clough to hand on settlement to a nominee of the purchaser of their shares (Lockwing as nominee and Shareholder as purchaser) a cheque for Detail's total assets. This fact, being in breach of the fiduciary obligations of the directors, denied Detail of the capacity to meet its tax obligations in the current year. Furthermore the complete indifference and lack of concern in this and other matters of Mr. Clough, and more importantly his accountant Mr. Gelder, must raise considerable doubt on any objective assessment of the bona fides of the taxpayers. There remains the further fact that the taxpayers' bank manager had been informed that the restructuring of Detail was for "tax reasons" and that he was not to hand to the taxpayers their purchase moneys until the assets of Detail were in the hands of Lockwing.

23. All of these matters in my opinion clearly distinguish this matter from Slutzkin's Case and Hennessey's Case. They satisfy me that this was no mere sale of shares capable of explanation as an ordinary business transaction. Viewed objectively it had all the hallmarks of tax avoidance, of an arrangement which had the purpose of at least preventing the operation of the Act in respect to the current profits of Detail (s.260(d)). Many aspects of the transaction went far beyond what would normally be seen as an ordinary business transaction. As Gibbs C.J. said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 62 ALR 545 at p 555

"However when Lord Denning in Newton v. F.C.T. spoke of

ordinary business or family dealing he intended to

refer to what was normal or regular, rather than to

what had become common or prevalent."

I can see little which is normal or regular in the manner in which the taxpayers went about the sale of their shares in Detail.

24. The learned trial judge approached the question differently attaching much significance to what he saw as the extent of the taxpayers' knowledge. He concluded that this knowledge of what was being done and was intended to be done was only such as might be inferred from the terms of the written agreement. He laid emphasis upon his finding that there was nothing to indicate any belief on the part of Mr and Mrs Clough that the transaction into which they were entering was dishonest or in any way fraudulent upon the Commissioner. Furthermore they did not believe that it would result in Detail's assets being the source of the purchase money for its shares. He also held that there was nothing at the settlement which aroused or ought to have aroused their suspicion that they might have been involved in any ingenious tax avoidance scheme. For my part I would not approach the matter in this way. Whether or not there is an arrangement such as is contemplated by s.260 is a matter for objective assessment and the attitudes of the taxpayers are little if anything to the point. It is after all the "purpose or effect" of the arrangement and not the motives and intentions of the taxpayers which determines the issue. If it were otherwise the law would place a premium on naivety, ignorance and calculated abstention from knowledge and understanding. In any event however all the surrounding circumstances in this matter were in my opinion such that the taxpayers, and more particularly their accountant, whose knowledge and intention may be imputed to the taxpayers, (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Bidencope [1978] HCA 23; (1978) 140 CLR 533 at 547, per Gibbs J.) should have been alerted to a scheme of tax avoidance, with particular reference to their obligation to hand over the assets of Detail to a third party and the requirement that as vendors they not be paid until this was done. In this regard their evidence was that they were led to believe that the proper course was specifically to refrain from ascertaining what was the true position, that is to engage in calculated abstention from knowledge or enquiry, an attitude which they mistakenly understood had assisted the taxpayers in Slutskin's Case.

25. Counsel for the taxpayer also relied upon the decision of Waddell J. in Harrison v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4144, a matter in which that judge held that Slutzkin's Case was applicable to deny the operation of s.260. However in my opinion it can readily be distinguished in that the circumstances in that matter were very different from those of the present case. That Court found at p 4150 that the transaction under consideration could be correctly described as no more than a realisation by the shareholders of the benefit of their shareholdings in a way which would not attract tax. The purchase price on that occasion was the total cash assets of the companies less their respective income tax liabilities and the assets of the companies were represented by money in the bank. The objective facts and the character of the acts done and transactions entered into indicated that only the sale of a capital asset was involved. The position is very much otherwise in these appeals.

26. In my opinion s.260 can be applied by the Commissioner to the facts of this matter. There was an agreement or arrangement as I have found to avoid a liability imposed by the Act upon Detail and to prevent the operation of the Act upon the profits of Detail. I have already discussed the impending liability of Detail to pay primary tax and undistributed profits tax under Division 7 if it failed to make a sufficient distribution under that Division. To the extent that any distribution of profits was made by Detail the taxpayers would be assessed to tax. Dixon C.J. referred to the expression "preventing the operation of the Act" in Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258 at p 278 when he said:

"The expression 'preventing the operation of the Act in

any respect' is generally regarded as difficult, but I

treat it as simply meaning the operation which the Act

would have in a given case if it were not for the

contract agreement or arrangement made for the purpose

(or having the effect) of preventing it. It is the

operation of the Act in relation to the distribution

and taxability of the profits of a private company that

in part the plan was designed to 'prevent'."

27. The facts in Hancock's case were more complex than those in this matter, in that the taxpayer George Hancock received in the end not merely a portion of the company's profits which were liberated by the arrangement but also the return of his shares and portion of the shares of the Lefroy family. This circumstance, the Chief Justice contended, should be disregarded and he explained his reasoning in a passage set out in full in his reasons by the trial judge. I need only to refer to the salient features, namely the following statements which are particularly relevant here and which appear on pages 281-282 of the Chief Justice's reasons:

"When the purpose is to assess a taxpayer who has

reached a situation which but for a scheme swept away

by s.260 would or might spell liability to tax, it does

not appear to me to be necessary to trace the identity

of moneys as if one were seeking to identify in an

investment trust funds which had been misapplied . . . .

But it seems to me that what matters must be the

resulting financial situation, one of change if not

invariably of betterment, and the factors which would

but for the void scheme have made it taxable . . . It is

the result that exposes the taxpayer to liability: a

result necessarily involving the employment by the

taxpayer of a distribution of the profit fund."

28. The Chief Justice was of opinion that the fact that the taxpayer, having received shares as well as cash, -

". . . should not be regarded as inconsistent with his

having derived income, once the disguising elements of

the 'arrangement' are stripped away under s.260."

29. It is pertinent to note that he gave no consideration to the question which was debated both by the trial judge and on the appeal as to under which section of the Act the "income" was assessable. Sections 44, 47 and 108 were however extensively canvassed on the hearing of this appeal.

30. Kitto J. made reference to s.44 when he said on p 289 of Hancock's case:

"Of course it was a capital receipt in the hands of the

Hancocks, being part of the purchase price of their

shares; but if the sale and transfer of those shares

had not been arranged and carried out there would have

been nothing to give it the character of capital, and

it would have retained the character of assessable

income by reason of its being a distribution by a

company to a shareholder: see s.44(1) and the

definition of 'dividend' in s.6."

31. In this matter the application of s.260 by the Commissioner avoids the transfer by the taxpayers of the shares in Detail to Shareholder. The Commissioner is therefore entitled to act on the basis that when they each received the purchase monies payable for their shares they were in receipt of amounts which almost wholly comprised the profits of Detail. The profits available for distribution amounted to $744,238, after deducting issued capital of $108, and they were on 4 March 1980 withdrawn from Detail's bank account. This amount can in fact be traced into the hands of the taxpayers who received their respective portions thereof as shareholders in Detail, their respective transfers to Shareholder being avoided against the Commissioner as one of the elements of the s.260 arrangement. In these circumstances the amounts received by each are taxable, a conclusion consistent with the end result of a substantial number of cases decided under s.260. Some of such cases are referred to hereafter and it is pertinent to note that in no instance is the particular section of the Act identified under which the amounts were held to be taxable.

32. In F.R. Mayfield v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No.1) [1961] HCA 57; (1961) 108 CLR 303 at 319 Menzies J. said:

"So far as the Commissioner is concerned, these

transfers are therefore void and the Mayfields stand to

be taxed as though they had remained shareholders of

Mayfield Investments Ltd. up to the time of its

dissolution. Had they done so, nothing else would

have stood in the way of a lawful assessment, and it is

the steps that would do so that s.260 avoids.

It follows as an immediate consequence that whatever

the members of the Mayfield family received out of any

distribution of profits by Mayfield Investments Ltd. is

taxable. Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation,

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Newton, Newton v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation and Hancock v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation."

33. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ellers motor Sales Pty. Ltd. (supra) Walsh J. said on page 620 after referring to Bell's case, Newton's case and Hancock's case:

"According to those authorities it is appropriate to

look at the end result of the transaction from the

point of view of Harcourt and from the point of view of

those who were its shareholders before the transfer of

the shares to Holdings. The end result was that the

profits had gone out from Harcourt and that an

equivalent amount had come into the hands of its

shareholders. To adopt the phrase used by Fullagar J.

in Newton's case ((1957) 96 CLR at p 656), it may be

said in my opinion that 'the only real money' which

figured in the transactions was Harcourt's money."

34. Substituting "Detail" for "Harcourt" in this statement, it applies exactly to the circumstances of this matter. The profits of Detail or an equivalent amount thereof came into the hands of those who were its shareholders before they transferred to Shareholder. Walsh J. went on to adopt a statement by Menzies J. in Mayfield (No. 2)(supra) when the latter judge said at p 334 of that case:

"A substantial identity between what the company

distributed and what the assumed members received is

sufficient"

Equally, the conclusion of the High Court in Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 34; (1953) 87 CLR 548 at p 574 can be applied here, namely -

"Then if this arrangement be treated as void, what

remains? Simply this, that on 3rd February 1948,

77,000 pounds, consisting entirely of profits, was withdrawn

from the company's bank account, and 11,000 pounds of it

passed, indirectly but by steps which are clearly

traceable on the face of the bank's ledgers, into

Bell's bank account; and Bell is to be considered as

remaining at that time a shareholder in the company,

his transfer to Corlett being ex hypothesi void as

against the commissioner as an integral part of the

arrangement. This means that the application of s.260

in this case is to eliminate those features of the case

upon which the exclusion of the 11,000 pounds from assessable

income depends, and by that means to establish the

correctness of the assessment appealed against."

35. It is significant that each of the above cases was decided subsequent to the case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Blakely [1951] HCA 17; (1951) 82 CLR 388, the case upon which the trial judge relied in deciding that if s.260 was applicable the taxpayers in these circumstances could not be assessed under s.44. In none of the authorities to which I have referred was Blakely's case seen, or even considered, as a possible bar to an assessment under that section. Furthermore it was not a case decided by reference to s.260 and was an authority much doubted thereafter, probably because of the differing grounds upon which the various judges relied. A number of these matters were referred to in the reasons of Gibbs C.J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slater Holdings Ltd. [1984] HCA 78; (1984) 156 CLR 447, when he said with the concurrence of other members of the Bench, on page 459 -

"It is unnecessary finally to decide whether the reasons

given by the majority in Federal Commissioner of

Taxation v. Blakely and Federal Commissioner of

Taxation v. Uther were in all respects correct. On any

view, those decisions do not assist the respondent in

the present case, which is plainly distinguishable from

them."

36. In that case the company paid to its members moneys derived from a gift and from capital profits. The former Chief Justice said in conclusion on page 461 -

"For the reasons I have given I consider that the amount

received by the taxpayer was, as it purported to be, a

dividend; it was a distribution of money paid by the

company to a member and was paid wholly out of profits

derived by the company. It was accordingly assessable

under s.44(1)(a) of the Act."

37. Counsel for the Commissioner contended, correctly in my view that the amounts received by each of the taxpayers were assessable as dividends received by them. The taxpayers argued that this could not be so because the amounts distributed by Detail were not wholly out of profits but comprised $108 which was admittedly a capital asset. In this regard they relied upon the reference by Gibbs C.J. in Slater Holding's case supra at page 459 to Uther's case [1965] HCA 42; (1965) 112 CLR 630. He said:

"However, what appears to be implicit in the judgment of

Taylor J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Uther

is the suggestion that to come within s.44(1)(a) the

distribution must have been made wholly out of profits;

it is not enough that there is a distribution of a mass

of assets which contains profits. This view may be

supported by the fact that the section does not refer

to 'dividends to the extent to which they were paid to

him by the company out of profits', since, in the light

of the construction given to s.51 of the Act, the

inclusion of the phrase 'to the extent to which' would

no doubt have allowed a dissection or apportionment to

be made of the distribution: cf Ronpibon Tin N.L. v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation."

38. It is my opinion that such reasoning is inappropriate when the assessability of the distribution arises as a consequence of the application of s.260. In so far as the distribution was a distribution of capital that section has, by its wording, no application. I emphasize the following words -

". . . shall so far as it has or purports to have the

purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly

-

(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;

(b) relieving any person from liability to pay any

income tax or make any return;

(c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or

liability imposed on any person by this Act; or

(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any

respect,

be absolutely void."

39. In respect of the distribution of $108 as a capital item the arrangement is not in my view void as against the Commissioner. It is however void in respect of the balance of the distribution which was a distribution "wholly out of profits". In this way no apportionment or dissection of the moneys paid such as may be undertaken pursuant to s.51 is required. Instead s.260 operates to strike down, as against the Commissioner, only that part of the arrangement as a whole as has "the purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly altering the incidence of any income tax . . . etc." An apportionment or dissection is thereby made of the arrangement, not of the moneys paid.

40. In this regard I refer to the reasoning of the Privy Council in Newton's case [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1 at page 10:

"In the case the commissioner must accept the

arrangement in so far as it had the effect of creating

special dividend rights in the original shareholders -

for that did nothing to avoid tax: but he can ignore

the arrangement in so far as the original shareholders

transferred those dividend rights (with the shares) to

Pactolus for money - for it was that transaction which

gave the character of capital to the money received by

the shareholders . . .

The commissioner can not avoid or ignore the taking up

of 400,000 1 preference shares: for that did nothing

to avoid tax."

41. An apportionment of the purchase price was made by Menzies J. in Mayfield v. Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) [1961] HCA 58; (1961) 108 CLR 323 when he said at page 334:

"Furthermore, I consider that notwithstanding the

overdraft arranged by Argo as an interim financial

measure for the provision of funds to pay for the

shares, the payments made by Argo and Kentish on 21st

April 1954 for the purchase of the taxpayers' shares in

Mayfield Holdings Ltd., which totalled 56,900, came to

the extent of 49,200 from the profits of the company

which were distributed shortly afterwards."

42. The shareholders in Mayfield's case (No. 2) sold their shares for 56,900 pounds pursuant to an arrangement which was void as against the Commissioner. Of this amount 49,200 pounds was identified as comprising the profits of the Mayfield company which found their way as portion of the purchase price into the hands of the former shareholders and the balance comprised capital items. The total amount of 56,900 pounds received by them was dissected into distributable profits and return of capital and only the portion comprising the former was assessable consequent upon the application of s.260.

43. On the question of additional tax imposed by the Commissioner under s.226 of the Act I agree with Lockhart J. for the reasons he has given, that the three matters should be remitted to the Supreme Court for the purpose of dealing with the question.

44. The appropriate orders are that each of the appeals be allowed and the respondents must pay the costs of the Commissioner both of the hearing at first instance and the appeals.

These three appeals from the Supreme Court of New South Wales concern s. 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act"). The Commissioner issued amended assessments to each of the respondents for the year of income ended 30 June 1980 on the basis that the monies received by them from the sale of their shares in Detail Furniture Co. Pty. Ltd. ("Detail") was assessable income and not capital as previously claimed by them in their returns of income. The Commissioner assessed on the basis that there was an arrangement, for the purposes of s. 260, constituted by a deed of sale of the shares held by the respondents in Detail and the transfer of those shares, which had the purpose or effect of altering the incidence of income tax and was avoided by s. 260 leaving assessable income in the hands of the respondents. The amended assessments increased the taxable income of the respondents as follows: Gregrhon Investments Pty. Limited ("Gregrhon") from $2,243 to $148,766, Norman Cecil Clough from $37,468 to $297,736 and Nancye Jean Clough from $20,670 to $297,736.

2. The respondents' appeals to the Supreme Court were heard by Lee J. who allowed them and set aside the amended assessments. It is from Lee J.'s judgment that these appeals were brought. They were heard together by consent before the Supreme Court and this Court.

Facts and Findings of Supreme Court

3. The respondents were the sole shareholders in Detail, a company incorporated in 1964 which carried on a furniture manufacturing business. Norman Cecil Clough and Nancye Jean Clough were the directors of Detail and also controlled Gregrhon. Sales of Detail's products were made by a partnership, Detail Furniture Sales, the partners being Gregrhon and Mrs. Clough. Mr. and Mrs. Clough each held two ordinary $2 shares and Gregrhon held 50 X-Class $2 shares in Detail. The paid-up capital of Detail was thus $108. Nothing turns on the rights attaching to the respective shares. Detail traded successfully at Padstow and later at Peakhurst, New South Wales and in 1977 obtained a contract to renovate the Wentworth Hotel (now the Sheraton-Wentworth Hotel) in Sydney. By the end of 1979 the contract for the hotel was nearing completion and had resulted in a very substantial increase in the business and gross profits of Detail.

4. In 1979 there were many companies willing to purchase or provide purchasers of shares in companies which had current profits or accumulated profits or reserves. Mr. Clough and his accountant, a Mr. Gelder, saw advertisements which appeared in various publications, including The Chartered Accountant in Australia, a fair sample of which is as follows:

"COMPANIES PURCHASED WITH RESERVES, PROFITS OR

5. The substantial profits which Detail was then making were liable to primary tax at the rate of 46 cents in the dollar. Accumulation of profits and subsequent declaration of dividends would have resulted in the shareholders being liable to tax on the amount received. Retention by Detail of the profits beyond a certain level would have resulted in Detail's liability to undistributed profits tax.

6. Mr. and Mrs. Clough had purchased land at Forster on the North Coast of New South Wales on which they had built a block of flats. They had bought further land on which they intended to build another block of flats. They needed funds to enable them to complete the purchase of the second block of land and proceed with the building of the flats.

7. Mrs. Clough did not play any active role in the affairs of Detail or the partnership. She acted as requested in all matters with which these appeals are concerned as requested by her husband and Mr. Gelder. Mr. Clough discussed with Mr. Gelder the possibility of himself, Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon selling their shares in Detail. They discussed advertisements of the kind to which I have referred. Mr. Gelder's practice as an accountant has been since 1969 principally in the taxation field. Mr. Gelder approached a Mr. Goldspink who was at that time active in the business of purchasing shares in companies with undistributed profits. Mr. Goldspink provided Mr. Gelder with a specimen form of agreement for the sale of shares in Detail. It was similar in many respects to a specimen form of agreement received by Mr. Gelder earlier from a firm called Allied which was interested in purchasing companies with undistributed profits. The Allied form of agreement, however, made no provision for payment of Detail's assets in the form of cash to a third party as Mr. Goldspink's specimen agreement did, a significant difference for reasons which appear later.

"We confirm our offer to purchase all the

Mr. Gelder filled out the "corporate questionaire" form furnished to him by Mr. Goldspink and gave him an operating statement for Detail covering the period 1 July 1979 to 3 March 1980 which showed that the operating net profit of Detail for that period was $604,445 and that the unappropriated profits brought forward from 30 June 1979 were $127,131. These figures were repeated in the "Corporate questionaire" form and no liabilities were shown there.

10. On 1 February 1980, Mr. and Mrs. Clough, with the assistance of Mr. Gelder, formed a company called Trazian Pty. Limited ("Trazian") in which Mr. and Mrs. Clough were shareholders and directors. Gregrhon did not become a shareholder of Trazian. Detail then agreed to sell and Trazian agreed to purchase the business of Detail and of the partnership for a consideration equal to the book value of Detail's net tangible assets. Trazian agreed to take over Detail's liabilities. Mr. Gelder's evidence established that the amount paid on 4 March 1980 by Trazian for Detail's business was $738,846 which allowed for the fact that at the time of settlement the sum of $5,500 stood to the credit of Detail's account with Trazian, giving a total of $744,346, the "Cash at Bank" figure mentioned earlier. 11. The sale by Mr. and Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon of their shares in Detail was made by deed dated 4 March 1980 between them as vendors and Shareholder Pty. Limited ("Shareholder") as purchaser, a company controlled by a Mr. Wynyard, the brother of Mr. Goldspink. The deed stated that the date to which income tax of Detail had been paid or provided was 30 June 1979 (item 10 in the Schedule), that the date to which Division 7 tax, if any, had been paid or provided was 30 April 1979 (item 11). Submissions on behalf of the parties

27. I summarise the primary submissions of counsel for the appellant as follows:-

(a) the primary Judge erred in holding that the decisive issue in the case was in severing what was really on the facts one undivided transaction into two parts, the second and critical part being that the sum received by the respondents for the sale of their shares came to them through Lockwing from the assets of Detail, a use of Detail's assets which denuded it of funds and produced the consequence that the tax liability of Detail was by reason of the unlawful use of its funds avoided or evaded.

(b) the primary Judge also erred in holding that, unless the respondents had knowledge of the Lockwing/Shareholder side of the taxation, the case was indistinguishable from Slutzkin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1978) 140 CLR 314 and s. 260 could not therefore apply. Slutzkin's Case was said to be plainly distinguishable.

(c) the primary Judge's approach paid insufficent attention to the fact that the sum received by the respondents was approximately equal to the current year's profits and accumulated profits of Detail, less the promoter's fee, and that they controlled the new company, Trazian, which left them in control of the new company which carried on the old business substantially as before; what counsel described as "business as usual".

(d) the primary Judge failed to have regard to a number of critical matters and incorrectly analysed the transaction, especially those aspects of it in which the taxpayers were directly or, through Mr. Gelder as their agent, knowingly concerned. It was more than a mere realisation of shares in a dormant company in a manner designed to ensure that the realisation itself did not attract a tax liability.

(e) If it is necessary for the operation of s. 260 that the respondents be parties to the aspects of the transaction which included the receipt by them of the "capital" for the sale of their shares through Lockwing which in turn came from the assets of Detail and that this use of Detail's assets denuded it of funds producing the consequence that the tax liability of Detail was, by reason of the unlawful use of its funds, avoided or evaded, then that is the correct conclusion on the facts of the case. A taxpayer does not have to be personally and subjectively aware of each and every step in a transaction for there to be a conclusion that he is a party to the whole transaction. It was common ground that Gregrhon and Mrs. Clough were bound by what Mr. Clough agreed to do and that all respondents were bound by any arrangement made by Mr. Gelder or their bank manager, Mr. Ray. The critical steps in the transaction were made possible by reason of an express term of the deed of sale (viz. clause 3(iv) earlier mentioned) and by their participation in what was a plain breach of fiduciary duty. Also, Mr. Clough and Mr. Gelder deliberately turned a blind eye to what was going on although they in fact directly received the benefit of it. It was an uncritical and uncaring breach of fiduciary duty and direct receipt of the benefits from the breach.

(f) The part of the arrangement which s. 260 avoids is the deed for the sale of the shares and the transfer of the shares. Once that is avoided what is left in the hands of the respondents is assessable income.

(g) Although it was not critical to his Honour's conclusion, he erred in holding that s. 44 of the Assessment Act did not apply. If s. 260 treats as void the transfer of shares from the taxpayer to Shareholder, one then finds a payment of money from Detail to Lockwing for no consideration and the moneys used directly or indirectly to fund the payments to the respondents. There is therefore a dividend within the meaning of that word in para. 6(1)(a) of the Assessment Act. Alternatively, there is by operation of sub-s. 47(2B) a deemed dividend paid by Detail to the respondents and deemed also to be paid out of profits derived by Detail. Alternatively, there is a deemed dividend, under s. 108, of the payments made on behalf of or for the individual benefit of the respondents.

It was submitted by counsel for the respondents:

(a) there cannot be an arrangement within the meaning of s. 260 unless it is at least bipartite and consensual. Reliance was placed upon Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Lutovi Investments Pty. Limited [1978] HCA 55; (1978) 140 CLR 434, especially at 443-4. There cannot be an arrangement if one of the parties is ignorant of the terms. The only arrangement to which the respondents were parties was the deed for the sale of the shares and that is not enough to make them a party to any relevant arrangement for present purposes. The arrangement in the present case consisted of no more than the deed and the transfer of shares in consequence of it. The sale of Detail's business to Trazian was a transaction to which the purchaser (Shareholder) was not a party; nor was there any suggestion in the evidence that Shareholder knew of that transaction. The conduct of Shareholder and its associated corporate entities and individuals was not known, nor was it intended that it be known, to the respondents as vendors. Reliance was placed on clause 8(a) of the deed. Thus there was no relevant "arrangement" for the purposes of s. 260.

(b) Detail, by reason of the sale of its business and the business of the partnership, had become redundant from 19 February 1980 and no longer carried on its business. The respondents were merely seeking a way of realising their interest in Detail that would not attract tax. There was no immediate tax liability or need to make any distribution or to pay Division 7 tax.

(c) As to s. 44, the primary Judge was correct in concluding that the payment to Lockwing was not a dividend paid to "a shareholder" out of profits; nor was it a distribution by the company to a shareholder. The cheque to Lockwing was not a distribution once and for all of any part of the Detail's profits; the company's money remained in its current account until the cheque was negotiated and/or paid after settlement. The cheque to Lockwing when negotiated and/or paid gave rise to an obligation to repay a like amount to Detail unless the new shareholder and/or directors altered that obligation. It was at most the exchange of one chose in action for another and was not a distribution. If there was a distribution it was not wholly out of profits.

(d) As to sub-s. 47(2A) and (2B) and s. 108 it was submitted that there was no distribution for the same reason as in relation to s. 44. If there was a distribution it was made by Detail, so s. 47(2A) could not apply. It was submitted, in support of the respondents' notice of contention, that the primary Judge wrongly held that the payment by Detail to Lockwing, at the direction of Shareholder, was deemed to be a dividend paid to Shareholder as a shareholder of Detail. Once s. 260 has done its work Shareholder cannot be seen to be a shareholder of Detail for the purposes of the relevant assessment. For the same reason the primary Judge's decision that the payment could also be a distribution for the individual benefit of Shareholder within the meaning of s. 108 was erroneous.

Findings

28. Section 260 is not directed to fraudulent conduct or at sham transactions. It is directed to transactions which are real but which the legislature nullifies so far as they operate to avoid tax. An arrangement of the kind to which s. 260 is directed includes any kind of concerted action by which persons arrange their affairs for any of the stated purposes or with any of the stated effects including the purpose or effect of altering the incidence of income tax; and to that extent the arrangement is absolutely void as against the appellant. The word "arrangement" is apt to describe transactions of a looser or more general kind than contracts.

29. Section 260 alters nothing that was done except for the purposes of income tax. It entitles the appellant to look at the result and to ignore all the steps which were taken pursuant to the avoided arrangement.

30. It is, of course, well established that the purpose or effect of an arrangement must be seen from the terms of the arrangement itself or from the overt acts by which it operates or by which it was implemented. The determination of the requisite purpose or effect requires the application of objective tests, not an analysis of subjective states of mind of the persons concerned. The whole of the circumstances of each case must be considered to determine whether there is an arrangement within the meaning of s. 260 which had the purpose or effect of which the section speaks.

31. I stated earlier the relevant facts but it is necessary at this stage to draw the threads together. Detail was a trading company with large current year profits which would in the ordinary course have rendered it liable to substantial primary tax. The respondents, who were the directors and shareholders of Detail, could have chosen not to distribute the profits with the consequence that Detail would have paid a large amount of undistributed profits tax. Alternatively, Detail could have made a sufficient distribution and thereby exposed Mr. and Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon, as the shareholders of Detail, to personal tax. Mr. and Mrs. Clough had what they regarded as a "tax problem". Mr. Clough said in evidence that he and Mrs. Clough "would be in difficulty in paying the tax in that period of time" especially as they needed funds to complete the building of the second block of flats. Their accountant who advised them at all material times on the relevant steps involved, namely, Mr. Gelder, was himself an experienced accountant in income tax matters.

32. The problem that confronted Mr. and Mrs. Clough was basically the same problem that confronted the taxpayers in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1; Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1963) 108 CLR 258; and Mayfield v. Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) [1961] HCA 58; (1963) 108 CLR 323. The first step that was taken was to sell the business of Detail to a new company (Trazian) controlled by the Cloughs. The new company took over the current contracts of Detail, its employees and business and carried on the business of Detail just as Detail had carried it on before. The price which the purchaser of the business, Trazian, agreed to pay to Detail was equal to the price which formed the basis for the ultimate sale of the shares of the respondents in Detail, an amount equal to the net tangible assets of Detail less the procuration fee paid to Mr. Goldspink. Trazian was, of course, controlled by the Cloughs just as Detail had been. The Cloughs funded the purchase by Trazian of Detail's business by lending it the moneys which they obtained from the sale of their shares in Detail to Lockwing as the nominee of Shareholder. Mr. Clough described the transaction as "a formality". His bank manager, Mr. Ray, who was involved in material elements of the settlement, described it as "restructuring . . . mainly for taxation purposes". Cogent evidence of the fact that Mr. Clough regarded it as a formality was that he never bothered to understand the consequences of the transaction as it might affect the financial affairs of himself and his wife. Almost simultaneously, the respondents sold their shares in Detail to Lockwing for a price equal to its net tangible assets less the fee payable to the promoter, Mr. Goldspink. The price made no allowance for any primary tax liability of Detail. Detail had net tangible assets of $744,346 and a potential liability to primary tax of between $250,000 and $300,000. The purchase price was $711,346. The incorporation of Trazian and its acquisition of Detail's business with no change from what existed before can only be viewed realistically as directly related to the sale of the shares by the Cloughs and Gregrhon in Detail. The retention of the businesses under the control of the Cloughs was vital to the arrangements.

33. It was an express condition of the deed of sale of shares from the respondents to the Shareholder interests that a bank cheque in the sum of $744,346 (the value of the net tangible assets of Detail) was to be paid to the nominee of the purchaser, namely, Lockwing, on settlement (Clause 3(iv)). This transfer of what was in effect the whole of the assets in Detail to the purchaser's nominee was a breach of fiduciary duty by the directors of Detail as it denied it the capacity to meet its income tax liabilities. Mr. Clough said that he neither knew nor cared whether this was a loan or a gift or who the recipient was. The moneys were in fact used by Lockwing to pay the respondents the purchase price of their shares in Detail. Mr. Clough regarded it as strange that the purchaser was prepared to pay a price for the shares which took no account of Detail's pending primary tax liability. Both Mr. Clough and Mr. Gelder gave evidence, which was accepted by the trial Judge, that it was in their interests to refrain from further enquiry.

34. The settlement was attended by all interested parties in the office of the bank manager of the Clough's, namely, Mr. Ray. Mr. Ray was instructed in writing that no funds were to be made available to the purchasers until the shareholders had complied with their contractual obligation to, in effect, ensure that the value of Detail's assets was handed over by cheque to Lockwing. The purchase price of the shares was in essence received by the respondents through Lockwing from the assets of Detail. This use of Detail's funds denuded it of funds and produced the consequence that the tax liability of Detail, which had been pending, did not arise.

35. Viewed objectively, to my mind it is plain that there was but one transaction involving a series of integrated and mutually dependent steps including the sale and transfer of shares by the respondents to the Shareholder interests and an interlocking flow of funds. Whether the Cloughs knew that the moneys which were received by Lockwing, in effect representing the value of the assets of Detail, were to be used for the purchase of the shares from them is not the issue. There is no warrant for severing the transaction into the events of which the Cloughs had actual knowledge and the events of which the primary Judge found they were ignorant.

36. The trial Judge regarded as critical the question whether the Cloughs knew that the source of payment of the purchase price of their shares was the money which they had caused to be paid as directors of Detail to Lockwing pursuant to the obligation imposed by clause 3(iv) of the deed. I respectfully disagree with this approach. It introduces subjective elements of the Cloughs' state of mind and knowledge to what is a determination of the relevant circumstances viewed objectively from the terms of the arrangement itself and the overt acts by which it operated.

37. Plainly this is a case where the arrangement served no business, professional or family purpose. Its genesis lay in the desire of the Cloughs to avoid tax being paid either by themselves or by their company, Detail. The basis question is whether the steps taken to achieve this end by themselves, with the assistance of their accountant, are severable from the actions of the Shareholder interests and the use by them of Detail's funds. For the reasons which I have already given no severance of these steps into two segments can be supported.

38. The respondents placed considerable weight upon Slutzkin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1978) 140 CLR 314 and Hennessey v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1975) ATC 4,007.

39. Slutzkin is plainly distinguishable from the present case. In Slutzkin Francis Richard Holdings Pty. Limited, the target company, was not carrying on any business at any relevant time and had become redundant. No tax problem confronted it. It had no problem with respect to impending primary tax liability, nor with respect to any undistributed profits tax. All that the taxpayers sought to do was to realise their shares and the assets of the target company in a manner designed to ensure that the tax liability which would be payable if accumulated profits were distributed by way of dividend or on a liquidation would not be attracted. The relevant activities were capable of explanation as ordinary business dealing. It was simply a realisation by taxpayers of their shares in a dormant company intended to ensure that the realisation itself did not attract a tax liability.

40. In Hennessey's Case the target company was a dormant company with no substantial current year profits. Its only tax problem was to pay tax on its small income by way of interest for the relevant year. There was no obligation on it to make a sufficient distribution in the relevant year of its current year's profits. Zelling J. held that the transaction was a bona fide sale of shares and was wholly explicable as an ordinary business dealing. Hennessey is plainly distinguishable from the present case.

41. In these circumstances it is unnecessary for me to consider the further submission of counsel for the appellant that the respondents, by themselves and their agents, were privy to the elements of the alleged arrangement involving the use of the funds of Detail to pay for the shares.

42. In my opinion s. 260 operates to strike down as against the respondent the deed relating to the sale of shares from the respondent and the subsequent transfer. What does it leave exposed? There is left a payment of money, namely, $744,346 ($744,454 less $108) from Detail to Lockwing at the direction of Shareholder for no consideration and the receipt by the Cloughs and Gregrhon of varying sums making a total of $711,346 (i.e. $744,346 less Mr. Goldspink's fee of $33,000).

43. The question arises whether ss. 44, 47(2B) or 108 apply to render the monies received by the respondents assessable income in their hands. I turn to s. 44.

44. Sub-section 44(1) at the relevant times was as follows:

"44(1) The assessable income of a shareholder in a

company . . . shall . . .

(a) if he is a resident - include

dividends paid to him by the company

out of profits derived by it from any

source;

. . ."

45. The other provisions of s. 44 are immaterial for present purposes. Sub-section 6(1) of the Assessment Act, defines "dividend" as including:

"(a) any distribution made by a company to any of

its shareholders, whether in money or other

property;

. . ."

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Blakeley [1951] HCA 17; (1951) 82 CLR 388 is the first case which requires consideration. The two shareholders of Bob Blakeley Pty. Limited, who were husband and wife, appropriated the assets of the company and discharged its liabilities. The company ceased to carry on business, but the company was not wound up and no liquidator was appointed. The Commissioner of Taxation assessed Mr. Blakeley to income tax in respect of a proportion of the amount which he had received as a result of the appropriation of the assets. It was held that no part of the amount received by Mr. Blakeley could be regarded as a dividend, but the members of the High Court differed as to the grounds for this conclusion. Latham C.J. held that the appropriation of the assets by Mr. Blakeley was not a distribution of the company by way of dividend out of profits of the company within s. 44 because an appropriation by shareholders of the assets of a company by their own act could not be regarded as a distribution by the company. Fullagar J. (with whom Dixon J. agreed) accepted that there was a distribution and said at pp 406-7:

"I would not be prepared to deny that there was a

'distribution' in this case. There was clearly a

'distribution' in Stevenson's Case. But the point

in this case is, as it was in Stevenson's Case, as

to the nature of the receipt. There was not a

distribution of profits, or a distribution out of

profits. What was received was capital. There

was no detachment or severance from the funds of

the company of money or other assets as

representing a profit made by the company. There

was simply a realization of a share investment

(per Starke J. in Thornett's Case). 'The

shareholders . . . receive nothing but the ultimate

capital value of the intangible property

constituted by the shares . . . The shareholder

simply receives his proper proportion of a total

net fund without distinction in respect of the

source of its components, and he receives it in

replacement for his share' (per Rich, Dixon and

McTiernan JJ. in Stevenson's Case). There is, in

my opinion, nothing in the Act which gives the

character of income to this receipt, which was

according to general principles a capital

receipt."

46. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Uther [1965] HCA 42; (1965) 112 CLR 630, Kitto J. in a dissenting judgment criticised the judgment of Fullagar J. His Honour said at p 639 that the effect of the amendments to the law first made by the Income Tax Assessment Act 1934, and repeated when that Act was passed in 1936, was to make shareholders in a company which is a going concern assessable to tax on a principle fundamentally different from that of the previous legislation. His Honour said at p 639:

"The criterion for the inclusion of a shareholder's

receipts from the company is no longer the

'dividend' character of the receipts, that is to

say their income character when considered from

the shareholders' point of view; it is the profit

character - from the company's point of view - of

the source from which distribution should be

made."

The majority of the Court in that case (Taylor and Menzies JJ.) however, approved the decision of Owen J. [1964] HCA 80; (1964) 111 CLR 318 who, sitting at first instance, had followed Blakeley's Case. Taylor J. expressly declined to reconsider the decision of the High Court in Blakeley's Case.

47. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slater Holdings Limited [1984] HCA 78; (1984) 156 CLR 447 Gibbs C.J., with whom the other members of the Court (Mason, Brennan and Deane JJ.) agreed, analysed Blakeley's Case and other cases. The Chief Justice said that he found Kitto J.'s criticism of the judgment of Fullagar J. in Blakeley's Case compelling and at p 457 said this:

"Fullagar J. was right in saying that the

distribution made to the shareholders in Federal

Commissioner of Taxation v. Blakeley was a capital

receipt according to general principles, but he

gave insufficient weight to the change that had

been effected to the law by defining 'dividend' so

as to include a distribution made by a company to

any of its shareholders. . . . There were two

possible grounds for holding that the

distributions in Federal Commissioner of Taxation

v. Blakeley and Federal Commissioner of Taxation

v. Uther were not assessable income. The first,

which appears to have been accepted by Fullagher

J. in the earlier case, is that the receipt in the

hands of the shareholder was capital in nature,

representing as it did, the value of the

shareholder's interest in the company. However,

as I have already said, Kitto J. pointed out in

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Uther that to

take that view would be to disregard the words of

the Act. The second possible ground was that the

distribution was not made out of profits. The

fact that a distribution is itself of a capital

nature does not mean that it did not have its

source in profits; that proposition was recognised

as correct by Lord Reid in Staffordshire Coal &

Iron Co. Limited v. Brogan (1963) 1 WLR 905 at

910 and is consistent with the language of Dixon

C.J. in Parke David & Co. v. Commissioner of

Taxation (1959) 101 CLR at p 530, but in any

case is not novel. However, what appears to be

implicit in the judgment of Taylor J. in Federal

Commissioner of Taxation v. Uther is the

suggestion that to come within s. 44(1)(a) the

distribution must have been made wholly out of

profits; it is not enough that there is a

distribution of a mass of assets which contains

profits. This view may be supported by the fact

that the section does not refer to 'dividends to

the extent to which they were paid to him by the

company out of profits', since, in the light of

the construction given to s. 51 of the Act, the

inclusion of the phrase 'to the extent to which'

would no doubt have allowed a dissection or

apportionment to be made of the distribution: cf.

Ronpibon Tin N.L and Tongkah Compound N.L. v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1949) 78 CLR

47 at p 55 . . . It is unnecessary finally to

decide whether the reasons given by the majority

in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Blakeley

and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Uther were

decisions do not assist the respondent in the

present case, which is plainly distinguishable

from them."

48. The remarks of Kitto J. in Uther's Case and of Gibbs C.J. in Slater Holdings as to the significance of the change to the definition of "dividend" in sub-s. 6(1) are in point in this case and bear directly upon the characterisation of the distributions made by Detail. Also, the correctness of Blakeley's Case is now doubtful.

49. Sub-section 44(1) is directed to the source of the profits derived by the company out of which the dividend is paid. As Kitto J. observed in Uther's Case, in the passage expressly approved by Gibb C.J. in Slater Holdings at p 457, it is the profit character from the company's point of view as the source from which the distribution is made that is relevant. As s. 260 has operated to avoid the agreement for the sale of the shares of the Cloughs and Gregrhon and the transfer of those shares the respondents are treated as remaining as shareholders of Detail. Thus there is left exposed a payment of money from Detail to Lockwing for no consideration and the money was used to source the payments to the respondents which are the subject of these proceedings. Having regard to the inextricably connected flow of funds what is left exposed are payments of money by Detail which give rise to assessable income of the respondents pursuant to s. 44. The source of those payments was the profits derived by Detail which was the reason for the tax scheme in question being put into operation. As Walsh J. said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ellers Motor Sales Pty. Limited [1972] HCA 17; (1972) 128 CLR 602 at 620:

". . . it is appropriate to look at the end result of

the transaction from the point of view of Harcourt

(the target company) and from the point of view of

those who were its shareholders before the

transfer of the shares to Holdings. The end

result was that the profits had gone out from

Harcourt and that an equivalent amount had come

into the hands of its shareholders. To adopt the

phrase used by Fullagar J. in Newton's Case (1957)

96 CLR at p 656, it may be said in my opinion

that 'the only real money' which figured in the

transaction was Harcourt's money."

50. In the present case the profits of Detail went from Detail and came into the hands of its shareholders.

51. Counsel for the respondents submitted that the monies received by them were not dividends in their hands because the moneys distributed by Detail, namely, $744,346, included its paid up share capital of $108 and it is not permissible to dissect the receipts into profits of $744,238 and capital of $108. It is true that the distribution to the extent of the capital of $108 is not struck down by s. 260, but that is because the $108 was share capital and not profits of Detail constituting assessable income. There was no alteration of the incidence of income tax with respect to the $108 which s. 260 could annihilate. Section 260 avoids an arrangement "so far as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect of . . . altering the incidence of any income tax . . ." There is no difficulty in dissecting the distribution of Detail's monies, leaving in the respondents' hands the sum of $744,238 being payments made out of Detail's profits.

52. It is unnecessary to consider ss. 47 or 108.

53. It remains to consider s. 226 of the Assessment Act. That section, in the form which it took at relevant times, imposed additional tax where a taxpayer failed to furnish information relating to a matter affectin his liability to tax (sub-s. 226(1)) or omitted assessable income from his return (para. 226(2)(a)). The appellant has power to remit any such additional tax or any part of it in any case for reasons which he considers sufficient. The appellant remitted additional tax payable by the respondents to the extent of 40% of the tax avoided plus a 20% per annum interest component. The respondents appeal against this remission on the basis that it should have been 100% of the tax paid by them by reason of the application of s. 260. They assert, in brief, that the appellant failed to take into account the following facts said to be relevant, namely:

(i) that at all material times until the enactment of the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 ("the TUCT Act") the prevailing view held by lawyers in this country was that the amounts assessed to the respondents were not assessable;

(ii) that the purpose and effect of the TUCT Act was to make vendors in the position of the respondents responsible for the company's tax; and

(iii) that the respondents had under the TUCT Act paid a total of $337,291.20 to the appellant, all of which had been paid prior to the date of the assessments in issue in these proceedings.

54. The appellant disputes the fact asserted in (i) above and submits that this Court should not deal with this question on appeal, but should remit it to the Supreme Court for determination because there was limited evidence on the matter before the primary Judge and his Honour's findings allowing the appeal did not require him to deal with the point.

55. There was discussion before us as to the effect of certain press releases of statements by the Federal Treasurer on 30 October 1984 and 16 April 1987 relating to the "doubling-up" of tax liabilities in what counsel said were described as "s. 260 pre-tax company profit stripping cases".

56. The question of the remission of additional tax under s. 226 was not fully dealt with in the evidence before the Supreme Court and we do not have the benefit of any findings on the point by his Honour. In all the circumstances the matters should be remitted to the Supreme Court for the purpose of hearing evidence and submissions and deciding this question. The Jurisdiction of Courts (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 1987, which by sub-s. 4(3) transferred certain proceedings from the Supreme Court to this Court, does not affect this matter.

57. I would allow the appeals and refer the matters back to the Supreme Court for the purpose of dealing with the s. 226 question. The respondents are to pay the costs of the appellant at first instance and on appeal.

2. I want shortly to state why I disagree with the conclusion of the learned primary judge that s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") does not apply in the circumstances of these cases.

3. Section 260(1) relevantly provides:-

"Every contract, agreement, or arrangement made or

entered into, orally or in writing, whether before

or after the commencement of this Act, shall so

far as it has or purports to have the purpose or

effect of in any way, directly or indirectly -

(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;

(b) relieving any person from liability to pay

any income tax or make any return;

(c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or

liability imposed on any person by this Act;

or

(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any

respect,

be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner,

or in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but

without prejudice to such validity as it may have

in any other respect or for any other purpose."

4. The ten steps of what occurred in early May 1980, are set out by Lockhart J.. That outline shows that Transia Corporation Limited ("Transia") was the short term financier of the scheme. Transia is probably another example of taxation industry humour like "Pactolus" in Newton v. The Federal Commissioner for Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1.

5. In that case, Lord Denning, speaking for the Privy Council, said at p 9:-

". . . when one looks at the way the transactions

were effected in Jaques v. Federal Commissioner of

Taxation [1923] HCA 70; (1924) 34 CLR 328; Clarke v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 46; (1932) 48 CLR 56, and

Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1953) 87

CLR 548 - the way cheques were exchanged for

like amounts and so forth - there can be no doubt

at all that the purpose and effect of that way of

doing things was to avoid tax."

6. The effect of what occurred in this transaction was that Transia had recouped the funds it lent to Shareholder Pty. Limited ("Shareholder"); Detail Furniture Co. Pty. Ltd. ("Detail") had been stripped of the whole of its cash assets and had no other assets; Mr. & Mrs. Clough, as directors and shareholders of Trazian Pty. Ltd. ("Trazian") were carrying on the same businesses which Detail and Detail Furniture Sales had previously carried on; and Trazian owed to Mr. & Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon the amount which they had lent to Trazian to purchase the business and assets of Detail.

7. It seems to have been ignored that Trazian also owed $191,840.00 as well as advances at book value, for the business, Detail Furniture Sales, to Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon.

8. The money received by Mr. & Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon for the sale of their shares was the entire net operating profit and reserves of Detail, less the fee of $33,000.00 paid to Mr. Goldspink. Mr. & Mrs. Clough controlled the furniture manufacturing and sales businesses, and Detail was entirely denuded of resources to pay any current year primary tax and any undistributed profits tax.

9. The learned primary judge concluded that it was beyond argument that, as far as the Shareholder interests were concerned, the object of the transaction was to strip Detail of its assets and use those assets to pay the respondents the purchase price of the shares in Detail less the fee of $33,000.00. He found, nonetheless, that the respondents as vendors, and the Shareholder interests were at arms length and that there was nothing to suggest that Mr. & Mrs. Clough believed the transaction they were entering into was dishonest or that it would result in Detail's assets being the source of the purchase money for their shares. He concluded:-

"The appellants in the present case in my view bona

fide believed that the sale of the shares did no

more than pass any liability for tax to

Shareholder and whether Shareholder could devise

means to pay less tax than they the appellants

would have had to pay if Detail had made a

distribution was a matter that did not concern

them. They had no knowledge that the Company's

profits would be used to pay for their shares. In

my view they cannot, under s.260, be made parties

to a tax avoidance scheme, devised by another, of

which they were not aware."

10. In my respectful opinion, I do not think it is possible to sever the events in this case so neatly. I acknowledge that hindsight is a tempting siren, to be avoided.

11. This was not merely a sale of shares to a third party, with the vendors after settlement being indifferent as to how the purchaser dealt with the tax obligations of the company purchased. The retention of the business was an integral part of the scheme and it is to be noted that there was a correspondence between the sale price to Trazian and the sale price of the shares in Detail to Shareholder.

12. It is not necessary, even in the criminal law, before a person can be a party to an arrangement or scheme, that that person have full knowledge of all the details and steps of that arrangement or scheme; a fortiori, when that person is wilfully blind. Here, both Mr. Clough and his accountant, Mr. Gelder, admitted in evidence it was in their interests not to make inquiry.

13. In my opinion, in the context of s.260, calculated ignorance is not bliss.

14. In my view there was but one transaction constituted by a series of interlocking steps, the flow of funds being circular. The applicability of s.260 to that arrangement is not dependent on Mr. Clough and his accountant having actual awareness of every aspect of that arrangement.

15. Moreover, in this case, the overt acts of the arrangement into which the respondents entered include what was arranged by Mr. Gelder and Mr. Ray, their bank manager. In particular, clause 3(iv) of the deed of sale required that a bank cheque for $744,346.00 was to be paid to Shareholder's nominee, Lockwing Pty. Limited ("Lockwing"). The primary judge found that the effect of this was that the cash assets of Detail were used to fund the purchase from the respondents of their shares. Mr. Ray had been instructed in writing that no funds were to be made available to the purchasers until the respondents had complied with the obligation contained in clause 3(iv). This payment was a clear breach of fiduciary duty owed by the directors of Detail; it is quite inexplicable as part of an ordinary business dealing, by directors and shareholders of Detail acting bona fide.

16. In this case there was no commercial, professional or family purpose to be served. Mr. Clough admitted that he had a "tax problem" in February 1980. Detail, for the first eight months of the financial year after 30 June 1979, had a net operating profit of $604,445.00 and was liable for primary tax at the rate of 46 cents in the dollar on net profit and Division 7 undistributed profits tax to the extent it failed to make sufficient distribution of its after-tax profits. Mr. & Mrs. Clough and Gregrhon would be obliged to pay additional tax on any distribution made by Detail by them.

17. The attitude of Mr. Clough to the whole transaction was that it was "a formality" and Mr. Rae described the transaction as "restructuring . . . mainly for taxation purposes".

18. In my opinion s.260 operates to strike down as against the Commissioner the deed of sale of the shares.

19. Concerning the conclusion that s.44(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 renders the monies received by the respondents (apart from the $108.00 capital) assessable income in their hands, there is nothing I wish to add to the reasons of Fisher J. and Lockhart J..
