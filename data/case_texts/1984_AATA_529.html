Re Clifford Brian Amess and Commissioner of Superannuation [1984] AATA 529 (14 December 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CLIFFORD BRIAN AMESS

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A84/18

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr A.N. Hall (Deputy President)

Mrs H.E. Hallowes (Member)

Dr A.H. Marsh (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - benefit classification certificate - recommendation by Commonwealth Medical Officer that certificate not to issue - delay in considering - recommendation not accepted by delegate of Commissioner for Superannuation - recommendation changed - certificate issued specifying anxiety - applicant then on sick leave prior to retirement on grounds of invalidity due to depression/anxiety - whether Commissioner obliged to issue certificate within a reasonable time - whether Commissioner estopped from issuing certificate by acquiescence - whether limit on material to which Commissioner and Tribunal could properly have regard - purposive approach to interpretation - whether applicant "not likely" to continue to be eligible employee until maximum retiring age - decision of Commissioner affirmed

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16(2), 16(3), 16(4), 66(2)

Martin v Commonwealth of Australia (1975) 7 ACTR 1

Turner v Nominal Defendant [1981] FCA 60; (1981) 51 FLR 342

Thompson v Goold & Co (1910) AC 409

R v Skurray (1967) 86 WN (Part 1) (NSW) 1

Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Ganke (1975) 1 NSWLR 252

Re Homsy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALN N27

Cessnock Municipal Council v Attorney-General (1956) LGR 287

Re Bundy and Secretary, Department of Housing and Construction (1980) 2 ALD 735

Laidley v The Borough of Plattsburg (1894) 10 NSW WN 161

De Bussche v Alt (1878) 8 Ch.D 286

Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 659

Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation (No. G86 of 1983)

HEARING

CANBERRA

14:12:1984

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

1. The applicant in this matter, Mr C.B. Amess, joined the Commonwealth Teaching Service in 1979 at the age of 49 years. Initially, he was only able to secure appointment as a temporary teacher at the Belconnen High School. However, he was successful in obtaining appointment as a permanent teacher at the Woden Valley High School from the commencement of the 1980 academic year.

2. Mr Amess became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") on 1 August 1980. As such, he was required by the Commissioner pursuant to s.16(2) of the Act to undergo a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer. He was examined on 25 August 1980 by Dr M. Rajasingham who certified on Mr Amess' Medical Report Form that she was of the opinion that the applicant met the general medical standard required for permanent officers of the Public Service. This opinion was confirmed by Dr Helen S. Turner who was then employed as Senior Medical Officer in the Commonwealth Department of Health and who signed a recommendation to that effect as "Adviser to the Commissioner for Superannuation". There is no evidence of any notification having been given to Mr Amess either by the Commonwealth Medical Officer or by the Commissioner for Superannuation as to the outcome of this medical examination.

3. There appears to have been a delay of just over one year before the medical report was considered in the Commissioner's Office. We were told that there were in the order of 30,000 such forms to be checked each year and that the work of processing the forms was constantly in arrears (Tr.47).

4. On 7 September 1981 a recommendation was signed by Mrs K. Nolan, a Clerk Class 4 employed in the Commissioner's Office (The Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office - AGRBO) recommending that a benefit classification certificate (BCC) should not be issued in respect of Mr Amess. This recommendation was not accepted by the Commissioner's delegate. Further inquiries were directed to be pursued, initially in respect of a prior Superannuation scheme of which Mr Amess had been a member (see letter dated 18 September 1981) and later concerning a previous history of anxiety related chest pains mentioned in the applicant's Medical Report Form as having occurred during his employment at the Belconnen High School during 1979. The AGRBO file discloses that these latter inquiries were initiated on 25 January 1982 by Mr D.H. Grayson, a senior officer employed in AGRBO. However, it was not until 30 March 1982 that a formal request was sent to the Senior Medical Officer requesting a copy of a medical report from Dr E.J. Cassar (to whom the applicant had been referred for specialist medical opinion relating to his medical problems at the end of 1979) and asking for a comment on the likelihood of recurrence of chest pains should Mr Amess again be placed in a situation of stress in his teaching career.

5. On 6 April 1982 Dr Turner replied to the Commissioner's Office as follows (Ex. T4/24):-

"When examined on 25 August 1980, Mr Amess stated

that he had been referred to Dr Cassar because of

discomfort in the upper chest. No report was

obtained from Dr Cassar as CMO was satisfied that

there was no cardiovascular basis for the pain.

However I have given this matter further

consideration. A Benefit Classification

Certificate should be given for Anxiety ICD 300.0."

Following receipt of this revised recommendation from Dr Turner a BCC was issued pursuant to s.16(4) of the Act on 12 May 1982 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation. The substance of this certificate was as follows (Ex. T4/26):-

"After considering the report(s) of the medical

examination(s) undertaken by the above (i.e. the

applicant) in relation to his/her proposing to

become or becoming an eligible employee under the

Superannuation Act, I am of the opinion that he/she

is not likely, by reason of or for a reason

connected with the undermentioned physical or

mental condition(s), to continue to be an eligible

employee until he/she attains his/her maximum

retiring age.

Physical/Mental Condition(s)

ANXIETY"

6. On 12 November 1982 Mr Amess requested reconsideration of the decision to issue a BCC. Following further investigation, a delegate of the Commissioner decided, on 13 January 1984, to confirm the original decision to issue a BCC. Mr Amess has applied to this Tribunal for review of that decision.

7. The review is complicated by the fact that, with effect from the close of business on 11 January, 1983, Mr Amess was retired from the Public Service on the grounds of invalidity. The medical condition necessitating retirement was specified in the Retirement Instrument signed on 29 November 1982 as "Depression/Anxiety 100%" (Ex. T4/35). The evidence is that Mr Amess suffered a severe nervous collapse towards the end of 1981 due to the stress of his teaching duties at Woden Valley High. He has not worked since the end of the 1981 school year.

8. In the light of the evidence relating to the applicant's retirement condition the Commissioner has formed the opinion, pursuant to s.66(2) of the Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of Mr Amess was caused or substantially contributed to by anxiety or by a condition connected with anxiety and that as a consequence his retirement benefits were limited to a lump sum payment of $12,720.36 (Ex. T5/54). This second decision is not presently before the Tribunal for review. For the purposes of this review we have concentrated our attention, therefore, on the medical evidence available as to the applicant's medical condition in 1979 and 1980.

9. As earlier mentioned, the decision under review arises pursuant to s.16(4) of the Act. In order to put that provision in context it is necessary to set out the provisions of s.16(2), (3) and (4) which are as follows:-

"16(2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes

of this section, require a person (other than a

person to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to

become or becomes an eligible employee to undergo,

within such period as the Commissioner specifies,

such medical examination or examinations by an

approved medical practitioner or practitioners as

the Commissioner determines.

(3) A report or reports of the result or

results of the medical examination or examinations

shall be furnished to the Commissioner.

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the

report or reports, and such other matters (if any)

as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he

is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical

or mental condition or conditions referred to in

the report or reports, to continue to be an

eligible employee until the person attains his

maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue

a benefit classification certificate to that

effect, being a certificate in which the relevant

condition or conditions is or are specified."

10. Mr T. Higgins who appeared on behalf of the applicant placed a number of submissions before us challenging the power of the Commissioner to issue a BCC in the circumstances of the present case. It will be convenient, however, if we leave those submissions aside until we have indicated our findings on the essential questions of fact that arise in this matter. In reaching those findings we have had regard to the s.37 documents lodged with the Tribunal on behalf of the Commissioner and to the information contained on the files of the AGRBO, the ACT Schools Authority and the Department of Health relating to Mr Amess. We have also had regard to the evidence of the applicant and his wife; to the evidence of Dr John Howe, the applicant's treating general practitioner who was called to give evidence on the applicant's behalf; and to the evidence of Mr D.H. Grayson, Dr H.S. Turner, Dr E.J. Cassar and Dr B.J.W. Hughson, all of whom were called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent.

11. The evidence establishes that prior to attending his entry medical examination on 25 August 1980 Mr Amess consulted Dr Howe for assistance in completing the statements relating to his personal medical history in the Medical Report Form (Ex. T4/13-14). The answers and information provided in Parts A, B and C of this Form were completed in Dr Howe's handwriting (Ex. T4/12-14). The applicant signed a declaration on the back page that the personal statement on pages 2 and 3 was true and correct to the best of his knowledge.

12. In Part B headed "Applicant's Statement of Personal Medical History" the applicant was asked a number of questions, including whether he was now suffering or had ever suffered from mental or nervous conditions or anxiety state. The applicant answered "no" (see Questions 1(b) and 1(c)). In answer to a similar question with respect to "disease of the heart or blood vessels" (see Question 2(d)), the answer "yes" was inserted together with the following explanation - "Coronary artery spasm - anxiety related - now clear of symptoms".

13. In Part C of the Medical Report Form the answer to Question 2(d) above was amplified by Dr Howe as follows:-

"Seen by Dr Howe in November 1979 for discomfort in

upper chest. Subsequent referral to Dr Cassar

revealed a normal ECG with exercise (December 79)

and he said it (discomfort) was probably due to

anxiety from job at Belconnen High School

(stressful situation). Coronary arteries found to

be normal. No symptoms since ceasing job at

Belconnen. No medication required."

In an adjacent column headed CMO's comments which also carried the notation "notes are required on all affirmative answers", there appears a tick indicating (presumably) that the CMO has noted and accepted the explanation provided.

14. In August, 1980, neither Dr Rajasingham nor Dr Turner considered that the events of November/December 1979 warranted further investigation. When Dr Turner changed her recommendation in April 1982, she did so on the basis of a reconsideration of the information contained in the Medical Report Form and not on the basis of any further reports from the applicant's treating doctors. She said that she had not felt the need to obtain a report from Dr Cassar or Dr Howe because she was satisfied from the information on the form that she should recommend a BCC for anxiety. Dr Turner said that Commonwealth Medical Officers were meant to take heed of a person's occupation. She said that she may have thought that an anxious teacher was perhaps a significant risk. She added "I would have decided that there was a significant possibility that he was not again able to work until the normal retiring age" (Tr.121).

15. Dr Turner denied any knowledge of the fact that, concurrently with the request from the Commissioner for Superannuation in March 1982 for further information in relation to the applicant's entry medical examination, arrangements were being made by the ACT Schools Authority for the applicant to be medically examined in connection with his proposed invalidity retirement. This issue was pursued at some length by Mr Higgins. However, we accept Dr Turner's denial and regard it as no more than coincidental that, whilst the Commissioner's Office was still considering the question whether the applicant's superannuation entitlements needed to be qualified by the issue of a BCC, the ACT Schools Authority was considering retiring Mr Amess on the grounds of invalidity. Although Mr Higgins had access to the files of the AGBRO, the ACT Schools Authority and the Department of Health, he was unable to refer us to any material on those files that would throw doubt on Dr Turner's evidence in that regard.

16. However, we do not accept that the evidence contained on the Medical Report Form was sufficient to justify a finding by Dr Turner that the applicant had suffered in 1979 from an anxiety state in the sense in which that expression is used in paragraph 300.0 of the Manual of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Injuries and Causes of Death - Volume 1 (World Health Organisation, Geneva, 1977). Dr Turner acknowledged that she had no special qualifications in psychiatric medicine and that in forming the opinion that she did she had probably relied upon the apparent relationship of the applicant's coronary spasm combined with anxiety and a stressful situation at work (Tr.144). Dr Hughson who is an experienced psychiatrist, and who was called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent, was quite positive that, although the information disclosed on the Medical Report Form should have prompted further investigations, because of the risks of recurrence of a stress related illness, that information alone did not support a diagnosis of "Anxiety ICD 300.0" (Tr.159/160).

17. There is, however, further evidence available to us from the applicant, his wife and his doctors, which was not available to Dr Turner or to the Commissioner, and which throws light upon the applicant's medical condition in 1979 and 1980.

18. The applicant's evidence was that, as a temporary teacher at Belconnen High School in 1979 he was given a group of children who were extremely difficult to control. He was also given work for which he was not qualified such as teaching physical education and geometrical drawing. He said that as a temporary teacher he had no grounds for refusal of any duties that the Headmaster might see fit to give him. Mr Amess said that he consulted Dr Howe because he was concerned about his job and was undergoing some strain. Mr Amess' evidence in this regard was somewhat vague, due no doubt to his current medical problems. We have made allowance for that in evaluating his evidence and, on matters of detail, have preferred the evidence of his wife. It seems clear however, that one of the main reasons for Mr Amess seeking medical advice was that he was under stress at work, he was experiencing chest pains and he was, quite naturally, concerned about his heart.

19. Dr Howe's records indicate that Mr Amess had first consulted him on 28 May 1979 and that there were four consultations during that year. It appears that it was on or about 10 November 1979 that, in view of the applicant's complaints of chest pains, an ECG was taken, which showed anterior coronary ischaemia. On 21 November 1979 Dr Howe referred Mr Amess to Dr E.J. Cassar for specialist advice. Dr Cassar is a physician specialising in cardiology.

20. Mrs Amess said that following the consultation with Dr Howe her husband had rung her at work and said that Dr Howe had told him that he had a critical heart condition and that he could fall dead within a week. Dr Howe, it may be noted, had no recollection of having made these comments. Both Mr and Mrs Amess said, however, that they had been greatly worried by this advice and were greatly relieved when Dr Cassar's more intensive investigations had disclosed that Mr Amess did not have a serious heart condition.

21. The evidence is rather confused as to the length of time during which Mr Amess had suffered from chest pains. Mrs Amess said, that prior to August, 1979, her husband had never complained of chest pains nor had he suffered from chest pains since being reassured by Dr Cassar in November, 1979, that he did not have a serious heart condition.

22. However, in the letter of referral from Dr Howe to Dr Cassar dated 21 November 1979 Dr Howe said:-

"He came to see me for unexplained chest pain over

the last two to three years. He gets "?" angina

on waking, retiring and hoeing. His present job

is rather stressful (teacher)."

Dr Cassar had also taken a history of left parasternal discomfort for two to three years, the nature of which had changed in August 1979 in so far as pain was coming on with exertion. Dr Cassar said that the applicant, at all times, was trying to indicate to him that it was the stressful nature of the job at Belconnen High School that had brought on the problem (see Dr Cassar's notes on the back of Dr Howe's letter of referral - Ex. G).

23. In his evidence Dr Howe said that he had recorded in his notes that Mr Amess had retrosternal chest pains since swallowing a piece of crystal in 1973. The fact that this incident occurred was confirmed by Mrs Amess, who placed the incident, however, in 1975. It was said by Mrs Amess that, as a consequence, there was an irritation in her husband's chest which would cause fits of coughing that would affect his voice. We note, in this regard, that on 24 March 1981 Dr Cassar wrote to Dr Howe saying that Mr Amess had mentioned that "for some years now he has had irritation in the tracheal region which sounds inflammatory in nature". Dr Cassar went on to suggest that, perhaps, laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy were necessary, but he left it to Dr Howe to follow up if considered appropriate. Whether these matters were pursued, we do not know.

24. In his evidence, however, Dr Cassar said that he had taken a history of at least five years of loss of speech under stressful events that had no other explanation (Tr.97). Dr Cassar said that the applicant had made no mention to him of swallowing a piece of crystal. He claimed that the applicant had been unable to explain his loss of speech and had asked him for an explanation.

25. Finally, it needs to be noted in respect of the events of 1979, that the applicant was worried over the temporary nature of his appointment at Belconnen High School. Mrs Amess explained the problems most clearly in her evidence (Tr.36):-

"He had a number of problems. Every holidays they

would put him off. He was a temporary teacher, so

when the holidays came around they would put him

off with no guarantee that he would be re-employed.

We had just bought a house, had obligations. I

think there was about 16,000 teachers in Australia

unemployed at the time and also he was doing a lot

of subjects at Belconnen High that I do not think

he really should have been asked to do, including

the physicial education responsibility."

Mr Amess said that when he had been declared redundant he had become concerned about the fact that he had no job and no income to meet his financial responsibilities.

26. There is no doubt in the light of this evidence and the evidence of Dr Howe and Dr Cassar that the applicant was in a state of considerable anxiety in November 1979. There was evidence before us that since his marriage in 1970 he had moved from place to place to allow his wife to pursue her career (Ex. T4/20). At the age of 49, however, he was without a secure job and facing a very competitive job market. His teaching duties at Belconnen High School had required him to teach difficult children in subjects for which he was ill-qualified and this had caused him considerable stress. He had the worry of a suspected serious heart condition.

27. The significance of Mr Amess' anxiety, and whether it reflected a psychiatric illness was, however, a matter of some divergence of opinion. Dr Howe considered that the applicant's anxiety was related to his chest pains and his fears that he had a serious heart complaint. He had not regarded the anxiety as other than a normal reaction to Mr Amess' concern. Asked whether in November 1979 he had formed any view as to whether Mr Amess was suffering from any mental or neurotic illness Dr Howe replied:-

"Only to the extent that he was an anxious sort of

gentleman, but no specific psychiatric problems."

At no stage had he prescribed any medication to treat any psychiatric condition. Dr Howe said that once the applicant was reassured that he had no serious heart condition, the chest pains subsided and his anxiety had become much more settled. This had confirmed his original opinion about the source of his anxiety.

28. Dr Howe said that when Mr Amess had consulted him on 7 August 1980 for assistance in completing his Medical Report Form, he had appeared to be in good health, both mentally and physically. He agreed that he had written "No" to the question about "anxiety state". He added (Tr.79) - "I think, perhaps, I should have qualified that by saying 'significant anxiety state'". It is consistent with this evidence that Dr Howe wrote in a report dated 7 September, 1981 (Ex. T4/18) that - "I have no record of undue stress or psychiatric illness until 20 July 1981 when Mr Amess was seen. On this occasion he was found to be under great stress relating to pressures of work."

29. Dr Cassar, however, gave evidence that it had been his opinion in 1979 that from the history given by Mr Amess and from his appearance, he had had a chronic anxiety state or a chronic anxious personality over a number of years (Tr.106). Dr Cassar said that the applicant had offered the view that stress was causing his problems. In Dr Cassar's opinion, the stress was due to his employment at Belconnen High School. Dr Cassar had formed the opinion that Mr Amess had mild coronary artery disease with superimposed coronary artery spasm. Anxiety, he said, was one of the factors related to spasm. Thus, whereas Dr Howe thought the chest pains were causing anxiety, Dr Cassar attributed the chest pains to anxiety.

30. Dr Cassar said that although he was not a specialist in psychiatric medicine he had, as a consultant physician, had training in psychiatry and considered that he was qualified to give an opinion as to the applicant's personality and his previous psychiatric state. When a patient has chronic anxiety, he said, he manifests that in a different way to someone who just presents anxious about a symptom. Such a person manifests an appearance of stress constantly, whatever he is told or however one tries to relieve his anxiety.

31. The evidence indicates that Dr Cassar saw Mr Amess again on 18 December 1979 and 8 January 1980. Dr Cassar said that at these visits Mr Amess' anxieties did not appear to be allayed. However, when he saw him again on 24 March 1981 for a final review, his symptoms of anxiety had at that stage ceased.

32. In a letter to the applicant's solicitor dated 16 August 1982 (Ex. T4/33), written at the request of Mr Amess in support of a claim for employee's compensation following his invalidity retirement, Dr Cassar said that at the time of his original consultation on 29 November 1979 he had formed the opinion that Mr Amess "had anxiety state with secondary coronary artery spasm". He added:-

"On delving into the reasons for his anxiety state,

it was my opinion that this was a direct

consequence of your client not having security of

tenure in his position as a school teacher in the

Belconnen High School, stating that his future

employment was in doubt following the Christmas

school break of December 1979. At last review in

March 1981 there was no evidence of ongoing anxiety

state."

33. Mr Higgins cross-examined Dr Cassar extensively as to the reason why he had not used the expression "anxiety state" in any of his correspondence with Dr Howe between 29 November 1979 and 24 March 1981 (the period covered by the various consultations). In this regard, Dr Howe's evidence was he had not understood from the letters written to him by Dr Cassar that it was Dr Cassar's opinion that the applicant did have an anxiety state.

34. In our view, however, a fair reading of the correspondence between Dr Howe and Dr Cassar was adequate to convey quite clearly Dr Cassar's opinion that Mr Amess was not suffering from serious heart disease but that his angina-like heart pains were due to anxiety-induced coronary artery spasm. In his letter dated 29 November 1979 Dr Cassar said (inter alia):-

"Thank you for referring this extremely anxious

teacher from Belconnen High School. It would

appear that his anxiety is mostly being contributed

to by his job which I understand is temporary, is

only definitely continuing until the next school

break and his future employment is completely in

doubt. Understandably then significant stressful

symptoms are occurring and are complicating the

already diagnosed coronary artery ischaemia on

resting ECG as per my comments of ECG dated

November 10 last.....

I suggest that mild coronary artery disease is

present with superimposed coronary artery spasm and

apart from removal from his stressful situation at

Belconnen High we can offer medication Isoptin as

you were already thinking of in a dose of 40 mgs

b.d. to commence with."

Dr Cassar concluded by saying to Dr Howe that "I suggested that his problem was a minor one, that it should respond to the therapy offered....".

35. In his letter dated 18 December 1979 Dr Cassar reported that Mr Amess' exercise ECG had proved normal and added - "he has been informed that his long-standing recurring pains are due to coronary artery spasm and that Isoptin 40 mgs b.d. for at least 12 months is recommended". Dr Cassar commented that this medication had proved to be completely effective and added "Hopefully he will find a suitable job which will further relieve his anxieties".

36. Following some complications flowing from the Isoptin, Dr Cassar was consulted again about the suspension of that medication - a course of action in which he concurred. In a letter to Dr Howe dated 8 January, 1980, Dr Cassar said that his advice to Mr Amess had been that if angina occurred Mr Amess was to use anginine sublingually as necessary. Dr Howe was asked to advise if serious angina occurred so that an appropriate alternative to Isoptin could be commenced.

37. In our view, the observations made by Dr Cassar to Dr Howe are consistent with the evidence that he gave before the Tribunal that in his opinion Mr Amess had a long-standing history of anxiety related symptoms. We reject therefore any suggestion that Dr Cassar formed that opinion in retrospect in the light of any knowledge that he may have acquired in 1982 as to Mr Amess' pending retirement on the grounds of invalidity. In this regard, Dr Cassar said in his evidence that he did not know the circumstances surrounding Mr Amess' retirement in 1982. However, he added that, if those circumstances were related to anxiety, this would support his opinion that there had been a chronic anxiety state dating back to at least 1976 (Tr.111-112).

38. Dr Cassar's evidence was also challenged by Mr Higgins on the basis of alleged inaccuracies or inadequacies in the history recorded in his report dated 29 November 1979. Dr Cassar had noted that "Mr Amess does not smoke to excess". The evidence of Mrs Amess was that her husband had never smoked during the 15 years that she had known him. However, in the circumstances of this case, we do not regard the findings as to smoking as having any significance so far as Dr Cassar's ultimate diagnosis was concerned. The other respect in which Dr Cassar's evidence was challenged was in his alleged failure to get a history in relation to the swallowing of a fragment of crystal in 1973 or 1975. However, we accept Dr Cassar's evidence that he asked Mr Amess for any explanation that he might be able to offer for his loss of voice and that Mr Amess told him he could not explain it. Whilst we also accept the evidence of the applicant and his wife that the incident occured, we do not consider that the evidence enables us to find whether the fits of coughing and loss of voice are related to that incident or whether they are psychosomatic in origin (see Tr.100).

39. Finally, Dr Cassar was asked the following questions during his examination in chief by Mr Whithear on behalf of the Commissioner (Tr.88):-

"Doctor if you had been asked at that time (i.e.

1979) what the prognosis was for Mr Amess, given

the medical situation that you assessed, would you

have seen him as being able to cope with employment

until he reached maximum retiring age?---No, I

would not have.

What would have lead you to that conclusion

specifically?---Predominantly the anxiety

personality which he manifested."

His evidence in this respect was not challenged in cross-examination.

40. Dr Hughson gave evidence that the currently accepted view of the origin of an anxiety state is that it occurs on the basis of a current precipitant, a stress of some kind or other acting on a vulnerability, personal vulnerability which is commonly described in terms of a range of personality characteristics that could ultimately be some biological phenomena not yet understood. It is almost invariable, he said, that stress is associated with the onset of such condition.

41. Dr Hughson first saw Mr Amess on 28 September 1983. He said that, in his opinion, Mr Amess gave evidence of personality characteristics which would pre-dispose him to an anxiety state. Dr Hughson said that he had been unable to form any independent assessment of the applicant's mental state in 1979 or 1980 because the applicant had been unable to remember or describe any symptoms of his condition at that time. However, he had available to him the reports of Dr Howe and Dr Cassar and had been prepared to accept Dr Cassar's opinion that the applicant had an anxiety state in 1979 and the beginning of 1980. Anxiety and depression, he said, were very common disorders and the great majority of people suffering such conditions were seen by non-psychiatry practitioners. Anxiety, he said, is notorious for the fact that the symptoms tend to wax and wane in severity or to remit for periods of months or years. However, recurrence is more the rule than not.

42. There was also in evidence before us a medical report by Dr John Riley, a psychiatrist practising in Richmond, Victoria. Dr Riley first saw Mr Amess on 15 December 1981 and made no reference in his report to the events in 1979. He made the comment, however (which was substantially the same as Dr Hughson's opinion) that "by personality he probably has an obsessive and rather rigidly conscientious personality and this has made him vulnerable to stressful demands placed on him by his duties as a teacher of special problem children" (Ex. T4/21). There was also in evidence the report of Dr R.G. Myers, a consultant psychiatrist in Melbourne who first saw the applicant on 14 December 1981. Dr Myers' opinion was as follows:-

"According to the information available now it

appears likely that in 1979 Mr Amess experienced

some anxiety, largely a 'situational reaction'

apparently precipitated by being declared redundant

in his employment. It seems that these symptoms

were relatively transient and that when he obtained

further employment he recovered. This seems to

have been insufficient evidence upon which to

conclude in August 1980 that Mr Amess 'was not

likely by reason of, or for a reason connected with

'anxiety' to be able to continue working until age

65'.

The symptoms in 1979 and those in 1981 each

appeared to have represented an anxiety reaction;

in the first instance largely in reaction to

insecurity of tenure then redundant, and in the

second to the stress of teaching disturbed

children. Thus Mr Amess appears to have suffered

two episodes of the same syndrome (pattern of

symptoms) largely as a consequence of separate

stresses. Whether this means that the two

episodes can be regarded as 'separate and discreet

conditions' seems to be a semantic or legal

argument rather than a clinical issue."

43. As will be apparent, there was some conflict in the medical evidence available as to whether or not the applicant suffered an anxiety state, in the sense in which that expression is understood in psychiatry, in 1979. In our view, however, despite the criticism levelled at Dr Cassar's evidence by Mr Higgins, the evidence of Dr Cassar is to be preferred to the evidence of Dr Howe or any of the other witnesses who were called to give evidence or whose reports were otherwise before the Tribunal. Dr Cassar is the only specialist medical witness who had the opportunity to observe Mr Amess and to form an assessment of his condition at the end of 1979 and the beginning of 1980. We accept that by reason of his qualifications and experience as a specialist physician with a particular interest in cardiology, it was within Dr Cassar's competence to diagnose an anxiety state. We rely upon the evidence of Dr Hughson as support for accepting Dr Cassar's opinion. Given the absence of any significant evidence of heart disease, we think it is more probable, as Dr Cassar did, that the applicant's chest pains were primarily due to stress than that the chest pains were primarily the cause of Mr Amess' anxiety, as Dr Howe considered.

44. We do not accept the submission advanced by Mr Higgins that Dr Cassar's opinion that the applicant suffered an anxiety state should be seen as having been formed with the wisdom of hindsight in 1982. We consider that if Dr Cassar had been approached by the Commonwealth Medical Officer in August 1980 for his opinion as to the applicant's medical condition at the end of 1979 and the beginning of 1980, Dr Cassar's opinion would have been that which he gave in evidence before the Tribunal. In this regard, we are greatly influenced by the fact that Dr Cassar was a consultant physician to whom the applicant had been referred by his treating doctor and who had the opportunity to assess his condition at that time.

45. Having regard to the medical history given by Mr Amess both to Dr Howe and Dr Cassar, we find on the probabilities that the applicant had experienced chest pains for a longer period than the two to three weeks indicated by Mrs Amess. We accept Dr Cassar's opinion that the chest pains were probably cardiovascular spasm, in the causation of which stress was the significant factor. In our view the probabilities are that the stress that occurred in 1979 was itself an accumulation of pressures from being required to teach difficult children, unfamiliar subjects, and from unaccustomed physical exertion and uncertainties about career and (consequentially) financial security. Mr Amess' concern to achieve security of tenure seems to explain his apparent anxiety over the medical examination on 25 August 1980 (Tr.27) and the fact that he consulted Dr Howe to have him complete the personal medical statement section of the Report. However, the other stress inducing factors that occurred during 1979 were factors that could be expected to recur even in a permanent teaching position.

46. The question before us is whether the decision to issue a BCC specifying anxiety is the correct decision in accordance with the provisions of s.16(4) of the Act. As we indicated at the outset, Mr Higgins raised a number of legal arguments which, if sustained, would require that question to be answered in the negative. We shall deal with these arguments in turn.

The Power to Issue a BCC

47. Mr Higgins submitted, firstly, that on the proper construction of s.16(4) of the Act the Commissioner was obliged to consider the question posed by that sub-section and to issue any BCC considered necessary within a reasonable time or as soon as practicable after the report or reports were obtained. In his submission, a delay of approximately 21 months between the date of the medical examination and the date upon which the Commissioner purported to issue a BCC was such an extraordinary lapse of time that Parliament could not have intended the power to be exercisable in such circumstances.

48. What this submission entailed, as we understand it, is that we should imply into s.16(4) words of limitation that Parliament did not see fit to enact, so that the opening words of the sub-section would read - "The Commissioner shall, within a reasonable time after receipt of the report or reports, consider those reports etc." or "The Commissioner shall, as soon as practicable after receipt of the report or reports consider etc.". No authority for importing such a major limitation upon the plain language of the sub-section was cited to the Tribunal. The only cases referred to (Martin v Commonwealth of Australia (1975) 7 ACTR 1 and Turner v Nominal Defendant [1981] FCA 60; (1981) 51 FLR 342) turned upon questions of construction of language in fact employed in the enactments then under consideration. Thus in Martin, the question was as to the meaning to be applied to the words "as soon as practicable". In Turner, the question was whether there was "sufficient cause" shown for extending the time to give notice to the nominal defendant. Neither case involved the implication of words having a substantial limiting effect.

49. As Lord Mersey said in Thompson v Goold & Co. (1910) A.C. 409 at 420:-

"It is a strong thing to read into an Act of

Parliament words which are not there, and in the

absence of clear necessity it is a wrong thing to

do."

Professor Pearce, in his valuable summary of the cases on this point, (see Pearce on Statutory Interpretation in Australia, 2nd Edition, p.19) refers to R v Skurray (1967) 86 WN (Part I) (NSW) 1 and to Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Ganke (1975) 1 NSWLR 252 where the courts were prepared, in effect, to interpolate the words "within a reasonable time" into the relevant provisions to fill a hiatus that was felt to exist. Skurray was a decision of the Court of Criminal Appeal on a Case Stated as to the proper interpretation of s.36 of the Auctioneers, Stock and Station, Real Estate and Business Agents Act 1941-1957 (NSW) which required licencees under that Act to pay moneys received by the licencee for and on behalf of any person, pending ultimate disbursement, into a bank trust account. It was an offence to fail to do so. Wallace P. in the leading decision said, at p.3:-

"It is well settled and good sense, that where a

penal provision requires an act to be done either

without a time being stipulated, or even where the

act must be done 'forthwith' a reasonable time is

implied sufficient to enable performance to be

effected."

50. This decision was followed and applied to a different legislative context in Ganke. In that case the Court of Criminal Appeal held that the Commissioner of Taxation has power to specify in a notice in writing given pursuant to s.264(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), a date on or before which the information required under that section is to be furnished to him, provided that a reasonable time for the furnishing of the information is allowed by the notice. Failure to comply with such a notice will constitute the offence provided for by s.223(1) of the Act.

51. Both these cases are, in our view, distinguishable from the present situation. The implication of words of limitation was consistent with the principle of necessity referred to by Lord Mersey. As Wallace P. pointed out in Skurray, it would be quite unacceptable that a provision such as s.36(1) of the Auctioneers, Stock and Station, Real Estate and Business Agents Act 1941-1957 (NSW) could mean that an offence was committed if moneys received by an agent were not banked within say a few minutes after receipt. Equally, in Ganke, it would be quite unacceptable if the time specified by the Commissioner within which information was to be provided was so short as to make it inpracticable for the person upon whom the notice was served to comply. Considerations of justice necessitated some amelioration of the otherwise onerous requirements that these sections imposed.

52. However, in our view, s.16 of the Superannuation Act stands in a different position. It imposes a duty upon a public official to be exercised for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976. It is, in our view, a statutory function to be exercised in the circumstances prescribed by Parliament for the protection of the Superannuation Fund against claims under the Superannuation Act by reason of early invalidity retirement. If Parliament had intended that the functions directed to be performed must be performed within a limited period or that the power to issue a BCC was to lapse if not exercised within a "reasonable" or a specified time, nothing would have been easier than to say so.

53. Mr Higgins pointed to the unfairness involved in the Commissioner having the power to issue a BCC at any time, even up to 19 years after the person's contributory service began (see s.66). In our view, however, no arguments of necessity compel the implication that Mr Higgins sought to introduce into s.16. On the contrary, there are, we think, sound reasons why such an implication cannot have been intended to be made. The inherent vagueness in expressions such as "within a reasonable time" or "as soon as practicable" is, we think, totally inappropriate in the context of s.16(4). If, as Mr Higgins suggested, each case needs to be looked at in the light of its own circumstances, taking into consideration the consequences that are likely to flow to the individual person in the event of delay in reaching a decision, the administration of the Act could well become unmanageable. In many cases it could not be known whether a certificate issued by the Commissioner was within power until there had been either a judicial determination of that issue or a decision of this Tribunal. Uncertainty would be likely to abound. To protect against arguments of lack of power, the Commissioner would need to investigate the circumstances of each case to ensure that he was armed with sufficient evidence to negate any challenge that might ultimately be mounted against the certificate. There is no justification, in our view, for making an implication which would lead to such a potentially unpredictable and unmanageable system. If there is a case for limiting the time within which the Commissioner's powers are to be exercised, that is a matter for Parliament.

54. The conclusion that we have reached in this regard is consistent with the conclusion reached by a differently constituted Tribunal in Re Homsy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALN N27. In that case a not dissimilar question, arising under s.16(10) of the Act, was considered. At N28 the Tribunal said:-

"The only time limit the Act appears to impose on

the issue of a benefit classification certificate

(under s.16(10)) is that the employee shall not

have completed 20 years' contributory service, and

the specific provisions included in s.16 seem to

the Tribunal to prevent any construction of the

section that would read into it a requirement that

the Commissioner's powers must be exercised within

a reasonable time. The absence of any other time

limit means that even though an eligible employee

may have answered all questions honestly to the

best of his knowledge and belief and even though he

may have continued in the employment of the

Commonwealth or a Commonwealth authority for a very

long period (up to 19 years) in the belief and

expectation that he was covered for normal

superannuation benefits, he could be deprived of

those benefits by the issue of a benefit

classification certificate under sub-s.(10)."

55. Furthermore, it is our view that the construction we have placed on s.16(4) is supported by the decision of the Full Court of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Cessnock Municipal Council v Attorney-General (1956) LGR 287. One of the questions in that case was whether a power to be exercised by the Minister for Local Government to submit to the Governor of New South Wales a proposal to unite local government areas into a municipality or shire had to be exercised within a reasonable time after the receipt by the Minister of a favourable report from the local Land Board. A report favouring the union of certain local government areas had been submitted to the Minister in October 1948. Following protracted consideration of the matter, the Minister decided to proceed in 1956. The Cessnock Municipal Council, one of the Councils affected by the proposal, sought an injunction to restrain the Minister from proceeding. The Act was silent as to the time within which the Minister could exercise his powers. Myers J., who heard the matter at first instance, said (at 288):-

"A considerable amount of argument and a

considerable number of authorities have been cited

to me on the question whether the Act requires the

report to be submitted to the Governor within a

reasonable time; that is, whether the power

conferred on the Minister must be exercised within

a reasonable time after he receives the report. I

do not propose to discuss the cases. I only

mention the following as some of those to which I

was referred - In Re Broughton (1897) 18 LR

(N.S.W.) 247; (1899) AC 251; 20 LR (NSW) 10;

Thicknesse v Lancaster Canal Co. (1838) 4 Mee &

Wel.472; [1838] EngR 284; 150 E.R. 1515 and Bradley v The Board of

Works for the Greenwich District (1878) 3 Q.B.D.

384. In my opinion the authorities show that

there is no rule that a power conferred by a

statute must or must not be exercised within a

reasonable time where no time is expressly

provided. I think that they establish that where

no time is expressly provided, then unless there is

something in the statute which requires the

contrary to be implied, the act may be done at any

time."

56. On appeal to the Full Court of the Supreme Court of NSW, the decision of Myers J. was upheld. Referring to the argument that a limitation upon the Minister's power should be read into the section so as to require that any proposal, if it is made at all, must be made within a reasonable time after receipt of the report, Owen J. (with whom Walsh J. agreed) said at 290:-

"I can see nothing in the terms of s.19 which

requires such an implication to be made and it is,

I think, in the highest degree unlikely that the

legislature would have intended to limit in this

way the power of the Minister to submit a proposal

for consideration by the Governor in Council and to

leave it to the courts to decide whether there had

been unreasonable delay in the exercise of that

power."

Herron J. (at 294) said:-

"It was next argued that the Minister's discretion

under s.19 to submit the matter to the Executive

Council must be exercised within a reasonable time.

I am unable to find any authority to support the

supposed rule that a power conferred by statute

must be exercised within a reasonable time where no

time is expressly provided, nor have we been

referred to any authority which we think compels

such a decision. The authorities, sparse though

they be, appear to establish that where no time is

expressly provided, unless there is something in

the statute which requires the contrary to be

implied the act may be done at any time."

57. There being no express provision in s.16(4) requiring that the Commissioner is to perform the functions conferred upon him by that section within a specified time and there being nothing within s.16 or elsewhere in the Act, to which we were referred or of which we are otherwise aware, from which a limitation as to time can properly be implied, we reject Mr Higgins' submission that the functions can only be lawfully exercised "within a reasonable time" or "as soon as practicable". In our view, the fact that some 21 months elapsed between the date of the medical examination and the date of issue of the benefit classification certificate did not deprive the Commissioner of the power to issue a certificate, if the circumstances of the case otherwise justified that course.

Estoppel

58. Mr Higgins' next submission was that a decision had in fact been taken by a delegate of the Commissioner in September 1981 not to issue a BCC and that, having made that decision, the Commissioner could not change his mind (cf. Cessnock Municipal Council at 290-1 and 292-3). In our view, however, this submission cannot be sustained. Although there was a recommendation by Mrs Nolan on 7 September, 1981, that a BCC should not issue, we are satisfied that that recommendation was rejected.

59. It was then submitted that the Commissioner should be held to have acquiesced in the initial recommendation made to him by the Commonwealth Medical Officer and to be estopped by the delay which occurred in this matter from denying that he had in fact formed the opinion not to issue a BCC. Mr Higgins acknowledged that estoppel cannot be used to prevent the exercise of a statutory function (see the authorities cited in Re Bundy and Secretary, Department of Housing and Construction (1980) 2 ALD 735; cf. Re Powers and Commonwealth of Australia (Nos. Q81/61 and Q82/13 - decision delivered 19 October 1983). He submitted, however, that the Commissioner could be estopped from denying that he had, in fact, exercised his discretion not to issue a BCC (cf. Brickworks Limited v The Council of the Shire of Warringah [1963] HCA 18; (1963) 108 CLR 568 per Windeyer J. at 577.

60. In Brickworks the plaintiff company had received notice of the Council's consent to a proposed land use by the company, signed by the President of the Shire. The Council itself had at no time repudiated the advice given by the President. The Council was held in those circumstances to be unable to deny that consent had been given.

61. Mr Higgins was obliged to concede that, in the present case, the Commissioner was not under any statutory duty to notify an eligible employee whether he had decided not to issue a benefit classification certificate. His only statutory obligation is to serve on the person to whom a certificate relates a copy of any benefit classification certificate that is issued (see s.16(12)). It was not suggested that any letter or other notification had been sent to the applicant from the Commissioner's Office advising that a BCC would not be issued. Thus, Mr Higgins was unable to point to any positive representation such as was relied upon in the Brickworks case. That case is therefore distinguishable and affords no assistance to the applicant. Furthermore, the decision, in our view, turned on the finding that there had been an express admission of fact, not on any question of estoppel (see per Windeyer J. at 577).

62. In support of the proposition that the Commissioner could be estopped by acquiescence Mr Higgins referred us to the decision of Owen CJ. in Equity in Laidley v The Borough of Plattsburg (1894) 10 N.S.W. WN 161. This was an application for an interlocutory injunction against a local council in which the issues were not fully canvassed and in which his Honour expressed only the most tentative views upon the question whether a public authority could be estopped by acquiescence. At 162 his Honour said:-

"If an individual can be so estopped, it appears to

me at present that a borough (which is only a

section of the public created by Act of Parliament)

can be similarly estopped. That is a point which

will have to be decided at the hearing."

We were not referred to the ultimate decision of the Court on this point and our own researches have failed to find any further reference to the case. In our view, it is not an authority for the proposition cited by Mr Higgins.

63. As Thesiger L.J. said in De Bussche v Alt (1878) 8 Ch.D 286 at 314, acquiescence "is no more than an instance of the law of estoppel by words or conduct". In principle, therefore, we fail to see how the Commissioner can be estopped from performing his statutory functions because of alleged acquiescence arising from delay (see Maritime Electric Co Ltd v General Dairies Ltd (1937) AC 610; Southend-on-Sea Corporation v Hodgson (Wickford) Ltd (1962) 1 QB 416; Western Fish Products Ltd v Penrith District Council (1981) 2 All ER 204; and the other cases cited in Bundy at 750-2). Particularly is this so when those functions are not required by law to be performed within a stipulated time.

64. If we were wrong in that view of the law, we should add that we do not see any basis of fact upon which estoppel by acquiescence could be found to be established against the Commissioner. The principles with respect to estoppel by acquiescence as it applies between private citizens are fully discussed in Chapter XII of Spencer Bower and Turner "The Law Relating to Estoppel by Representation" at pp.283 et seq. Applying those principles to the facts of this case there is no evidence to indicate that the Commissioner was aware, at any time prior to the issue of the BCC, that Mr Amess was under stress in his employment and that he had been obliged to give up that employment by reason of ill health at the end of 1981. The first advice received by the Commissioner's Office with respect to Mr Amess' pending retirement was on 17 May 1982 (Tr.81). There is simply no factual basis upon which it can be asserted, therefore, that the Commissioner stood silently by allowing the applicant to prejudice his own position in relation to his employment in the belief that the Commissioner had decided not to qualify his superannuation rights.

The Matters to be Considered by the Tribunal

65. Mr Higgins submitted that s.16(4) imposed limitations upon the material to which the Commissioner and this Tribunal could properly have regard in deciding the question posed by that sub-section. He pointed out that the Commissioner was required to consider "the report or reports (i.e. of the medical examinations that the eligible employee was required to undergo), and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant". He submitted the Commissioner was not entitled to range at large "over the realm of possibly relevant material". The only medical reports to which he could properly have regard were the reports furnished by the examining medical officers. In his submission, the letter of 6 April 1982 from Dr Turner in which she altered the recommendation that she had originally made on 26 August 1980 to one recommending the issue of a BCC specifying "anxiety" was not part of the medical "reports" to which the Commissioner could have regard. In his submission, the letter did not form part of anything. It was an opinion which was based on no information and, therefore, cannot rationally be described as being a medical opinion.

66. Mr Higgins further submitted that the reference to "such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant" clearly excluded the Commissioner from having regard to reports of other medical examinations. The Commissioner might have regard to opinions of others on the medical examinations, or to statistics about the likelihood of early retirement, or to the nature of the employment being undertaken, but that was the limit of the types of "other matters" to which reference could properly be made.

67. As will be apparent, these submissions involved an application of the well known Latin maxim expressio unius est exclusio alterius. As Professor Pearce correctly observes in Statutory Interpretation in Australia, 2nd Ed., p.45, this maxim is applied by courts with extreme caution. The learned author adds "When it is followed, it is used more often as a bolster to a predetermined interpretation rather than as a rule that produces a result in itself".

68. In the present case we can see nothing whatsoever in the language of s.16(4) that indicates an intention on the part of the legislature that the Commissioner (or on review this Tribunal) should be limited in the way Mr Higgins has contended. The question at issue, after all, is a medical question. It will in many cases involve a careful consideration of the medical antecedents and history of the eligible employee in question. There may be all sorts of medical reports relating to the medical history of the employee which would have a critical bearing upon the question to be decided upon the Commissioner. It is true that the condition specified in the BCC must be a condition referred to in the medical reports obtained by the Commissioner under s.16. However, it is inconceivable, in our view, that Parliament would have intended that the Commissioner should be denied access to other medical reports (e.g. from the applicant's doctors) if those reports would assist in reaching the correct or preferable decision for the purpose of the Act in respect of the particular employee. Such a construction, far from promoting the purpose or object underlying the Act would frustrate it (cf. s.15AA Acts Interpretation Act 1901). It is a construction that we therefore reject.

"Likely"

69. Finally, Mr Higgins submitted that before the Commissioner could issue a BCC he had to form the opinion that Mr Amess was "not likely by reason of or for reason connected with the physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age." "Likely" in that context, he submitted, meant something that would probably happen. Notwithstanding the decisions of the Tribunal that have held to the contrary, (see for example Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation, (1979) 2 ALN 659) Mr Higgins submitted that it was not enough that there should be a "real risk" that the person will not reach maximum retiring age. He informed us that the Full Court of the Federal Court in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation (No. G86 of 1983 - decision handed down 31 July 1984) had been inclined to the view, although it was not necessary to decide the question, that the word "likely" imported "probability".

70. Turning to the evidence, Dr Turner gave as her reason for recommending the issue of a BCC that she considered an anxious teacher "a significant risk". In the present case, however, we do not rely upon her opinion in view of Dr Hughson's evidence (Tr.159/160). We rely upon the evidence of Dr Cassar whose opinion in 1980 was that the applicant was not likely by reason of his anxiety state to continue to be an eligible employee until he reached maximum retiring age. That opinion, as we earlier noted, was not tested in cross-examination.

71. We also had in evidence before us relevant extracts from the Handbook for Commonwealth Medical Officers which provides guidance for Commonwealth Medical Officers (inter alia) as to the medical standards considered necessary for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 (see Part III Section 7 and Part IV Section 8). The general medical standard in Part IV of the Handbook has reference only to the Superannuation scheme and is based on ability to effectively perform duties until maximum age for retirement (usually 65 years) - see Sec.8.A(1).

72. The general medical standard is not, of course, part of the Superannuation Act. It is there to provide guidance for Commonwealth Medical Officers, with a view, no doubt, to ensuring some consistency in the assessment of medical conditions and the establishment of acceptable levels of risk for the purpose of the Superannuation Act (cf. Linden at 661). In respect of mental disorders, the standard as set out in Sec.13(1) is as follows:-

"13. Central Nervous System

(1) Mental disorders

(a) Standard not met where an applicant

suffers from a chronic or recurrent

psychotic or psychoneurotic illness or

manifests definite evidence of chronic

personality disorder.

(b) Standard could still be met even though

a person has suffered from or shows

symptoms of nervous, emotional or mental

illnesses. Each case should be

considered on its merits on the basis of

the guidelines in the following

paragraphs. It should be noted,

however, that mental disorders are a

common cause of prolonged sick leave

leading to a high incidence of early

retirement.

(c) Standard met provided there has been

freedom from recurrence and absence of

signs for 3 years and the mental

disorder represents no more than:

(i) a single episode which is not of a

chronic or recurrent nature,

(ii) a stated episode, the diagnosis of

which has not been completely

substantiated, or

(iii) a single episode of minor

emotional disturbance requiring

medical attention resulting from

stress, such as may occur after

bereavement, marital discord,

illness, or similar happening.

(d) Standard met in cases of a more complex

nature than those above if, after

through investigation, there is clear

evidence of satisfactory treatment,

freedom from recurrence for at least 5

years, no current signs and symptoms and

no control by drugs. A specialist's

report should be obtained in all such

cases.

(e) Recommendations should take account of

the nature of the duties likely to be

performed by the applicant during his or

her working life; certain occupations

(e.g. telephonist) are prone to

aggravate any tendency towards neurosis

or psychosis."

73. There was considerable debate about the meaning of this standard. The drafting, naturally enough, lacks the precision that one might expect in an Act of Parliament. In our view, however, it is clear enough that para 1(a) deals with the most severe cases of mental disorder of the psychotic or psychoneurotic kind or of chronically disordered personality. A person suffering from those conditions does not meet the standard. Paragraph 1(b) appears to us to relate to cases that do not come within the severe prescriptions of para 1(a). These include, in particular, cases of non-psychotic nervous, emotional or mental illnesses. Each such case is required to be considered on its merits on the basis of the guidelines in paras (c), (d) and (e). The guidance given in that regard is of considerable importance. It is based, no doubt, on long experience. Mental disorders, it is said, "are a common cause of prolonged sick leave leading to a high incidence of early retirement".

74. In the present case, we have found on the evidence of Dr Cassar that the applicant had a long-standing anxious personality and a manifested anxiety state at the end of 1979 and early 1980 in response to stress at or relating to his work as a high school teacher. Applying para 1(c) of the guidelines, the applicant could not meet the standard at his medical examination in August 1980 because his manifested anxiety state was only some months previous to that date. There had certainly not been an absence of signs for 3 years. It is unnecessary in those circumstances to consider whether the anxiety state came within any of the circumstances specified in para 1(c)(i), (ii) or (iii).

75. Having regard to the applicant's age, the circumstances in which the anxiety state manifested itself in 1979 and to the applicant's anxious personality or predisposition it was, in our view, "likely" both in the sense of "something that might well happen" and "more probable than not" that the applicant would be exposed to similar stressful conditions in his future employment as a school teacher and that, if that occurred, he would suffer a recurrence of his anxiety state. Such a recurrence would be likely to entail incapacitating illness. On any view as to the meaning of the word "likely", we think that the evidence of Dr Cassar justifies a finding that in August 1980 the applicant was not likely, by reason of his anxious personality and previous history of anxiety state, to continue to be an eligible employee until he attained his maximum retiring age.

76. In our view, none of the arguments advanced by Mr Higgins by which he sought to limit the powers of the Commissioner (and, on review, of this Tribunal) are sustainable. As we have concluded on the merits of the case that the Commissioner's decision was correct, we would propose to affirm the decision under review.
