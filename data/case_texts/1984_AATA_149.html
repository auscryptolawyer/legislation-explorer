Re Lois Lillian Dawes and Commissioner of Superannuation [1984] AATA 149 (11 May 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: LOIS LILLIAN DAWES

And: COMMISSIONER OF SUPERANNUATION

No. A83/97

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.C. Jennings Q.C., (Deputy President)

E. Smith (Senior Member)

Dr. A.H. Marsh (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Benefit Classification Certificate - whether death caused by or connected with a condition specified - high gastric reduction - death from post-operative complications - Superannuation Act 1976 ss.16(4), 81(2).

Re: Hegedues 4 ALN 139.

HEARING

CANBERRA

11:5:1984

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the applicant is entitled to benefits in accordance with the provisions of Part VI of the Superannuation Act 1976 upon the basis that subsection 81(2) of that Act does not apply in relation to the death of the eligible employee who was her spouse.

DECISION

Terence Franklin Dawes was born on 14 January 1954. In 1976 he was first employed as a bus driver by the Department of the Capital Territory. He became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), on 21 June 1979 and as a result of a medical officer's recommendation on 31 July 1980, a benefit classification certificate was issued pursuant to s.16(4) of the Act ("the Certificate").

That certificate specified two physical conditions by reason of which a delegate of the Commissioner of Superannuation declared his opinion that Mr. Dawes was not likely to continue to be an eligible employee until he attained his maximum retiring age. Those conditions were described as "Overweight, Hypertension". At that time, Mr. Dawes weighed 116.3 kg. and was 184.5 cms in height. A scale of average heights and weights produced in evidence revealed that he was more than 50% over the average (or ideal) weight for a man of his age.

In February 1981, Mr. Dawes consulted a specialist surgeon, Dr. Dyason, about his weight problem. He was then 122 kg. He was advised to undergo an operation known as high gastric reduction. He was accordingly admitted to the Woden Valley Hospital for surgery on 1 March 1981. Post-operative complications led to his death there on 20 May 1981.

Relying on a certificate issued by Dr. Turner in October 1981 that in her opinion "the cause of death was substantially contributed to by the physical condition 'overweight' specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate for the following reasons -

1. The surgical procedure was for

obesity, and pneumonia was a direct

result of the surgery

2. An obese person is more prone to post

operative pneumonia than a person of

normal weight for height"

a delegate of the Commissioner, made a determination on 9 November 1981 that in his opinion, death was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the Certificate, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions.

Although he referred, when making that decision, to both conditions specified in the Certificate, it was common ground that the reference to hypertension has no relevance for the purpose of this application.

The applicant sought a re-consideration of that decision and on 12 August 1983, the Acting Commissioner decided to confirm it.

Section 154 of the Act confers jurisdiction on the Tribunal to review reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or varied by the Commissioner. Accordingly, the present application for review was made on 30 August 1983.

The decision of the respondent, which is the subject of review, was made pursuant to s.81(2) of the Act, which so far as is relevant, is in the following terms :-

(2) Where -

(a) an eligible employee who dies before attaining

his maximum retiring age is survived by a

spouse;

(b) there was in force in respect of the eligible

employee, immediately before his death, a

benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the

eligible employee's death was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a physical or

mental condition or conditions specified in

the certificate or by a physical or mental

condition or conditions connected with such a

condition or conditions

the spouse is entitled . . . ".

It is unnecessary to refer to the various types of entitlement.

In order to determine whether it accepts or rejects the opinion of the respondent, the tribunal accepted as evidence all the documents which accompanied the statement lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the oral evidence of Dr. Clarke, and a written statement by Mrs. Lois Dawes, the widow of the deceased. That statement had been tendered in evidence to the Coroner who investigated the cause of death, and was sworn to, in those proceedings, by the applicant.

Mrs. Dawes told the Coroner that the deceased was concerned about his weight because it was affecting his family relationships. About April 1980 when he had an annual medical checkup, his doctor advised him that if he did not get his weight and blood pressure down he could lose his job. He was terrified that this might happen. Mrs. Dawes also said that his weight worried him a great deal and he made efforts to reduce by both diet and exercise. However, she said, his weight never stopped him doing anything.

After learning from a friend of an operation known as "high gastric reduction", he was referred by a Dr. Dick to specialist surgeon Dr. Dyason in February 1981.

Dr. Dyason explained the nature of the operation to the deceased and showed him a diagram. He was told that it consisted of inserting a row of staples across the stomach in order to reduce its volume. The operation was recommended because of the risk of heart disease in later life if he continued to be overweight.

The evidence as to conversations between Dr. Dyason and the deceased concerning risks involved in the operation consisted of a transcript of Dr. Dyason's evidence before the Coroner and the written statement of Mrs. Dawes, to which we have made reference. They were not entirely consistent, but as we did not have the advantage of seeing or hearing either we make no positive findings about these conversations.

In any event, it does not seem material to determine in what detail, if any, the deceased was warned about the risks involved in undergoing this particular operation.

What may be material is the question of whether this was elective or essential surgery. We are satisfied it was the former. It is not necessary to determine what weight the deceased gave to a number of considerations which motivated him to consult Dr. Dyason and accept the advice that he was a suitable candidate for surgery. It was never suggested that his overweight made surgery absolutely necessary as a life preserving procedure.

The Coroner found that the cause of death was pneumonia and the respondent expressed his opinion in the decision under review upon the same basis, but it was apparent from the evidence before us that Dr. Clarke did not see that question quite so simply. He was unable to say whether or not pneumonia was the immediate cause of death.

Because it is necessary for us to make a finding as to the cause of death, and because the central issue in this case concerns the connection (if any) between the cause of death and the physical condition of "overweight" described in the Certificate, it is necessary to refer to events which followed the operation of the deceased on 2 March 1981.

The purpose of the operation was to convert the stomach into two compartments with a small opening known as the stoma between the top or smaller compartment and the lower compartment. This is effected with a stapling gun.

Evidence given to the Coroner and written reports and oral evidence produced to the Tribunal described this operation in considerable detail. Suffice it to say for present purposes, that the first deterioration in the condition of the deceased following the operation can be attributed to the fact that at the time of the operation the stapling gun misfired, thus preventing it from achieving its purpose, which was to penetrate the anterior and posterior layers of the stomach.

This could well have led to a significant leekage of gastric contents from the staple line and necessiated further surgery. X-rays within the first week revealed a fluid collection in the chest, which also required draining.

On 13 March, it became apparent that the left lower lobe had consolidated and collapsed requiring decortication on 16 March and drainage of a subphrenic collection of fluid by making an opening in the diaphragm. That drain did not prove adequate and a further operation was performed by 19 March to drain the large abcess which had by that time developed. Further difficulty was experienced in achieving control of abdominal sepsis, and at the end of March the patient developed an episode of acute renal failure.

Despite this he made steady progress during April but there was persistent evidence of consolidation and collapse of the lower lobe and an encysted left pleural effusion in association with the fistulous tract which arose from the posterior wall of the stomach and passed through the diaphragm and out through the chest drain.

A factor which contributed to the need for a further operation when the fistula failed to heal was his inability to tolerate a nasal gastric tube. The purpose of this tube was to maintain suction and drainage of the stomach contents and thus hopefully achieve spontaneous healing.

Dr. Clarke attributed this inability to the deceased's inadequate personality. He described his frustration tolerance as very limited. He thought that there was a reasonable possibility ("more than 50%") that healing of the fistula without need for further operation may have been achieved but for this unfortunate inability. He thought it had compromised his chance of recovery.

The final operation was a long and difficult one due, amongst other things, to the fact that the fistula was found to be densely adherent to the diaphragm and the spleen, necessitating removal of the spleen in order to gain access to the fistula. Further decortication of the left lower lobe was undertaken to allow the left lung to re-expand.

The deceased appeared to be making a satisfactory recovery from his operation, but 4 days later he developed a high fever. He had pneumococcal septicaemia which is unresponsive to penicillin treatment and sometimes occurs in patients who have had a splenectomy.

During the days that followed, although he was clearly unwell, he did not appear to be desperately ill. However, about midnight on 20 May, he developed a distressing cough and had difficulty breathing. Oxygen gave symptomic improvement. He was described as alert and stable immediately before he collapsed at 6 a.m. while taking to nurses who were washing him. His heart contractions then ceased.

Dr. Clarke described the causes of death in this way:-

"The mechanism of his death was clearly

cardiogenic. I remain, however, unclear as

to what caused this. He had been moderately

hypoxaemic for several hours but this was not

sufficient to disturb his level of

consciousness. His acid-base balance was less

than perfect but by no means seriously

abnormal for a young man. His electrolyte

status was within normal limits. Pulmonary

embolisation was excluded by a post-mortem,

and while it is possible that he did indeed

have a septicaemia, no obvious abcess was

found at post-mortem to which the septicaemia

could be ascribed. Pneumococcal septicaemia

unresponsive to penicillin therapy, however,

may occasionally occur in patients who have

had a splenectomy. It would, nonetheless, be

unusual for this to cause an acute asystole,

rather than general circulatory failure

characterised by hypotension. There was no

failure characterised by hypotension. There

was no post-mortem or histological evidence of

pericardial effusion or a myocarditis.

On the other hand, Mr. Dawes had had a very

formidable illness with recurrent abdominal

and thoracic sepsis, a significant

entero-cutaneous fistula, collapse and

consolidation of lobes of both lungs, the

effects of a splenectomy and other major

operations and a transient renal failure.

These insults must have taken a very

substantial toll on metabolic function even

though one is not able to precisely define the

mechanism".

It will be seen from the description of his period in hospital that

"overweight" played no obvious or apparent fole in the multiple causes or possible causes of death. Dr. Clarke was quite positive that there was no problem arising from his cardiovascular system which could be said to have caused death and no evidence clinically or at post-mortem that could be attributed to obesity. He further said that his pulmonary problems were not the result of his obesity.

Although his obesity would not have facilitated his reccovery from chest infections, the same sequence of events culminating in death might well have occurred in a perfectly fit and slim person, Dr. Clarke said.

For the applicant, counsel first argued that the Certificate is required to specify a medical condition and that "overweight" is not such a condition. He based this submission on a review of prior decisions of this Tribunal which led to and were discussed in Re Hegedues and Commissioner for Superannuation 4 ALN No.139. He cited the reasons at paragraphs 86 to 87, which are as follows:-

"86. Mr. Wilde referred to the views expressed by the

Tribunal on the meaning of "connected with" in s.66(2)

in Re Vaughan and Commissioner for Superannuation

((1979) 2 ALN p.366 (Note 36)) and Re Burr and

Commissioner for Superannuation ((1980)) 3 ALN p.N1

(Note 1)). What emerges from those cases and from Re

Brown and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation

((1981)) 3 ALD 185) is that there must be at least a

real or demonstrable connection. We do not think that

it is enough merely that the condition specified in the

benefit classification certificate and the condition

that caused the incapacity resulting in the retirement

are sometimes or, perhaps, not uncommonly associated and

do not understand any decision of the Tribunal to have

so decided.

"87. The word "connected" and its cognate expressions have

been considered by the Courts in many cases in different

contexts. We do not, however, think that they throw

more light on the expression "connected with" in s.66(2)

of the Superannuation Act than is provided by the

following meanings given in the Shorter Oxford English

Dictionary:

"'connect':

1. Trans. To join, fasten or link

together. Const. to, with.

2. To associate in occurrence, action or idea.

. . .

4. intrans. To become united or joined."

"'connection':

2. Relation between things one of which is

bound up with, or involved in, another."

Considered in this light, we think that two conditions must be linked

together on some sustainable medical basis before they can properly be regarded as "connected" for the purposes of s.66(2)."

We do not accept that this passage warrants the conclusion for which counsel contended. We therefore reject his first submission that "overweight" is so imprecise as to be incapable of definition. To say that two physical conditions must be linked together on some sustainable medical basis is not to preclude one of them being descriptive of a physical condition which does not necessarily have medical implications.

It was then argued that "overweight" should be treated as if "obesity" had been the condition described in the Certificate. Although the doctors tend to refer to "obesity" rather than overweight, and even to "morbid obesity" which is strictly reserved for persons who are double their ideal weight, there does not appear to us to be any legal consequence whatever description is adopted.

Our task is to find the cause of death and to determine whether the words of s.81(2) are applicable, accepting that the condition referred to in the Certificate which is said to be relevant is "overweight".

We accept the evidence of Dr. Clarke (whom we found to be a most impressive and fair witness) that the causes of death were recurrent abdominal and thoracic sepsis, a significant entero-cutaneous fistula, collapse and consolidation of lobes of both lungs, the effects of a splenectomy, other major operations and a transient renal failure.

We are satisfied that none of those conditions was caused or contributed to by "overweight" except in the limited temporal sense referred to by Dr. Clarke, both in his evidence before the tribunal and in his correspondence with the Acting Commissioner.

He said that the obesity was such that a surgical solution was sought for a medical problem and that complications from the surgery led to pneumonia. That was the limit, he said, to the connection between obesity and the cause of death.

The reference to pneumonia was an answer to a specific question addressed by the Acting /Commissioner, who, in turn, was relying on the Coroner's finding that pneumonia was the specific cause of death. Nothing turns on that reference for present purposes.

Counsel for the respondent accepted the reference in Hegedues to the need for the physical conditions to be linked "on a sustainable medical basis", and argued that there was no break in the chain of causation from the day Mr. Dawes decided to undergo surgery. He said the reason for the operation was Mr. Dawes' condition of being "overweight" and therefore the fact that the complications which followed the operation might have no direct connection with that condition was irrelevant.

If, as we have found, death was caused or substantially contributed to by a number of physical conditions which were not connected on a sustainable medical basis with "overweight", is it sufficient for the respondent to say that Mr. Dawes would not have died from post-operative complications if he had not elected to have that surgery because he was concerned to reduce his weight? We think not.

Section 81(2) for present purposes is concerned only with physical conditions which cause or substantially contribute to the cause of death or which have a connection with the physical condition referred to in the Certificate. It is not, therefore, concerned with events which are no more than temporal incidents leading to circumstances which in turn create the physical conditions which cause or substantially contribute to the cause of death. The decision to consult the surgeon, to consider and then accept his advice, to enter hospital and to submit to surgery, are not events which constitute connecting links in a chain of physical conditions.

We therefore conclude that, for the purposes of s.81(2) of the Act, the death of Terence Franklin Dawes

(1) was not caused, and was not substantially

contributed to, by a condition specified in

the benefit classification certificate

applicable to him, namely, "overweight"; and

(2) was not caused, and was not materially

contributed to, by a physical condition or

conditions connected with the condition

"overweight".

It should perhaps be added that, during argument, there was discussion as to

whether the quality of any of the incidents which occurred during the time Mr. Dawes was in hospital was in any event such as to constitute a break in the chain of causation linking "overweight" with death. We are of the opinion that it is more probable than not that the precipitating cause of the post-operative complications was in fact the misfiring of the stapling gun. It would follow, in our view, that any connection between the condition "overweight" and the cause of death arising from the decision to undergo surgery, would be severed by the intervening event which we are satisfied was probably a proximate cause of death.

The decision of the respondent under review will be set aside and a decision substituted that the applicant is entitled to benefits in accordance with the provisions of Part VI of the Superannuation Act 1976 upon the basis that s.81(2) of the Act does not apply in relation to the death of the eligible employee who was her spouse.
