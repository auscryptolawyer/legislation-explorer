ADMINISTRA TIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Asset Betterment - Onus of proof - Mixture of capital and income unable to be dissected - Whether permissible to deal with penalties in absence of the ground having been raised in the objection.

Income Tax Assessment Act, sees 190(a) and (b)

FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088

X88 90 ATC 640

AAT Decision No 7150

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

Subject to removing the 25% culpability component from the wife's penalty, the decisions on objections for both husband and wife in each of the three years under review are affirmed.

REASONS FOR DECISIONS

Before: Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)

Date: 2 July 1991

Place: Sydney

1. The applicants in this case are husband and wife ("H" and "W"). The applications for review in both cases were heard together by consent.

2. This couple have at all relevant times operated what has been referred to as a "take-away" business in West Bowyang, New South Wales. Both are of Greek origin. H arrived in Australia in 1963 and met his future wife in this country. For present purposes, it will suffice if I commence this epic in 1978, when the couple, having earlier acquired a cafe in Blue Hills, sold it for some $39,000. The couple then returned to Greece, intending to remain there permanently. The proceeds from the shop somehow found its way to Greece, just how and what happened to this money on arrival in Greece was not made clear. In addition, it is alleged that the husband already had some $20,000 to 23,000 in Greece. Things did not work out as expected and the couple, with their three children, returned to Australia in 1981. From here on, the plot thickens. It is alleged that when the couple returned to Australia, they brought with them some $81,000 in Australian currency. Whilst there is some conflict just how the couple got hold of so much Australian cash in Greece, I am not prepared to draw any adverse inferences from this conflict in the evidence, putting it down to language difficulties (both had a very poor command of English). Both H and W gave a graphic and not inconsistent description of how the cash was brought into this country, everyone's pockets being stuffed with dollars lt had the ring of truth about it. Included in this $81,000 was an amount of $5,000 which was a gift from the godfather of the couple's eldest child, given for his benefit. The balance between the money taken out of Australia and the money returned here was said to be a gift from H's father ("Here you are, take this money too, because you may be need it, you got family, three kids.")

  1. Pausing here, I am satisfied that it is more probable than not that this couple did indeed bring some $81,000 in cash into this country when they returned in 1981. The pity of it is that they kept no written record as to what they did with it and how this money found its way back into various bank accounts as alleged, if only because the Commissioner, after an audit, included all of it as income in the years 1985, 1986 and 1987 from a take-away business the couple acquired after their return to this country, having found a number of bank accounts, the interest from which was not returned.
  2. As a result of the audit, the amended taxable income of the partnership was increased to $39,232 in 1985, to $95,254 in 1986 and to $65,183 in 1987, the partnership having previously returned as net profit from the business $590 in 1985, $9,963 in 1986 and $11,554 in 1987.
  3. On receipt of the amended assessments, the couple's (then) accountant lodged an objection, asserting that of the total increment to the taxable income of the partnership over the three years in dispute, some $113,353 (later adjusted to $113,253) constitute "rebanking" of capital which should be excised, being monies brought back into the country and held in cash at the couple's residence. Thus the objection states:

After a potential burglary scare, the Taxpayer considered it too great a risk to hold the Capital on her premises, and the decision was made to 'gradually' deposit all the funds at the banks. The taxpayer and her husband were advised not to bank all the proceeds in a lump sum and that "it would be better for them to regularly bank the funds over a period of time."

6. The reason advanced why the money was not "rebanked" after arriving in Australia was that H was told that having that much money would disqualify him from obtaining unemployment benefit (in fact, there was no means test for receiving such benefits at the time). Suffice it to say that H, after being "on unemployment" for a short time, succeeded in finding a job working night shift and earning - and saving -a good deal of money ("$14,000 or $15,000 in the bank from the work I done, I'd been working for 12 hour shift)". Throughout this time, it is alleged that the couple kept the $81,000 "in the fridge, because that's the safest place if you get a fire, maybe save something."

7. Leaving aside the peregrinations of this couple managing a shop for friends in an outer suburb of Newcastle, they duly arrived in Blue Hills in December 1984, having acquired a lease over a shop involved in "take-away". At this point in time, the $81,000 ($5,000 of which belonged to the eldest child) was still kept intact, simply being moved from one fridge to the next. What happened to this horde from now on assumes some significance. I can do no better than put it in H's own words:

I have money already. I present to the bank a bit each week, because I'm thinking -1 was in the business and nobody notices the way I go every week. I say: look, if I take in one go 40 or $80,000, where you find there. You declare you've got no money and we give you any blame and now that $80,000, where come from? And I still do it individual every week because people know I am in business and I just release a little bit at a time.

8. The hearing took two days. H and W both gave evidence and the Commissioner called the auditor who had had previous interviews with the taxpayers and their accountant. The thrust of the Crown's argument - indeed, its only argument - was to the effect that there never was any $81,000, that the couple arrived back in this country penniless, and that all the various amounts banked in various bank accounts from time to time did not come out of the refrigerator but were takings from the business. The applicants sought to rebut this with sworn evidence and banking records from Greece which sought to establish that the money was withdrawn from various Greek bank accounts. Since it was never part of the taxpayers' case that the money taken from Greece had been withdrawn from Greek banks, it is hardly surprising that the records, collected by the taxpayers' (then) accountant and sent on to the Commissioner, failed to support the assertion that some $81,000 had been withdrawn from Greek banks prior to this couple's return to this country. The position of both parties having by now been set in concrete, the case was fought right to the bitter end on the wrong issues - was there or was there not some $81,000 which was included in the amended assessments - when in fact the applicants should have been asked to identify, year by year, how much of their bankings of cash came from their capital horde brought back from Greece, and how much from the shop takings. This should have been obvious from the start once it was realised - or should have been -that the amounts banked cumulatively, year by year, was more than the amount claimed to have been brought into Australia as part of the couple's capital, so that the amounts banked must contain a mixture of capital and income which the taxpayers have the onus to dissect. In short, both parties misconceived the real issue from the start and two days of evidence were taken up with trying to establish or rebut matters which, in the end, were destined to be irrelevant to the outcome. I sounded a warning early on that the way the case was being conducted it was unlikely to succeed (cf p. 22 tr.). Later, I repeated my warning to Mr Hughes, who appeared for the applicants (tr. pp 38-39), but all to no avail, save that Mr Hughes now conceded that:

if it came down to an argument that those deposits that were included as income were separate deposits that could not have gone through the books, yes, I could only assert that $81,000 is explicable, and the balance couldn't be explained.

Dr Gerber: I see, and therefore the presumption is they are of an income nature?

Mr Hughes: Yes, yes. If it was purely the $81,000 that we could assert the presumption there - we would not be able to rebut that presumption. But that's not been asserted that the balance is in fact of an income nature or not necessarily not of an income nature. What we are saying is that they should not have been included in the audit as income.

At p. 44 of the transcript, I sounded a further warning:

Even if every conceivable aspect of your clients' evidence is accepted as truthful evidence, on that evidence have you discharged the onus? Let's assume they took money out of Australia and brought money back in, H is unable to say how much. Dates are totally confused. Quantum is not really clarified, and you, by your own concession, conceded that the only thing you can salvage from the (audit) is the $81,000. I would have thought in those circumstances the parties should be able to reach some kind of reasonable compromise.

9. Despite three warnings on the question of onus, the case proceeded as though I had not spoken; the whole thrust of the cross-examination being based on whether or not the family returned to this country with $81,000.

  1. No matter how often the Courts and Tribunals have pointed out that the onus is on applicants to show not only that the amount of the assessments, year by year, is excessive, but they must establish the substantive liability imposed by the assessments; cf FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088,it apparently needs to be said again in this case. It is not the function of the Commissioner (or a Tribunal on review) to undertake a tracing exercise where it is alleged and conceded - that there has been a "mixing" of capital and undeclared income.
  2. It is thus purely in deference to the parties and the manner the case was conducted that I will briefly deal with the evidence, irrelevant though it was.
  3. H deposed that he kept a cash book and that whenever money was withdrawn from the till, it was written down in the cash book. Suffice it to say that, apart from some cheque buts, no cash book or other books of account were tendered or, indeed, shown to the auditor. This was explained by the fact that the relevant accountant who prepared the returns had retired and could not be traced. The manner the books were kept and the business conducted, as explained by H in evidence, has not persuaded me that all business income has been accounted for. So be it.
  4. The Crown called the auditor who interviewed this couple and completed the audits in this case. He stated that he first interviewed H in the presence of his agent on 1 February 1988. Questions and answers were recorded and the record of interview was signed by H. During this interview, H was asked what bank accounts he operated. He identified two accounts with Westpac - one a cheque account, the other a joint savings account - a loan account with the National Bank, as well as a savings account with the ANZ Bank which contained the children's child endowment payments. The source of the funds deposited in these accounts - save the ANZ accounts - was conceded to come from the business.
  5. It is not disputed that the investigation revealed numerous other accounts which were not disclosed at the first interview.
  6. A subsequent interview took place between the auditor, his supervisor and H and W on 23 May 1988. On this occasion, the method of bookkeeping employed by this couple was investigated and can best be described as "cowboy". Thus, although wages were paid in cash, which was not included in gross sales in the 1985 and 1986 returns. The rent was first stated by H to have been paid by periodic debits from the Westpac cheque account. When this was shown to be untrue, H conceded that the rent was, in fact, also paid in cash, being deposited directly into the landlord's bank account. It was further admitted that house rents were paid in cash. At this interview, the auditor revealed that H had not only concealed a number of bank accounts from him on the previous occasion but, for good-measure, the partnership returns had failed to include the interest in the years under-review. A settlement on that issue was reached between the parties at this interviews
  7. The major "discovery" of undisclosed bank accounts was the one with the Commonwealth Bank of Australia, in which an amount of $41,276 was deposited in 1985 and $404 in 1986. The auditor's notes state: "It was agreed that these deposits came from the business and this account was the trading account until the Westpac account was opened. Mr H was informed that-some deductions for expenses may be allowed if he can produce his cheque butts. He will look for the cheque butts and contact his agent for the full details".
  8. A joint savings account with Westpac (also not previously disclosed) contained an amount of $19,907. At first H doubted whether this was his account, but when it was pointed out that mortgage payments came out of that account, his wife stated that the deposits "came from the business, as there was no other possible source of funds".
  9. The interview then turned to another account which had not previously been disclosed.

Once again Mrs W stated that the deposits came from the business. Thus, the adjustments were $36,996 in 1986 and possibly $13,522 in 1987 which needs to be checked further.

19. I propose to set out the record of interview relating to two more accounts verbatim.

The next account discussed was account No. ....,, a flexible caposit account. Mr H advised that the $17,000 deposited to open the account came from Greece, and specifically from his rental property. When questioned further, Mr H advised the money was brought into the country by his sister in US dollars. When asked if he had any records to verify this, Mr H replied that he did not, as it was brought into this country in cash. Mr H was asked if there was any records in Greece, such as a passbook, to show the withdrawal from his rental account. He replied that he could not supply this information, as he was fearful of retribution from Greek authorities. They were then asked where they exchanged the US currency for Austrian. They replied that they cashed the money at various locations.

The discussion finally turned to account No , a joint savings Westpac account. The funds were used to buy the house in November 1985. Mr H doubted whether the deposits were from the business. He stated that it could have been money from overseas which was deposited on a regular basis into the account. However, he was not sure of this. He also suggested it could have been proceeds from a Commonwealth

Bank loan.

  1. Having heard the evidence of the auditor and read his reports, i am satisfied that the audit was properly conducted and that both taxpayers were given ample opportunity to demonstrate the source of the unexplained deposits. It is in my opinion significant that at no time did either partner depose that some of the monies were part of the $81,000 brought from Greece in 1981.
  2. On the evidence available to the auditor, he had no option but to treat the unexplained deposits as income from the business - indeed no plausible explanation leading to any different conclusion was offered. Having observed the couple in the witness box, the kindest thing I can say about H's evidence is that it was unimpressive. W was an honest witness but could shed little light on the events which led to this review. To the extent that it was sought to establish that the unexplained deposits contained the $81,000 brought from Greece, that attempt has failed. Even if, although it is unlikely, that the $81,000 brought from Greece in 1981 remained intact, having lain frozen in time in various refrigerators, it cannot help these taxpayers over the evidentiary stile of having to identify, year by year, how much of this capital source found its way into the various undisclosed bank deposits.
  3. It follows from the above that the conclusion the Commissioner arrived at after the audit and after considering the objections cannot be disturbed.
  4. That leaves the issue of penalties. There is no formal objection against penalties. In earlier days, Boards of Review adopted the attitude that a failure to object against penalties was fatal to it being reviewable by the Boards. However, the jurisprudence involved in reviewing decisions on objections by this Tribunal is radically different. True it is we cannot accede to applications to amend which seek to raise new grounds; we can, however, enlarge the scope of the relief sought; of Professor Grbich's clear analysis in Case X88 90 ATC 640.1 take the view that our purpose is - within the limits of sec 190(a) of the Tax Act to reach the correct decision that the decision maker should have made on the whole of the available evidence. Penalty is not an issue which can take the Commissioner by surprise or one which he is unable to meet at a hearing before this Tribunal without being forewarned. I therefore propose to treat the issue as being "open". The Commissioner imposed on both H and W a 25% "culpability" component, and 14.026% interest on the amount of tax avoided.
  5. H, although a hard worker and a credit to the take-away business, is extravagantly careless in keeping his books of account, to the point where one could be forgiven for entertaining the suspicion that he might be just a teeny weeny bit tax-shy. i can see no good reason for reducing what on any view is a fair amount of penalty. Indeed, had the Commissioner struck his customary "culpability" component of 40%, I would have been reluctant to disturb it.
    1. I see the case of W in a different light. She is more sinned against than sinning. Her attitude to her husband and his business activities is very middle-European. She works just as hard as he does, but to regard her as an equal partner is to misconstrue what I perceive to be the realities of the relationship. I find it difficult to taint her with culpa. ln all the circumstances, I believe justice would be done if the culpability factor in her case be removed entirely, leaving her only with a liability for simple interest. If husband and wife are one, and the husband is that one - as was held by our Victorian forepersons - this is an example par excellence of that attitude.
      1. Subject to removing the 25% culpability component from the wife's penalty, the decisions on objections for both husband and wife in each of the three years under review are affirmed.

This is to certify that this and the preceding 7 pages are a true and correct copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein.
