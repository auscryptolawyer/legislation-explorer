Re Taxation Appeals [1989] AATA 177 (21 July 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT88/856-8 and NT88/2118

AAT No. 5231 Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS DIVISION

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Peter M. Roach(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Assessable Income - small manufacturing business - Whether income to be calculated on "accruals" basis.

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - Trust - Whether expenditure to advantage of household of Directors of Trustee-company incurred in the course of deriving the assessable income of the Trust - Whether "private"

Income Tax - Practice and Procedure - Whether depreciation allowable following disallowance of deduction claim as capital expenditure - Whether allowable in later years.

Income Tax - Practice and Procedure - Assessment following dissection of Cash-book - Whether expenditure on superannuation contributions not claimed in return or referred to in the objection allowable as alternative to claim contended for.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - S.51(1), S.82AAE, S.190(a), S.226,

Co. of South Australia Ltd [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108

HEARING

SYDNEY

21:7:1989

ORDER

The decision of the Tribunal is that the determination of the Commissioner upon the objections under review shall be varied and that taxable income for the years ended 30 June 1982 and 1983 respectively shall be reduced by $7,492 and $8,276 and additional tax reduced to $8,000 and $13,000; and that the decisions of the Commissioner in all other respects be confirmed.

DECISION

THE ISSUES

These three references relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1985 inclusive and the affairs of a registered company ("the Company") acting as trustee of a trust ("the Trust"). They involve:-

(a) procedural issues as to the scope of relief available

to be had; and

(b) a range of substantive issues, any one of which might

of itself been sufficient to bring a dispute to

hearing before the Tribunal.

The difficulties experienced in the conduct of the references and in endeavouring to assess the evidence should serve as a reminder to all in business of the need to carefully and precisely maintain records to a standard appropriate to their endeavours.

2. The substantive issues can be identified as:-

(a) whether the Applicant was liable to bring its sundry

debtors to account in the determination of its taxable

income in all or any of the years in question;

(b) whether the Applicant was entitled to deductions for

(i) overseas travelling expenses of an officer and

employee of the Company;

(ii) overseas travelling expenses for his wife - a

lady who was also an officer of the Company;

(iii) expenditure on the maintenance and insurance

of, and the provision of telephone and

electricity services to, the residence occupied

by the couple;

(iv) expenditure on the purchase of equipment for,

and the repair of facilities at, that home;

(v) expenditure on food, liquor and cigarettes

consumed at home by the couple and their guests,

said to be in the course of entertainment of

business associates of the Trust;

(vi) expenditure on the provision of other entertainment

for business associates outside the home;

(vii) expenditure on providing for the sustenance of

the couple in public restaurants and the like;

(viii) travel expenses interstate for the couple;

(ix) expenditure on litigious issues, being

expenditure allegedly incurred to the advantage

of the Trust in relation to matters which arose

from events preceding the establishment of the

Trust;

(x) moneys spent on the purchase of mint coins,

perfumes and other items said to be intended to

be used, and used, by the Trust as gifts; and

(xi) the provision of superannuation benefits for

the couple as "employees" of the Trust;

(c) whether the cost of new commercial sewing machines

should be wholly allowed as deductions to the Trust

in the year of purchase as either "repairs and

maintenance" or depreciation - and whether some

special rate of depreciation - should be allowed; and

(d) (i) whether the Trustee is liable to additional

tax for incorrect returns; and

(ii) given that the Trustee was liable to such

additional tax, whether the remissions of

additional tax granted by the Commissioner

were inadequate.

3. Important and difficult questions have also been raised as to the scope of the Tribunal's authority to grant relief. Consideration of those issues points up once again the need to consider further procedural reforms with a view to giving full effect to decisions on substantive issues with the maximum efficiency and all economy consistent with justice. In order to consider those questions in an appropriate context, I propose to first assess the evidence as of no such questions of procedure or jurisdiction needed to be addressed.

THE BACKGROUND

4. One Director of the Company ("Mr. Officer") was the principal person to give evidence. The only other Director was his wife ("Mrs. Officer"). She did not give evidence. I am not persuaded that she played any substantial part in the affairs of the Trust, whether as a Director or an employee. Bearing in mind the heavy responsibilities of the persons who direct the affairs of inanimate trustees, I shall refer to them as if they were trustees. I am not persuaded that Mrs. Officer did other than assent to decisions taken by her husband. No evidence was given of the creation of the Trust or the terms of any trusteeship, but it was common ground that the Trust existed and that the income-earning activities hereafter referred to were activities carried out as activities of the Trust. During his working career, Mr. Officer had developed skills in manufacturing and marketing clothing. His specialty was industrial clothing made to standards specified by public authorities. Requisitioning authorities in the public sector were required to purchase from approved tenderers. For any particular class of garment the authorities usually nominated the two lowest tenderers as approved suppliers. Before the Trust was established Mr. Officer had worked in association with, first, one and, later, another person. One of those associations resulted in litigation between Mr. Officer and his associate. On another occasion, also before the Trust was established, litigation broke out between Mr. Officer and a fabric supplier.

5. The essence of the business of the Trust lay in procuring orders for garments from customers; the purchase and cutting of appropriate cloth; the sewing of garments; and delivery of the made-up garments to customers. For the most part, but not exclusively, orders came in consequence of approved tenders. Orders also came from a small group of privately operated linen and cleaning services. Accounts were rendered for the price of goods supplied. In its invoices the Trust claimed to reserve title to the goods to itself pending payment. Having regard to the nature of the goods and their proposed use, the reservation of the claim to title to garments which were probably unidentifiable and probably put to use immediately following delivery would seem to have been of little value. During the period in question the work of sewing garments was entrusted to seamstresses who made up garments for agreed rates using commercial sewing machines provided by the Trust. A machine would be delivered to the seamstress and, while she continued to sew for the business, it remained with her. The machines were maintained at the expense of the Trust. When the cost of maintenance and repair became excessive the machines were disposed of and replaced. If a seamstress ceased working for the business, her machine would be transferred to another seamstress. At all material times the machines remained the property of the Trust. The Trust also came to derive substantial income by way of rent and interest. How that came to pass was not explained, but it seems to have contributed little if anything to the difficulties experienced.

6. Having decided to have a company assume the responsibilities of a trustee, Mr. Officer chose to conduct all financial operations through a single bank account: that of the Trust. There was no impropriety involved in that course, but it was a risky one - as many former solicitors can testify. It was a decision the implementation of which would call for detailed, complete, precise and accurate recording. Mr. Officer assumed that responsibility and, in my judgment, was quite capable of discharging it. I accept that he maintained quite detailed cheque butts and banking records; and that he transposed the information recorded on the cheque butts to a columnar Cash Book which he personally maintained whereby the particular debits were aggregated in categories which formed the basis for presentation of claims in income tax returns. It was at that point of transposition from cheque butts to the Cash Book that some entries were made which were to lay the foundation for many of the problems now presented before me. I instance by way of example only:-

(1) some overseas travelling expenses of Mr. and Mrs.

Officer, including one item of $3,278 (4 February

1982), were aggregated under a category "Petrol

M.V. Travel";

(2) a television receiver and a barbeque acquired for

the home of the couple were classified as

"Advertising, Entertainment and Travel Expenses";

(3) a personal life assurance premium paid in respect

of the life of Mr. Officer was recorded under the

classification "Insurance"; and

(4) in addition, it is possible that a payment of

$3,800 by way of superannuation contributions to a

fund for Mr. & Mrs. Officer as employees may have

been classified as "telephone expenses".

It should be said immediately that in relation to the first three examples the "error" represented possible "private" expenses as probably deductible, whereas in the fourth example the "error" only misdescribed expenses probably deductible.

7. I mention these matters by way of example only in order to lay a foundation for a consideration of questions which have arisen as to the extent of the jurisdiction of the Tribunal to grant relief. That problem in large measure arises out of the circumstances giving rise to the assessments in dispute before me. I shall first recount that history.

INCOME TAX RETURNS

8. The Trust maintained its primary records relating to sales and purchases; to debtors and creditors; to receipts and expenditures; and of banking transactions. These matters were recorded in its primary records, including a Cash Book maintained by Mr. Officer. Cheques in large amounts were often recorded as paid to Mr. Officer and recorded as "Cash Expenses". Some records were also maintained of amounts claimed for reimbursement by Mr. Officer as expenditure incurred in the interests of the Trust. Those latter records were sometimes supported by receipts. The primary responsibility for the accuracy of all of those records lay with Mr. Officer.

9. The records so compiled were presented to the accountants to the Trust. They also acted for Mr. and Mrs. Officer. Thereon it became their responsibility (inter alia) to prepare income tax returns for the Applicant in accordance with sound accounting conventions and practice and in accordance with the requirements of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") and Regulations. To discharge that responsibility the accountants first had to identify, and then bring to account in the income tax return, only such items as were relevant to the assessment of income tax. That involved decisions, for example, such as that not to bring to account "debtors". It also involved decisions as to which items were relevant to the income tax return and to the assessment of taxable income. For example, decisions were called for as to whether particular items as described in the primary records qualified as deductions, either wholly or partly, and, if the latter, to what extent. Similarly decisions were called for as to whether claims should be made in relation to particular items of expense as depreciation or as a wholly allowable deductions.

10. The task of the accountant was (inter alia) to claim all that was available as deductions and to avoid claiming what was not allowable: a difficult task. It is a task which is sometimes so difficult that, even when fully informed, experts differ.

11. Such decisions having been made as to each and every element of expenditure, the second task was to gather that information into appropriate accounting form. That exercise properly, conveniently and sensibly called for a course of aggregating items in groups identifying sums of expenditure referred to in the single items by some appropriate description which would convey to those who would read the accounts, be they Directors of the Trust, or assessors in the service of the Commissioner, a meaningful and accurate overview of the affairs of the Trust.

12. In the ordinary course of accounting practice, journals and working papers would have been prepared whereby the accounts presented with the income tax returns could be reconciled back to the primary records. Unfortunately in this case those working papers and journals were not available for consideration by the Tribunal, or by the investigating officers of the Commissioner, or by the accountants who bore responsibility for the conduct of the litigation. However, it is appropriate to say at this point that, if in the course of writing up a cheque butt, or posting a cash book, or preparing journal entries, an item of expense properly allowable as a deduction had been inappropriately or incorrectly described so as to be claimed under an incorrect classification, that would not of itself result in any understatement of taxable income. But if an item which was not allowable as a deduction was described in terms suggesting eligibility for deductibility and a deduction was so claimed, an assessor could easily be led into error and into the allowance of the claim.

THE INVESTIGATION

13. After the first returns (sic) of income in question had been lodged with the Commissioner in November 1982 (sic), officers of the Commissioner began to conduct an investigation into the affairs of the Trust. That examination was based upon the primary records of the company: the records which have been the basis for all three calculations which have now been completed and presented before me. The investigating officers discovered what they judged to be a number of errors and discrepancies and other factors which ultimately resulted in the assessments in dispute being issued. The principal factors to which they gave effect when the assessments issued were:

(a) An alleged failure to account on an "accruals"

basis - a contention which occasioned no

difficulties on the figures;

(b) The exclusion of claims made in the returns of the

Trust in the second and later years to deduct

'carry forward' losses - again a matter which

occasioned no difficulties on the figures;

(c) Discrepancies as to amounts claimed under

particular headings in the returns of income,

which claims, in the view of the investigators

exceeded what was supported by primary records;

(d) The failure to bring to account as assessable

income all 'sales' said to be recorded in the

primary records; and

(e) The incorrect claiming (or overclaiming) as

deductions of some amounts not allowable (or not

wholly allowable) as deductions.

The investigators prepared draft figures reflecting the results of their examinations. They notified the Applicant of their workings and invited submissions from the Applicant as to the accuracy of those workings.

THE DIFFERENCES

17. Most of the elements so summarised are capable of further analysis and that will be made in due course as the need arises. Meanwhile I proceed to address the issues as presented in the summary:-

A. Discrepancy in Rental Income $5,324 (1982).

18. At the hearing the Commissioner's representative conceded that to that extent the assessment was excessive.

B. Alleged Discrepancy in Gross Sales $7,192 (1983).

19. Upon the hearing the Commissioner's representative conceded that, as to $6,779 the Commissioner's calculations had been in error. That left an alleged discrepancy of $413. Upon the hearing Mr. Officer gave evidence and the Cash Book (Receipts and Payments) which was the basis of the Commissioner's assessments and also of the Applicant's opposition to them, was tendered in evidence. It seemed to be assumed on both sides that, so far as they went, the primary entries constituted a correct record of all the transactions they recorded. On the basis of an analysis conducted by his officers the Commissioner initially determined that there had been an understatement of gross sales by $7,192. That was subsequently acknowledged to be in error as to $6,779. At the hearing there was presented for the Applicant an analysis of the same record suggesting that there had been an overstatement of sales receipts by $1,000. The Applicant presented an analysis of bank deposits as per Cash Book which was said to be reconciled to the monthly summaries in the Cash Book. It was not suggested either in the course of evidence or argument that that summary was incorrectly done or that there was any other error in the computation of gross sales. On balance, I think it more probable than not that there was no understatement of gross sales. It was not contended that I should act on the basis that there had been any overstatement of sales and I do not propose to do so.

C. Alleged Discrepancies Between Deductions Claimed and Cash Book. ($9,624 - 1982 and $8,591 - 1983)

20. This item relates to two years of income. As they give rise to distinct issues, I propose to deal with the years separately. In relation to each year of income I propose to set out the detail of the respective calculations and recalculations. In doing so I will record not only the items in which there was some difference in assessment but also those in which the Commissioner's examination confirmed the amount claimed in the return. That factor is significant, even if only to indicate that the return was accurate in relation to many items.

22. For convenience I propose to group together issues having common characteristics. On that basis my findings are as follows:

(a) Advertising $825

Light and Power $123

Rates and Taxes $15

As to the first item I am satisfied that the expenditure was incurred but I am not persuaded that the expenditure was other than for a private purchase for Mr. & Mrs. Officer. As to the other items I am satisfied that the expenditure was incurred but that it was also incurred in meeting the private and personal commitments of both Mr. & Mrs. Officer. That being so, the three claims will be further considered under the heading "Domestic Expenses".

(b) Filing Fees $70

I am satisfied that the expense was incurred and was deductible. The claim will be allowed.

(c) Telephone $3,904

I am not persuaded that the outgoing claim was incurred under this heading. Accordingly the adjustment is to stand.

(d) Repairs and Maintenance $3,920

I find that in this year of income the Trust "wrote off" the full price of commercial sewing machines which it had purchased. It claimed a deduction as "Repairs and Maintenance" - a description I consider to be quite misleading. Furthermore, I am satisfied that the machines constituted plant and equipment of a capital nature and that the effective life of the machines exceeded one year. It follows that I consider the cost of the units to be depreciable, but not depreciable at the rate of 100% per annum. I am not persuaded that the effective life of the machines was less than ten years. That being so I am satisfied that depreciation might have been claimed at 15% per annum in each year based on diminishing value. Assuming that there is power to allow a deduction, it will amount to $279.

(e) Motor Vehicle Expenses $548

The accountant's analysis of the Cash Book asserted that it recorded motor-vehicle expenses at $11,430 - not $10,882 allowed by the Commissioner. The assertion was incorrect. The revised calculations included in "Motor-Vehicle Expenses" an item from the column "Travel Expenses" more particularly identified - I use the term loosely - as "Cash Expenses". I am not persuaded that there is any merit in the claim or that anything further is allowable than has been allowed.

23. I now turn to consider the new claims raised by the accountants in their revised calculations.

(a) Accountancy $135

The Commissioner did not dispute the Trust claim to have expended $1,213 on a wholly deductible basis. The Applicant now claims a deduction for a further $135. I am satisfied that the Cash Book records $1,348 as having been paid to the accountants in January 1982 but I note that in the 1981 income tax return prepared in 1981, $135 was claimed and allowed as a deduction. I am not persuaded that anything further is allowable in the 1982 year of income.

(b) Bank Charges $220

The original claim for a deduction of $520 was not challenged. By the revised calculations a further $220 is claimed. The accuracy of the Cash Book upon which the disputed assessment was based was not disputed. As in my calculations it recorded Bank Charges at $738, a further $218 might be allowed.

(c) Insurance $280

I am not persuaded that there was any out-going such as would give rise to any further deduction. If anything, excessive claims have already been allowed.

(d) Superannuation $3,800

Upon the evidence presented before me by the principal witness and in the form of the primary records I am satisfied that there was an outgoing in the sum of $3,800 during the year of income which represented superannuation contributions made by the trustee which might have constituted an allowable deduction available to be brought to account the computation of the taxable income of the taxpayer. Although the evidence before me is insufficient to indicate how it happened, I am satisfied that in some way a transposition error was made which resulted in nothing being claimed for superannuation and in the claims in relation to telephone being excessive by more than that sum. But, even if the claim now made is one which could be allowed, I would have to be satisfied that it should be. To be satisfied as to that I would need to be persuaded as to all of the elements necessary to found a claim to a deduction under Subdivision AA - Contributions to Superannuation Funds for Benefit of Employees - of Division 3 of the Act. As it is the evidence does not even establish the remuneration level of either employee. Further, I note that the claimed contributions ($3,800) far exceed 5% of wages (claimed and allowed at $16,000). There is nothing before me which persuades me of "special circumstances" as contemplated by S.82AAE of the Act such as would result in more than $800 being allowed. Perhaps "special circumstances" did exist. But if they did, the evidence presented has not persuaded me that they did. C - $8,591 - The 1983 Year

25. On this occasion it is to be observed that the gross over-claims amounted to $10,432. The accuracy of the Commissioner's workings in that regard is not disputed. However, the Commissioner's workings also indicated that claims for "advertising, entertainment and travel" were understated by $1,841. By that assertion they claim that the Commissioner should have allowed as a deduction a sum of $4,800 represented by a cheque paid to solicitors in January 1983. 26. As to the matter of litigious expenses, I find that before the Trust was established fabric had been purchased from a supplier. Upon delivery Mr. Officer contended that the goods were defective and refused to effect payment. The vendor instituted proceedings against Mr. Officer in a Victorian Court and Mr. Officer defended the proceedings through Sydney solicitors. He was unsuccessful and in January 1983 $4,800 was paid from the funds of the Trust in satisfaction of the claim. It was contended that the Trust had had the benefit of the materials. It may have been so, but I am not persuaded on the balance of probabilities that it was so. The evidence was too vague and imprecise.

27. My conclusions as to 1983 under this head are that, as to the adjustments actually made by the Commissioner, I am not persuaded that the Commissioner was incorrect in the courses he followed; as to the fresh claim for a deduction for litigious expenses - $4,800 - I am not persuaded that anything is allowable; and as to the matter of depreciation ($546), I am satisfied that that amount is allowable if there is power in the Tribunal to allow it.

D. Other deductions disallowed $14,630 (1982)

28. This item referred to a variety of claims in relation to which my findings are as follows:

(a) Telephone - $342

Electricity - $187

Rates and Taxes - $280

I find that the amounts making up these claims represented payments made by the Trust in relation to the provision of telephone and electricity services to, and rates and taxes payable in respect of, the private residence of Mr. & Mrs. Officer. I shall defer further consideration until dealing with the subject of "domestic expenses".

(b) Freight and Cartage - $679

I am not persuaded that this item bore any relationship to any income earning activity of the Trust.

(c) Motor Vehicle Expenses - $4,196

The terminology used was not only a misnomer, it was quite misleading. As to $203.60 I am satisfied on the balance of the probabilities that it related to an interstate airfare incurred in the course of the business of the Trust. It will be allowed. As to $3,278 I am positively satisfied that the claim relates to overseas travelling expenses to be considered in greater detail hereafter. As to the sums of $500 (cash to Mr. Officer) and $214.68 (paid to a travel service), I am simply not persuaded as to what these items are or why the amounts were drawn on the Trust.

(d) Debt Collection - $5,319

This claim can be considered in two parts: one a single item relating to $4,759; and the other, three items for a total of $559. I find that the payment of $4,759 was a payment made to the solicitors to Mr. Officer in relation to proceedings in which he was involved arising out of a partnership of which he had been a member prior to the establishment of the Trust. Except in so far as it might be said that the outlay was an expense incurred by the Trust in securing the services of Mr. Officer, I am not satisfied that it had any relationship at all to the income-earning activities of the Trust. As to the amounts of $559 I find that they constituted the payment of amounts due by Mr. Officer to his solicitors in relation to the conduct of the Victorian litigation previously mentioned. I am not persuaded that they related to the income-earning activities of the Trust either. I conclude that no part of this claim is allowable.

30. The figures for the 1983 year need to be explained. Unfortunately, even now they are not clear to me. In its return of income the Trust had originally claimed only $16,852. Upon investigation, as appears from paragraph 21, the Commissioner's investigators identified out-goings of $18,693 under this head and allowed a further deduction of $1,841 being the excess over the amount claimed. However, following a further appraisal of the situation, prior to the issue of the last amended assessment it was determined that only some claims should be allowed, thereby increasing the amount in dispute to $18,123.

31. I accept that Mr. Officer entertained persons from a limited group frequently and liberally. I accept that there was some commercial advantage for the Trust to be had in doing so, particularly as his principal commercial rivals were not in the position to compete with the Trust in that way. I accept that he put on a major function each year for the principal persons with whom the Trust dealt and I accept that from time to time some of those persons were entertained in his home. I also accept that the Trust paid for many meals consumed by Mr. Officer when attended by his wife or by other persons with some capacity to assist the business. However, in so far as he was joined by his wife, I am not persuaded that the occasion was for either of them an income-earning occasion or a meeting undertaken in the course of employment. I find that such occasions were wholly "private" in character but with the cost met out of the funds of the Trust. I accept that the Trust also bore the expenses of providing liquor and cigarettes to the home of Mr. Officer so that he might provide those things for himself and others.

32. Not only were the claims large but to a Tribunal accustomed to considering a wide variety of claims and of hearing unusual explanations for commercial conduct, the claims remain remarkable. Included in the category classified as "meals", in the year of income ended 30 June 1983, there are fourteen transactions to a total value of $623 which reflect the cost of providing meat for the household of Mr. & Mrs. Officer. Nor does the matter stop there. In the 1982 year in the "miscellaneous" category there was claimed the cost of purchase of a barbecue for the home ($227); and in the following year under the same classification claims for overseas travel expenses for Mr. and Mrs. Officer ($2,892 and $4,725); a claim for personal medical expenses ($130); repairs to the family swimming pool ($395); and the purchase of a television receiver for the home ($449). There was also a payment of $3,000 to a car dealer, the explanation for which was vague in the extreme.

33. As to the duty free purchases ($405 & $311) and purchases of gems ($517 & $102) and from the mint ($312 & $758), it was said that all of those outlays were to provide gifts for persons as a reward for services rendered to the Trust. I do not accept that evidence.

34. The major question which arises in the context of considering this multiplicity of claims is that they are claims by the Trustee and not by Mr. Officer as a self-employed trader. Therefore it was open to the construction that in his hands all of the benefits were what is commonly referred to as "fringe benefits", the cost of which should be allowable as a deduction to an employer. However, that is not the only construction open. An alternative view is that the "outgoings" of the Trust to the advantage of Mr. and Mrs. Officer were not incurred "in the course of" the Trust deriving its assessable income but really constituted distributions of Trust property to beneficiaries of the Trust. For the most part I favour the latter analysis, although some matters need to be the subject of closer consideration. However, the matter of "overseas travelling expenses" calls for special consideration.

35. According to the evidence of Mr. Officer, the Trust bore the expenses of having he and his wife travel overseas to Italy - generally recognised as a centre of world fashion - for the purpose of considering developments in relation to the garment trade. Among claims to deductions which were made are those for fares and for incidental expenses incurred while travelling. No claim was made for accommodation expenses because accommodation was available in the home of former neighbours who had returned to their country of origin. A typical day, it was said, involved both husband and wife going to a major department store, and at other times to small stores, to there examine in detail the design and, in particular, the techniques for the making up of garments on display. Through a relative of their hosts they had some remote connection with a six hundred bed hospital. I am not persuaded that the association was such as to establish the journey as significantly, let alone predominently, a business exercise. Their destination for this purpose was not one of the major fashion centres such as Rome or Florence but the town of Nuro on the island of Sardinia; a town said to have a population of the order of 35,000 - 37,000 people; a town said to be quite "like Goulburn".

36. I do accept that, as persons interested in the garment trade, while on holiday they showed some interest in garments, and in particular in the method of making up garments such as they marketed in Australia. But I am not persuaded that in doing so what they did was anything more than might have been expected of a taxation officer observing the workings of VAT while in the United Kingdom; or a traffic policeman observing traffic conditions and the provision for their regulation; or a bus driver reflecting on what he observed as to the public transport system operating in countries through which he had passed on holiday. I am not persuaded that any portion of the overseas travelling expenses gives rise to any deduction entitlement.

37. However, as to the other expenses, it would not be appropriate to conclude that none of the expenses incurred gave rise to allowable deductions. I identify as expenses wholly allowable claims for $500 in each year as the sum paid to a restaurant on the occasion of providing the annual dinner for "customers"; $203.60 and $215 (1982) which I accept as being, more probably than not, airfares incurred in the course of travel on business in Australia; and $54 (1983) for a present to a member of staff. But, I am not persuaded as to the matter of motel expenses ($435) or the payment of $3,000 to a car firm was in any way related to the income earning activities of the Trust. Save for what is said in the next paragraph, I make no particular comment about many identifiable and some unidentifiable items. I am not persuaded that the claims to which they give rise are allowable.

38. In relation to all of the other expenses of providing meals (whether in the home or in restaurants), and of providing, liquor and cigarettes, the evidence is not such as would enable one to proceed to make findings of fact on an item by item basis. Nor do I think that it is always appropriate to approach a problem such as this in that way. (cf the reasons for decision in NT87/8291 - 11 July 1989.) I accept that there was some "business entertainment" such as would give rise to allowable deductions and I propose to make a finding of fact in that regard to give effect to that view. If that finding is less favourable to the Applicant than it might have been, then that must be because the Applicant in its own interests failed to adequately record its activities so as to distinguish between those which were directed to the generation of assessable income and those which reflected the private drawings of a beneficiary from his Trustee or the unauthorised and improper application of trust funds by the Trustee in the interests of those whom it favoured. Whichever may be the case, I am far from being persuaded that the outlays referred to were wholly incurred in the course of carrying on the business of the Trust and the derivation of its assessable income. As an exercise in persuasion, the evidence presented substantially failed of its purpose. Of all the remaining expenses which have been referred to as "domestic expenses" or entertainment or travel, I would allow no more than a further $480 and $530 in the years of income ended 30 June 1982 and 1983 respectively.

F. The Accrual of Debtors ($25,190 and $10,942)

Cash or accruals basis

39. When the Applicant was about to commence business Mr. Officer consulted his accountant as to the management of accounts and generally. At the time the accountant took the view that the scale of activities contemplated was likely to be so small and the commitment to those activities so casual and so much on a part time basis - even a "hobby" basis - that he considered that a "cash" basis was the most appropriate basis for the presentation of the income tax returns of the Trust. After the close of the first financial year he stood by that judgment and he continued to do so in presenting the accounts and returns of the Trust in later years. He did so as late as November 1982 when presenting the first and second (sic) returns of income: those to 30 June 1981 and 30 June 1982.

40. Such a question can be a difficult one and it is usually an important one. The difficulty of the exercise for accountants is compounded by the circumstance that it is in the nature of the calling of an accountant in private practice advising taxpayers to advise them how not to unnecessarily pay tax which they are not required to pay. If, in contemplating such difficult questions the accountant merely takes the "safe" course of resolving all doubtful questions in favour of the Revenue, he is hardly rendering the quality of service expected by his client. On the other hand, if on a doubtful point he decides to accept the risk of error and the consequences which will flow should he be in error, he must not complain if the risks are unsuccessfully taken and the consequences follow. But having said that, there is no reason in a doubtful situation why the accountant should not explicitly declare to the Commissioner the basis upon which he is presenting returns of income. In such a case as this, if the Commissioner ultimately is successful in contending for an "accruals" basis of return, at least he has little foundation for then contending that he was misled by the representations made in the name of the taxpayer.

41. But in the circumstances of this case my view of the matter is that, whatever may have been the reasonableness of the expectations held by the accountant before the commencement of business, by the time the first year of income had closed (30 June 1981) and, a fortiori, by the time the first and second returns of income were being prepared and lodged together (late in November 1982), it should have been clear to those doing the returns that an "accruals" basis of return was not only the more appropriate but, indeed, in all the circumstances the only appropriate basis for the presentation of return.

42. In reaching that conclusion I have had regard to the circumstance that it was a manufacturing business; that it involved working a process of converting materials (purchased stock of raw materials on hand) into marketable garments (stock on hand) to be delivered to customers on credit (sundry debtors). As Dixon, C.J. said in Commissioner of Taxation (S.A.) v Executor Trustee & Agency Co. of South Australia Ltd (Carden's Case - [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108 @ 155)

"The computation of profits from manufacture and

trading has always proceeded upon the principle

that the profit may be contained in stock-in-trade

and 'outstandings'."

So it was in this case, and to that extent the assessments are to be upheld.

ADDITIONAL TAX FOR INCORRECT RETURN

44. There is then a question of additional tax for incorrect return. In considering the remission of additional tax imposed by the statute at 200% of tax avoided the Commissioner took into account his then standard practices in these matters and reduced additional tax to a figure equal to the sum of 10% per annum of tax avoided up to 14 February 1983; plus 20% per annum of tax avoided thereafter to the date of calculation of additional tax; plus 40% of tax avoided. When later the further amended assessment issued for the 1983 year, the culpability factor was reduced to 20%. That was generous.

45. However, the Applicant contends that by reason of the decision of the full bench of the Federal Court of Australia in Rabinov v F.C.T. (83 ATC 4437), the Commissioner has no right to impose additional tax in so far as the under-assessment of tax is attributable to errors in the fiscal characterisation of amounts actually expended.

46. In my view the contention for the Applicant over-states the effect of that decision. In Rabinov (ante) the taxpayers had withdrawn from their bank accounts the sum of $25,000 and each purchased a bank cheque made payable to Ezroh (Relief) Trust Fund and delivered the cheque to the trustees of the fund. Each claimed a deduction for $25,000 as constituting a deductible gift to that Fund. Later that claim was abandoned. But it was held by the trial judge that, although not a gift, each payment of $25,000 constituted expenditure actually incurred by the taxpayer. In each instance the taxpayer had made the payment which it had described in its return. The Federal Court adopted as a correct statement of the law the words of Smith J. in Cyprus Mines Corporation v F.C.T. (78 ATC 4468) when his Honour said (at page 4486):

". . . . . . unless the taxpayer includes as a deduction

expenditure which he has not incurred or over-states the

amount claimed as a deduction, then the section does not

operate to permit the (Commissioner) to impose additional

tax."

Their Honours went on to find that the transactions had given rise to "expenditure actually incurred" and, accordingly, they held that there was no liability under Section 226 of the Act to additional tax for incorrect returns. In their analysis of Section 226(2) their Honours said that, of the conditions provided for by the sub-section:

"it is possible to discern a common character, viz. that

facts are withheld from or falsely stated to the

Commissioner. It is the failure to make a full and true

disclosure of relevant information that attracts liability

to additional tax, not a failure properly to characterise

an amount which has been disclosed" (Emphasis added).

The phrase emphasised is important. It distinguishes the circumstances of this case from Rabinov (ante). Rabinov correctly identified a payment to the Ezroh Relief Trust Fund. He erred in representing that the payments so made gave rise to an entitlement to a tax deduction. But in the case of the present Applicant the avoidance of tax is not attributable only to such an error of characterisation. It rests on the proposition that the actual expenditure was "falsely stated" to the Commissioner. The $3,920 claimed as a deduction for "repairs and maintenance" for the 1982 year was expended, but it was not expended on "repairs and maintenance". As to the following year, in which $1,819 was claimed under the same classification, no evidence has even been advanced to establish that the moneys were expended, let alone that they were applied to matters of "repairs and maintenance". Similarly, in relation to much of the "domestic expenses", the representation as to what the expenditure had been was misleading.

47. Additional tax for incorrect returns related only to the 1982 and 1983 years. Periods to be taken into account were generously taken as only 1250 and 885 days. A culpability factor of 40% was taken into account on the first assessments but was lowered to 20% in relation to the further 1983 assessment.

48. The conclusions I have reached as to additional tax for incorrect returns are that:

(a) by reason of the accruals issue, assessable income

has been omitted;

(b) as to deductions, there is no scope for the

application of Rabinov;

(c) a culpability factor of 20% of tax avoided was

generous but ought not be disturbed. However, 40%

overall on the earlier assessments was excessive. I

will reduce that element to something of the order of 30%;

(d) by reason of I.T.R. 2517 the compensation factor

should be reduced; and

(e) additional tax should be reduced to reflect

reductions in taxable income.

GROUNDS OF OBJECTION

49. Ultimately the assessments presently at issue before me were made, and notice thereof given to the Applicant. Those assessments were subject to challenge at two levels. The first level was the formal one, which in large measure results in the ambit of the jurisdiction of the Tribunal being defined. I refer to the Notices of Objection. For the moment I say only that the objections challenged in general terms the assertions of the Commissioner. The second level of challenge was in the presentation of evidence before the Tribunal.

50. There is a significant practical difference in the two levels of challenge. That arises because the principal objectives of the first level of challenge were to ensure that a right to seek relief was preserved to the Applicant; and that the Commissioner had an opportunity to reconsider his position in light of the challenge made by the objection. Furthermore, it was essential that all those formal steps to protect the position of the Applicant be taken within a strictly limited period of 60 days from service of the notices of assessment. For that reason notices of objection are ordinarily responsive directly and only to what is communicated to a taxpayer at the time of the service of the notice of assessment. However, the challenge at the second level comes much later when endeavours (if any) to negotiate a settlement have reached an advanced stage and failed, after which a matter is "worked up" with a view to a hearing. At that point it is to be expected - as it certainly was in this instance - that what occurred was that all of the primary evidence was re-assessed by the accountants. The sequence was that it had been initially assessed by them in preparing the returns; then assessed by the Commissioner to give rise to the assessments; then re-assessed by the Commissioner to give rise to a further amended assessment and then re-appraised for the Applicant. The accountants who appeared before me on behalf of the Applicant sought to re-work the primary records with a view to establishing that the taxable incomes (losses) originally returned had been correct.

51. In doing so the accountants who appeared before me did not have available the working papers or journals which had been used by the accountant who had prepared the returns. (No explanation was provided as to why the original accountant was not called as a witness.) Had they had that information available they might have been able to then identify precisely all of the points of difference between the original accountant and the investigators, thereby to establish just how the differences arose. However, they did have available the working papers of the Commissioner's investigators with the result that they could offer some explanations at the hearing based upon those accounting records, suggesting that there had been:

(a) simple omissions by the Commissioner;

(b) failures to correctly identify disbursements as

giving rise to deductions;

(c) Amounts underclaimed; and

(d) Misdescribed claims whereby an amount properly

disallowed as not expended for the purpose claimed

was not allowed as a claim properly available under

the description by which it should have been claimed.

52. Insofar as items in the first two categories are concerned it is not suggested that the Tribunal could not find the assessments of the Commissioner to be excessive. However, as to the other two categories the Commissioner's representative contends that:

(a) in relation to items such as "bank charges" all that

was claimed in the return to be deductible was

allowed and that it is not now open to the taxpayer

to contend for, or for the Tribunal to allow, any

increase in deductible amounts so as to thereby

reduce the assessment of taxable income; and

(b) as to items such as "superannuation", no claim ever

having been made in the return, the Tribunal cannot

now allow such an item.

In both cases the argument for the Commissioner is rested upon the proposition that there was nothing in the objections to assessment presented to the Commissioner giving notice of any challenge to the accuracy of the assessments on any such basis. Furthermore, it was argued for the Commissioner that if a claim is to be allowed on an item such as "superannuation", the claim must first be made to the Commissioner, if necessary by notice of objection out of time, and that only following disallowance of such an objection could the matter come before this Tribunal for consideration. It was acknowledged by the Commissioner's representative that the logic of the argument was that, had the problem arisen prior to 1 July 1986 and had the last day for objection arisen prior to 1 July 1986, no relief could ever have been granted as of right to an Applicant in relation to an item such as "superannuation". The procedural rules of the period gave priority to "protecting public revenue" (c.f. Molloy v F.C.T. ((1938) [1938] HCA 29; 59 CLR 608 at 610)) by enabling the revenue to retain monies to which it would not have been entitled had everything relevant been fully known at the point of assessment and the assessment then been correctly made. (It seems that protection of the citizen from an excessive assessment was not a matter of such high priority). On the other hand the taxpayer looks to the circumstance that section 190(a) of the Act now no longer limits the Tribunal to granting relief on grounds stated in the objection. That, it is argued, is a means of achieving a just result with reasonable efficiency and due economy without requiring the Applicant to initiate a fresh objection to be preceded by an application for an extension of time within which to object.

JURISDICTION TO REVIEW

53. To appreciate the respective arguments they need to be seen in the context in which such issues arise. Most assessments which result in challenges by way of objection follow upon the lodgment with the Commissioner of income tax returns. Those returns may be simple or complex; the accounting records giving rise to them may be detailed, complicated and sophisticated, or they may be virtually non-existent. The essential objection of the taxpayer is to the assessment of his taxable income at a figure exceeding the figure acknowledged by him. The draftsman in approaching the task of casting grounds of objection - grounds which must be set forth "fully and in detail" - is usually assisted by an "adjustment sheet" as providing a guide to the matters identified by the Commissioner as causing the differences between the Commissioner and the taxpayer. Sometimes the adjustment sheet is quite precise in identifying what that difference is. (For example, it may identify specifically a claimed deduction as wholly disallowed.) Sometimes it is less so. (For example, an adjustment: add "profit on the sale of shares" - without identifying the shares or the profit calculation). Difficulties rarely arise in such cases. Another common feature of an adjustment sheet is the provision of an explanation as to "why" the adjustment has been made. Such explanations are often incomplete and are also sometimes quite inaccurate. Taxpayers must always be alert not to rely on them (F.C.T. v Reynolds 81 ATC 4131). But provided the last reservation is borne in mind, the information provided by the Commissioner is usually enough to enable a sufficient objection to be drawn.

54. However, there is another quite different way in which issues arise, such as by assessments which follow investigations of which the taxpayer may or may not be aware. But whatever the background, the assessment once made by the Commissioner is communicated to the taxpayer by a notice of assessment. That notice of assessment advises him of the taxable income assessed; of the tax, additional tax and provisional tax and other charges levied; and as to the due date for payment and other relevant dates. If it is to be contended that the assessment of taxable income is excessive it is that assessment as so notified that is to be objected to.

55. Sometimes the notice of assessment is not attended by any information indicating how the figure determined upon by the Commissioner for taxable income was fixed upon. Sometimes such a notice of assessment is attended by an adjustment sheet which tells the taxpayer of the extent of the adjustment, but communicates little or no information as to the basis for the adjustment. In one common form the taxpayer is advised by the adjustment sheet that his taxable income has been increased from a taxable income (or loss) returned by a particular figure "as a result of an investigation" (or equivalent). Sometimes, as with an "assets betterment assessment", an adjustment sheet (as delivered with the Notice) which provides little information is supported by other calculations (not so provided) which record considerable detail explaining the Commissioner's calculations.

56. In the circumstances of this case, prior to assessment the Commissioner's officers had provided draft calculations for the taxpayer's consideration and invited its submissions upon the drafts. I am not persuaded that, when the assessments issued attended by adjustment sheets which in themselves communicated little, the taxpayer did not understand the assessments by reference to those draft calculations which had previously foreshadowed the issue of the assessments which later issued. But those calculations formed no part of the assessment and they were not presented with the assessment in explanation of it. That being so, in my view, in order to object effectively to the assessments raised it was sufficient that the objections be appropriately drawn so as to draw the attention of the Commissioner to the matters objected to with a measure of clarity and precision proportioned to the advice received from the Commissioner with the notices of assessment as to his basis for issuing the assessments in the sums he did.

57. At that point one difficulty which now arises can be identified by reference to a simple example. In putting it, I suppose that in completing its return of income a taxpayer which had incurred telephone expenses of $1,000 had claimed a deduction for telephone expenses of $5,000; that superannuation contributions which had been made in the sum of $4,000 and which were allowable had not been claimed in the return, or allowed for by the Commissioner in any assessment; and that the reason for the overclaim on the one hand and the underclaim for the other was a simple clerical error - $4,000 in superannuation payments having been posted to the incorrect column in the Cash Book and thereby identified as "Telephone Expenses". Following an examination the Commissioner might have assessed taxable income at $54,000 rather than $50,000. If no explanation at all had been proferred with the notice of assessment as to the basis for the adjustment the taxpayer in objecting could have done little more than object in general terms that:

(a) the calculation of taxable income was excessive;

(b) the calculation of assessable income was excessive;

and

(c) that allowable deductions were undercalculated.

58. However, if the notice of assessment had been attended by an adjustment sheet which stated that: "Telephone Expenses $1,000 overclaimed at $5,000" and which increased taxable income by $4,000, the objection might reasonably have been expected to have dealt specifically with the matter of "Telephone Expenses". That could have been by asserting the claim as made to have been wholly allowable. That is what is likely to have happened if the accuracy of the initial claim of $4,000 had not been checked or, if upon checking, the error had not been found. On the other hand, if the error had been found before the time for objection had expired - a mere 60 days from service of the notice of assessment - it might have been expected that the objection would have specifically claimed that the assessment was excessive by reason of the failure of the Commissioner to have allowed a claim for superannuation contributions in the sum of $4,000. In many cases a period of 60 days allowed for the purpose is quite sufficient to enable such matters to be identified. In other cases it is not so.

59. If the actual error is identified prior to objection no problem will ordinarily arise. Nor would any serious, substantial problem arise in allowing relief in relation to such a clerical error upon a hearing provided that the Commissioner had had reasonable notice prior to the hearing of the matters to be contended for by the Applicant. But if the matter comes to hearing with the Commissioner having no reason to expect such an issue as to clerical error touching a matter unrelated to "telephone expenses" to be put forward as a basis for alleging the assessment to be excessive, fairness would require that either the matter be adjourned to enable the Commissioner to properly prepare, or that the Applicant should be denied a hearing on the question sought to be newly raised, leaving it to him to initiate other proceedings directing the attention of the Commissioner to the newly perceived issue thereby to provide the Commissioner with the opportunity to consider, and if necessary investigate, the claim.

60. In the present case all of those problems, and more, arise. The fact that they do highlights the continuing need in such cases for care and precision in the preparation of objections. It is against that background that I now proceed to make findings as to what may be allowed.
