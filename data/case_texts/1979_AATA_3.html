Harrison and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 3 (1 February 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007



Superannuation - Benefit Classification Certificate deemed to be in force - Past history of a condition specified in Certificate - condition not apparent at time of medical examination for permanent appointment -Invalidity retirement on ground of condition specified in Certificate - Application for review

Superannuation Act 1922 - Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16, 66, 154, 184 - Superannuation Amendment Act 1978, s.77.

DUDLEY EDWIN HARRISON

THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

AAT Decision No 137

No: 78/12024

R.K. Todd (Senior Member), V.J. Skermer (Member), Dr. M. Glick (Member)

1 February 1979

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

No: 78/12024

Re: DUDLEY EDWIN HARRISON

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The Tribunal affirms the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, acting pursuant to s. 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to confirm upon reconsideration the decision taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 4 October 1977 that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 15 June 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s. 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DATED this 1st day of February 1979.

R.K. TODD

Senior Member

V.J. SKERMER Member

DR M. GLICK Member

REASONS FOR DECISION

In this application the applicant seeks review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, acting pursuant to s. 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976, to confirm upon reconsideration the decision taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 4 October 1977 that the benefit entitlement of Mr. Harrison on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 15 June 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s. 66(2) of the Act. The decision was based upon an opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant from the service of the Commonwealth was caused or was substantially contributed to, by a condition specified in a benefit classification certificate which was deemed by s. 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to have been issued and which was still in force immediately before the retirement of the applicant, or by a condition connected with such a condition.

To explain this we should, before setting out our findings of fact, refer to the rather complicated statutory provisions which govern a case such as this. They are certainly not easy for a layman to follow. When the applicant in this case, being unrepresented, had completed his opening we invited the Commissioner's representative to outline those provisions to us, without adverting to the facts or in any way putting the Commissioner's case to us. When done in an uncontentious way, we feel that this will often be of advantage to the applicant and to the Tribunal in a wide range of applications. It was ably so done in this case, as it has been in other cases involving the Office of the Commissioner for Superannuation.

By way of introduction, where the question is, as here, one of the extent of the benefits available upon retirement having regard to s.66 of the Superannuation Act 1976, it is necessary to understand that a person applying for a permanent position in the Public Service is medically examined and if he meets certain prescribed medical standards is accepted for membership of the Superannuation Scheme. A contributor coming in to the Public Service after the commencement of the "new" Superannuation Scheme on 1 July 1976 is affected first by the provisions of s.16 of the Superannuation Act 1976. That section provides in ss. (2), (3) and (4) thereof as follows:

"16. (2) A person who proposes to become, or becomes, an eligible employee shall undergo such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner requires.

(3) A report or reports of the result or results of the medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner.

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or conditions is or are specified."

The effect of these provisions is seen when s. 66 comes to be considered when an eligible employee retires on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age. The question then arises whether there is in force in respect of the employee, immediately before his retirement, a benefit classification certificate. If there is, the final question is whether the incapacity which was the ground of the employee's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate or by a condition connected with such a condition.

These provisions, however, would not be apt to cover the case of a person who, like the present applicant, was a contributor to the Provident Account pursuant to the provisions of the superseded Act, that is to say the Superannuation Act 1922. Broadly, the position under the superseded Act was that if a medical examination indicated that it was more likely than not that the employee would not attain his full retirement age, he would be accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account, rather than to the Superannuation Fund. A further alternative was of course non-acceptance of the employee for the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account. The present applicant was accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account on 26 November 1970, having been medically examined on 23 September 1970. As under the superseded Act there was no provision for the issue of a benefit classification certificate, it was necessary, when the 1976 legislation was passed, to give life to s.66 in such a case, and to make some equivalent provision accordingly. This was sought to be achieved by s.184 of the Superannuation Act 1976. This section provided, (subject to the pre-requisites contained in s.184 (2)(a)(b) and (c)) that "for the purposes of this Act ... there shall be deemed to be in force, on the commencing day, ... a benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner under section 16(4) specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act".

Such a certificate is a notional concept, not an actual document, although it has been the practice of the Commissioner in a situation in which retirement occurs on the ground of invalidity, and a question of the applicability of s. 66(2) arises, in fact to issue an actual certificate purporting to reflect what in the view of the Commissioner should be the condition or conditions specified in the deemed certificate. It should be said that the difficulties arising from this concept are as ephemeral as they are real, for from 24 April 1978 s.184 of the Superannuation Act 1976 was by s.77 of the Superannuation Amendment Act 1978 repealed, and a new section substituted by the terms of which provision is made for the issue of an actual certificate with attendant provisions. With this, however, we are not presently concerned.

It should be noted that some nice questions can arise in relation to the continuance of a deemed benefit classification certificate. Under s. 66(2), it is necessary to be satisfied that there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before his retirement, a benefit classification certificate. By s. 184 such a certificate is deemed to have been in force on 1 July 1976 but how is it, for the purposes of s. 66(2), that there can be satisfaction that there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before his retirement, a benefit classification certificate? The point has not been directly raised here, but without wishing to finally decide the point, we consider that we are entitled to regard the benefit classification certificate as having continued in existence to the date of retirement by application of the general presumption of continuance, and by the fact that s. 16(6), which is specifically incorporated by s. 184, seems to assume such continuance. Section 16(6) gives a right to request revocation or variation of a benefit classification certificate, and, as was pointed out to us, invocation of the procedure involved is the only way in which a contributor can find out what condition or conditions are specified in the notional certificate. For present purposes, however, it is enough to say that such a procedure would be purposeless or impossible unless the challenged notional benefit classification certificate had remained in force.

"Accept for Provident Account. Report from Dr. J. Moon is satisfactory. Has good record of attendance and has had no episodes of anxiety state over past 8 years. Has been total abstainer from alcohol for past 8 years."

Dr. Calanchini, who gave evidence, evidently wrote those final comments after receiving the report from Dr. Moon that was referred to. His summarization at the end of his clinical notes, which as it turns out will be of critical importance in the case, was as follows:

"PH (scilicet 'past history') of anxiety state and alcoholism. No recurrences of either over past 8 years. Accept Provident Account."

Some time was occupied at the hearing in investigating a visit by the applicant to Dr. Moon which occurred in 1969 and which appears to have been treated by the Senior Medical Officer in 1978, with hindsight, as having involved the seeking by the applicant of medical attention, and as having constituted a "psychiatric episode". It is unnecessary to canvass this issue in detail having regard to the reasoning which, as will appear, leads us on wider considerations to the conclusion that the case for the applicant cannot be supported. It is enough to say that the applicant had had a long association with Dr. Moon in the course of which he would be called in by Dr. Moon when he had people in his care with an alcohol problem and who could be helped by the applicant as being someone who had been through a similar problem and had succeeded in overcoming it. The applicant said in evidence that he "never ever had professional services from Dr. Moon".

Dr. Calanchini in his evidence stated that he understood the applicant to have had medical treatment from Dr. Moon for nervous disorder in July 1969. Dr. Calanchini's approach was understandable for the applicant had stated that he had had "medical treatment for nervous disorder July 1969" when he answered the questionnaire. Dr. Calanchini was asked what other evidence, apart from the July 1969 involvement with Dr. Moon there was before him at that time of previous neurosis or anxiety state, and answered "none at the time of the examination".

It is true that the applicant indicated in his answers to the questionnaire, as stated above, that he had "medical treatment for nervous disorder July 1969". It is also true that on the very first page of the transcript the applicant, in opening his case, said in a statement that was later in a general way affirmed on oath that he had asked Dr. Moon about a problem over two friends who were alcoholics and whom he was seeking to help. For months, he said, he did not get any sleep and he went back to Dr. Moon and asked for advice. On the whole, however, we have decided to disregard the July 1969 incident, with the result that the Commissioner's case must rest on the restriction of the applicant to the Provident Account having been based only on the finding of "past history of anxiety state and alcoholism", as we feel bound to assume, in accordance with Dr. Calanchini's finding, that in September 1970 the applicant displayed no sign of neurosis or anxiety state.

As Dr. Calanchini's finding, and our understanding of the matter, are equally clear, we think it unnecessary to set out or to discuss the correspondence from Dr. Moon in which it was made equally clear that in his opinion the applicant was not in 1970 currently affected by any condition of anxiety.

The problems, however, do not end there. The doctor recorded that the applicant was anxious and tense from not having had any alcohol for 8 years, and that he had been an alcoholic. Asked whether the expression "anxiety state" conveyed an isolated episode or something of a continuing nature Dr. Calanchini said:

"If someone told me that they had nervous problems and an anxiety state which was the result of withdrawal from alcohol and that they had not had a drink for the past 8 years, I would not call it a temporary, isolated state. ... I would expect he would always have some anxiety and tension regarding alcohol."

Dr. Calanchini was then asked the following questions and gave the following answers:

"Q. I would now like to ask why you recommended him for the provident account and not for the superannuation account? What were the reasons or what was the condition which in your view necessitated him being put in the provident account rather than the superannuation account? ... A. The history of anxiety state and the history of alcoholism.

Q. When you say anxiety state, you told us he did not have it at the time you saw him? ... A. No.

Q. -Can you explain to us why you say the anxiety state was the relevant factor if it was not present at the time? ... A. With anyone with a history of nervous disorder I would have grave doubts that they would not have a relapse.

Q. So it was the history of it? ... A. Yes.

Q. Which in your view is significant? ... A. That is right and the history of alcoholism.

Q. (Mr. Todd): What was the history of nervous disorder upon which you relied? ... A. You mean what were his symptoms? He did not have them.

Q. No. What was the history? ... A. He told me he had an anxiety state or nervous disorder.

Q. Is that independent of this July 1969 business? It is true he answers "yes" to mental or nervous conditions and "yes" to anxiety state? ... A. Yes.

Q. What detail of that do you rely on for saying that he had a past history or is it just that bare statement? I do not say that critically? ... A. He had a history of nervous disorder. He admitted a history of nervous disorder. He suffered from nerves and he said this resulted from alcoholism. What typifies an anxiety state? Periods of tension, apprehension, emotional instability, inability to cope with day-to-day problems."

Dr. Calanchini was also asked some questions relating to the criteria which would have led him to recommend admission to the Provident Account rather than to the Superannuation Fund. He was asked by a member of the Tribunal, and answered as follows:

"Q. At that point of time it was more on the possibility that at some future time he might have a return to alcoholism or anxiety neurosis, rather than that he did have anxiety neurosis at the time of the examination? ... A. Yes.

Q. It was expectancy rather than fact at the time? ... A. At the time of my examination he showed no signs of any nervous condition, no.

Q. It was something that possibly might happen in the future? ... A. Yes."

He was then asked by the representative of the Commissioner and answered as follows:

"Q. I want you to hypothesize with me for a moment. If you had been asked at the time you completed your report in 1970 to prepare a certificate and to state in the certificate the mental or physical condition by reason of which you considered him unlikely to attain his maximum retiring age, what physical or mental condition would you have specified in that certificate? ... A. Anxiety state and alcoholism, a history of these conditions."

The conclusion in our opinion is that the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant on 1 July 1976 must be taken to have specified, as a physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act, the condition or conditions "Past history of anxiety state and alcoholism".

The question therefore is whether a medical history of a condition, physical or mental, may be described as a "condition", for the purposes of the statutory provision, even though the condition itself be not apparent at the time of the examination. We think that it may, for the following reasons.

The reference in s. 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 is to a Benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force on the commencing day (namely 1 July 1976) and issued by the Commissioner under s. 16(4) of that Act, but which specifies "the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act" (underlining supplied). Section 5 of the superseded Act (the Superannuation Act 1922) provided for consideration of a medical report and thereafter for a decision that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of that Act or to the Provident Account. Thus that legislation did not spell out what appears in s. 16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976, namely the criterion therein described "that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age." We nevertheless think that a similar concept must be implicit in the provisions of the superseded Act, which offered to accept employees of acceptable health and physical fitness as contributors to the Fund, but as an alternative to accept them as contributors to the Provident Account only, with its lesser benefits. This being so, it is we think inevitable that we should conclude that the enquiry being made when an applicant was being examined was one directed to an assessment of the likelihood of the examinee being able to work until his maximum retiring age. Accordingly, if the medical history of the examinee reflected that he had been affected by a condition that did not currently affect him, but which by its essential nature was to be seen as one having a disposition or capacity to recur, that fact could not be ignored. The possession of such disposition or capacity, where the history revealed to a significant degree the possibility of recurrence must be seen as constituting a condition within the meaning of s.184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

This is not the place, nor is there any call, to catalogue the various physical or mental conditions that might fit this analysis. It is enough to say that the condition "anxiety state" is one which does so with particular ease. The "Handbook for Guidance of Medical Officers" applicable at the date of entry of the applicant, in clause 100 (neuropsychopathies), clearly recognized this. For instance, it is stated in clause 100(b) that where there has been "freedom from recurrence and absence of signs and symptoms for three years, consideration can be given to a reconsideration for acceptance for superannuation benefits after deferment for the maximum period, or for Provident Account immediately". In clause 100(c), which deals with cases of a more complex nature than those dealt with in 100(b), it is provided that where there is clear evidence of satisfactory treatment and freedom from recurrence over a period of five years, candidates "may be considered for superannuation pension benefits", that is to say for the Superannuation Fund itself.

We have given a good deal of attention to the problem of what was done at the point of acceptance of the applicant to the Provident Account, as it is the part of the case which we have found most difficult. Curiously enough, the applicant seems to have hardly challenged the Commissioner's assertions about this aspect of the problem at all. He certainly did of course challenge any conclusion that he was suffering from anxiety state at the time of his examination, but we have in any event accepted, on the evidence of Dr. Calanchini, that in September 1970 the applicant displayed no sign of neurosis or anxiety state. It is appropriate here to say that in his answers to the questionnaire and to Dr. Calanchini's questions the applicant appears to have acted with a candid honesty that does him the greatest credit. He seemed to be rather bitter about it, as several other people known to him apparently did not so act and profited thereby. As far as the Tribunal is concerned we simply add that he is entitled to be praised for what he did. But in any event he did not really contest this aspect of the case, and although we have reached our conclusion independently of any concession by him, that conclusion is nevertheless thereby fortified.

We turn now to consider what was the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement.

The applicant ran into quite a deal of difficulty in his work. He commenced with Telecom Australia (then still the PMG) in 1970. Prior to this he had been a building foreman on construction sites in the north-west of Western Australia. He had applied for a job as a mail sorter. He told the employment officer about his former alcoholism, was asked if he would work in the stores branch and, having said that he would, started work immediately. Eighteen months later he was asked to transfer to Port Melbourne, where the work was quite different. Instead of working with three men he was now working with 80. He was made a senior storeman and then a store supervisor. It was plain from his evidence that although there had been some difficulties in his work environment arising from high staff turnover, the real difficulty for him was that he was continually being sought out, by people who knew his background, for help with their problems with alcohol and it is obvious that this placed a very real strain upon him. He explained this more than once but the following passage in his evidence seems to emphasise the point: "I found I liked the work. The only trouble I found at work was people trying to make use of me and the knowledge I had of voluntary institutions and my ability of knowing people in public life to be able to help them out. This was the greatest problem I found at work. It became such a worry that I had to get away from there. My home life was being disrupted by this. I did not know what it was to get a full night's sleep so I asked for a transfer and I was transferred here to Little Bourke Street in the city where I was quite happy until I went away on leave in 1974, and out of the blue had a coronary occlusion which I have seen many doctors about." He was in hospital for a week and off work for four weeks. The applicant is convinced that he had a serious heart attack and said that he often had angina pectoris which he put down to heavy lifting, which he had to do at work. Because of the lifting he decided that he should go for a medical examination with a view to retirement. Before this he had been consulting a Dr. Hepper. Dr. Hepper did not give evidence but the applicant agreed that Dr. Hepper had been treating him for depression over a period of 16 months up to November 1977 "following the infarct in 1974" as it was put in a certificate given by Dr. Hepper that was before us.

We are quite satisfied that the applicant is absolutely genuine in his belief that he has, and had, a heart condition that now affects him and which in 1977 led to his retirement. But we regret that we are unable to accept that it did so lead. In the first place, on his own evidence the applicant, who now lives in the country, walks 15 miles a day, "may be more". He "can ride a bike 10 miles, no problems". He goes jogging round the neighbourhood. Asked what he cannot do, he said: "If I hurry myself all of a sudden I am likely to black-out; if I lift anything heavy suddenly; if I was to bend down and keep bending down for 5 minutes I am likely to black-out, and have a pain in my heart. I could probably do heavy physical work for about 20 minutes or half an hour but that is my limit". We feel bound to say that, despite the strong views which the applicant entertains about the seriousness of his heart condition, the amount of physical exercise of which he is capable in our opinion rules out any marked restriction of his cardio-respiratory performance or reserve. It is much more likely that his anxious disposition has caused him to dwell unduly upon the possibility of heart trouble. In other words, we are disposed to think that the pains which he has are really not pathological but have, and had, a neurotic basis.

That this is so was the view of Dr. Sullivan, who gave evidence. He is a senior medical officer at the Commonwealth Department of Health, and examined the applicant, in connection with his retirement, on 17 January 1977. On examination, the applicant told Dr. Sullivan that he had been taking tryptanol and anginine. He was having difficulty in tolerating the pressures in the stores area where he worked. There seemed to be many unhappy inter-personal enmities. In January 1974 he had had a coronary episode and was off duty over five weeks. Dr. Sullivan estimated that he had a good exercise tolerance because he told him that he could walk up to 15 miles. Dr. Sullivan recorded his observations that the applicant was a hyper-kinetic type, with a thin frame, and had a mild facial tic. Examination of heart, lungs and abdomen did not reveal anything abnormal. His blood pressure was 160/100. He recommended retirement "as an anxiety neurosis". He felt that as at the date of the examination his cardiac condition was such that he was quite capable of performing his duties, and that but for the anxiety neurosis there would have been no question of retiring him. He felt that the length of the applicant's hospitalisation in 1974 was indicative that he had suffered a mild lesion only.

We accept Dr. Sullivan's conclusions in the matter. We are bound to conclude, having regard to the evidence before us that the applicant had a severe neurosis, as Dr. Sullivan put it, that his worries about his health have become quite out of proportion to his actual physical disability, and that this was so at the point when he retired.

It follows that we endorse the conclusion to which the Commissioner for Superannuation came, namely that the incapacity, which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant, was his anxiety state at the time, and that the incapacity was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate.

In our opinion the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, acting pursuant to S.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 to confirm upon reconsideration the decision taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 4 October 1977 that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 15 June 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 was correct, and should be affirmed.
