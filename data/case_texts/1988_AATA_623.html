De Marchi and Commissioner for Superannuation [1988] AATA 623 (24 August 1988)

De Marchi and Commissioner for Superannuation [1988] AATA 623 (24 August 1988)

Last Updated: 2 July 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Superannuation - retirement on ground of invalidity before maximum retirement age - whether pre-existing condition specified in benefit classification certificate - spondylosis caused or substantially contributed to incapacity resulting in early retirement

Superannuation Act 1976 s.66 (2)

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation No 411: decided on 30 September 1980 (note only at (1980) 3 ALN N1)

"caused or substantially contributed to"

"spondylosis"

REASONS FOR DECISION

24 August 1988 Mrs R.A. Balmford, Senior Member

Professor R.W. Webster, Member

Mr C.G. Woodard, Member

Introduction

  1. This is an application under section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent made on 5 March 1987 confirming a decision made on 28 July 1983 by another delegate, being a decision "to be of the opinion under section 66(2)(c) of the Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Demarchi's [sic] invalidity retirement was caused or was substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate that was in force in respect of Mrs Demarchi [sic] immediately before her retirement, or by a condition, or conditions connected with that condition".
  2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") and numbered Tl/1 to T30/84 ("the T documents"), together with other material lodged at the hearing, to some of which it will be necessary to refer. Evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant, and by Dr Shaw and Dr Jamieson, both of whom are general surgeons, Dr Love, an orthopaedic surgeon, and Dr McInnes, a Senior Commonwealth Medical Officer. The applicant was represented by Mr De Marchi, of De Marchi and Associates, solicitors, and the respondent by Mr Whithear of the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office. One other medical practitioner who had prepared a report on the applicant's condition at the request of the respondent gave evidence before the Tribunal, but, for reasons which were apparent at the hearing, the Tribunal ruled that the whole of that practitioner's evidence, both written and oral, would be disregarded. Neither Mr De Marchi nor Mr Whithear sought to contest this ruling.

3. Sub-section 66(2) of the Act reads, so far as relevant;

"(2) ... where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled [to substantially reduced benefits]."

Initial Findings of Fact

4. Mrs De Marchi, who was born in 1925, joined the Postmaster-General's Department in 1973, her duties involving cleaning tables, serving in a canteen, kitchen work, and operating a tea trolley.

  1. In 1974 she slipped while loading a dishwasher and suffered pain in her left hip. She was off work for one or two weeks, and then returned, with no residual pain. In 1976 she strained her back lifting a trolley, and was off work for two or three weeks. She said at the hearing that she had had occasional residual pain after that episode.
  2. She became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 18 January 1979, by virtue of a permanent appointment with the Australian Telecommunications Commission ("Telecom"), the successor of the Postmaster-General's Department.
  3. Following a medical examination pursuant to sub-section 16(2) of the Act, the respondent on 24 October 1979 issued a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") in respect of Mrs De Marchi specifying the conditions of overweight, hypertension, spondylosis, bronchitis, and history of pinched nerve right wrist.
  4. On 22 January 1982 Mrs De Marchi slipped on a wet floor while holding a heavy tray and suffered pain in her back. After a considerable period of sick leave, she was examined on 28 February 1983 by Dr McInnes, who reported that he considered that she was "unfit for further employment and should be retired on grounds of invalidity". Mrs De Marchi was retired on invalidity grounds with effect from 17 March 1983, that being before she attained her maximum retiring age in terms of paragraph 66(2)(a).
  5. According to a report before the Tribunal from Mrs De Marchi's then general practitioner, Dr Kollios, she did not consult him until 16 February, when she complained of back pain, but gave no history of trauma precipitating the attack of pain. She subsequently returned to him on a number of occasions complaining of back pain, which already by 22 February was radiating into her right leg and continued to do so. In March Dr Kollios referred her to Dr Love, who saw her on a number of occasions, the last being in July 1986.

Discussion; the BCC

  1. Mr De Marchi was concerned to demonstrate that the applicant did not suffer from spondylosis in 1979, and that the BCC accordingly was incorrect insofar as it specified that condition, and should be varied accordingly. However the decision to issue the BCC is not before the Tribunal in these proceedings. The power of the Tribunal to review decisions of the respondent under the Act derives from section 154 of the Act, and is a power to review only those decisions which have been confirmed or varied under sub-section 154(4). At the time of hearing of this matter the decision to issue the BCC had not been so confirmed or varied, and accordingly it was not open to the Tribunal to review that decision. (Such a review would, in any case, have required the Tribunal to consider and determine an application under sub-section 29(7) of the AAT Act for extension of time to lodge the application for review.)
  2. Accordingly, our consideration of this matter must proceed on the basis that a BCC specifying the condition "spondylosis" was in force in respect of Mrs De Marchi immediately before her retirement in terms of paragraph 66(2)(b). We note, however, that Dr Love said, in his report of 13 April 1986:

"In view of the fact that degenerative changes were present on the x-rays that I saw when first consulted in March of 1982, her condition is likely to have existed for some time prior to that. No time frame can be put on it but it would be a matter of years.

It is therefore reasonable to assume that the degenerative condition of her spine existed prior to her entry into the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund in 1979."

At the hearing he said, commenting on that statement, "I am guessing, but I would say you are looking at a period of over 10 years or so".

Discussion: The Incapacity

  1. Given that the requirements of paragraphs 66(2) (a) and (b) are met, in order to ascertain whether paragraph 66(2)(c) applies to Mrs De Marchi, the first question to determine is, what was the incapacity which was the ground for her retirement. No formal document effecting that retirement was before the Tribunal, but the accuracy of the statement in the respondent's Reasons for Decision that she was retired on invalidity grounds with effect from 17 March 1983 was not challenged. As has been said the retirement followed a medical examination of Mrs De Marchi on 28 February 1983 by Dr Mclnnes. At that date Mrs De Marchi had been on sick leave since 22 January 1982.
  2. Dr Mclnnes said in his report, prepared on a standard medical report form, under the heading "Brief history of the condition(s) including origins and the time first noted by contributor":

"Injured back when she slipped and fell at work on 22/1/82. Has had continuous pain in back and Right thigh since. Anti-inflammatory medication caused dyspepsia and had to be stopped. Had mild pain since straining back lifting in 1976. X-rays show degenerative changes.

0/E overweight woman. Back - tender over lower lumbar and sacroiliac areas. Movements slow and restricted. SLR slightly restricted on both sides. BP 155/90. Heart, Lungs, Abdomen normal. Varicose veins and slight oedema both legs."

And under "Prognosis":

"Unlikely to improve. There are underlying degenerative changes."

He ticked the box opposite the statement: "I consider that the examinee is unfit for continued employment and should be retired on grounds of invalidity". In a letter to Telecom dated 1 March 1983 he said, after reporting that he had examined Mrs De Marchi on 28 February 1983 and referring Telecom to an earlier examination of her of 25 November 1982: "It is now evident that she is permanently incapacitated for work, and I have recommended her retirement."

  1. Dr McInnes' report of the examination of 25 November 1982 was to the effect that Mrs De Marchi was suffering from chronic low back strain due, on the balance of probability, to the fall of 22 January 1982, that she was at present unfit for duty as a cafeteria attendant, and that she should be reviewed in three months.
  2. No medical report other than those of Dr McInnes appears to have been obtained in connection with Mrs De Marchi's retirement. The space on the later medical report form for completion as a Recommendation of the Department of Health has been completed "Retirement recommended", signed by Dr McInnes as "Advisor to the Commissioner for Superannuation" and dated 1 March 1983. The conclusion is inescapable that the retirement was based on Dr McInnes' reports and recommendation, and we so find.
  3. The Tribunal, presided over by Mr Todd, Senior Member, as he then was, said in Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 411: decided on 30 September 1980) (note only at (1980) 3 ALN N1) at paragraph 22:

"The phrase 'incapacity which was the ground for ... retirement' is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. It would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between 'incapacity' and a '-condition' is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions. We understand Dr Kay's difficulty with the phrase 'coronary insufficiency' as having been that he did not regard it as a disease process. In Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th ed, coronary insufficiency is defined as 'Prolonged precordial pain or discomfort without conventional evidence of myocardial infarction; subendocardial ischemia due to a disparity between coronary blood flow and myocardial needs'. It is thus a result, a symptom, which is precisely what Dr Kay meant when he declined to regard coronary insufficiency as a disease. But it is we think appropriate to regard it as having constituted the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. Likewise, coronary sclerosis may on the evidence be aptly regarded as having been the physical condition which caused or substantially contributed to the coronary insufficiency."

17. In the present case, the incapacity apparent from Dr McInnes' report was continuous pain in Mrs De Marchi's back and right thigh. That was, to adopt the words of the Tribunal in Burr, "the outward result" that led to her "being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform [her] ... duties". As we have found, the decision to retire Mrs De Marchi was based on Dr McInnes' report. Thus we find that the incapacity which was the ground of Mrs De Marchi's retirement, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act, was continuous pain in the back and right thigh.

Discussion; caused or substantially contributed to

  1. The next question is whether, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c), that incapacity was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition "spondylosis" (being a condition specified in the BCC) or by a physical or mental condition connected with the condition "spondylosis". It was not suggested that any other of the conditions specified in the BCC was relevant to this matter.
  2. Dr McInnes in his report following the medical examination of 28 February 1983 inserted "Low Back Strain" under the Heading "Condition" and "100 per cent" under the heading "Percentage". The text of the form preceding those headings was illegible in the copy before the Tribunal, but from experience of other matters in this jurisdiction the Tribunal believes that it read: "I consider that the examinee is suffering from the following medical condition(s) (in order of severity) and with estimated percentage each contributes to the incapacity". In his letter of 1 March 1983 to Telecom Dr McInnes said:

"It is considered that the condition ‘chronic low back strain' resulting from the incident of 22nd January 1982, contributed materially to the necessity for retirement."

  1. None of the specialist medical witnesses, however, described the condition in terms of "low back strain".
  2. There was some discussion at the hearing about the meaning to be attributed to the word "spondylosis" appearing on the BCC as specifying a condition. An extract, described as being from Dorland's Medical Dictionary, included in the T documents, defines "spondylosis" simpliciter, without any preceding adjective, as "ankylosis of a vertebral joint; also a general term for degenerative changes due to osteoarthritis".
  3. Dr Shaw considered the definition above from Dorland's Medical Dictionary to be imprecise, and distinguished between spondylosis, as being properly defined as degenerative changes in the secondary cartilaginous joints of the spine, and osteoarthritis, being changes in the synovial joints. He concluded "It is perhaps being a bit pedantic".
  4. Dr Jamieson said, and Dr McInnes agreed, that spondylosis was "not a precisely defined condition" and that "spondylosis, spondylitis, osteoarthritis are commonly used interchangeably".
  5. Dr Love, the only specialist orthopaedic surgeon whose evidence was before the Tribunal, said "you could use the words osteoarthritis, spondylosis and degeneration interchangeably". It was reasonable to assume that a person over the age of 50 would have some spondylosis present. Later asked whether Mrs De Marchi's present back condition was "spondylosis or something else", he said "spondylosis is a reasonable term. It is a term of what one would say is the diagnostically destitute. That is, because we do not have the technology to define with the precision we would like, that is the best diagnosis we can come up with".
  6. The clinical notes of the doctor who examined Mrs De Marchi at the medical examination in 1979 referred to in paragraph 7 supra were before the Tribunal and include the following: "strained back (heavy lifting) - slight pain persists - no treatment now". At the time of that examination, Mrs De Marchi was 54 years old.
  7. Having considered this evidence, the Tribunal finds that "spondylosis" as used on the BCC is used as a general word referring to degenerative changes in the spine.
  8. Each of the specialist medical witnesses saw both the spondylosis and the trauma of the fall in January 1982 as contributing to the incapacity which led to Mrs De Marchi's retirement, namely continuous pain in the back and right thigh (paragraph 17 supra). They varied in the amount of significance which they attributed to each of those components.
  9. Dr Jamieson said in his report of 25 March 1988 "Certainly the previous spondylitis would to some extent predispose her spine to damage from the fall but it would seem that the main precipitating factor for the pain problem was the fall itself"; and in his report of 29 March 1988 that the spondylosis "would have played only a very minor role in the symptoms which precipitated her retirement". At the hearing he said "you cannot say that the previous spondylosis did not in some way contribute to her having the disc prolapse".
  10. Dr Shaw said at the hearing that there were obviously the two processes, degeneration and trauma involved; it was a question of degree. He added that [he] "would have thought on the balance of probabilities that it was more likely that the trauma was more significant than the degeneration".
  11. Dr Love said in his report of 13 April that he had expressed the view, when treating Mrs De Marchi, that "it was likely that she had an aggravation of a pre-existing degenerative disorder of the lumbar spine and that this aggravation had occurred due to the fall at work". At the hearing, asked to comment on Dr Shaw's report, he said:

"Well, the last paragraph of the second paragraph of page 5 says, 'Thus I believe that it was trauma which was responsible for the injury'. He has not quite said what he means by the injury in that sentence. 'The contribution of the previous existing sponylitic degeneration was not a substantial contribution'. I do not quite agree with that. I think the contribution of the spondylitic degeneration is significant in that it laid the ground work or it laid a set of circumstances present upon which trauma could have a much worse effect. That is, if you have already degenerative part and you apply force to it, it will fail more easily than a part that has not previously been rendered degenerative."

  1. Dr Love was, as has been said, the only orthopaedic specialist among the medical witnesses. Further, he treated Mrs De Marchi for a period of 12 months commencing shortly after the fall in January 1982, and formed his views at that time, and in that context. Dr Shaw and Dr Jamieson, on the other hand, examined Mrs De Marchi only for the purposes of these proceedings, Dr Shaw in October 1987 and Dr Jamieson in March 1988. All of these witnesses depended on Mrs De Marchi's account of the fall to enable them to measure its severity and thus the proportional extent of its contribution, vis a vis the spondylosis, to the incapacity which was the ground for her retirement. In March 1982, when Dr Love obtained his history, the fall and its consequences were recent, and Mrs De Marchi's recollection of them is likely to have been more accurate than when she came to describe them four to five years later, and in the context of these proceedings. We note that it was three weeks after the fall before Mrs De Marchi consulted Dr Kollios, and that when she consulted him she gave no history of trauma precipitating the pain.
  2. For these reasons, given the conflict in the medical evidence, we prefer the view of Dr Love as to the contribution of the spondylosis to the view of the other two specialist witnesses. On the basis of the evidence before us, we find that the incapacity, namely continuous pain in Mrs De Marchi's back and right thigh, which was the ground of her retirement, was substantially contributed to by the condition "spondylosis" specified in the BCC. Accordingly, the decision under review will be affirmed.
