Bailey and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 215 (24 December 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007

Superannuation - determination of retirement benefits -benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force -physical or mental conditions specified - whether incapacity caused by condition specified in Certificate

Superannuation Act 1922, s5

Superannuation Act 1976, s 16,66,154,184

ARCHIBALD KEITH BAILEY

THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No.123 of 1979

AAT Decision No 352

Mr A.M. Hall (Senior Member)

Mr V.J. Skermer (Member)

Dr A.H. Marsh (Member)

24 December 1979

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. 123 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: ARCHIBALD KEITH BAILEY

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made on 20 September 1979 and in substitution for that decision the Tribunal decides that the benefit entitlement of the applicant on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 11 October 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined by section 66(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DATED this 24th day of December 1979.

Sgd. A. N. Hall

(A. N. Hall)

Senior Member

Sgd. V. J.Skermer

(V. J. Skermer)

Member

Dr A. Marsh

(Dr A. Marsh)

Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No 123 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: ARCHIBALD KEITH BAILEY

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

In this application the applicant seeks review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made on his behalf by his delegate on 28 October 1977 and confirmed by the Commissioner on reconsideration under section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") that the benefit entitlement of the applicant on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 11 October 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under section 66(2) of the Act. The Commissioner's decision was based on his opinion that the applicant's incapacity which was the ground for his retirement "was substantially contributed to by the mental condition chronic anxiety and that this condition was connected with nervous condition, the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate deemed to have been in existence immediately before Mr Bailey's retirement on 11 October 1977".

For a full discussion of the quite complex statutory provisions relating to benefit classification certificates see Re Harrison and the Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 78/12024). Suffice it to say that it was not disputed, in this application, that the applicant was a contributor to the Provident Account established under the Superannuation Act 1922 immediately before the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976 on 1 July 1976; that his period of contributory service was then less than 20 years and that he had not then attained his maximum retiring age. He was, therefore, a person to whom section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 applied and, by force of that sub-section there was "deemed to be in force", on 1 July 1976, in respect of the applicant "a benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner under sub-section 16(4) specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act" (ie. to the superannuation fund established under the 1922 Act).

It was also not disputed on the evidence before us that as the applicant had ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age and as there was in force in respect of the applicant, immediately before his retirement, a benefit classification certificate, the provisions of subsection 66(2) of the Act were potentially applicable to the applicant subject to the Commissioner or, on review, this Tribunal, being "of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his (i.e. the applicant's) retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition or such conditions" (see s66(2)(c)).

Finally, there was no dispute on the evidence before us that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's early retirement was contributed to in the percentages indicated below by the following mental or physical conditions from which the applicant was found to be suffering on examination by Dr M.C. Clarke, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, on 7 October 1977:-

Chronic Anxiety - 90%

Cardiac arrhythmia - 5% . (P.A.T.)

Late onset diabetes 5% (mild)

The two issues which were in contention, however, were:-

(i) what physical or mental conditions should be taken to be specified in the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force by virtue of section 184(2) of the Act; and

(ii) whether the chronic anxiety state which was the substantial ground for the applicant's early retirement "was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition or such conditions" within the meaning of s66 (2)(c) of the Act.

The Tribunal had before it as Exhibits T1 to T11, the Commissioner's reasons for decision and findings of fact, the Application for Review, the documents lodged with the Tribunal by the Commissioner and the correspondence between the Tribunal Registry and the parties. Included within Exhibit T4 were copies of the medical records held and reports received by the Commonwealth Department of Health and the Department of Veterans' Affairs in relation to the applicant, together with an extract from the relevant medical standard applicable at the time of the applicant's re-entry into the Public Service in March 1972. We also had before us as Exhibit 1 a copy of a letter to the Commonwealth Medical Officer dated 5 October 1977 from Dr D. Armstrong, the applicant's doctor, and as Exhibit 2 a copy of a letter dated 15 November 1967 to the Commonwealth Medical Officer from Dr J.C. Martin, who was then attending the applicant, together with copies of the medical record cards held by the Department of Health in relation to the applicant.

Mr T. Higgins, who appeared on behalf of the applicant did not call any evidence in support of the applicant's case. He relied on the documentary evidence before the Tribunal and upon his cross-examination of Dr M.C. Clarke who was the only witness called by Mr F Purnell on behalf of the Commissioner.

The documents disclosed that the applicant, who is now 55 years of age, was first accepted into the Provident Fund established under the 1922 Act following a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 10 December 1954. The notes of that examination disclose that the applicant was recommended for the Provident Account "because of nervous history". The applicant continued in the Provident Account until some date in 1970 or 1971 when he resigned from the Public Service.

On or about 17 February 1972, however, he reentered the Public Service and, following a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 20 March, 1972, he was again recommended for the Provident Account. Pursuant to s5 of the 1922 Act, the Superannuation Board through its delegate accepted the applicant as a contributor to that account by a determination in writing dated 5 April, 1972. No reasons for the decision were recorded in the determination and there is no other evidence before us to establish directly what the reasons were, notwithstanding that section 184 (2) of the Act clearly assumes that those reasons will be ascertainable.

Where in the medical report relating to a person who has applied for permanent appointment to the Public Service, the section at the end headed "Reasons for Recommendation and Remarks" clearly identifies a mental or physical condition or conditions which may lead to that person's early retirement and where the Superannuation Board or its delegate has accepted that recommendation without comment, it is proper, in our view, to accept that the Board has adopted the reasons given by the Commonwealth Medical Officer in support of his recommendation. This was the view which the Tribunal adopted implicitly, if not explicitly, in Harrison's Case (supra). The problem in the present case, however, is that the "Reasons for Recommendation" in the Medical Report are not clearly expressed.

What the medical report dated 20 March 1972 in relation to the applicant disclosed under "Reasons for Recommendation and Remarks" was as follows:-

"Was in provident account previously. Various disabilities - the important one of these is the stomach - at age 48 he should be fit to work for the next ten years."

It appears that the report was signed by Dr George Spence and that the recommendation was concurred in by Dr Isaacs who was then the Senior Commonwealth Medical Officer. Annexed to Dr Spence's medical report was a letter dated 10 March, 1972, from the Deputy Commissioner, Repatriation Department, Sydney, addressed to the applicant. Omitting formal parts the letter was as follows:-

"With reference to your request the disabilities determined as due to your war service are:-,

Due to War Service Gun Shot Wound Left Elbow Involving Ulna Nerve

Gun Shot Wound Abdomen and Left Hip Effects of Wounds (loss of weight and weakness) Bilateral Otitis Media Perceptive Deafness Nervous Condition

Duodenal Ulcer Cervical Spondylosis Haemorrhoids "

It is clear that this letter was available to Dr Spence at the time of his examination of the applicant as there are numerous references to the letter in the applicant's answers to questions regarding his personal medical history. It is also clear that Dr Spence was aware of the applicant's previous employment in the Public Service as the applicant disclosed in answer to question 20 on the Medical Report that he was previously a contributor to the Provident Account. We take it that Dr Spence had available to him the medical records held by the Department of Health relating to the applicant's previous medical history as a Commonwealth employee.

The letter from the Repatriation Department listed "nervous condition" as one of the applicant's disabilities due to war service. In addition, the medical record cards held by the Department of Health relating to the applicant disclosed that he had been diagnosed by a Commonwealth Medical Officer as suffering from "anxiety state" following a medical examination on 21 May, 1954, and that he had been certified as unfit for work from 20 May, 1954 to 4 June, 1954. As a result of this "nervous history" the applicant had been recommended for acceptance into the Provident Fund following a further medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 10 December, 1954.

Prior to the applicant's resignation from the Public Service in 1970, or 1971 the only other reference in medical records held by the Department of Health to any history of "nervous disability" was in the notes of a medical examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 23 November 1967 in relation to a request by the applicant that he be given a sedentary job. This request was supported by Dr JC Martin in a letter dated 15 November 1967 in which Dr Martin stated that he had known the applicant professionally for 12 years. He referred to the applicant' s symptoms and complaints as backache, fatigue and diarrhoea and as "relevant knowledge" referred, to "loss of weight and weakness, also quoted by Repatriation as 'effects of wounds'" and to "G.S.W. (sc. gunshot wound) abdomen involved some bowel resection and I know that he has periodic attacks of diarrhoea presumably due to this". Dr Martin agreed with Dr A Merrifield a practicing psychiatrist to whom he had referred the applicant for psychiatric assessment "that there is an element of basic personality disorder". He said that, in his opinion, this would account for only 20-30% of the applicant's fatigue state. He added that he "would regard the remainder of his condition as of organic origin". The clinical notes recorded by the Commonwealth Medical Officer following his examination of the applicant concluded:-

"I agree condition is one of nervous disability - a large organic element due to gross GSW involving abdomen, bowel, thigh and eardrum. He is unfit for a job involving much walking or standing but is fit for a more sedentary light job."

Whether Dr Spence in fact referred to these medical records we do not know, but it is clear that the Repatriation Department's letter put him on notice that the applicant had an accepted disability referred to as "nervous condition" and it is clear that he had access to the reasons which caused the applicant to be accepted into the Provident Account in 1954 and to his subsequent medical history so far as the Department of Health records went.

It is puzzling, therefore, why the applicant should have written "No" in answer to the question - "Have you now or have you previously had any of the following conditions?

(a)

(b)Mental or nervous conditions.
(c)Anxiety state.
(d).."

and even more puzzling why Dr Spence did not question the correctness of those answers. Indeed there is nothing in the Medical Report to indicate that Dr Spence directed his attention to any previous history of nervous condition or, if he did, that he considered it to be of any consequence. His only comment regarding the applicant's mental condition was a note in paragraph 1 of the Medical Officer's Report that the applicant had "no obvious disability to general view. Mentally alert".

Despite the fact that Dr Spence made no specific reference to "nervous condition" as a reason for recommending that the applicant be accepted for the Provident Account, Mr Purnell submitted on behalf of the Commissioner that the statement by Dr Spence - "was in provident account previously" should be seen as incorporating by inference the ground upon which the applicant was accepted into that account in 1954, namely "nervous history". The other statements under the heading "Reasons for Recommendation and Remarks" - namely "Various disabilities - the important one of these is the stomach - at age 48 he should be fit to work for the next ten years" - should, in Mr Purnell's submission, be seen only as "remarks" not "reasons".

We find ourselves quite unable to accept these submissions. In our view, a fair reading of the doctor's first statement conveys no more than the factual information that the applicant was previously a contributor to the Provident Account. It is in the nature of a "remark" rather than a "reason". But if we are wrong in that conclusion we think that the absence of any other evidence in the medical report to indicate that Dr Spence specifically investigated whether the ground of "nervous history" which had caused the applicant to be accepted as a contributor to the Provident Acc6unt in 1954 was still relevant militates strongly against Mr Purnell' s submission that "nervous condition" was necessarily in Dr Spence's mind when he referred to the Provident Account. Still less, in our view, is that reference capable of being seen as conveying unequivocally to the Superannuation Board that the ground which caused the applicant's exclusion from the superannuation fund in 1954 automatically continued to apply as a ground for his exclusion from the superannuation fund in 1972.

The second statement, on the other hand, reflects, in our opinion, the reasons for the doctor's recommendations. His reference to "various disabilities" is arguably wide enough to incorporate by reference all the war-caused disabilities set out in the annexed letter from the Repatriation Department including "nervous condition". But the question remains whether that is the proper construction to apply in all the circumstances.

In a case such as the present, where there is no direct evidence to establish the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, it appears to us that the proper test to apply when considering the material before the Board when it reached its decision is - what condition or conditions must have influenced the Board to accept the person into the Provident Account rather than the Fund. Unless, on a fair consideration of that material, the Tribunal can feel satisfied that a particular physical or mental condition was, in fact, a reason which influenced the Board's decision, the Tribunal would not, in our view, be Justified in finding as a fact that the condition should be taken as being specified in the benefit classification certificate which is deemed, by section 184 (2) of the Act, to be in force in respect of the applicant.

Applying this test to the material before the Board, we can see no ground on which the Board could have inferred that the "nervous condition" referred to in the Repatriation Department's letter concerned Dr Spence in the slightest degree. Of the various war caused disabilities listed by that Department, Dr Spence makes it quite plain that "the important one of these is the stomach" (emphasis added), by which we think he must be taken as referring to the "gunshot wound to the abdomen" and, possibly to the duodenal ulcer. Our conclusion in this regard is reinforced by the fact that in relation to each of the war caused disabilities which the applicant disclosed by a "Yes" answer in the questionnaire relating to his personal medical history, Dr Spence obviously satisfied himself as to the present state of that disability and recorded his comments. Thus, Dr Spence noted (inter alia) that the applicant had had "no trouble for more than six months now" in relation to his duodenal ulcer; that he had "no trouble now - previous history only" in respect of haemorrhoids; and that, in respect of his hearing defects, the applicant "manages general sounds well". In answer to question 19 - "Have you ever applied for or received a war service pension, been retired on invalidity grounds under the DFRB Act or under any other scheme? Please give details.", the applicant replied - "Yes. 100% G.R. Pension Repat.". Dr Spence's comment in relation to this answer was - "Not for any disability which would cause deterioration of health".

The absence of any comment opposite the applicant's "No" answer with respect to "mental or nervous conditions" and "anxiety state" is, in our view, capable of only two explanations - either that Dr Spence inadvertently overlooked the apparent inconsistency between those answers and the "nervous condition" listed as a war -caused disability, or, more probably, that he did not consider the matter of sufficient importance to comment upon. If, in his assessment, "nervous condition" was an important feature of the applicants prior medical history which was likely to lead to his early retirement it is, in our view, unthinkable that Dr Spence would have failed to make some express reference to that fact. The absence of any such reference on the face of the report is, in our view, very significant. Perusal of Dr Spence's clinical record card which was tendered as part of Exhibit 2 gives no ground for concluding that this omission was due to inadvertence.

We had before us as part of Exhibit T4 an extract from the medical standards applied by Commonwealth Medical Officers in the assessment of fitness of applicants for permanent appointment to the Public Service as in force at the time when the applicant re-entered the service in March 1972. In relation to neuropsychopathies, paragraph 100 provided guidelines to assist Commonwealth Medical Officers in assessing whether candidates who had suffered from or shown symptoms of neuropsychopathies e.g. nervous, emotional or mental illnesses, should be excluded from contributing for the superannuation pension or to the Provident Account. It was emphasised that neuropsychopathies are a common cause of prolonged sick leave leading to early retirement and that "experience in this respect has been most unfavourable." The guidelines make it clear that, except in the case of applicants suffering from chronic or recurring psychotic or psychoneurotic illnesses or who manifest definite evidence of chronic personality disorders, the Commonwealth Medical Officers were allowed some discretion in how they assessed an applicant who had suffered from or shown symptoms of neuropsychopathies. Whilst it is clear that Dr Spence should have been aware of these provisions when assessing the fitness of the applicant, there is nothing on the face of his report to suggest that he applied his mind specifically to paragraph 100 of the medical standard and there is no basis therefore on which we think it can be properly inferred that the Superannuation Board must have reached the conclusion that paragraph 100 of the medical standards was relevant to the applicant.

So far as the evidence before us discloses, the Superannuation Board must have accepted Dr Spence's recommendation. Despite the list of war caused disabilities annexed to Dr Spence's report we think it is clear, from a consideration of his recommendation in the light of the comments elsewhere in the report, that of the various disabilities referred to in the report and in the letter from the Repatriation Department the only condition which Dr Spence saw fit to comment upon as important was the disability to the applicant's stomach due to the gunshot wounds. In the circumstances we think the only proper inference to draw is that the Board must have accepted Dr Spence's recommendation in the terms in which it was expressed by Dr Spence. The consequence of this conclusion is that, in our view, the only condition which can be taken to be specified in the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force by virtue of section 184(2) of the Act is the disability to the applicant's stomach.

This finding concludes this application for review in favour of the applicant as it is clear on the evidence before us that the chronic anxiety state which followed the applicant's collapse at work in November 1976 from paroxysmal atrial tachycardia was in no way caused by or substantially contributed to by the disability to the applicant's stomach nor by any condition connected with the disability to the stomach.

Before concluding, we should mention that Mr Higgins submitted, as his primary argument on behalf of the applicant, that even if one assumed that the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force in relation to the applicant included a reference to "nervous condition", the chronic anxiety state which was the substantial contributing factor to the applicant's retirement in October 1977 could not be seen as causally related to whatever nervous condition the applicant had suffered from as a consequence of the gunshot wounds to his stomach. However, the only expert medical evidence before us on this question was that of Dr Clarke who said that, in his opinion, the two conditions were causally related, because "an anxiety state is an anxiety state". In other words, as we understood his evidence, although an anxiety state may be triggered by an infinite variety of causes, a person who has suffered from an anxiety state (albeit a mild anxiety state) due to worrying over his gunshot wounds is likely to develop a chronic anxiety state given a serious disruption to his health such as a heart attack. Dr Clarke, however, had only seen the applicant on one occasion and then only for half an hour. He had not, prior to the hearing, had access to the Repatriation Department's medical records nor to the full details of Dr Merrifield's reports relating to the applicant. Had it been necessary for us to resolve the question, therefore, we may have felt it advisable to require some evidence to be given by Dr Merrifield, in view of his long association with the applicant, before reaching any concluded view on this very difficult question.

In this regard it will be recalled that on the 21 May 1954 the applicant had been diagnosed by a Commonwealth Medical Officer as suffering from an anxiety state and had been certified as unfit for work from the 20 May to 4 June 1954. The cause of that anxiety state was unclear from the clinical notes, although it appeared that it may have been related to disruption to the applicant's domestic relationships when he was transferred to Canberra and was obliged to live in a hostel leaving his wife and three children living in Yass. There was also the evidence of the medical examination of the applicant by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on the 23 November 1967 in which the Commonwealth Medical Officer agreed that the applicant's condition at that time was one of nervous disability with a "large organic element" due to the gross gunshot wounds (inter alia) to the abdomen which the applicant had suffered.

There was also placed before us, as part of Exhibit T4 and as Exhibit T6, medical records held by the Department of Veterans' Affairs (formerly Repatriation Department) in relation to the applicant. These records disclosed that the applicant had been admitted to a Repatriation Hospital from the 16th May to the 3rd June 1960. He was shown as having a history of being "nervous -some years". It was stated that he was admitted as complaining of having abdominal pains of some years standing and nervousness. The records of the Department of Veterans' Affairs also disclosed that the applicant was referred by Dr C.J. Martin to Dr A Merrifield on the 23 June 1967 for psychiatric assessment and report. Dr Merrifield reported that the applicant was a worrying personality. He expressed the opinion that the applicant did not seem to suffer from depression but only from anergia. Dr Merrifield stated that he felt the applicant's basic problem was "a personality disorder". This was the opinion of Dr Merrifield referred to in Dr Martin's letter dated 15 November 1967 (Exhibit 2) to which we earlier referred.

There was a reference to a further medical examination by the Repatriation Department on the 22 February 1968 and a specialist report by an unidentified specialist on the 19 November 1969. In the specialist's report the opinion was expressed that the applicant's symptoms "are compatible with a mild anxiety state". There is also evidence in a letter from Dr Merrifield dated 18 September 1978 (part of Exhibit T4) that apart from his initial examination of the applicant in 1967, Dr Merrifield had also seen the applicant in 1971 and 1978. However, no details were given of Dr Merrifield's assessment of the applicant in 1971.

This additional evidence was not before Dr Spence at the time when he conducted his medical examination on the 20 March 1972. The specialist's report dated 19 November 1969 does provide some evidence of re-occurrence of an anxiety state in the applicant. Had this more complete evidence been available to Dr Spence, it is conceivable that he may have attributed greater weight to the "nervous condition" referred to as a war caused disability. However that may be, we are not persuaded that the history of "nervous condition" can be seen on the face of Dr Spence's report to have been a significant factor in his assessment of the applicant. We fail to see, therefore, how it could fairly be inferred that it was a factor which must have influenced the Superannuation Board or its delegate.

Accordingly our decision is that the decision of the Commissioner be set aside and in substitution for that decision, the Tribunal decides that the benefit entitlement of the applicant on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 11 October 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined by section 66(1) of the Act.
