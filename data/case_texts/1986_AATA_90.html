Re Roy James Hastings and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 90 (7 April 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ROY JAMES HASTINGS

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V85/57

AAT No. 2612

Superannuation

COURT

HEARING

CANBERRA

7:4:1986

ORDER

The Tribunal considers that the Commissioner's decision should be set aside, and in lieu thereof, the Commissioner be directed to deem that the applicant had been retired on 15 August 1980 on the ground of physical incapacity to perform his duties.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent, who refused to direct that the applicant should be deemed to have been retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity pursuant to ss.7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) ("the Act").

2. At the hearing before the Tribunal, it became apparent that there were difficulties with arranging witnesses during the week which had been set aside for the hearing, and that a large number of medical witnesses were proposed to be called by both parties. It also became apparent that, depending on which interpretation of ss.7(2) of the Act the Tribunal adopted, there may be no need to call further witnesses, and the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the 'T documents') and one further medical report (Exhibit R1) would be sufficient to enable the Tribunal to properly review the decision. The following was therefore suggested by this Tribunal, and agreed to between the parties (pp. 67-71, 109-111, 139 and 151-152 of the transcript):

(1) The major issues between the parties were first,

the interpretation of the words "his duties" in

ss.7(2) of the Act and, secondly, whether the word

"may" in that sub-section was facultative or

whether it indicated a discretion was to be

exercised either in a broad or a limited manner. A

third related issue then arose; if there was a

discretion incorporated in ss.7(2), had that

discretion been properly exercised and was it the

preferable decision on the merits.

(2) If the words "his duties" were interpreted to mean

the duties of the applicant at the relevant time,

namely as a Senior Health Inspector, then there was

no need for either side to adduce further evidence,

as the delegate of the Commissioner had already

decided that the applicant was unfit for those

duties and there was no contest as to that finding.

On the other hand, if a broad interpretation of the

words "his duties" appeared to this Tribunal to be

appropriate, then the matter would have to be

referred back to the Commissioner.

(3) If there was no discretion incorporated into

ss.7(2), then again, there was no need for either

side to adduce further evidence. However, if there

was a discretion imported into ss.7(2), then the

matter could either have to be referred back to the

Commissioner or the Tribunal could request the

parties to call further evidence.

3. These procedural matters as agreed between the parties meant that the hearing could proceed in less time than the week allocated and with consequent cost savings to the parties. The hearing therefore proceeded with the 'T documents' and the additional medical report.

4. The background facts to the issues are that the applicant was born on the 30th day of August 1925. On 23 April 1971, after twelve years of service, he was retired from his position as a Clerk of the then Commonwealth Department of Excise and Customs on the ground of invalidity. The incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was ischaemic heart disease. He was granted a pension pursuant to s.67 of the Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth.) which, because he had not attained compulsory retiring age, was subject to periodic review.

5. Three such reviews were carried out on 26 April 1972, 25 October 1974 and 6 October 1975, but, as the reasons for the decision of the delegate state (T2/1):

"....it was decided to defer further review action

indefinitely because it appeared there had been

insufficient improvement in Mr Hastings' condition

since retirement to warrant continued monitoring of

his fitness."

6. The medical conditions adverted to in the three medical reviews included multiple conditions of not only angina, but pains in the right arm, right ankle, right knee, haemor rhoids, backache, breathlessness, dizziness, bronchitis, nerves and irritability over small matters, gall bladder and gross obesity. These medical reports recorded that the applicant had been employed at least part-time prior to October 1974, although his pension seems to have continued notwithstanding that notation.

7. The delegate, in his reasons for decision, continued:

"In October 1981 during the course of routine

examination of appointment medical papers for the

purpose of determining the need for the issue of

benefit classification certificates, it was establish

ed that Mr Hastings had been appointed to the

Department of Health, Northern Territory Public

Service, in Tennant Creek as a Senior Health

Inspector."

8. The applicant underwent a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr. Talbot, on 24 March 1980 to ascertain his health for the purpose of employment with the Northern Territory Public Service. At that examination, the applicant disclosed not only his heart condition, but also his nervous fatigue, mental or nervous condition, anxiety state, depression or difficulty sleeping, gall bladder disease, dizziness, pneumonia or pleurosy, bronchitis, arthritis and a few other disorders. In addition he mentioned his retirement on a pension. Whilst some of those disorders were undoubtedly past rather than present conditions, others were not. Dr. Talbot specifically noted that he could find no evidence of ischaemic heart disease and also noted the applicant's obesity, together with the information provided by the applicant that his angina was relieved by taking "Anginine" and weight reduction (T8/1-4). Dr. Talbot recommended that, although the applicant did not meet the general medical standard, he met the standard for a Senior Health Inspector with the Department of Health in the Northern Territory, but further recommended that the applicant should be re-examined in six months in order to determine whether the general medical standard had been met by that time.

9. The applicant was therefore appointed as the Senior Health Inspector with the Northern Territory Department of Health on 8 April 1980. The position was a permanent appointment (T9/1, T10/1) and s.3(5) of the Northern Territory Public Service Act provides that he was eligible for superannuation even though he was appointed on probation.

10. On 15 August 1980, after a period of a little over four months, the applicant's appointment, as stated by the Northern Territory Department of Health, was "annulled" pursuant to s.31(9) of the Public Service Act 1976 (N.T.). The reason given for this annulment was "conduct, diligence and efficiency" (T10/1). Section 36(9) of that Act, however, does not provide for "annulment" but provides that the Chief Executive Officer may "terminate" the appointment of a "probationer", that is, a person with less than six months' service. That inconsistency between the N.T. Act and the apparent annulment aside for the moment, in a subsequent letter written from the Northern Territory Depart ment of Health to the respondent dated 31 May 1982, it was stated (T17/1):

"Had Mr. Hasting's appointment not been annulled, he

would not have been retired on the grounds of

invalidity as his termination was not due to his

medical condition."

The applicant was subsequently informed by a letter dated 11 March 1982, (T11/1) that:

"2. The Department of Health, Alice Springs, has

advised that you were permanently appointed to the

Northern Territory Public Service on 8 Apr 1980. In

accordance with Section 76(1) of the Superannuation

Act 1976 you became an eligible employee on and from

that date and ceased to be entitled to receive

invalidity retirement benefits from that date. You

were required to contribute to the Superannuation Fund

on and from the first payday following 8 Apr 1980 at

the minimum rate of 5% of your salary for

Superannuation purposes.

3. The Department of Health, Alice Springs, has also

advised that your appointment was annulled on 15 Aug

1980 under Sub-section 31(9) of the Northern Territory

Public Service Act 1976 on conduct/diligence/ef

ficiency grounds. In these circumstances there is no

legislative provision under the Superannuation Act

1976 for the resumption of payment of invalidity

retirement benefits to which you were entitled prior

to 8 Apr 1980.

4. The consequence of your permanent appointment to

the Northern Territory Public Service on 8 Apr 1980 is

that you have been overpaid invalidity benefit since

that date. Your invalidity pension has been ceased

with effect from 1 Apr 1982. You will be contacted

shortly regarding the total amount of the overpayment

which you will be requested to repay."

This letter of the respondent concisely delineates the nub of the review. The second sentence in para. 3 implies that an officer eligible for invalidity benefits places the whole of his pension rights at risk by accepting another appointment which may be terminated during or after the probationary period.

11. Section 76(1) of the Act provides:

"Upon a person to whom invalidity pension is payable

again becoming an eligible employee, his entitlement

to that pension is, by force of this sub-section,

cancelled."

12. An "eligible employee" in s.3(1) of the definitions contained in the Act includes:

...

"(b) a person who is a permanent employee"

Sub-section 7(2) provides:

"7 (2) Where a person has, before attaining his

maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible

employee otherwise than by reason of death or by

reason of his having been retired, or his services

having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of

physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties,

but the Commissioner is satisfied that, at the time he

ceased to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason

of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his

duties, the Commissioner may direct that the person

shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have

been retired on the ground of that physical or mental

incapacity."

13. The applicant, by becoming employed as a permanent employee with the Northern Territory Department of Health, thereby became an "eligible employee" and his entitlement to his invalidity pension was cancelled. However, upon the termination of that employment he ceased to be an "eligible employee" on 15 August 1980. The issue then arises as to whether, pursuant to ss.7(2) of the Act, the applicant should be entitled to a resumption of his invalidity pension by virtue of the respondent deeming him to have "retired on the ground of ... physical or mental incapacity". The answer to this question rests in part on the interpretation of "his duties" and "may" in ss.7(2) as well as the exercise of any discretion, if it exists.

14. Returning to the background history, on 15 April 1980, seven days after the applicant commenced his job with the Northern Territory Department of Health, he alleged, as reported by the doctor submitting the report, that he had suffered two injuries at work, first when lifting jugs of water and second, when lifting chests (T27/1). As a result of these injuries, the applicant complained of pain in the lower back, ankle and the left side of his neck. He went on sick leave for a period of four days and then took a further four days on sick leave due to a respiratory condition (T17/6). The applicant then sought and obtained special leave to attend divorce proceedings in the Family Court on 13 May 1980. This special leave was to be from 13 to 22 May without pay (T17/4). However, on 17 May 1980, when in Adelaide, the applicant obtained certificates for unfitness for work from a Dr. Nicholas Johnson which certified unfitness for work up until 20 November 1980 (T28/9).

15. On 6 June 1980, the applicant saw Dr. Talbot and alleged that he had suffered an aggravation of his work injuries as a result of a car accident. Dr. Talbot examined him and noted (T17/2):

"Muscle injury left neck and back lifting samples a.m.

15/4/80, recurred pulling boxes p.m. 15/4/80, and

again packing 13/5/80.

Responding to therapy, ache right shoulder, sore left

neck, twinges low back. Fully mobile."

Dr. Talbot considered that the applicant's prognosis was "Good with therapy and future caution re excess strain" and the doctor considered that the applicant was "unfit for continued employment and should be granted further sick leave until 1 month then attend for review" (T17/2).

16. One month later, on 10 July 1980, the applicant again saw Dr. Talbot. Dr. Talbot noted (T17/9):

"Injury to existing Cervical Spondylosis 50%

Lumbar strain 10%

Capsulitis Right Shoulder 30%

Haemorrhoids 10%"

and further noted the conditions:

"1) Low back strain April 1980: no loss movement,

minimal discomfort, now

2) Wrenched neck April 1980: has cervical

degenerative changes ++, now full movement but

pain and tenderness soft tissues left C2-C5

3) Discomfort at present from intermittent

haemorrhoids, on therapy

4) Still discomfort from capsulitis right shoulder

5) Skin lesion right eyebrow"

Dr. Talbot's prognosis was "Fair with further attention to weight normalisation and further physiotherapy" and considered that the applicant was "fit for continued employment in the position presently occupied if avoids excess physical strain and continues therapy".

17. At the time of that report, the applicant was on sick leave because of a haemorrhoid condition for which he underwent an haemorrhoidectomy on 15 July 1980 (T15/1).

18. On 20 June 1980, the applicant's employers requested his probation report. That report included the applicant's unimpressive leave record over the four months of 46-1/4 days on sick leave and 9 days special leave without pay which left a total of only 16-3/4 days worked over that period. The report also contained a number of comments made about the applicant's adverse work relationships with staff and included comments such as (T17/8):

"He has shown he is not physically capable of

carrying out the duties - past injury"

and under 'Recommendation' said:

"An incorrigible situation:-

of working days between 8-4-80 to 7-7-80 he has worked

only 16-3/4 days and just caused so much disruption.

The rest of his days have been spent mainly in

Melbourne or Adelaide covered by 5 certificates of

sick leave whilst on leave with out pay. Two other

certificates cover him, whilst in A/S for an ilness

(sic) and a possible recurring injury of long

standing."

Also contained in that report was the statement "There are no other positions" and a further statement "I couldn't recommend for another section".

19. In addition to these medical reports and reports from the Northern Territory Department of Health, the delegate had before him psychiatric reports from Dr. Radeski and Dr. Earle Williams as well as the unreported Reasons for Decision given by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in relation to the applicant's workers' compensation application, (Re Roy James Hastings & The Northern Territory (1984) 5 ALN No.367) in which both Dr. Willims and Dr. Radeski gave evidence. Dr. Williams was of the view in August 1981 that the applicant had suffered from 'compensation neurosis' and considered him to be totally incapacitated whereas Dr. Radeski considered the applicant had no psychological illness at all. Mr. Ballard (Senior Member) in Re Hastings (supra) preferred the evidence of Dr. Radeski to that of Dr. Williams. The delegate, having regard to all of this information before him, stated at T2/11:

"I am satisfied that Mr Hastings was unfit to perform

the duties of a Senior Health Inspector at the time

his services were terminated."

On the material before us we agree with the conclusion of the delegate. We are further of the view that the finding of the delegate was justified on the basis of physical incapacity alone. In our opinion, there was no need to rely on the more con troversial psychiatric evidence to reach a conclusion as to incapacity.

20. Looking now at the first major issue in this review, being whether "his duties" in ss.7(2) refers to the applicant's duties in his employment as Senior Health Inspector or whether that phrase is to be interpreted more broadly as including duties which may have been allocated to the applicant by the Northern Territory Department of Health, such duties being "sedentary duties" (T2/12) or "light duties" (T2/13) as referred to by the delegate of the Commissioner.

21. In considering the interpretation of this phrase, the Tribunal notes that the wording used in ss.7(2) is different from the wording used in both State and Federal workers' compensation legislation which refers, in various ways, to "incapacity for work", as distinct from "incapacity to perform his duties" as appears in ss.7(2). (Tribunal underlining)

22. "Incapacity for work" has been interpreted as encompassing both the particular employment (including "duties") performed by a worker, as well as the broad range of employment reasonably available to a worker. In that context, the use of the words "his duties", when compared with "work", appears to be more limited. It is reasonable to infer, because of that well- known use of the phrase "incapacity for work", that a different concept was intended as being appropriate for this Act. It is the use of the word "his" in the phrase "his duties", that personalises and limits the duties to being those performed by the worker in his job at the time, as distinct from being "duties" in a general sense.

23. We do not think that much hangs on the use of the word "duties", as distinct from "occupation" or "job". We assume the word "duties" to mean the aggregate of those individual functions required to be done by a worker in performing a particular job or occupation. We therefore consider that the words "his duties" in ss.7(2), should be interpreted as meaning those duties which the employee was required to perform in his/her job or occupation at the relevant time, and should not be interpreted to include a broader range of duties involving other jobs or occupations not performed by the employee at that time.

24. We consider that this more narrow interpretation of the words "his duties" is supported by the following. First, there are difficulties associated with the Commissioner making an assessment as to whether an employee is incapacitated for employment in a broader range of duties or occupations other than the particular duties being performed although there are similar difficulties experienced under workers compensation legislation.

25. Secondly, we note the absence of any statutory provision in the Act, such as exists in workers' compensation legislation, which deems a worker to be totally incapacitated for work if suitable employment is not made available or is not reasonably available to the worker. Such statutory deeming provisions are necessary to safeguard the worker's entitlement to compensation if he is regarded as being fit for certain types of employment but such employment is unavailable to him. If the interpretation sought by the respondent were to be accepted by this Tribunal then, in the absence of a similar deeming provision in this Act, if the employee was found to be unfit for his previous job but fit for much lighter or less demanding employ ment then, regardless of whether such employment could be found by or for the employee, the employee would not be regarded as incapacitated to perform "his duties" and therefore not entitled to invalidity benefits.

26. Thirdly, the provisions of s.75 of the Act predicate that whilst an employee may have been retired on the grounds of invalidity, in that he was no longer able to perform his particular job or duties because of physical or mental incapa city, that situation may change. For example, the employee's health may be restored such that he may be able to perform suitable duties which may be different from the duties previously performed by him at the time of invalidity. In such a case, the Commissioner is required to find employment for him. The very existence of s.75, including the use of the words "duties" as distinct from "duties performed by him" appears again to distinguish between "duties" as compared with "his duties", the former referring to the broad spectrum of "duties" and the latter referring to specific "duties" performed by the employee.

27. Fourthly, it would be inappropriate for the Commis sioner to be required to consider the capacity of an employee to perform duties or a job other than the one performed by him at the time of invalidity, when the Commissioner has no control over the availability of that employment.

28. For these reasons, therefore, we consider that it was inappropriate for the delegate, in considering whether the applicant was, at the relevant time, incapacitated from perform ing "his duties" on the basis of physical or mental incapacity, to give consideration to what other jobs the applicant may have been capable of performing which may or may not have been available to him in the Northern Territory Department of Health. Therefore, apart from the fact that the Northern Territory Department of Health quite clearly did not regard any other jobs as being available to the worker, we are satisfied that it is an inappropriate matter for consideration by the delegate as such considerations go beyond the scope of the words "his duties" as contained in ss.7(2).

29. For these reasons, we consider that the applicant has satisfied the Commissioner of the requirement that, at the time when he ceased to be an "eligible employee", namely at the time of his cessation of employment with the Northern Territory Department of Health on 15 April 1980, he was unfit to perform his duties as a Senior Health Inspector by reason, at least, of physical incapacity to perform those duties and to that extent, that portion of ss.7(2) has been satisfied.

30. The next issue to consider is the interpretation of the word "may" in ss.7(2). It is clear from the authorities that the word "may" is prima facie to be interpreted in its ordinary and natural meaning as importing a discretion. It is further accepted that the onus lies on those who assert that the word "may" has a compulsory meaning, to show that interpretation to be appropriate in the context of the Act taken as a whole (see Re Gleeson [1907] VicLawRp 71; (1907) VLR 368 at 373, Ward v Williams [1955] HCA 4; (1955) 92 CLR 496 at 505).

31. There are, however, a number of conflicting authori ties as to whether "may" is, in the context of a section of an Act, to be interpreted as discretionary or whether it is to be interpreted as obligatory. There appears to be no pattern or common approach by which the outcome of any interpretation can be predicted. Each case is dependent on the particular facts or, as has been said by Lord Penzance in Howard v Bodington (1877) 2 PD 203 at 211:

"I believe, as far as any rule is concerned, you

cannot safely go further than that in each case you

must look to the subject matter; consider the

importance of the provision that has been disregarded,

and the relation of that provision to the general

object intended to be secured by the Act; and upon a

review of the case in that aspect decide whether the

matter is what is called imperative or only

directory".

32. There are, however, some factors which have been addressed by courts as being relevant considerations to assist in interpretation. One consideration has been whether or not the power to be exercised is for the benefit of identified persons. If it is not, then the courts appear reluctant to interpret the power as imposing a mandatory obligation on the decision-maker (see Ward v Williams (supra) and Julius v Lord Bishop of Oxford (1880) 5 App Cas 214 at 222).

33. Another consideration is whether the prerequisite to the exercise of the power is conditional on the existence of certain events or, that the person exercising the power must be satisfied as to certain requirements. In such a case, the power has, on occasions, been interpreted as having to be exercised in an obligatory manner. Examples of this can be found in Barrenger v Coward (1965) Tas SR 243 where the legislation in question, which stated that justices "may" amend a complaint if satisfied as to certain things, was interpreted as mandatory. In Re Calman v John Fairfax and Sons Pty Ltd (1953) 54 SR (NSW) 86, an Act provided that on proof of certain matters, a judge "may" order the production of documents or disclosure of information. That provision was interpreted in a mandatory fashion.

34. More recently, in the High Court in Finance Facilities Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 12; (1971) 127 CLR 106, s.46(3) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-68 provided that the Commissioner "may" allow a private company a rebate of taxation upon being satisfied of certain alternatives, one of which being that a shareholder would not pay a dividend to another private company during a specified period. It was argued by the Commissioner that the provision to allow a rebate was entirely discretionary. The High Court held, however, that once the Commissioner was satisfied in terms of the section, he was obliged to allow the rebate. It was the view of the majority that the Commissioner should not have a discretion to refuse the rebate once he was satisfied that the circumstances entitling the taxpayer to receive it have been established. Windeyer J cited, in particular with approval, the comments made by Jervis CJ in Macdougall v Paterson [1851] EngR 970; (1851) 11 CB 755 at 767 when His Honour said:

"The word "may" is merely used to confer the

authority: and the authority must be exercised, if

the circumstances are such as to call for its

exercise"

and further, at page 773:

"...we are of the opinion that the word "may" is not

used to give a discretion, but to confer a power upon

the court and judges; and that the exercise of such

power depends, not upon the discretion of the court or

judge, but upon the proof of the particular case out

of which such power arises."

35. In this application, the word "may" appears in the context of an Act which is designed to give benefits to employee contributors and their dependants upon retirement or termination of employment. Sub-section 7(1) of the Act contains an inter pretation of what is meant by retirement on the ground of invalidity. Sub-section 7(2) is somewhat confusingly worded but provides that an employee is deemed to have retired on the ground of invalidity upon certain conditions being fulfilled as set out in the sub-section. The consequence of being deemed to have retired on the ground of invalidity, is that the employee thereafter is entitled to the invalidity benefits which are set out in Part V Division 4 of the Act.

36. The prerequisites for an employee being deemed to have retired on the grounds of invalidity are these -

(1) that the employee must have ceased to be an

eligible employee before attaining his maximum

retiring age;

(2) the employee must have ceased to be an eligible

employee -

. otherwise than by death, or

. by reason of his having been retired on

the ground of physical or mental

incapacity to perform his duties, or

. by reason of his services having been

terminated on the ground of physical or

mental incapacity to perform his duties

(3) the Commissioner must be satisfied that at the

time the employee ceased to be an eligible

employee, he was unfit to perform his duties by

reason of physical or mental incapacity;

(4) the Commissioner may direct that the person be

deemed to have been retired on the ground of that

physical or mental incapacity.

37. It is in relation to this fourth requirement that the legislation uses the expression "may". In looking at whether or not the word should be interpreted as importing a discretion or whether it should be interpreted in a mandatory fashion, the Tribunal notes that if the Commissioner does have a discretion then, notwithstanding that an employee may fulfill the first, second and third requirements, (the third of which requires that the Commissioner is satisfied that the employee was, at the relevant time, unfit to perform his duties due to physical or mental incapacity), the Commissioner could nonetheless refuse to deem the employee to be so retired. In that case there will then be no entitlement to an invalidity benefit. There is nothing contained in s.7(2) of the Act to suggest that the discretion is in any way limited. There are no factors which the Commissioner is statutorily required to take into account in exercising any discretion. Therefore, the discretion, if it exists, is a broad one although, of course, any discretions must be exercised having regard to the particular provisions and policy of the Act (see Howard v Bodington (supra)).

38. Bearing in mind that one of the aims of the Act is to grant invalidity pensions to persons who are unable to perform their duties on the grounds of physical or mental incapacity, it would appear to be contrary to the policy and spirit of the Act for a Commissioner to be given a power to prevent entitlement to benefits on the exercise of a discretion when, but for the exercise of the discretion, those invalidity benefits would otherwise fulfill the criteria. To this extent therefore, the decision and fact situation in Finance Facilities Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) would seem to be apposite.

39. It is to be noted therefore, that a discretion, if it does exist, could be exercised by the Commissioner against the beneficiaries for whom the Act was devised. That element alone should indicate some caution in the manner in which the word "may" should be interpreted.

40. Furthermore, if the Commissioner has a discretion, notwithstanding that the Commissioner is satisfied that the necessary requirements for invalidity are met, the Commissioner may nonetheless exercise discretion in such a way as to result in a refusal of benefit to the employee. This again is similar to the situation considered by the High Court in Finance Facilities Pty Ltd (supra).

41. Both counsel for the applicant, Mr. Wells, and counsel for the respondent, Mr. Whithear, referred the Tribunal to the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973 ("the DFRDB Act") and in particular, to s.37. Section 37 of the DFRDB Act contains a similar deeming provision in that it provides:

"37. Where a contributing member has been retired

otherwise than on the ground of invalidity or of

physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties

but, after his retirement, the Chief of Naval Staff,

the Chief of the General Staff or the Chief of the Air

Staff or a person authorized in writing by the Chief

of Naval Staff, the Chief of the General Staff or the

Chief of the Air Staff, as the case requires, informs

the Authority that, at the time the member was

retired, grounds existed on which he could have been

retired on the ground of invalidity or of physical or

mental incapacity to perform his duties, he may, for

the purposes of this Act, be treated as if he had been

retired on that ground."

42. The word "may" in that section has been interpreted as indicating that the authority has a discretion (see Re Britt and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority [1985] VicRp 11; (1984) 57 ALR 199). However, there is, in our view, a very clear distinc tion between s.37 of the DFRDB Act and ss.7(2) of the Superan nuation Act (supra). It would seem essential in the case of s.37 of the DFRDB Act that the Authority have a discretion, otherwise the Authority would be required to rely on the views expressed by the Chiefs of Staff without exercising any independent judgement. The situation, however, pursuant to s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act (supra), is very different. The Commissioner must satisfy him or herself of the requirements set out in the section before the deeming provisions come into operation. There is no requirement for him or her to take into consideration the assessment of others as there is in s.37 of the DFRDB Act. This difference between the two legislative provisions suggests not only that the interpretation given by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Re Britt (supra) should not be applicable to ss.7(2) of the Superannuation Act (supra) but, further, illustrates why, on the contrary, the word "may" should be interpreted in a mandatory rather than a discretionary fashion in s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act (supra).

43. A further consideration in the interpretation of the word "may" in ss.7(2), is that the sub-section is also used, as in the applicant's case, when a person again ceases to be an "eligible employee". If an employee who became entitled to an invalidity pension, later became an "eligible employee" because he or she obtained permanent employment thereby losing entitle ment to the invalidity pension, then ss.7(2) becomes relevant in deciding whether or not the invalidity pension should be resumed. In such a situation, it would seem to be contrary to the policy and spirit of the Act for the Commissioner to be able, in exercising his discretion, to prevent a resumption of that pension even though he or she is satisfied of the existence of the prerequisites for invalidity. The very existence of such a discretion which could place an invalidity pension at risk if an employee was fortunate enough to find employment, and may be sufficient to deter an employee from otherwise accepting or looking for employment and instead choosing to remain on a pension. Such a consequence should, in our view, be avoided.

44. For these reasons, therefore, we consider that the use of the word "may" in ss.7(2) does not import discretion but, to use the words of Jervis CJ in Macdougall's case (supra), confers authority on the Commissioner which must be exercised if he is satisfied that the employee fulfills the prerequisite circumstances.

45. This interpretation is, in our view, fortified by the existence of s.79 of the Act, which disqualifies the employee from entitlement if the retirement was due to wilful action. That situation is therefore not one which need be the subject of any exercise of discretion by the Commissioner pursuant to ss.7(2). Similarly, the provisions of s.75 cater for situations where, subsequent to the grant of an invalidity pension, the Commissioner is satisfied that the employee's health has been restored sufficiently to enable the employee to perform suitable employment, that such employment be made available to him. That also is not a matter which need be the subject of any discretionary exercise pursuant to ss.7(2).

46. For the reasons given above, the Tribunal is of the view that once the prerequisites of ss.7(2) are found to exist, and the Commissioner is satisfied that at the time the employee ceased to be an "eligible employee" he was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner is conferred with the authority and is required to deem the employee to have been retired on the ground of that physical or mental incapacity.

47. Referring to the facts in this case, the first requirement of ss.7(2), in our view, has been satisfied in that the applicant ceased to be an "eligible employee" before attaining his maximum retiring age. The second requirement of ss.7(2) has also been fulfilled in that the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by death. The third requirement has also been satisfied in that the Commissioner, having found that the applicant was unfit for his duties as a Senior Health Inspector at the time of his termination of employment, was so satisfied on the basis, quite correctly, of at least physical incapacity of the applicant to perform his duties.

48. As to the final requirement, we consider that the Commissioner is required to direct that the applicant be deemed to have been retired on the ground of physical incapacity.

49. It therefore follows that there was no need for the delegate of the Commissioner to consider whether a "prudent and responsible Chief Executive Officer or Public Service Commissioner (in the Department of Health in the Northern Territory) would have considered retirement on the ground of invalidity appropriate in all the circumstances of the case" (T2/12).

50. For these reasons, therefore, the Commissioner's decision should be set aside, and in lieu thereof, the Commissioner be directed to deem that the applicant had been retired on 15 August 1980 on the ground of physical incapacity to perform his duties.
