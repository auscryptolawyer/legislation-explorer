TT91/5 and Commissioner of Taxation [1991] AATA 820; (1991) 22 ATR 3559; 92 ATC 109 (9 December 1991)

Last Updated: 27 May 2009



Income tax - Assessable income - Acquisition of shares by a sharetrader - Shares acquired for their dividend potential - Whether profits from sale of shares assessable income - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1987 sec 25(1), 25A.



Californian Copper Syndicate v Harris (1904) 5 TC 159

London Australia Investment Co Ltd v FC of T 77 ATC 4398

Whitehouse v Jordan [1980] 1 All ER 650

FC of T v The Myer Emporium Ltd 87 ATC 4363

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

RESPONDENT

AAT Decision No 7562



Before: Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)

Place: Hobart



The Tribunal varies the decision on the objection by reducing the applicant's taxable income by $59,919 and the penalty for "incorrect return" to nil.

DEPUTY PRESIDENT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. TT91/5

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT



REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)

Date: 9 December 1991

Place: Hobart



The taxpayer, Mrs Copperfield, is engaged in domestic duties, being the mother of three children. Her husband is a stockbroker and senior partner in D Copperfield & Co ("Copperfield"), the leading firm of stockbrokers and financial advisers in Blue Hills. Mrs Copperfield has, over the years, traded in shares and declared the profits (or losses) in her respective returns. The issue in this case is whether some 249,999 shares in Springrump Holdings Limited ("Springrump"), purchased by Copperfield for the taxpayer between 27 April 1985 and 20 June 1986 were acquired as a long term investment for their potential dividends, as alleged, or were part and parcel of the taxpayer's trading activity. The matter is of some significance since some 38,801 shares in Springrump were sold on 14 May 1987 for a net profit of $59,919. For good measure, although a share trading profit of some $24,937 from the sale of other scrip was shown in the taxpayer's 1987 return, prepared by a firm of accountants, the profit on sale of part of the Springrump shares was not disclosed and only came to light after an audit.



2. Any person who buys shares, allegedly for their dividend yield, and sells them for a hefty profit some 14 months later, needs a plausible explanation why the shares were sold. This onus is hardly less when the evidence discloses that the person is not only a sharetrader but has not previously held shares for investment. Whilst time may be relative to Prof Einstein, the Commissioner took the view that shares held for a mere 14 months are not acquired as a "long term investment".



3. As I see it, the principal question raised by this case is: What was the taxpayer's purpose - a small time trader - in buying so large a number of shares in a lack-lustre company with no history of yielding substantial dividends? If the proffered explanation is that the shares were acquired "as a long-term investment for dividend income producing purposes" (doc T7), this must then be tested by examining the reason why the "tree" was sold (or rather severely pruned) before it could produce the anticipated "fruit".



4. Turning to the evidence, this consisted of written statements by both the taxpayer and her husband. These statements appear to me to have been prepared in a lawyer's office before the hearing. What followed has become a set pattern: At the hearing, the statement is identified by the witness and the contents sworn to be true and correct, whereupon the document is tendered as exhibit "B" and counsel sits down saying: "that is the evidence".



5. Using carbon 14 to date this artefact, it seems this practice goes back to AD 1990, give or take a year. Speaking for myself, I deprecate this innovation. It may save time, but at the expense of being able to observe the demeanour of the witness as he/she gives his/her evidence. Indeed, I find little evidentiary value in a document prepared after a long conference with the lawyers and "settled" by counsel. Some years ago, Lord Denning MR, referring to a medical report prepared in this fashion, had this to say:



In short, it wears the colour of special pleading rather than an impartial report. Whenever counsel "settle" a document, we know how it goes. "We had better put this in", "We had better leave this out", and so forth. (Whitehouse v Jordan [1980] 1 All ER 650, 655).



6. Dealing with the reason for acquisition of the Springrump shares, it seems that a Mr Uriah Heep, an old friend of the family, was a "whiz" at turning unprofitable companies into "blue chip" investments. Heep had recently demonstrated that skill with the Blue Hills Coke Company. Having turned that company around, he sold his holding in that company and acquired a controlling interest in Springrump.



7. In "her" proof of evidence, the taxpayer stated:



I can recall discussing the takeover with Uriah when it was on and also after he had completed the takeover, and also discussing with him his plans for the company.



I recall that Uriah informed me that Springrump was not performing well at the time he took it over, and that he wanted to get into sound businesses and improve the financial position of the company. I recall that he mentioned that he wanted to get into solid industrial businesses that were going to produce good cash flow. It is a long time ago and I do not have a clear recollection of precisely what he was going to do as soon as he took over the company.



I was interested in acquiring shares in Springrump because I knew Uriah well and I was aware that he had successfully turned around the fortunes of the Blue Hills Coke Company. I did not have any shares in the Blue Hills Coke Company and I had not made any profit on the sale of shares in the Blue Hills Coke Company.



I discussed with David (husband) the buying of the shares and it was agreed that we would put some money into Springrump, and accordingly in late April and in May 1985 I purchased 120,000 shares for the total sum of $14,443.00. I really wanted the shares and I intended that I would keep them indefinitely. At the time I purchased them I had no plans to resell them, but I expected to receive dividends in the future. When the shares were purchased they were purchased by my husband's firm on my account, and my account was debited with the total purchase price.



At the time that I purchased the shares in Springrump I did hold other shares, but the total value of the other shares was about $10,000.00. I had bought and sold shares in about six companies, and made some gains from that share trading. There were only a few companies involved and up to June 1985 the gains were about $5,500.00.



We stayed in touch with Uriah during 1985 and he appeared to be doing well in his management of the company. I was given advice by David that the position of the company was improving and it was therefore decided that we (sic) should buy some more shares, and on 15th August 1985 we (sic) purchased another 40,000 shares at the price of 21 cents per share. Subsequently further shares were purchased on 11th March 1986 and on the 2nd June 1986 after I had received my first dividend. We (sic) took up rights to purchase another 53,500 shares. These shares were also bought as a long term investment.



8. To complete the picture, on 20 June 1986 the taxpayer acquired a further 8000 shares in Springrump, which by then had risen to 35 cents. This last purchase brought the taxpayer's total holding to 221,999 shares (reduced to 88,801 as a result of a capital reconstruction some time prior to May 1987).



9. On 14 May 1987, the taxpayer sold some 38,801 shares in Springrump, leaving her with a round parcel of 50,000 shares in that company.



10. The reason advanced for the sale of the 38,801 shares was to obtain a sufficient deposit to acquire a property situate at 27-29 Wellington Street, Blue Hills. The need to acquire the property was claimed to be pressing; the price was reasonable ($127,900) and the property was bound to sell. The couples own domestic home was at 13 Waterloo Crescent. Given the peculiarities of title, the couple had, in the past, used part of the Wellington Street property as a right-of-way. It was said by the applicant that the Wellington Street property was:



situated directly on the water side of our Waterloo Crescent property. The Wellington Street property comprises two conjoined weatherboard cottages, and the back boundary is almost contiguous with the boundary of Waterloo Crescent. The use of the Wellington Street property affects the enjoyment of the Waterloo Crescent property because it is in the direct view line to the water and we had been using the driveway of the Wellington Crescent property as an access to our Waterloo Crescent property throughout our residence in Waterloo Crescent. More importantly, we used to park our vehicles on the Wellington Street access when we were not strictly entitled to do that. The Waterloo Street property had only a right-of-way over the driveway, and we were concerned that if the Wellington Street property changed hands, we would no longer be able to park our vehicles in the drive-way. We therefore decided to purchase the Wellington Street property, when we noticed it was up for sale.



11. In the husband's proof of evidence, it is stated that he advised the wife that the Wellington Street property would be a good investment:



as well as enhancing the value of our property if they were held in the same ownership. I was also very concerned that substantial development of the Wellington Street property could seriously adversely affect the value of the Waterloo Crescent property.



12. After having acquired the Wellington Street property, the taxpayer let the two houses on it (the rent claimed to cover the interest on the loan), and, after putting a covenant over the property, limiting vehicular access, sold it in 1989 for $192,500. The Waterloo Crescent property was sold in 1990 when the couple moved to Bl_cher Crescent.



13. To complete the factual narrative, Springrump paid dividends on 30 April 1986, 10 December 1986 and 22 May 1987, said by the taxpayer to amount to a combined return on invested capital to shareholders of about 34c per share. However, Springrump suffered from lack of liquidity, The October 1987 crash put a nail in its coffin, the final straw being the collapse of Equiticorp, which resulted in loans made by that company to Springrump being called up - as Mr Copperfield put it laconically in his proof of evidence, "it is unlikely that the company will pay dividends for some time". The point, however, is somewhat academic since the taxpayer sold the balance of her holding in the company some four weeks before the hearing for 8 cents a share.



14. Despite rigorous cross-examination of both the taxpayer and her husband, neither deviated from their assertion that these shares were bought as an investment. The reason advanced for their acquisition is plausible, as is the explanation why a goodly parcel of the shares were sold within 14 months of purchase. The husband deposed that his own financial commitments left him with insufficient resources to finance the Wellington Street property (he was buying shares in Copperfield at the time). Both impressed me as witnesses of truth. On that finding, that is really the end of the matter.



15. Counsel for the respondent placed much reliance on the decision in London Australia Investment Co Ltd v FC of T 77 ATC 4398. In that case, the taxpayer was a public investment company whose principle object was to invest in securities for the purpose of producing dividend income of not less than 4% on capital. In the tax years in question, there was a steady rise in share prices and in consequence the dividend yield of many shares held by the taxpayer fell. This caused the taxpayer to sell some shares which, in turn, persuaded the Commissioner to tax the profit on the surplus made on sale. At first instance, Helsham J held that the profits were not "caught" by sec 26(a) but nevertheless upheld the assessments on a finding that the buying and selling of the shares was done as part of the taxpayer's business since "it was an integral part of the taxpayer's business to deal in shares, in the sense that switching of investments was desirable to produce the best dividend returns, indeed, it was necessary if the appellant's policy of investing in shares with growth potential was adhered to". His Honour thus purported to tax the surplus under sec 25 of the Act. On appeal to the High Court, the appeal was allowed in part, albeit on grounds not relevant to this reference. Of the majority (Gibbs and Jacobs JJ; Barwick CJ dissenting), Gibbs J held:



Although the company's business was to invest in shares with the primary purpose of obtaining income by way of dividends, the conduct of the investment business required that the share portfolio should be given regular consideration, and that shares should frequently be sold when the dividend yield dropped, which for practical purposes usually meant when the shares went up in value. The taxpayer systematically sold its shares at a profit for the purpose of increasing the dividend yield of its investments. The sale of the shares was a normal operation in the course of carrying on the business of investing for profit. It was not a mere realisation or change of investment.



The present case is in my opinion indistinguishable from the decision in Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Ltd v FC of T [1946] HCA 60; (1946), 73 C.L.R. 604.



The test laid down by Lord Justice Clerk in Californian Copper Syndicate v Harris (1904) 5 TC 159 is applicable to any business, and if the sale of the shares is an act done in what is truly the carrying on of an investment business, the profits will be taxable just as they would have been if the business had been that of banking or insurance. (at p 4403).



Jacobs J held:



The massive scale of the activities in the years in question practically compels the inference that the investment policy was one which in its inception and throughout the course of carrying it into effect would in the expected state of the rising market require frequent and regular realisations of shares whenever they rose in market price before dividends from them were increased. ...



... The investment policy of the company was such that on a purchase of any particular parcel of shares the company had a then present purpose, intention or expectation of not awaiting an increase in dividends if the market price rose in expectation of such an increase but of anticipating the rise in dividend by realisation of the shares at an enhanced price. ... But when with such an investment policy, which envisages regular and frequent sales of the shares acquired, operations are conducted on such a very large scale, the proper conclusion is that the acquisitions and disposals of shares were part of a business of acquisition and disposal. (at p 4411).



16. I have not been persuaded that the London Australia Investment case, like the other banking and insurance cases, have a significant bearing on the facts before me. There is a significant difference between a banking or insurance business, which involves investment activity, and the desultory investment activity in the present case. Once I accept that the Springrump shares were acquired as a long term investment, the fact that the taxpayer was also engaged in share trading and/or that her husband is a stockbroker cannot "taint" the nature of the transaction in dispute by "deeming" it as property acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale. Even a dog is said to be allowed one bite before it is regarded as having a dangerous disposition involving its owner in strict liability. Exit the first limb of sec 25A.



17. Again, it can hardly be maintained that there was either a massive scale of operation in share dealing in this case, nor was the rising market in the shares the precipitating event which caused a parcel to be sold. It follows that I reject the Crown's submission that the profit on sale arose from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme within the second limb of sec 25A.



18. It is trite to observe that, save as a trader, a person may purchase shares in order to enjoy the income from them, albeit in the expectation that at some future time they will be sold at a profit if and when an opportune occasion presents itself. In such a case, the profit on sale will only be taxable if sale was the main or dominant purpose of acquisition. In the instant case, it was stated that Uriah Heep had the reputation of turning run-down companies into "blue chip" investments. This implies an awareness that the shares are likely to increase in value, and with it the prospect of realising that gain by sale. However, a mere awareness is not enough to make the profit assessable under either sec 25 or 25A. The Lord Justice Clerk put it succinctly in the Californian Copper Syndicate case:



It is quite a well settled principle in dealing with questions of assessment of Income Tax, that where the owner of an ordinary investment chooses to realise it, and obtains a greater price for it than he originally acquired it at, the enhanced price is not profit in the sense of Schedule D of the Income Tax Act of 1842 assessable to Income Tax. But it is equally established that enhanced values obtained from realisation or conversion of securities may be so assessable, where what is done is not merely a realisation or change of investment, but an act done in what is truly the carrying on, or carrying out, of a business. The simplest case is that of a person or association of persons buying and selling lands or securities speculatively, in order to make gain, dealing in such investments as a business, and thereby seeking to make profits. There are many companies which in their very inception are formed for such a purpose, and in these cases it is not doubtful, where they make a gain by a realisation, the gain they make is liable to be assessed for Income Tax.



What is the line which separates the two classes of cases may be difficult to define, and each case must be considered according to its facts; the question to be determined being - Is the sum of gain that has been made a mere enhancement of value by realising a security, or is it a gain made in an operation of business in carrying out a scheme for profit-making? (supra at pp 165-6)



19. The above quotation has stood the test of time, having been repeatedly cited with approval; cf the FC of T v The Myer Emporium Ltd 87 ATC 4363 at 4367.



20. The position is, of course, significantly different if the acquisition and disposal of shares (or any item of property) is part of a business. Such a business is presumed to aim at a resale at a profit. In that situation, the purpose of resale need not be the sole purpose or even the primary or dominant purpose for the profit to be "caught" under the first limb of sec 25A, it is sufficient if it is merely one of the purposes. Jacobs J put the issue succinctly in the London Australia Investment case;



And in this context the word "purpose" is hardly if at all distinguishable from intention or expectation. The dominant or primary purpose may be to obtain income from the items of property acquired but if there is a purpose or intention or expectation of selling at a profit if and when a suitable occasion arises, then one condition of carrying on a business of buying and selling at a profit is satisfied. If a man makes a business of acquiring property with a dual purpose of enjoying it or its profits and of reselling it eventually at a higher price than he paid for it, then not only the income from the property but also the profit on resale will be income in the ordinary sense of the term, and within the second limb of sec [25A]. (at pp 4409-4410)



21. As I understand the decision of Jacobs J, on which counsel for the Commissioner placed much reliance, his Honour assumed that the acquisition and disposal was part of the business.



22. Having expressly found in this case that the Springrump shares were not acquired in the ordinary course of the taxpayer's business, but for the purpose of deriving income by way of dividends, I am satisfied that the profit is not taxable pursuant to sec 25A.



23. For much the same reason, I am satisfied that the proceeds from the sale of the shares cannot be viewed as income according to ordinary concepts. Having rejected the submission that the impugned share were acquired in the ordinary course of carrying on the taxpayer's business, I am satisfied that what was done was the mere realisation of an asset.



24. Much reliance was placed by the Crown on the Myer case (FC of T v The Myer Emporium Ltd) 87 ATC 4363. The case is said to be authority for the proposition that a receipt may constitute income even if it arises from an isolated business or commercial transaction entered otherwise than in the course of carrying on the taxpayer's business. That, of course, is true but requires qualification. As the Court put it:



The important proposition to be derived from Californian Copper and Ducker (Ducker v Rees Roturbo Development Syndicate Ltd [1928] AC 132) is that a receipt may constitute income, if it arises from an isolated business operation or commercial transaction entered into otherwise than in the ordinary course of the carrying on of the taxpayer's business, so long as the taxpayer entered into the transaction with the intention or purpose of making a relevant profit or gain from the transaction. (at p 4367)



(The "relevant profit" referred to above is deemed to constitute income "if the property generating the profit or gain was acquired in a business operation or commercial transaction for the purpose of profit-making by the means giving rise to the profit". (at p 4367)).



24. On the facts found by me, viz that the impugned shares were not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit, but for their dividend potential, the Myer principle can have no application.



25. For the above reasons the decision on the objection is varied by reducing the applicant's taxable income by $59,919 and the penalty for "incorrect return" to nil.
