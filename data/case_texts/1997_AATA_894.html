Re IAN FRANCIS GRIGG

DECISION

Tribunal Senior Member J.A. Kiosoglous MBE

Dr M. Miller AO (Member)

Air Marshal B. Gration (Member)

Date 1 August 1997

Place Canberra

Decision The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that there are special circumstances within the meaning of sub-s.119U(2) of the Superannuation Act 1922 and therefore the period for the making of an election under sub-s.119U(1) is extended to 20 April 1995. The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the applicant’s entitlements in the light of this decision taking into account any amount which may be owed by the applicant to the respondent by way of arrears of contributions and the repayment of amounts previously received by the applicant on resignation in refund of his contributions to the fund.



(Sgd)

J.A. KIOSOGLOUS

(Senior Member)

REASONS FOR DECISION

1 August 1997 Senior Member J.A. Kiosoglous MBE

Dr M. Miller AO (Member)

Air Marshal B. Gration (Member)

  1. This is an application for the review of a decision made by the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 3 December 1996 to confirm the original decision of the delegate made on 18 August 1996 that there were no special circumstances to justify the extension of time for the making of an election under sub-s.119U(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 (“the 1922 Act”).
  2. The applicant, Ian Francis Grigg, was born on 31 October 1931. He joined the Commonwealth Public Service on 21 December 1949 at the age of eighteen years. He moved to the ACT in 1954. In 1967 he occupied the position of Senior Adviser in the Parliamentary Government division, and went on to become Principle Private Secretary to Mr McMahon, the Prime Minister at that time. When Mr McMahon was defeated in the Federal Election of 1972, the applicant became a First Assistant Secretary of the Department. In July of 1973 the applicant resigned from the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet to take up the position of Chief Executive Officer of the Federal Chamber of Automotive Industry (“FCAI”), which position he still occupies.
  3. The relevant legislation is sub-ss.119U(1) and (2). There was some argument that s.136 applied as well as, or instead of, s.119U. Counsel for the respondent, Ms T. Munoz, referred the Tribunal to Re Welsh & Commissioner for Superannuation (1978) 2 ALD 101 at 104 in which the Tribunal reviewed the authorities and delineated the preferred approach to s.119U late elections (although in that case the Tribunal was considering s.135, which is in all relevant respects almost identical with s.136). It was held (at page 104) that “s.119U appears to stand apart from those elections to which s.135 may have potential application”. Section 136 of the 1922 Act does not therefore, appear to apply to this matter.
  4. In full the relevant legislation states:

“119U.(1) Subject to this section, where, after the commencement of this section:

(a) a contributor to the Fund who has not attained the age of sixty years ceases to be such a contributor by reason of his resignation or discharge;

...



he may, within twenty-one days after he ceases to be such a contributor, elect that this Division shall apply in relation to him.

(2) The Board may, if it is satisfied that there are special circumstances that justify it in so doing, extend the period for the making of an election under the last preceding subsection.”

ISSUES AND CONTENTIONS

  1. Counsel for the applicant, Mr K. Cush, contends that special circumstances exist because at the time Mr Grigg resigned from the Public Service, on 6 July 1973, he believed that he could not preserve his superannuation benefits. He stated that he was given to understand this by his personnel officer, and as the result of a phone call he made to the Superannuation Board. He contends that he probably filled out the form SB2 (Exhibit A2), which does not refer to preservation rights. He contends that he did not realise that he could have preserved his superannuation rights until late 1994 or early 1995. He made a late election shortly afterwards. Mr Grigg contends that had he known of the preservation option, he would have preserved his superannuation benefits.
  2. The respondent contends that there are no special circumstances which would warrant an extension of time for election from 21 days to 21 years. It contends that the applicant held positions at a very senior level while working in the Public Service and was well able to make inquiries and judgements which he ought to have exercised at the time of cessation.
  3. The issue before the Tribunal is therefore whether or not there were special circumstances to justify an extension of time in which to make an election from 21 days to 21 years.

THE DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE

  1. The Tribunal had the following written materials before it:
    • (a) the documents filed pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1 - T28);
    • (b) the applicant’s statement of facts and contentions dated 12 March 1997 (Exhibit A1);
    • (c) letter of the applicant dated 13 March 1997 and a copy of the form SB2 (Exhibit A2);
    • (d) letter from MLC to Mr Brazil, the applicant’s solicitor, dated 10 March 1997 (Exhibit A3);
    • (e) letter from MLC to the applicant dated 28 May 1974 (Exhibit A4);
    • (f) MLC’s schedule of superannuation benefits (Exhibit A5);
    • (g) letter to Mr Brazil from Mr Heraud dated 20 June 1997 (Exhibit A6);
    • (h) respondent’s statement of facts and contentions dated 2 April 1997 (Exhibit R1);
    • (i) Superannuation Board Circular 1970/9 dated 30 June 1970 on Preservation of Superannuation Pension Rights (Exhibit R2);
    • (j) Superannuation Board Circular 1971/5 dated 27 May 1971 on Preservation of Superannuation Rights (Exhibit R3);
    • (k) Superannuation Board Circular 1971/7 dated 27 May 1971 on Preservation of Superannuation Rights (Exhibit R4);
    • (l) Superannuation Board Circular 1972/8 dated 23 June 1972 on Superannuation Preservation Brochure (Exhibit R5);
    • (m) record of title search done on the applicant’s residential property (Exhibit R6); and
    • (n) pages 113 - 114 of the respondent’s Superannuation Register (Exhibit R7).

ORAL EVIDENCE

  1. In this matter, apart from Exhibit R7, the only contemporary evidence the Tribunal had before it was the evidence of the applicant. As the applicant’s evidence was a major factor in this decision, it has been set out here in some detail. He said that on resignation in July 1973 he understood that his only entitlement was to a refund of his superannuation contributions. He did not realise that in 1971 the 1922 Act had been amended so as to allow public servants who resigned before the age of 65, to preserve their superannuation benefits. He said that he had been disappointed at the small amount of money he had received for 24 years of service and had he known that the option was available, he would have preserved his superannuation.
  2. The applicant commenced employment with FCAI almost immediately after leaving the Commonwealth and applied at once to join the superannuation fund to which FCAI subscribed. As it was, there was a period of a few months in his new position before he was covered by the MLC scheme. This caused the applicant concern at the time, as he was married with 4 children, and had he suffered an accident, they would not have been protected. The applicant received his superannuation refund in December 1973. It was put into a savings account. The applicant could not remember how long for, but that it was “used as family protection”. The refund remained in that account for some years.
  3. It was not until “late 94 - early 95” that the applicant became aware of the preservation option. He was approaching retirement age and had consulted his accountant about retirement planning. His accountant raised the issue of reasonable benefit limits and explained to him the benefit that he would have gained had he preserved his superannuation, which he could have done in 1973. On 20 April 1995 the applicant contacted the Commissioner for Superannuation to find out whether or not he was so entitled (T7). In response, a delegate of the Commissioner stated that he would treat the applicant’s letter as an application for an extension of time under the Act (T9).
  4. In cross examination the applicant was shown both Form SB2 (Exhibit A2) and Form SB2A (Exhibit R4). He accepted that he did not remember exactly which form he had signed, 23 years after the event. SB2A is similar to SB2, having replaced SB2 after the 1971 amendment to the 1922 Act. Where SB2 is an “Application for Pension, Lump Sum Payment, or Refund of Contributions”, SB2A is an “Application for Refund of Contributions or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights”. The respondent suggested to him that he may have signed SB2A. The applicant vehemently denied this, saying “I cannot recall signing SB2A clearly because if I had signed that, I would have recognised the conditions on the bottom of the form”. (transcript page 27). He would have read “preservation” in the title and gone on to read the information provided on the form, which clearly explains the preservation option. He said that as he occupied a position of great responsibility, it was essential that he read closely all documents which were put before him, especially if he had to sign them. He felt therefore that it was impossible for him to have been given form SB2A.
  5. The respondent then questioned the applicant about a telephone call he made to the Superannuation Board (T10). Although it was 23 years after the event, the applicant recalled that he contacted the Board on the suggestion of his personnel officer, “I was taking the normal prudent steps you would take in employment to ensure that your cessation of employment comes to a proper conclusion” (transcript page 28) but that he could not recall exactly what was said, nor could he recall if he made the call before or after his resignation. He said that the advice he received from the personnel officer was “not expansive”. He was part of a small Government Department, with only one personnel officer, who carried out all the personnel tasks for the 120 employees of the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet. He added that at the time he believed that preservation of superannuation was only available if one transferred to another Commonwealth Department. It was put to him that he could not recall what inquiries he made so he could not tell the Tribunal whether he made inquiries and whether his understanding of superannuation was correct. The applicant maintained that he had made a short phone call about “procedural steps”. He recalled that the person he spoke to was probably a male, but he could not recall his name.
  6. It was put to the applicant that his knowledge of superannuation was only an assumption that he made. He responded that he was “unaware that there was an eligibility for preservation of superannuation ... The previous 70 years had been a non-preservation situation”. (transcript page 30). It was also put to him that as a First Assistant Secretary at the time, he was well equipped to make inquiries. The applicant responded that he had been busy and unaware that there were any alternatives. Looking after his own needs had a lower priority.
  7. The applicant was then examined about his membership of the MLC Superannuation Fund. He joined it within 3 months of leaving the Commonwealth. It was not compulsory as it had been in the Commonwealth Public Service, but merely encouraged.
  8. The respondent then showed the applicant the staff circulars which gave notice of the 1971 amendments to the 1922 Act and the new ability to elect to preserve one’s superannuation (Exhibits R2, R3, R4 and R5). The applicant denied ever having seen any of them. He pointed out that at the bottom of Exhibit R5 it said “It is not intended that there should be a general issue of the brochure to all contributors”. The respondent put to him that he must have seen them as he occupied a position of seniority. The applicant responded that he would not expect to see them with the functions and responsibilities that he had (transcript page 33). He said that such staff circulars would not be sent to all officials in the Department. When shown Exhibit R3, he said that at that time he had been with the Prime Minister’s office in Parliament House or possibly interstate or overseas. He did not see it, nor would he have expected to see it. In response to a question from the Tribunal, the applicant said that he would expect that those staff circulars would have been sent to the personnel officer who was located in East Block, with the intent that they be given to someone who was resigning. The applicant reiterated that had he seen and read the pamphlets at the time, he would have acted differently. He added that at that time information was distributed on a “need to know” basis. He was not sent any information on the economy or the budget for that reason. He was responsible for handling questions to the Prime Minister and Parliament, and for compiling and helping with Ministerial statements and Prime Ministerial statements. In the early 1970s superannuation issues did not have a high profile, so Mr Grigg did not have to assist the Prime Minister in that area.
  9. In re-examination, the applicant said that prior to his resignation he had never received any information from the Superannuation Board about his entitlements. When he spoke to the personnel officer he was not told about any rights that he might have had in respect to preservation of his benefits.
  10. The applicant was then examined about his move to the private sector. He said he was approached to fill the position and he moved in order to gain some private sector experience. His long term plan had always been to return to the public sector.
  11. The applicant was shown Exhibit R7, the respondent’s Superannuation Register at pages 113 to 114. On the entry under the applicant’s name, was the date “17.12.73” and a pencil written comment, “SB2 and RF papers to Mr Lorenz Pres?”. The applicant had never previously seen the entry and could make no comment on it. He could not confirm that he had received his refund in December 1973.
  12. Various scenarios were put to the applicant, all involving him speaking to Mr Lorenz in 1973 about his superannuation options. The applicant responded that he did not recall the name of the person he spoke to, and that had he been informed of his option to preserve his superannuation, he would have done so.
  13. In response to having had put to him in cross examination that he had been disappointed at the small sum - approximately $6000 - that he had accrued as a result of 23 years in the Public Service the applicant stated that he would have been. It was also put to him that between 1973 and late 1994, he had seen newspaper articles about late election. He replied that he had seen articles in the papers, but had not paid much attention as he assumed that they did not apply to him.
  14. The applicant agreed that it had taken him about a year from the time he received the response from the Commissioner for Superannuation in respect to his superannuation late election (T9), to proceed with the matter. He said this was because “It is an expensive exercise which requires legal input. It is a distraction from a job and there are a lot of issues that had to be weighed up before I decided to proceed”. (transcript page 51). He said he did not pursue matters himself with the Department, but put the application in the hands of his solicitors.

THE PRESUMPTION OF REGULARITY

  1. Exhibit R7 was the only evidence adduced by the respondent which dated back to the time of the applicant’s resignation and referred to the applicant directly. It was shown to the applicant in cross examination, and not unreasonably, the applicant had never seen it, and could not comment usefully on its contents. The Tribunal asked to what extent the respondent sought to rely on Exhibit R7, as in Mr Grigg’s entry, there had been an erasure and some overwriting. The respondent replied that they would be making submissions in respect to the irregularities and the quality of the evidence. The respondent also said that the presumption of regularity applied.
  2. Both parties referred briefly to the presumption of regularity in government procedures. On the presumption, Cross on Evidence (Vol 1 1997 Butterworths at page 7069) says “ ... it requires proof of the performance of the act whose validity is in issue; but in truth the presumption is merely a principle of expediency whereby it falls upon the impugning party to assert and justify the grounds of his attack.” The respondent submitted that the presumption of regularity applied and therefore the applicant would have filled out SBA2 and had received sufficient notice of the option to preserve his superannuation. The applicant submitted that that presumption had been displaced. The applicant referred to Exhibit R7, the pencil written entry, which stated that Mr Grigg had received form SB2 as opposed to SB2A, which he should have been given. There was also irregularity in that if the pencil written entry is to be believed, the form was probably sent to Mr Lorenz. In no way could the applicant be responsible for the irregularities in the Superannuation Register. The respondent also pointed out that according to Exhibit R7, Mr Grigg received his refund 5 months later, as opposed to the usual 1 - 2 months. No evidence was put before the Tribunal to show any regularity in the procedures used to retire the applicant. The Tribunal therefore accepts that there were irregularities on the part of the Superannuation Board, if not the personnel section of Mr Grigg’s Department, which caused Mr Grigg to be ignorant - if not misinformed - of his rights. Had he been informed correctly, he would have acted differently. The presumption of regularity is duly rebutted.

THE QUALITY OF THE EVIDENCE

  1. The respondent drew the Tribunal’s attention to the recent case of Brisbane South Regional Health Authority v Taylor [1996] HCA 25; (1996) 139 ALR 1, in which the High Court raised quality of the evidence as a serious factor in the consideration of time limits. McHugh J said at page 8 that:
“The enactment of time limitations has been driven by the general perception that ‘where there is delay the whole quality of justice deteriorates’. Sometimes the deterioration in quality is palpable, as in the case where a crucial witness is dead or an important document has been destroyed. But sometimes, perhaps more often than we realise, the deterioration in quality is not recognisable even by the parties. Prejudice may exist without the parties or anybody else realising that it exists. ... ‘what has been forgotten can rarely be shown’.”.
  1. Deterioration of evidence is an issue in this case. The Tribunal believes that it has enough before it to find in favour of the applicant due to the existence of Exhibit R7, and the comportment of the applicant; not only because of his evidence before the Tribunal, but also because of his general behaviour in respect to superannuation. He took reasonable steps to establish his superannuation situation in 1973. His actions in respect to superannuation from then up to the present demonstrate a commitment to superannuation consistent with his claims before this Tribunal. The money he obtained from the Commonwealth remained unused in a bank account. In his new employment he sought at once to join a superannuation fund. The Tribunal also accepts that it is unlikely that he saw the superannuation circulars. In considering what to do in such a situation where there is such a paucity of evidence as in Brisbane v Taylor, the Tribunal has to balance fairness to the applicant in finding against him, and fairness to the respondent in finding against it. The evidence before the Tribunal is firstly consistent with the applicant’s claims, and secondly points to carelessness if not negligence on the part of the Commonwealth. The respondent did have one witness whom it chose not to call. He was apparently elderly and in ill health.

FINDINGS ON THE EVIDENCE

  1. Twenty-four years after the fact, there are issues in respect to the quality and quantity of evidence available to the Tribunal. Due to the paucity of contemporary documentation, the recollections of the applicant comprise almost the totality of his case. The credit of the applicant is therefore of central importance. The respondent did not seek to impugn the applicant’s credit, but only his recollection of events which occurred 24 years in the past. The Tribunal finds the applicant to be a witness of truth. The respondent did find a contemporary witness, a Mr Brian Lorenz, the secretary of the Superannuation Board at the time, but chose not to call him because, they said, he was elderly and in ill health (transcript page 12). The applicant submitted that this was probably because Mr Lorenz’s evidence would do little to help the respondent’s case.
  2. In respect to Exhibit R7, the Tribunal finds that the only weight it should be given is to displace the presumption of regularity in respect to government procedures, as clearly there was an irregularity in the erasure and overwriting, if not in the actual administration of the refund itself.
  3. In relation to Exhibits R2, R3, R4 and R5, the respondent tried to show that this information was available to the applicant at the time that he made the election in 1973. The Tribunal accepts the applicant’s evidence that it would have been freely circulated only to high level personnel who worked in related areas and to personnel officers. The applicant worked in policy and directly to the Prime Minister. As the Tribunal accepts the applicant’s evidence that at no time was the preservation option made known to him, the Tribunal must view the Superannuation Board circulars as evidence of a failure by the Commonwealth, (be it the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet’s personnel area or the Superannuation Board) to bring to the attention of the applicant material of which it was aware. Not only should the personnel area or superannuation area have been aware of this information, but it should have been aware that it was to be provided to any one leaving the Public Service. In this case, the Commonwealth failed to provide that information.
  4. Finding Mr Grigg to be truthful in his evidence and that the documentary evidence supports him, does not however dispose of the matter, as the Tribunal still has to decide whether special circumstances exist such as would satisfy sub-s.119U(2) of the 1922 Act.

SPECIAL CIRCUMSTANCES SECTION 119U

  1. The Tribunal has to decide whether or not special circumstances exist which justify the extension of the time period for making a late election from 21 days to 21 years. We must first consider the ambit of that discretion, and what circumstances should be taken into consideration. As the respondent pointed out, there is a difference between the requirements of the late election provision sub-s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1975 (“the 1975 Act”), and s.119U of the 1922 Act. Sub-section 157(1) refers to the Commissioner being “satisfied in all the circumstances”, whereas sub-s.119U(2) refers to special circumstances justifying an extension in time for a late election. Where sub-s.157(1) involves a “balancing exercise”, s.119U involves a more restrictive test; something unusual has to be present, which renders it unfair or inappropriate to deny the applicant an extension of time (Beadle v Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; (1985) 60 ALR 225).
  2. In Re Beadle & Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; (1984) 1 AAR 362 at page 364 when discussing the phrase “special circumstances” the Tribunal said:
“The qualifying adjective looks to circumstances that are unusual, uncommon or exceptional. Whether circumstances answer any of these descriptions must depend upon the context in which they occur. For it is the context which allows one to say that the circumstances in one case are markedly different from the usual run of cases. This is not to say that the circumstances must be unique but they must have a particular quality of unusualness that permits them to be described as special.“

In all the “special circumstances” cases the Courts and Tribunals have declined to be more specific. Re Beadle (which was impliedly approved by the Federal Court in Beadle) merely suggests that a case must appear markedly different in some respect from other similar situations - “unusual”, but not necessarily “unique”. In Minister for Community Services and Health and Another v Chee Keong Thoo [1988] FCA 54; (1988) 78 ALR 307 at page 324, this was defined as “something unusual or different to take the matter out of the ordinary course ...”

  1. In Beadle (supra) at page 228 the Federal Court also discussed the term “special circumstances”:
“Presumably in this context special circumstances must include events which would render the six months unfair, or inappropriate. For example, where the delay beyond six months was due to the claimant’s being misled by a departmental officer or was due to the negligence of a third party it might be thought the normal six months would be inappropriate; ... More difficult would be questions of ignorance, illiteracy, isolation, illness and the like. It would depend upon the circumstances of the particular case whether these constituted special circumstances.”

In the current matter the respondent argued that the applicant must have known as he occupied positions of a high level in both the public and private sectors. Ms Munoz submitted that “somewhere along the lines, from 1973 through to 1995, he could have exercised his, presumably outstanding faculty of judgment and inquiry, to inquire ...”. (transcript page 73). The respondent submitted that the Tribunal should not look at only 1973, the time he resigned from the Public Service, but the intervening 21 years as well. For further guidance the Tribunal was referred to Re Brisley & Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority 8 ALN N257. In that case the Tribunal found that whether or not special circumstances existed was determined at the date which the application for election was made. The applicant quoted the famous passage of Lord Denning in Roe v Ministry of Health [1954] EWCA Civ 7; [1954] 2 All ER 131 at page 137; “we must not look at the 1947 accident with 1954 spectacles” in support of the same point.

  1. In his evidence the applicant stated that he assumed that superannuation was portable only between different areas of the public sector. When he saw references to portability in the media in the intervening 21 years, he assumed that that was what was being referred to.
  2. In Beadle (supra) the full court of the Federal Court differentiated between deficiencies on the side of a Governmental Department (ie being misleading or negligent) and deficiencies on the part of the recipient of the benefit (ie illness, isolation or illiteracy). It is harder to show that deficiencies on the part of the recipient constitute special circumstances than deficiencies on the part of the conferring Government Department or agency. In Re Brisley the Tribunal said after citing Beadle, “all the relevant circumstances which surround the application ought to be considered”. In that case, another retirement benefits late election situation, the Tribunal went on to hold that:
“Whether a circumstance is special or not will be dependent upon, at least and inter alia, whether it is something outside the control of the applicant; whether it prevents a free election by the applicant; and whether it is a circumstance the effects of which cannot be avoided by the applicant.”

This extended the issue in Beadle to include unavoidable circumstances beyond the applicant’s control which might, for instance, include being out of the country, as in Re Foley and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 8731 25/5/93).

  1. The Tribunal’s attention was drawn to a number of late election cases, where the late election was not recognised. In Re Anderson & Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1984) 8 ALN N107 the applicant’s late election was not recognised as he had been given ample opportunity to put his case to the respondent and had not done so. In Re Shute & Defence Force Retirement Authority (1984) 6 ALN N107 the Tribunal declined to recognise a late election as the applicant had known of the option earlier, but had not exercised it due to financial problems. In Re Welsh & Commissioner for Superannuation (1978) 2 ALD 101 (a case concerning sub-s.119U(2)), the applicant had signed the form SB2A, which the Tribunal found adequately informed him of the option to preserve his superannuation, and that he had had the opportunity to inquire further but had failed to do so. In Re Game & Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (AAT Decision No. 6351 2/11/90), after the election option had been brought to the applicant’s attention, he had elected not to pursue it. In Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450 at page 455, the Tribunal found that the applicant would not have preserved his superannuation because he planned to become a farmer on resignation and had used his superannuation monies to buy a farm. In Re Pittorino and the Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 9735 16/9/94) the Tribunal found that the applicant had been aware of the preservation option, but had chosen not to exercise it. There is nothing in these cases which makes them inconsistent with this matter.
  2. On the evidence before it, the Tribunal finds there was a special circumstance which renders it unfair and inappropriate to deny the applicant an extension of time. Had the applicant been informed of the preservation option, he would have elected to preserve his superannuation. The Tribunal finds that it was due to an omission by the Commonwealth Government (be it the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet or the Superannuation Board), an unavoidable circumstance out of the applicant’s control, which prevented him from making a free election.

OTHER RELEVANT MATTERS

  1. The scope and nature of the legislation in issue was considered of considerable importance by Justice von Doussa in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239 at page 246. The statute in issue is the Superannuation Act 1922, a piece of beneficial legislation which concerns itself with retirement benefits (as opposed to, for instance as in Brisbane v Taylor, a statute of limitations issue). Section 119U confers a benefit on members of the Commonwealth Public Service. Discretions conferred under beneficial legislation, as the Federal Court stated in Chalk, should be applied beneficially.
  2. Subsequent conduct is also relevant if not often crucial in late election cases (see above paragraph 36). As soon as Mr Grigg commenced with his new employer, he applied for superannuation. The funds which he received from the Commonwealth were deposited immediately into a bank account and remained there for a number of years. He did not use the money to purchase shares or other property. It is open to the Tribunal to conclude that in the circumstances, had Mr Grigg been correctly informed, he would have preserved his superannuation.
  3. The length of time involved is an important consideration. The respondent argues 21 years is too long. In Re Foley the Tribunal allowed a late election 18 years after the fact. In Boardman the period was 10 years. The Family Court in In the Marriage of Catlin and Kent (1986) FLC 76 150 allowed 35 years. A lengthy delay does not of itself mean that special circumstances do not exist. In Beadle the court said that in the case of long time periods, “weighty facts” are needed to constitute special circumstances. In the Tribunal’s opinion, the mistakes on the part of the applicant’s employer do constitute sufficiently special circumstances. In Beadle itself, there was dicta that a special circumstance might be “where the delay was due to claimant’s being misled by a departmental officer ...” (at page 228). The Tribunal acknowledges that the length of time is considerable. However the Tribunal feels that the period of time alone is insufficient to deny this applicant his late election.
  4. The public interest has to be considered in the granting of such lengthy extensions of time so far beyond that which is granted in the legislation. In the recent High Court Case of Brisbane v Taylor, legislation in issue was the Statute of Limitations in respect to personal injury claims in Queensland. The plaintiff was trying to bring an action in personal injury 17 years after the event. The limitation period is 3 years in Queensland. The Court had a discretion to extend the time in which one could bring an action. McHugh J said at pages 8 - 9:
“The longer the delay in commencing proceedings, the more likely it is that the case will be decided on less evidence than was available to the parties at the time that the cause of action arose.

...

(there are) four broad rationales for the enactment of limitation periods. First, as time goes by, relevant evidence is likely to be lost. Secondly, it is oppressive, even ‘cruel’ to a defendant to allow an action to be brought long after the circumstances which gave rise to it have passed. Thirdly, people should be able to arrange their affairs and utilise their resources on the basis that claims can no longer be made against them.

...

The final rationale for limitation periods is that the public interest requires that disputes be settled as quickly as possible.”
  1. The first rationale was addressed above, under the heading “Quality of evidence”. The other three rationales are relevant in areas of private litigation. It is not evident to the Tribunal that it is unduly oppressive to the Superannuation Commission to make a late election after so long a period. The situation might be different, if the matter involved a private individual. Any monies given to the applicant as a result of this decision will come ultimately out of consolidated revenue. This is not a case where considerable financial detriment is caused to a defendant who felt herself “safe” after so many years. The final rationale in favour of time limits is the “swift settlement of disputes”. This matter is not a “dispute” in the litigation sense, but consideration of the exercise of a broad discretion under beneficial legislation. The public interest involved in the case of granting of extensions of time to commence litigation is different from the public interest involved in allowing extensions of time because of mistakes made by a Government Department or Agency. In the latter the duty on the Government is more stringent and a more benign view is taken of the circumstances of the applicant: Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239, Re Brisley & Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1984) 8 ALN N257.
  2. In this case, counsel for the applicant tried to submit that isolation was a special circumstance on the part of Mr Grigg. Presumably counsel was referring to the circumstances in Re Foley and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 8731 25/5/93), where the applicant in that case was in Canada, trying to obtain information about his superannuation options. It would take substantial argument to persuade the Tribunal that Mr Grigg was sufficiently isolated such as to constitute a “special circumstance” under sub-s.119U(2). Brief reference only was made to such an argument.
  3. The respondent argued that there were no special circumstances, as the applicant had all the resources and judgement at his command. “He could easily have done something”. (transcript page 71). As was discussed above in Beadle at paragraph 34, disadvantages under which an applicant labours may be considered to be special circumstances, but it is harder to show. In this case the Tribunal accepts that the only personal disadvantage that the applicant suffered was his ignorance and the misinformation with which he was provided. That was due to a deficiency on the part of the Department, which Beadle and other authorities (Re Brisley & Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1984) 8 ALN N257; Secretary, Department of Social Security v Hodgson [1992] FCA 338; (1992) 108 ALR 322; Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 9987 6/2/95) and Re Foley and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 8731 25/5/93)) accept as an unusual circumstance which may well permit an extension of time.
  4. In the circumstances, the Tribunal finds that the omissions of the Commonwealth in this matter constitute a special circumstance under sub-s.119U(2) of the 1922 Act, over which the applicant had no control. In light of these omissions, it would be unfair and inappropriate to deny the applicant recognition of an extension of time. The Tribunal acknowledges the difficulty of dealing with the matter almost a quarter of a century after the fact, but believes that there is sufficient evidence to support its finding.
