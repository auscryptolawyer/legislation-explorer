Re Jane Wilson Hornsey and Commissioner of Superannuation and Dorothy Lynette Pearce [1990] AATA 206; 6233 Superannuation 22 ALD 85 (6 September 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: JANE WILSON HORNSEY

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION and DOROTHY LYNETTE

PEARCE

No. T90/64

AAT No. 6233

Superannuation

22 ALD 85

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.C. Jennings QC(1) (Deputy President), B.C. Lock(1) (Member) and E. O'Farrell(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Definition of "spouse" of deceased eligible employee - married persons living apart at time of death - whether "but for absence resulting from illness" they would have been together at time of death - mental illness not disputed but other factors also material to Tribunal's opinion - decision of Commissioner set aside.

Superannuation Act 1976 - ss.3(1)(2)(a) Part VI, 154(2)

HEARING

HOBART

6:9:1990

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and the matter remitted to the Commissioner for appropriate finding in relation to each of the children of the applicant and the deceased.

There will be liberty to each party to apply.

DECISION

Prior to his death by suicide on 23 August 1987 John Neville Dawson ("the deceased") was an "eligible employee" within the meaning of s.3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). He was survived by his third wife (now Dorothy Lynette Pearce, but referred to hereafter as "Mrs. Dawson") to whom he was married in 1980, his second wife Jane Wilson Hornsey ("the applicant") and their three children who were born between 1972 and 1976.

2. On 24 November 1987 the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") granted a claim by the widow Mrs. Dawson that she be treated as a spouse of the deceased and therefore entitled to benefits under Part VI of the Act. That decision necessarily involved rejection of a claim by the applicant for orphan's benefits in respect of the children who were nevertheless subsequently accepted as "eligible children" entitled to lesser benefits.

3. At the request of the applicant the Commissioner reconsidered his decision with respect to Mrs. Dawson's rights pursuant to s.154(2) of the Act, but on 28 February 1990 he confirmed that previous decision. The applicant has now sought review of the original decision and Mrs. Dawson has been joined as a respondent.

4. The applicant was represented by Mr. Slicer and Mrs. Dawson was represented by Mr. Avery. Mr. Withear appeared to assist the Tribunal on behalf of the Commissioner. He made no submission on the facts as to which party was entitled to succeed, but he did make helpful submissions as to how relevant provisions of the Act should be interpreted.

5. Prior to the hearing directions were given as to filing of affidavits and the issue of subpoenas. At the hearing the Tribunal received the T documents produced by the Commissioner pursuant to the provisions of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. It also received two affidavits sworn for the purpose of these proceedings by Mrs. Dawson and others by her daughter Mrs. Brown, the applicant Mrs. Hornsey and her father Mr. T.I. Chambers. Detailed medical records relating to periods spent by the deceased in hospital in February and August 1987 and notes and correspondence relating to medical investigations and treatment of the deceased prior to and during those periods were produced by Dr. Wilson and Dr. Carter who were both called as witnesses. Oral evidence was also given by Mr. James Pearce who married Mrs. Dawson in October 1988, but who has been living apart from her since February 1989. The Tribunal was further assisted by production of files kept by the solicitor of the deceased and the solicitor for Mrs. Dawson, a Mrs. James who also gave oral evidence. Mrs. Dawson also called her mother Mrs. Hey concerning her peripheral involvement in the matrimonial relationship as it subsisted in 1987.

6. The Act does not empower the Commissioner to take evidence or even to interview parties for the purpose of arriving at the kind of decision he is required by the Act to make. It is not surprising therefore that he found difficulty in determining some of the questions which arose in the course of his investigations. It is equally not surprising that this Tribunal, which is empowered by its Act to take evidence and hear witnesses, had before it a great deal of information which was never in the possession of the Commissioner.

7. For these reasons we do not consider it helpful to review the manner in which the Commissioner approached his task, but rather to consider all the material now available and decide de novo what is the correct and preferable decision. Suffice it to say that in cases of this nature we are very conscious of the exceptional difficulties which may face the Commissioner especially in the light of the handicaps to which we have referred.

8. The relevant provisions of the Act are as follows:-

Section 3(1)(a) defines "spouse" in the following terms:-

"... in relation to a person who has died and

was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee or

a retirement pensioner, means -

(a) a person who was legally married to the deceased person at

the time of the person's death and who, at that time, was

living with the person on a permanent and bona fide domestic

basis."

9. Section 3(2) qualifies that provision in the following terms:-

"Where the Commissioner is of the opinion that a

person, would, but for a temporary absence or an

absence resulting from illness or

infirmity, have been living with another person at any

time on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis,

the first-mentioned person shall, for the purposes of

the definition of "spouse" in sub-section (1) of this

section, and of section 9, be deemed to have been

living with the other person on a permanent and bona

fide domestic basis at that time."

10. It was not contended on behalf of Mrs. Dawson that she was entitled to benefits as a spouse by virtue of the definition in Section 3(1)(b) of the Act as a person who was wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death. The Commissioner had previously been asked to so find in the alternative, but had properly declined to do so.

11. It was further conceded that Mrs. Dawson could not claim that she was a person who could be deemed to have been living with her husband at the time of his death, but for "a temporary absence".

12. Thus it was common ground that the basic question for determination by the Tribunal was whether it was of the opinion that "but for an absence resulting from illness, Mrs. Dawson would have been living with her husband on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of his death".

13. There was considerable uncontradicted evidence concerning a mental illness suffered by the deceased from time to time during the years preceding his death. There was also much evidence as to the effect of this illness on his behaviour and the effect of that conduct upon his wife. Finally there was much evidence as to the gradual breakdown of the marriage which was of course relevant to the Tribunal's ultimate task of arriving at an opinion as to the question posed by section 3(2).

14. One of the affidavits sworn by Mrs. Dawson (as Mrs. Pearce) for the purpose of these proceedings included as annexures 13 letters or notes sent or taken by the deceased to his wife when she was living alone at 71 Federal Street at some time after February 1987. They all reveal a desire on his part for reconciliation. Annexed to that affidavit sworn on 2 August 1990 is a copy of an affidavit she made on 23 April 1987 when she was threatening through her lawyer to seek an order of restraint under s.106A of the Justices Act (Tas) 1959. Although never filed, that affidavit outlines the basis upon which she was at least seriously considering seeking such an order.

15. The best evidence as to the date when the deceased wrote some of these letters to his wife is a note made by the solicitor Mr. McLeod of a conversation he had on 10 April 1987 with her solicitor Mrs. James:-

"She says Dawson written number of letters to her

client in last week completely contradictory although

only days apart".

However, there is nothing in any of the letters produced of a contradictory nature. Perhaps there were others not produced.

16. Mrs. Dawson also swore an affidavit for the purpose of the present proceedings on 29 June 1990 to which were annexed copies of all relevant correspondence which passed between the parties' solicitors between 27 March 1987 and 31 July 1987.

17. The solicitors' files reveal that Mrs. Dawson consulted Mrs. James on 9 October 1986, on 25 February 1987 and many subsequent occasions. Due to difficulty in interpreting the notes Mrs. James made of those interviews she was called to read from those passages upon which reliance was placed by the applicant's counsel. The notes made by Mr. McLeod did not require any oral evidence. His file contains a letter from the deceased to his solicitor dated 2 May 1987.

18. In 1981 the deceased became the patient of Dr. Wilson, a senior psychiatrist employed by the Mental Health Services Commission. His report to the Coroner sworn on 7 october 1988 appears as T29 (pages 112-119). Dr. Wilson answered a number of searching questions put to him by the Commissioner in a letter dated 11 November 1988 (T21 pages 76-79). His reply dated 2 December 1988 appears as T23. Dr. Wilson's records are marked Exhibit 5 and include another report dated 17 February 1987 (p 37-39/T5) which he made to Dr. Young, a Commonwealth Medical Officer who was required to report on Mr. Dawson's medical fitness for continued duty as a radio technical officer. Exhibit 5 contains notes made by Mr. Wilson of meetings with the deceased and/or Mrs. Dawson between January 1982 and 22 July 1987.

19. The facts which the Tribunal has found after considering all sworn and documentary evidence now follow.

20. The deceased was born on 20 October 1943 and was married for the third time on 23 February 1980, after a de facto relationship with his new wife of about a year. He was by all accounts a charming and intelligent man employed by the Department of Transport and Communications as a radio technical officer since July 1975. He was promoted in December 1982 to a responsible position at the Hobart Airport involving the safety of aircraft operations.

21. Unfortunately during the last ten years of his previous marriage he developed signs of progressive alcoholism which merged into psychotic problems resulting in admissions to an alcohol and drug dependency institution on three occasions during 1980. He was at first diagnosed as schizophrenic, but later in 1984 he was classified as suffering a bipolar affective disorder. That diagnosis remained the opinion of Dr. Wilson until the time of his death.

22. Dr. Wilson said that in later years he was more often depressed than elated and that his manic phases were infrequent, although they clearly showed "perceptual disturbance paranoid thinking and disinhibition".

23. It is very clear that he possessed a vulnerable personality in terms of self-esteem and close relationships. In his report to the Coroner Dr. Wilson said:-

"He exhibited hostile dependency in his

relationship towards women retaining considerable

hostility towards his second wife and her

resistance to allowing him access to his children and

his third wife often complained of his temperament

and irresponsibility in their marriage, with

occasional violence. He had a low stress

tolerance and often expressed anxiety about his

capacity to do his work."

24. There were five occasions prior to 1987 when the deceased was admitted to hospital for periods of between 10 and 25 days. On each occasion he was treated with drugs for depression, mania or alcohol abuse.

25. On 16 February 1987 his wife persuaded police officers to take him to the Royal Hobart Hospital. She signed an application for him to be placed under observation for three days. Dr. Carter who had not previously seen him certified that he should be admitted on that basis. He was sent from there to the Royal Derwent Hospital as there was no room in Ward 6A which is kept for mentally ill patients.

26. On 23 February 1987 he was told by a Doctor that he could be discharged the next day. However, the Doctor changed his mind and on 25 February when the deceased was insisting on discharge against medical recommendation, Mrs. Dawson said that she would not have him at the matrimonial home, 71 Federal Street. He therefore left of his own accord, against medical advice and went to their week-end cottage at Dunalley. He remained living at that address until 5 August 1987 when he voluntarily admitted himself to the Royal Hobart Hospital. He was on day leave from there when he committed suicide by carbon monoxide poisoning on 23 August.

27. The parties to the marriage did not live together on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at any time between 16 February and 23 August 1987. In actual fact their separation as a married couple appears to have begun earlier than that.

28. Mrs. Dawson told her solicitor on 9 October 1986 that since 5 September 1986 she was sleeping by herself on the couch. She said that as far as she was concerned the marriage was over and that "it could only be assisted by counselling". She complained of alcoholism, threatened violence and filthy abuse. She maintained that this situation existed by reason of his mental condition aggravated by alcohol.

29. When Mrs. Dawson swore the affidavit of 23 April 1987 she said that between August and December 1986 her husband was drinking virtually every day and continued to demonstrate aggressive behaviour towards her. She admitted to a slight improvement of about two weeks at the end of December. This presumably followed a 10 day period when he was in the Royal Hobart Hospital until 20 December.

30. In evidence before the Tribunal Mrs. Dawson said that they were living together as man and wife between October and 7 February 1987, but we are not satisfied that during that time there was a continuing bona fide domestic relationship between them. She said in her affidavit of 23 April 1987 that on or about 7 February he agreed to leave the house and to live at Dunalley, but returned 5 days later in "a highly upset and unstable condition". We were surprised that under cross-examination Mrs. Dawson had no recollection of any detail concerning the manner in which the deceased behaved during those days leading up to her decision on 16 February to call the police and have him placed in hospital. Her lack of recall can perhaps be explained by other matters to which we will have occasion to refer in more detail later.

31. However, whether the termination of the domestic relationship occurred in September 1986 or February 1987 there is no doubt that it did not resume in any respect after the deceased went to reside at Dunalley on 25 February.

32. It also appears to us to be beyond argument that the decision Mrs. Dawson made to terminate the marriage relationship was occasioned in large measure by the effects of her husband's illness upon her. She was no longer able to cope with its consequences. The difficulties which now face Mrs. Dawson concern other matters which followed that decision.

33. We have said that Mrs. Dawson consulted her solicitor on 9 October 1986. The interviews on that day occupied about 3 hours. She gave full details of her earnings and property.

34. The information she gave her solicitor was obviously for the purpose of receiving advice as to her entitlement in the event of a breakdown of the marriage. She told Mrs. James of the substantial financial assistance which had been given to them both by her father, Mr. Hey (who lent $30,000 free of interest to assist in providing a purchase price of $50,000 when he sold 71 Federal Street to her) and by Mr. Dawson Senior (who lent $14,000 free of interest which was used (indirectly) to assist in the purchase). Mrs. Dawson also gave full details of motor cars, chattels and debts. We have no doubt that at that time she was preparing herself for the probable failure of the marriage.

35. However, she did make an attempt to save the marriage by attending counselling at the Family Court which resulted in a written agreement on 16 October 1986 (annexure H to Mrs. Dawson's affidavit of 29 June 1990). We believe that the deceased failed to comply with the most important term of that agreement i.e. to continue to attend counselling sessions.

36. The interviews which Mrs. Dawson had with her solicitor on 25 February and more importantly on four occasions in March 1987 make it very clear that Mrs. Dawson made up her mind during those weeks (if she had not previously so decided) that her only course was to move for a final settlement of all property and financial matters. The legal correspondence between the solicitors began on 27 March 1987. Mr. McLeod then said that his client was surprised at the separation which began when he was discharged from hospital. He requested "joint marriage counselling before the total and irretrievable breakdown of the marriage has occurred". He sought information about an intended sale of Federal Street and warned of the possibility of a Family Court injunction should an agreement for sale be entered into.

37. Mrs. James replied on 2 April 1987 stating her client's instructions about a property settlement which the parties had discussed. She spoke of her own knowledge of previous difficulties and attempts at reconciliation and said: "Unfortunately this time the marriage has irretrievably broken down and as there appears to be an imminent sale of the home it would be in parties interests that that go ahead and the joint debts discharged before distribution of proceeds".

38. It is of significance that the proposal for settlement involved each party receiving real estate free of debt and the deceased keeping all monies due to him by way of annual and long service leave (about $7,000) and all superannuation contributions (and benefits). Such a proposal is indicative of finality not merely, as Mrs. Dawson claimed, as a means of resolving financial problems. She refers to this in paragraphs 11, 12, 13 and 14 of her affidavit of 29 June 1990. Her assertion that "at this stage neither of us had taken legal advice" is difficult to believe, but may be intended to refer to the consequences of a house sale. There is evidence in her instructions to her solicitor on 6 March 1987 and the letter which she wrote his father on the same date (pages 28-30 of exhibit 8) that she had discussed sale with her husband on Monday 2 March and that he then appeared to agree to a sale. She spoke to his father in Sydney the next day by telephone and then, after she had seen her solicitor suggested in a letter to him that the best solution to the marital problems would be for John to sell the Dunalley property and live in Sydney on superannuation and monies he would then have for investment.

39. There is little doubt that Mrs. Dawson was resolved to treat the marriage as finished from this time on. She confirmed in evidence that the letter her solicitor wrote on 2 April was in accordance with her instructions. Although the words "irretrievably broken down" have well known legal implications we are nevertheless persuaded that they truly represent the state of mind of Mrs. Dawson at the time they were written.

40. That she was the party whose decision sealed the fate of the marriage becomes more apparent when the evidence regarding the attitude of the deceased to reconciliation between February and August is carefully examined.

41. There is no doubt that during the months of March and April he earnestly desired reconciliation and made many moves in this direction. He wrote her genuine letters seeking forgiveness for his conduct, and he frequently met her at church in Hobart.

42. His visits to Dr. Wilson also provide acceptable evidence of his wishes. On 12 May 1987 Dr. Wilson recorded that "the reality of insecurity about his employment and apparent end to his marriage is at last getting through to him, had previously only superficial acceptance and was in fact denying the probable outcome". On 27 May he noted "nothing has changed in his circumstances and reality of his situation not pleasant ..."

43. Dr. Wilson referred in his report to the Coroner of Mr. Dawson's disappointment in June about his lack of reconciliation with his wife.

44. In his report to the Coroner Dr. Wilson described his fluctuating state during the last eighteen days in hospital. He said that his behaviour was not psychotic during that time. Daily hospital notes of his mood lead us to the conclusion that his belief that his marriage was lost was itself a major factor in producing his suicide (e.g. 18 August: "preoccupied with loss of love, lack of love and no future love. Claims that he has not cried since separation with wife. Attempted to cry tonight but to no avail - ")

45. There could be no stronger single indication against the possibility of reconciliation than the fact of the suicide itself. It was a final demonstration of his acceptance that his wife would have nothing of a reconciliation as she had so clearly shown.

46. Dr. Wilson was asked by the Commissioner (T21) for his assessment of the likelihood of a reconciliation and eventual cohabitation after separation. He replied that he did not think it was a likely outcome after March 1987 "as Mrs. Dawson had expressed the view that she could no longer have him home and that legal proceedings were being undertaken in terms of property settlement ... (sentences omitted) I do not think there were any possible circumstances that would lead to reconciliation as the matter had progressed so far." (T23) The passage we have omitted appears later at paragraph 50.

47. Mr. Avery asked the Tribunal to find that Mrs. Dawson remained hopeful throughout the period of separation in 1987 that a reconciliation could occur if her husband's mental condition could improve. She claimed that she saw the deceased at least every week until his death. Whilst this may have been true of March and April it is apparent to us that any remote hopes for any improvement in his mental condition became non-existent by the end of April. There is no hint of the possibility of a reconciliation in the letter of Mrs. James of 24 April 1987 which made "a more than fair offer in full and final settlement of all property". On the previous day an affidavit was sworn by Mrs. Dawson which must be taken to represent her firm and fixed view of the state which the marriage had reached by that date. In her instructions for the preparation of that affidavit she said:-

"I can no longer cope with the emotional strain

and violence in the marriage. Our financial position

is now quite serious ... I am tired of the harrasments

and now apply for sole occupancy of the Federal

Street house separation and restraining orders as I

am still afraid of what he may do to me

or my family ..."

The fact that she did nothing to seek the restraining order she was preparing for on that day in no way detracts from the accuracy of the affidavit as evidencing a final breakdown of the marriage in her mind.

48. Moreover Mrs. James received no instructions in 1987 to make any moves in the direction of a reconciliation. Indeed she thought that her client had done all that could be reasonably expected of her in October 1986. Dr. Wilson appears to have agreed with that view and clearly expressed his understanding of her final decision to terminate the marriage relationship.

49. We are not able to accept the evidence of Mrs. Dawson that she continued to see her husband "at least every week until his death" (paragraph 17 - affidavit 29 June 1990) excluding his final period in hospital when, she says in paragraph 19 "he did not wish to see me".

Relationship with Mr. Pearce

50. On 21 December 1987 the applicant swore a statutory declaration (T8) in which she stated:-

"That it has been stated to me that it is public

knowledge and readily acknowledged by the parties

that a de facto husband and wife relationship has

existed between Dorothy Lynette Dawson and

one Jim Pearce (or Pierce) since some date prior to

the death of John Neville Dawson."

That was sent to the Commissioner on 6 January 1988.

51. On 11 November 1988 (T22) the Commissioner asked Mrs. Dawson's solicitors for replies to a number of questions put for the purpose of his reconsideration of the decision of 24 November 1987. They referred to the allegation above and asked this question.:-

"Would your client please advise if the above

statement is correct, and if so, when her

association (not necessarily a de facto

relationship) began with the said gentleman."

In his reply of 16 May 1989 (T24) Mrs. Dawson's solicitor said:-

"In relation to the suggestion by Mrs. Hornsey

that it was public knowledge and readily acknowledged

by the parties that our client was having a de facto

relationship at the time with a Mr.

James Pearce this is stoutly refuted by our client and

indeed she instructs us that at the relevant time her

daughter Kate Bruce was residing with her at Federal

Street and certainly she was not living in any de

facto situation."

No mention was made as to when her association with Mr. Pearce began.

On 2 December 1988 in his report to the Commissioner (T23) Dr. Wilson said:-

"Mrs. Dawson's mother had informed me in May that

Mrs. Dawson had formed a relationship with another

man. I did not see Mrs. Dawson after March 1987".

(This is the omitted portion of the reply referred to paragraph 45 above)

52. In evidence Mrs. Hey confirmed that on Mother's Day in May 1987 she learnt accidentally of her daughter's association with Mr. Pearce and she informed Dr. Wilson (only) of that fact.

53. In paragraph 20 of the affidavit of 29 June 1990 Mrs. Dawson again "denied most vehemently" the allegation of the applicant concerning a de facto relationship. Of her association with Mr. Pearce before her husband's death she claimed "we were friends" and thereby at least by omission, failed to reveal any sexual association with Mr. Pearce at that time.

54. Mrs. Dawson commenced living in a de facto relationship with Mr. Pearce in November 1987 and they were eventually married on 15 October 1988. However, they separated on 13 February 1989 and have never resumed cohabitation. Two days later Mr. Pearce sought an appointment with and saw the applicant's solicitor a Mr. Crisp. He there produced a small pocket diary which enabled him to give certain information as to his association with Mrs. Dawson. Mr. Crisp prepared a draft statutory declaration which appears as T25. On the following day Mr. Pearce recovered the diary which Mr. Crisp had retained and declined to sign the declaration.

55. When Mr. Avery opened the case for Mrs. Dawson before the Tribunal he said:-

"She will say in quite unequivocal terms that

(her relationship with James Pearce) did not prior to

the death of her husband move from being one of friendship".

56. Before Mrs. Dawson gave evidence Mr. Pearce who was summoned to attend by the Tribunal at the request of the applicant's solicitors, was called to answer the suummons, but relieved from further attendance until later in the hearing. He said on that occasion that he had destroyed the diary which he had shown Mr. Crisp.

57. During her evidence Mrs. Dawson admitted that she had had a sexual relationship with Mr. Pearce during a period of some months before August 1987. She said that she could not remember dates, but thought it began about March. She maintained the relationship was "nothing more than an affair". She made every effort to dismiss it as being of little importance.

58. Mr. Pearce subsequently gave evidence and was examined by both counsel. We are satisfied by his evidence that the sexual relationship began in January 1987, and that he had sexual relations with Mrs. Dawson at Marion Bay Beach on Hobart Regatta day which was 10 February 1987. We are also satisfied that the relationship was pursued during the following months with considerable regularity and maintained with secrecy. Mrs. Dawson never mentioned it to her solicitor (not unnaturally) and her parents did not learn of it until May.

59. The finding as to 10 February is, we consider, an indication of the reason for Mrs. Dawson's failure to remember any detail as to the dates when her "affair" began or any details of the circumstances preceding the deceased's admission to hospital during the days before 16 February. We do not believe that she has no recollection of these matters which were obviously significant ones in her life.

60. Mr. Pearce admitted that he volunteered evidence to Mr. Crisp to spite Mrs. Dawson for terminating the marriage relationship and said that he had destroyed the diary as a condition of an agreement he subsequently made with Mrs. Dawson about their affairs. The diary, he said, and we so find, contained true and correct entries and was never tampered with for the purpose of creating false evidence.

61. Thus we conclude from these facts that Mrs. Dawson's resolve to terminate the marriage which first became evident in February 1987 was continually reinforced by the existence of a close relationship with Mr. Pearce. After March she made no further effort to consult with Dr. Wilson concerning her husband's health. She made no effort to advance his repeated requests for reconciliation. She repeatedly affirmed her desire for a full and final settlement of all financial and property affairs and armed her solicitors with the material necessary to seek a restraining order should her husband exhibit signs of violence or harrassment.

62. It was only the failure of the intending purchaser to complete the contract of sale and the absence of any further unacceptable conduct by her husband from May to July which were responsible for the lull in activity during those months.

Conclusions

63. We have noted earlier that the Tribunal is required to form an opinion as to the likelihood of the parties living together on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of death "but for an absence resulting from illness".

64. It is not sufficient to find that an absence from such a relationship began six months prior to death or that it began as a result of illness. The crucial question is the formation of an opinion as to the prospects of such a relationship existing at the time of death but for an absence from illness.

65. That task necessarily involves taking account of all the circumstances that are relevant to that question including the history of the marriage, the history of the illness which was clearly present at the time when the final separation began and all relevant events which occurred during the following six months.

66. We find that the mental illness of the deceased existed throughout the marriage, but that it was frequently quiescent. We do not regard the circumstances surrounding two previous separations and subsequent reconciliations as helpful in determining the prospects of a third reconciliation. We do not regard the circumstances of previous periods of hospitalization prior to 1987 as assisting us in forming the required opinion, except that all these events and many others combined to bring about two very clear conclusions.

67. Mrs. Dawson had resolved by April 1987 that she was no longer interested in attempting to save the marriage. For her it was finished and all that remained was for property affairs to be settled when the contract for sale of Federal Street was completed.

68. Dr. Wilson had come to the conclusion that any prospect of a reconciliation was unreal and that Mrs. Dawson was being realistic in treating the marriage as finished. Likewise he saw no prospect of any recovery by the deceased from his illness.

69. The illness from which the deceased was suffering throughout the marriage was of an intermittent nature. It was aggravated by alcohol abuse and marital unhappiness. It never physically prevented the parties living together except during short periods when he was placed in hospital either for observation or to ensure that he received the proper medication. It was essentially controllable.

70. During questioning by Mr. Avery, Dr. Wilson gave the following replies:-

"And from your knowledge of this family - Mr. and

Mrs. Dawson - there was nothing you were told by

either of them to lead you to believe that the

separation was other than as a result of Mr.

Dawson's illness? --- It is very difficult to say

other than. I would certainly agree that his illness

was a large factor in it, but whether it was the

total reason, I am not sure. I think that there are

times when people simply run out of compassion,

or whatever, for each other.

Yes. But from your understanding, if that

occurred in this case, it was primarily as a result of

the illness of Mr. Dawson? --- Largely so, yes.

I am not asking you to say whether it was 90 per

cent the reason, or 85, or 65 or whatever, but in

concrete terms - in layman's terms,

the illness caused the separation? --- Probably, yes.

Yes? --- It is not definite."

71. Later questions were put to Dr. Wilson on that subject by the Tribunal:-

"You were asked by Mr. Avery whether or not it

was his illness which caused the separation and you

said, "Probably." I take it that it was only during

the periods that he was in hospital in February and

August 87 that his illness actually

prevented him from being with his wife? --- I believe

so, sir, yes.

And that in relation to the other periods, the

separation appears to have been a question for his

wife as to what her attitude was to them living

together because he wanted a reconciliation? --

-- I believe initially it was a mutual agreement in

which he would go down to the shack as he had - - -

Yes. Well, whether it was mutual or otherwise,

her decision was essential for there to be

cohabitation? --- For him to come

back. Yes, indeed.

So that when you say that illness probably caused

the separation, you seem to me to be expressing an

opinion about the effect of his illness upon her? -

-- Yes, that would be correct."

72. These passages help to satisfy us that the absence which began as a result of illness did not continue for that reason alone. Illness never prevented the resumption of cohabitation after the deceased was discharged from Royal Derwent. The only fact which prevented his return was the clearly expressed refusal of his wife to entertain such a prospect. True in the broad sense the absence continued to result from illness because that is what began it, but it continued for an even more cogent reason i.e. the decision of Mrs. Dawson to finalise all matters which touched upon the marriage relationship including the sale of the matrimonial home, the equitable distribution of all chattels, and the maintenance of a regular sexual relationship with another man, albeit without the knowledge of her husband.

73. In his submission to the Tribunal Mr. Whithear adopted the approach taken by the Commissioner when he reconsidered his original decision. He urged that in determining whether there was an absence resulting from illness he could do so by determining whether the illness was the "proximate or direct cause" of the absence. (Paragragh 43 of T2)

74. Mr. Avery was also willing to adopt the words "primary", "substantial" or "overriding" as describing the main reason for the separation. He also said that the illness remained "the underlying" reason for the absence regardless of either the facts relating to Mr. Pearce or the lawyers (i.e. their correspondence).

75. Mr. Slicer preferred the word "substantial" when referring to causes, but stressed that the existence of illness must remain a continuing substantial cause of separation to the time of death.

76. We prefer not to select any epithet to describe the nature of the cause of the absence because it is unnecessary to do so. The Act uses no such word. The law reports abound with difficulties which have been occasioned by their use in statutes. It is not for us to add words to the statute and then try to interpret them.

77. What must be remembered is that we are not endeavouring to ascertain how many causes there were for the existence of an absence from cohabitation at the time of death, but whether in our opinion there was at that time a likelihood of resumption of cohabitation if absence resulting from illness were removed.

78. If it is conceded that when the separation began there was an immediate "absence resulting from illness", it still remains to determine whether the addition of other factors contributing materially to the continued absence precludes the possibility of a finding that but for the absence resulting from illness the necessary state of cohabitation would have been resumed. An answer to that question must depend on the nature of and the weight to be attributed to the additional factors.

79. We have found two such weighty factors one of which merges in the other. The existence of the relationship with Mr. Pearce was itself a factor which must have contributed to Mrs. Dawson's basic decision i.e. to terminate her marriage for all practical purposes. The nature of that decision, accepted as final and precluding as it did any real possibility of resumption, was of itself sufficient to provide an answer to the question posed by the statute. The Tribunal could not conceivably find a likelihood of a resumption of the marriage if it were satisfied that one party to the marriage had in reality so firmly rejected any reasonable possibility of its continuance. This is our view and therefore Mrs. Dawson must fail to qualify as a "spouse".

80. The decision under review is set aside and the matter remitted to the Commissioner for appropriate finding in relation to each of the children of the applicant and the deceased. There will be liberty to each party to apply.
