Re Taxation Appeals [1993] AATA 238; (1993) 26 ATR 1194 (1993) 93 ATC 364, (1993) Aust Sales Tax Cases 85-168 (30 July 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT92/120

AAT No. 8882

Number of pages - 9

Sales Tax

[1993] AATA 238; (1993) 26 ATR 1194

(1993) 93 ATC 364, (1993) Aust Sales Tax Cases 85-168

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.H. PASCOE (MEMBER)

CATCHWORDS

Sales Tax - goods applied to own use - samples of manufactured and imported products - when applied to own use - value for sales tax purposes of goods applied to own use as samples.

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 5) 1930

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 6) 1930

Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Stewart and Another [1984] HCA 11; (1984) 154 CLR 385

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 14 May 1993

30:7:1993

Counsel for the applicant: Mr de Wijn

Solicitors for the applicant: Mr J. Murray, Minter Ellisn

Morris Fletcher

Counsel for the respondent: Mr Davies

Solicitors for the respondent: Ms Browyn Simmonds, Australian

Government Solicitors Office

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent for reassessment in accordance with the Tribunal's findings.

DECISION

B.H. PASCOE This application is for the review of a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow an objection against an assessment of sales tax in respect of the period from 1 August 1987 to 31 December 1990.

2. The assessment was issued on 25 January 1991 pursuant to Section 25(2A) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930, and related to the deemed value of greeting cards and wrapping paper manufactured or imported and applied to the taxpayer company's own use as samples.

3. The basic facts of this matter were not in dispute. The taxpayer company is a manufacturer and importer of greeting cards and wrapping paper. In the course of that business, samples of its products are given to sales representatives and displayed in six showrooms throughout Australia. With manufactured cards, approximately 160 cards are taken from each production run and delivered to the sampling department. Wrapping paper is also taken out of production, cut into A4 size and approximately 160 pieces delivered to the sampling department. From each design of imported cards, again approximately 160 are removed and delivered to the sampling department. On arrival in the sampling department, the cards have an adhesive sticker affixed showing the design number and the words "sample only, no commercial value" are stamped on the back of the card. Each A4 size sheet of wrapping paper has an adhesive sticker affixed showing the design number. For both cards and wrapping paper, the 160 samples of each design are stored in a box or bin until ready for release. When the sampling department is advised that a particular design is to be released, the department distributes three samples to each of six showrooms and one sample to each of the company's sales representatives, of which there are between 120 and 130. The remaining samples are retained for use as replacement samples until the particular design is taken off the market and then they are sent to be pulped.

4. Following an audit of the taxpayer company's Sales Tax return by the Australian Taxation Office, it was requested by letter of 3 August 1990 to lodge a supplementary return covering the period August 1987 to August 1990 to include, among other things, stock manufactured and imported and applied to its own use as samples. The taxpayer company lodged a supplementary return on 21 January 1991. The Commissioner of Taxation did not agree with the value placed on the sample goods by the taxpayer company and issued an assessment pursuant to Section 25(2A) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930, as amended ("Assessment Act No. 1"). It is this assessment to which the taxpayer company objected.

5. Before the Tribunal, Mr de Wijn for the taxpayer company readily conceded that the cards and wrapping paper provided as samples were goods applied by the taxpayer company to its own use. However, as Mr Davies, for the Commissioner of Taxation agreed, the question to be resolved was in two parts. Firstly, at what point in time were the goods so applied and, secondly, what was the value for sales tax purposes of the goods so applied.

6. Section 17(1) of Assessment Act No. 1 states:-

"Subject to, and in accordance with the provisions of this Act, the

sales tax imposed by the Sales Tax Act (No. 1) 1930 shall be levied

and paid upon the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia by a

taxpayer and sold by him or treated by him as stock for sale by retail

or applied to his own use".

Whilst it was common ground that the relevant goods were applied to the taxpayer company's own use, the parties differed as to the time when they were so applied. The Commission took the view that the goods were applied to the company's own use when they were extracted or selected at the time of production or from the imported stock and transferred to the sampling department.

7. The taxpayer company argued that the goods were only so applied when actually distributed by the sampling department to showrooms and sales representatives.

8. Interestingly, counsel for both parties took comfort from the decision of the High Court in the case of Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Stewart and Another [1984] HCA 11; (1984) 154 CLR 385. In that case a supplier of lottery tickets also manufactured machines which could be used by its customers to sell the tickets mechanically. It supplied a number of machines to certain public benevolent institutions free of charge. The machines remained the property of the supplier. The question for the Court was whether the machines were applied by the manufacturer to his own use or were goods for use and not for sale by a public benevolent institution and, therefore, exempt from sales tax. It was held, by majority, that the machines were not applied to the manufacturer's own use, and they did not go into use, when they were made and kept ready for subsequent delivery to a public benevolent institution but they were applied to use only when supplied to such an institution. in his reasons Gibbs CJ said (at p 393):

"The word "applied" means "devoted to "or" employed for the special

purpose of" (Max Factor and Co Inc v Federal Commissioner of Taxation,

(1971) 124 CLR at p 362) and although in some cases goods may no

doubt be applied to the use of a person simply by keeping them in

reserve - eg. by keeping spare parts for use in the persons vehicles

or machinery - in the circumstances of the present case the

application of the machines to the use of the respondents occurred

when they were devoted to the respondent's purposes by supplying them

to the institutions. In other words, the respondent did not apply the

machine to their own use, and the machines did not go into use, when

they were made and kept ready for subsequent delivery to one of the

institutions. While the machines were so held, they were only of

potential use to the respondents, and they were not in fact used at

all."

Later (at p 394) he said:

"...until a machine was supplied, it could not be said whether or not

the supply would amount to an application to the use of the

respondents. When a machine was supplied to an institution which was

or would become the purchaser of the respondents' tickets it was so

applied. This conclusion is consistent with the decisions in Deputy

Commissioner of Taxation v Taubmans (NSW) Pty Ltd [1966] HCA 18; (1966) 115 CLR 570

where it was held that the defendant applied the colour cards to its

own use either when it used them directly or when it made them

available to the store keeper who stocked its goods (at p 574), and

with Max Factor and Co v Federal Commissioner of Taxation where it was

held that the company applied the cosmetics to its own use when it

gave them to its employees or to buyers employed by departmental

stores ((1971) 124 CLR at p 365)."

9. For the Commissioner it was argued that the circumstances of this case differ significantly from the facts in Stewart's case (supra). Here, it was said, a product that has been manufactured and would otherwise be sold is taken by the manufacturer, stamped "sample" in the case of a card or cut up in the case of wrapping paper, placed in a bin and clearly earmarked for use in advertising. However, in my view, the earmarking of goods for a specific use is not applying them to that use. They were not "devoted to" or "employed for the special purpose of" the manufacturer's own use until actually used for the purpose for which they were so earmarked.

10. In many respects it can be said that the sample stocks held for subsequent release by the sampling department were little different from the stocks of cards and wrapping paper held in the taxpayer company's warehouse for future sale and which would become liable to sales tax only when sold. Although the samples could no longer be used for sale they were, nevertheless, not put to the company's own use in advertising until provided to showrooms and employees for display to prospective customers. It follows that the cards and wrapping paper which were never so used but retained in the sampling department or pulped are not subject to sales tax pursuant to Section 17(1) of Assessment Act No. 1.

11. The next part of the question is the value for sales tax purposes of the cards and wrapping paper which were applied to the taxpayer company's own use when released by the sampling department. As pointed out by Mr de Wijn and conceded by Mr Davies, the Commissioner erred in including the value of imported cards based on his interpretation of Section 18(3) of Assessment Act No. 1.

12. Imported goods are dealt with in Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 6) 1930 as which has its own specific method of calculating sale value. Section 3 of the Assessment Act No. 6 states:

"Subject to, and in accordance with, the provisions of this Act, the

sales tax imposed by the Sales Tax Act (No. 6) 1930 shall be levied

and paid upon the sale value of goods imported into Australia by a

taxpayer and sold by him or applied by him to his own use."

13. Section 4(1A) of the Act then provides:

"For the purposes of this Act the sale value of goods imported by a

registered person who quoted his certificate in respect of the entry

of those goods for home consumption, and who applies those goods to

his own use, shall be the amount which would have been the sale value

of those goods for purpose of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 5)

1930-1931 if that person has not so quoted his certificate."

14. When we then go to Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 5) 1930 we find that Section 4(1) of that Act sets out a specific calculation as follows:

"For the purposes of this Act, the sale value of imported goods that

are entered for home consumption by -

(a) an unregistered person; or

(b) a registered person who does not quote his certificate in respect

of the entry,

shall be an amount equal to 120% of the sum of -

(c) the customs value of the goods as determined in accordance with

Division 2 of Part VIII of the Customs Act; and

(d) the amount of customs duty (if any) that is, or may become,

payable in respect of the goods."

15. It is quite clear that the value for sales tax of the imported cards is calculated by ascertaining the customs value of the cards, adding the customs duty and then increasing the total by 20%. In the hearing it was acknowledged for the Commissioner that the inclusion of the imported cards on the same basis as Australian manufactured cards in the relevant assessment was incorrect. However, it was said to have been done because the figures were not supplied by the taxpayer company. There was simply an estimate that the overseas content of the greeting cards used for sample purposes was between 10 and 15 per cent.

16. Irrespective of any reasons for not following the legislative rules for calculating sales tax on imported cards, the decision of the Commissioner in respect of this element of the objection to the assessment must be set aside and remitted back to the Commissioner for reassessment in accordance with this decision. Given that the relevant information is in possession of the taxpayer company, the necessary details should be provided to the Commissioner as soon as practicable.

17. In relation to the cards and wrapping paper manufactured by the taxpayer company, Section 18(3) of Assessment Act No. 1 sets out the basis of assessing the sale value of goods applied to own use. It states:

"For the purposes of this Act, the sale value of goods manufactured

by any person and applied to his own use shall be the amount for

which those goods could reasonably be expected to be sold by the

manufacturer by wholesale."

18. It is appropriate to note that this sub-section was amended by Act No. 88 of 1988, effective 23 August 1988. Consequently, part of the period which was subject to the assessment objected to was governed by the sub-section prior to amendment. The sub-subsection formerly read:-

"For the purpose of this Act, the sale value of goods manufactured

by any person and applied to his own use shall be:-

(a) if the goods so applied by the manufacturer are of a class

which the manufacturer himself sells by wholesale - the amount

for which those goods could reasonably be expected to be sold

by the manufacturer by wholesale; or

(b) in any other case - the amount for which the manufacturer could

reasonably be expected to purchase identical goods from another

manufacturer if the other manufacturer had, in the ordinary

course of his business, manufactured the identical goods for

sale and had sold them to the first mentioned manufacturer by

wholesale."

19. Apart from one witness for the taxpayer company who had worked in the sampling department for approximately 18 years and gave evidence on the procedure for receiving and issuing cards and wrapping paper samples, the other witnesses for the taxpayer company gave evidence aimed at supporting the argument by the taxpayer company that the sample cards and wrapping paper had little or no market value. The sales and administration manager gave evidence that all cards and wrapping paper sold by the taxpayer company are sold in unit quantities which, in the case of cards, are packs of either 5 or 10 cards together with envelopes or, in the case of wrapping paper, are packs of 10 or 15 sheets. He maintained that there is no market for individual cards without envelopes and that the taxpayer company had never sold individual cards. On the other hand, the company allows full credit to retailers who return unsold stock either in units or loose cards. Complete units are sometimes re-sold but all loose cards are dumped or pulped. He also gave evidence that the sample cards are identical to cards included in a unit including the printed recommended retail price. The only distinguishing feature of the sample card is the rubber stamp impression and batch number sticker.

20. The materials manager for the taxpayer company gave evidence as to the treatment of cards returned from retailers. He stated that the taxpayer company's own cards are thrown into a dump bin and sent away to be pulped. Cards manufactured by other competitors are also taken back and credited to the retailer but are retained and sold to a buyer at approximately $30 per 1000 cards. These cards are without envelopes.

21. An owner of newsagencies and a card shop gave evidence that there is little or no market for individual cards. They are always purchased in units of 5 or 10 complete with envelopes. On receipt of the cards ordered from the manufacturer, staff of the retailer remove the packaging, place each envelope inside the relevant card and place the groups of cards on a display rack. He stated that some six or seven years ago he did purchase a quantity of cards without envelopes for a price of between 3 and 5 cents per card but was unsuccessful in selling sufficient numbers of these cards.

22. The only witness called by the Commissioner was a Taxation Office auditor who prepared the calculation for the issue of the relevant assessment. She gave evidence that the calculation was based on information supplied by the taxpayer company. The calculation assumed a standard number of cards taken as samples in each period and then multiplied by the average retail price per card. The total average retail price was then reduced by average retail profit mark up to reduce the figure to estimated wholesale price. This was then reduced by 13.4% as the estimated cost of packing and envelopes and a further allowance of between 9.8% and 10.1% for discounts and rebates allowed to customers. The net result was said to be the net wholesale price for which the goods could reasonably be expected to be sold.

23. A similar calculation was done for wrapping paper, except that the number of samples (160) was divided by four to produce the number of sheets taken from production prior to being cut into the four sample A4 size sheets.

24. Both parties accepted that the question of value of the Australian manufactured goods fell to be determined by the wording of Section 18(3) of Assessment Act No. 1 and, for the period prior to 23 August 1988, was governed by paragraph (a) of that section as it then was being goods of a class which the manufacturer himself sells by wholesale. However, for the taxpayer company it was argued that it was necessary to ascertain the value of each individual sample card or piece of wrapping paper in the form and condition in which it was when applied to the taxpayer company's own use. That is, a card with a batch number sticker affixed and stamped with the words "sample only, no commercial value" and a quarter size sheet of wrapping paper, again with a numbered sticker affixed. Given the evidence that there was little or no market for individual cards without envelopes, it was said that the amount for which the goods could reasonably be expected to be sold by wholesale was nil. When asked by the Tribunal whether the taxpayer company's submission indicated that manufactured products used as samples could rarely, if ever, be subject to sale tax, Mr de Wijn submitted that it would depend on how they are treated. His response was:

"You have got to look at the particular facts in the particular case.

Nothing could be fairer than to say, you look at the thing at the

particular point of time that it is applied for the use. If it has a

value at that particular point in time, it is fair enough that you pay

sales tax on it. That is what the section is intended to do. If it has
a

value, then presumably you can do something with it. When you cease using

it as a sample you can sell it. And one can think of, perhaps, motor cars

or something like that that retain their value. They can be used as

samples but no one is going to stamp "sample" all over them and make them

unsaleable because of the very nature of the particular product. So I am

not at all inviting the Tribunal to say samples can never to taxable.

What I am inviting the Tribunal to come to is a perfectly fair

construction of the legislation which would only make taxpayers pay sales

tax on samples which had a value. And if it so happens that the samples

do not have a value - and in this case the samples in the end, when they

are all finished, are just scrapped - then why should there be sales tax

payable on them? But in the case of other products, they will have a

value which will be intrinsic in the good and they can be then disposed

of or dealt with."

25. On the other hand, the essence of the Commissioner's submission was that the words of Section 18(3) of Assessment Act No. 1 must be read as meaning the amount for which the goods could reasonably be expected to be sold had they not been applied to the manufacturer's own use.

26. In seeking to interpret Section 18(3) it is appropriate to consider the purpose of the legislation. The purpose is to levy sales tax on the wholesale selling price of manufactured goods. Where a manufacturer treats the manufactured goods as stock for sale by retail or applies them to his own use, sales tax is payable on the wholesale selling price. Sub-section (2) of Section 18 refers to goods treated as stock for sale by retail and sub-section (3) refers to goods applied to own use. The wording of the deemed sale value is the same in both sub-sections ie. "the amount for which those goods could reasonably be expected to be sold by the manufacturer by wholesale". By their very nature, neither form of treatment or application can be expected to result in an actual wholesale sale. In effect, the two provisions require a notional or deemed sale by the manufacturer to himself in the capacity either as a retailer or a consumer. With respect, the interpretation sought to be placed on Section 18(3) by the taxpayer company would result in few, if any, goods applied to own use being subject to sales tax on normal wholesale values.

27. By their very nature, goods applied to own use, when so applied, commonly cease to be identical with the goods normally sold be wholesale. In Mr de Wijn's example of a motor car, the sample car, on application to the manufacturers own use as a sample will become a registered vehicle with a consequent reduced value as a second hand vehicle. It is difficult to contemplate that the legislation intended the provision to apply to the sale value of goods after they had been applied to own use. Many examples can come to mind of goods applied to a manufacturer's own use by being consumed in the manufacturer's operations. For instance, where a manufacturer of detergent takes some of his manufactured product and uses it to clean the canteen dishes, does the fact that it ceases to have any commercial value once so applied mean that there is no sales tax payable?

28. All of the evidence demonstrated that the cards and wrapping paper used by the taxpayer company were identical to the goods normally sold save that the cards had the sticker and stamp affixed, and the wrapping paper was cut into A4 size sheets, presumably to save cost of the samples and for easier handling by the sales representatives. In my view, it is a proper interpretation of Section 18(3) of Assessment Act No. 1 to regard the amount for which those goods could reasonably be expected to be sold by the manufacturer by wholesale as being the amount for which they could have been so sold if they had not been taken out of production and prepared for use as samples.

29. I have indicated earlier that the point of time at which the goods are applied to own use is when they are distributed to showrooms and sales representatives and, therefore, that is the taxing point, I do not see any conflict with the view of the value for sales tax set out above because the goods, prior to that time of application, are treated in a manner which makes them useable only as samples. The value for sales tax purposes does not change, it is simply the point of time at which the sales tax can be levied. In exactly the same way, the cards and wrapping paper which continue through the production line, appropriately packaged and identified as being for sale to customers are subject to sales tax only when so sold.

30. Whilst not in any way conclusive as a measurement of value of the cards used as samples, it is, in my view, relevant that the taxpayer company allows full credit to customers who return any unsold cards including single loose cards. Whilst it was consistently argued by the taxpayer company that the wholesale market value of individual cards was negligible, it itself places a market value on individual cards by allowing credits to customers when any cards, whether of its own manufacture or of competitors, are returned. In other words, the taxpayer company buys back from customers individual cards at full wholesale value. The method of calculation of the appropriate price for individual cards returned for credit was not dealt with in the evidence.

31. The method of calculation of the sales value and resulting sales tax on samples used the Commissioner of Taxation explained in evidence was clearly based on estimates. It used average retail price of all cards and wrapping paper sold in the month of July in each year to arrive at estimated wholesale price, allowed an average estimated amount for packaging costs and customer discounts and rebates and multiplied the answer in each period by a standard number. This standard number was based on information supplied by the company. This information stated the number of designs for cards as 2454 and for wrapping paper as 531. These numbers were multiplied by 160 for cards and 40 for wrapping paper to produce the estimated total number of samples in each year. Although not clear from the evidence it would appear likely that these numbers were representative rather than actual in each relevant period. It would seem unlikely that this particular number of new designs were manufactured in each of the relevant periods. It is quite clear that this total calculation produced estimates and not specific calculations of the estimated wholesale selling value of the particular samples provided in any period. Without providing a specific alternative method, Mr de Wijn submitted that the methodology used was incorrect and that the profit content related to the whole of the end product sold, namely, a package of cards with envelopes or a package of wrapping paper and it was incorrect to simply make an allowance for the cost of packaging and envelopes. The submission on behalf of the taxpayer company was that it is necessary to value the individual goods at the time it was applied for use and not the value of a different product, a package of cards with envelopes, and then "work back by taking off bits".

32. In my view, the correct approach to arrive at the value of individual cards is to assume, as the hypothetical nature of the legislation requires, the manufacturer doing all things necessary to sell the goods in the normal course of business. Given that the end product sold is a group of cards and appropriate envelopes or sheets of wrapping paper, wrapped and sealed inside see-through packaging, it is appropriate to identify the relative costs of each of these component parts. The proportion of wholesale selling price of the unit package which is equal to the proportion which the cost of the individual card bears to the total cost of the unit sold should be regarded as the amount for which that card could reasonably be expected to be sold by the manufacturer by wholesale. This answer needs then to be multiplied by the actual number of sample cards of each design used in the relevant period. A similar calculation should be done for wrapping paper save that the number of samples issued needs to be divided by four to equal the number of full sheets actually applied to own use. It is recognised that the use of averages and estimates may well be a convenient way of dealing with a large number of designs and samples and it is likely that the parties can agree on a satisfactory method. However, this is not a matter appropriate to be canvassed by the Tribunal. It should be mentioned that, in the ascertainment of the unit wholesale selling price, allowance should be made for normal discounts and rebates allowed to customers.

33. Given the incorrect approach taken by the Commissioner in assessing sales tax on imported cards and the need for recalculation of the sale value, number of cards and wrapping paper applied for own use and the dates of that application, it is appropriate that the decision under review be set aside and the matter be remitted to the Commissioner for reassessment in accordance with the Tribunal's findings.
