ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Practice and Procedure - Consent to dismissal -Order of dismissal - Consent allegedly signed and delivered in error – Whether Tribunal empowered to set aside a final order regularly entered.

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - Consent to dismissal - Order of dismissal - Consent allegedly signed and delivered in error -Whether Tribunal empowered to set aside a final order regularly entered.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 S.23, S.25(1), S.35,

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 S.21(1A), S.34, S.42A(2)

Kretchmer v Repatriation Commission (N87/417 No. 4729)

Administration of the Territory of Papua and New Guinea v Daera Guba [1973] HCA 59; (1973) 130 CLR 353

REASONS FOR DECISION

10 August 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. From time to time this Tribunal sets aside Orders which have been made by the Tribunal despite the circumstance that they appear to be formally correct, duly executed and to have been regularly entered. In this matter the Applicant requests the Tribunal to make such an Order, and to set aside an order of dismissal which was in the following form: "DIRECTION

Before: P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date: 6 February 1987

Place: Melbourne

I direct that -

(a) by consent, pursuant to subsection 42A(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the applications in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984 be dismissed; and

(b) My direction be communicated to the applicant and the respondent.

(San) PETER M. ROACH

Senior Member"

  1. The Commissioner opposes the application on the basis that the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to entertain such an application. Despite that objection and notwithstanding its fundamental character, the Commissioner has responded to a request from the Tribunal for assistance with argument as to whether or not the Tribunal has power to set aside an Order so made. The assistance is appreciated. For the purposes only of identifying and resolving the issue as to jurisdiction, I have assumed that upon any hearing the Applicant could establish the facts referred to hereafter.
  2. Under cover of a letter of 27 April 1988 the Commissioner, at the request of the Applicant, referred to the Tribunal for independent review a decision he had made upon considering objections to assessments of income tax for the years of income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984.
  3. The Formal Documents submitted with that letter for the consideration of the Tribunal disclosed the following history:

26/2/87 The Commissioner issued Notices of Assessment of income tax for the years of income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984, fixing upon taxable income in the sums of $72,495 and $45,962. The assessments issued to:

"The Trustee for (Chancellor) Family Trust a/c Rita C. (Chancellor)".

27/4/87 By its tax agent, a company (Trust-co) objected to the assessments so raised. The objections were said to have been "signed for and on behalf of (Trust-co) in its capacity as Trustee of the (Chancellor) Family Trust on account of Rita C. (Chancellor).

21/9/87 The Commissioner gave notice to the taxpayer (as previously described) that the objections had been considered and had been disallowed.

18/11/87 By its tax agent," the taxpayer" - the term used to identify the (Chancellor) Family Trust - requested reference to this Tribunal for independent review and forwarded a cheque for $400 in payment of the required

fees.

5. The Tribunal file discloses:

27/12/88 The receipt of the Formal Documents.

20/12/88 The issue of a notice of telephone preliminary conference for 25 January 1989.

24/1/89 Receipt of a letter from the Commissioner of the same date identifying the application of the Applicant and stating (formal parts omitted):

"The abovenamed taxpayer's application(s) to the Tribunal has/have been resolved and a copy of the taxpayer's consent to the dismissal of the application is enclosed.

The Commissioner also consents to the dismissal of the application(s) and will amend the assessment(s) in accordance with the terms of settlement."

The document which attended that letter was a photocopy of a facsimile transmission. In its full text, it read as follows:

"The Deputy Registrar, Administrative Appeals Tribunal Box 9955 GPO MELBOURNE 3001

ATTENTION: TAX DIVISION

I do not wish to proceed with my application and hereby consent, pursuant to section 42A(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, to my application being dismissed without the Tribunal proceeding to a review.

FULL NAME IN CAPITALS: The Trustee for the (Chancellor) Family Trust

ADDRESS: C/- (Address of a firm of certified practising accountants)

ATO FILE NUMBER:

INCOME YEAR TO WHICH THE

APPLICATION FOR REVIEW RELATES: 30 June 1983 and 1984

CONTACT TELEPHONE NO:

Conditional upon the following': -

  1. A B deductions to be allowed at $8,900 in each of the years ended 30 June 1982 to 1984 inclusive.
  2. Taxable income of Mr. Patrick (Chancellor) to be increased by $15,000 and $314 in the years ended 30 June 1984 and 1985 respectively.

Signature (Sgd) ......

(P.A.C. Chancellor)"

6/2/89 The Order of dismissal was signed and returned to the Registry for copies to be made and despatched to the parties.

6. At the hearing some further information was disclosed. It appears that the signatory to the letter consenting to dismissal was a person who was contemporaneously taking advice from several sources. At the critical time he was overseas. Negotiations as to settlement had proceeded from May 1988 until January 1989 and by 15 December 1988 had resulted in the tax agent advising the Commissioner's office:

"After numerous meetings with our client the decision has been made to accept the offer proposed by the Australian Taxation Office.

Accordingly we await further communication from you."

The Commissioner prepared the document setting out the proposed terms for settlement and withdrawal of the reference and that text was faxed by the tax agent to the signatory in London. That was on 20 January 1989. The signatory completed the document and returned it by fax and it was so forwarded to the Commissioner. The Commissioner in turn copied the document and forwarded it to the Tribunal. It is to be noted from perusal of that document that the signatory did not explicitly identify himself as an officer of the Trustee or as a person authorised to act in the name of the Trustee. However, it does seem from the terms of the Trust Deed that he was one of the directors of Trust-co and that he was the person at whose insistence the present application is made.

7. It is also appropriate to observe that the document did not express terms appropriate to a bi-lateral agreement. It was a one sided arrangement - a consent to withdrawal on the part of the taxpayer upon condition that certain amendments to existing assessments would he made. As communicated to the Tribunal, it was attended by an undertaking by the Commissioner that he would amend the assessments

"in accordance with the terms of settlement" (of, ante).

In accordance with the practice of this Tribunal that undertaking was accepted. Fortunately, I know of no experience in which it has been necessary to consider a situation in which, following the dismissal of an application, the Commissioner has failed to implement such an undertaking. That is commendable but it is not a practice which lays a sure foundation for the exercise of, or for the definition of, the jurisdiction of the Tribunal.

8. The Order of dismissal having been made amended assessments issued in accordance with the undertaking of the Commissioner and a cheque refunding the $400 paid in fees was forwarded to the taxpayer at its address for service. By letter of 20 March 1989 the cheque for fees was returned to the Commissioner under cover of a letter which stated:

"We refer to a recent decision to withdraw an application before the Administrative Appeal Tribunal (AAT) on behalf of the abovenamed.

The managing director of the trustee is currently overseas and when he was informed that amended assessments had issued in relation to this matter he was astonished and bewildered.

Originally a facsimile was forwarded to the director in London on 20 January last, asking him to sign a statement that had been prepared by your office in relation to the withdrawal of an AAT application.

He signed the document and duly returned it by facsimile, not fully understanding the contents of the document. As this all took place during different time frames, the managing director could not discuss the contents or repercussions with us until a later time.

The document was later sent to your office by facsimile erroneously. The managing director completely misunderstood the nature of the document he was signing, and, under no circumstances did he desire that the application be withdrawn or understand that he was making such a request. He was under the impression he was acknowledging the terms of offer by your office which were to be the subject of review by the AAT.

He strongly requests that the amended assessments numbered 374../005, 274.../006, 233.../004 and 233.../003 be revoked and, the application before the AAT be proceeded with. Accordingly, the cheque refunding the referral fees is returned herewith.

A similar statement has been forwarded to the AAT notifying them of the decision and request."

9. No acknowledgement having been received, on 27 April 1989 the agent wrote to the Tribunal in the following terms (formal parts omitted):

"We refer to the abovenamed appeal (sic) and advise that it was not the intention of the applicant to withdraw the appeal.

Misunderstandings occured (sic) between the various parties involved and the withdrawal of the applications before the tribunal (sic) was incorrect. The position has been communicated to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Melbourne and all refunds of filing fees returned as appropriate.

It is requested by the applicant that the direction of 6th February 1989 be ignored and that the applications be proceeded with.

Your confirmation of this course is awaited."

10. As a result, as has happened with other requests made in relation to other apparently final orders, the Tribunal has taken steps to deal appropriately with the question raised. These reasons are a consequence of that action.

11. The Tribunal is a creature of statute. It has the authority conferred on it by Parliament and no more. That being so its authority to bind others is exhausted by the final exercise of that authority in any one of the ways provided for by the Act. If it errs in the exercise of that authority it can be corrected on appeal or by other appropriate application to the Federal Court of Australia. If it refuses or fails to exercise the responsibility attending the authority it is appointed to exercise, it can be compelled to discharge its duty by order of the Court. However, no such intervention or direction from the Court is necessary if its purported exercise of authority is a nullity. (Kretchmer v Repatriation Commission N87/417 No. 4729.) In that case, in a joint decision, a full bench of the Tribunal presided over by the President (Hartigan J.) directed that a matter which had been dismissed should be restored to the list for hearing. In that case the Order under consideration had been in the following terms:

"The Tribunal directs:

That in view of the failure of the Applicant to attend two preliminary conferences and his failure to notify the Tribunal of his intention to proceed with his application for review, in spite of a request to do so, the application be dismissed without proceeding to review the decision pursuant to Section 42A(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. "

In considering the application for reinstatement, the Tribunal held:

"In this particular instance the member purporting to exercise the power of dismissal in Section 42A was not a member who satisfied the requirements of Section 21(1A), and more particularly Section 21(1A)(a)(i). The Tribunal, as constituted by the member, thereby committed an error of law as to jurisdiction. That is, a jurisdictional error was committed when the Tribunal purported to exercise jurisdiction to dismiss when the prescribed facts or circumstances, upon whose existence the jurisdiction depended, did not exist, or put another way, the statutory conditions precedent to the exercise of the jurisdiction were not satisfied; cf. Hotop, S. Principles of Australian Administrative Law Sixth Edition at 248.

The consequence of such an error is to render the decision purportedly made void - a nullity; cf. P.P.P. v Head (1959) A.C. 83 at 111/112; Anisminic v Foreign Compensation Board [1968] UKHL 6; (1969) 2 A.C. 147 and 171 and Wattmaster v Button [1986] FCA 446; (1986-7) 70 A.L.R. 330 at 334-335. The concept of voidness is that the decision is a nullity and has been a nullity from the date of its purported birth and no Order of a court is necessary to establish its invalidity; Head (supra) at 111-112, and see Sykes Lanham & Tracy, Administrative Law Second Edition at 315."

12. The practical difficulty manifested by the decision can be seen by considering how a person not aware of limitations on the authority of a Tribunal member can identify which Orders of the Tribunal are valid and effective; and which are not. On the other hand, the decision confirms that a decision which purports to be made without authority is as much a nullity as a forgery. But the present case is not such a case. The Order now in question is not one which was deficient because a Member purporting to exercise the power of the Tribunal lacked the authority to do so. For that reason it is unnecessary to determine whether or not the Respondent is correct in his submission that the decision in Kretchmer was incorrect.

13. The present application has two aspects:

(1) Whether the litigation before this Tribunal should be re-activated after entry of a formal Order of Dismissal; and

(2) Whether the Tribunal has power to re-activate the litigation.
  1. Both problems have long been known to the Courts. Just as the community in establishing the Court system has recognised the need for disputes within the community to be resolved by litigation, so too it has been recognised that such litigation must be conducted under conditions which are fair to all concerned. The Courts must be accessible to all; the conditions for the conduct of the litigation must be fair to all; and determinations reached by such processes - including provision for appeals - fairly conducted must be final. The community may no longer recognise - if it ever did - the maxim "interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium" when so expressed, but the notion that there must be an end to all litigation is well understood and remains as important a principle as it ever was.
  2. The role of the Tribunal in the conduct of such litigation is to discharge the responsibilities expressly cast upon it by statute (Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975) and not to assume to itself greater power than was conferred on it. In considering the role of the Tribunal it is necessary to acknowledge that it forms no part of the judicial or court system, despite the circumstance that it sits in judgment within the fields of responsibility entrusted to it just as surely as any court. For that reason the law recognises that decisions of tribunals and institutions such as this are just as binding upon the parties as decisions of any court. (C.f. Gibbs J. Administration of the Territory of Papua and New Guinea v Daera Guba [1973] HCA 59; (1973) 130 C.L.R. 353 at 453.) It is therefore appropriate that in addressing issues such as this the Tribunal should be guided by the experience of the courts gathered over many years.

16. Decisions of the courts binding parties to litigation before them may come after a trial, or by reason of default in procedural obligations or by consent. Until set aside a decision in any one of those cases is as binding as a decision in any other. In the interval of time between the pronouncement of a decision and its formal entry a decision pronounced may be reconsidered and withdrawn. (David Syme & Co v Swinbourne [1909] HCA 92; (1909) 10 CLR 43.) But a decision once made and formally entered is final and it is not for the court which entered it to set it aside except in the exercise of specific powers. (Bailey v Marinoff [1971] HCA 49; (1971) 125 CLR 529; Gamser v Nominal Defendant [1977] HCA 7; (1976) 136 CLR 145, but c.f. Wentworth v Attorney General (N.S.W. ) [1984] HCA 70; (1984) 154 CLR 518 at 526 where the Court said:

"Of course the general principle is that there is no inherent part to set aside judgment by reason of changed circumstances ... but the rule is subject to exceptions ...... It is unnecessary to attempt to discuss the various exceptional cases in which the rule does not apply. The order in question in the present case was not one by which the litigation was concluded; it was merely an Order regulating the procedure to be followed in future conduct of proceedings.....")

17. Consent orders ordinarily derive their force from agreement and therefore may be set aside on any ground which would invalidate a compromise not contained in a judgment or order (Wilding v Sanderson (1897) 2 Ch. 534) Such circumstances may be matters of mistake (as in Wilding (ante) and Huddersfield Banking Company Limited v Henry Lister & Son Limited (1895) 2 Ch. 273); or fraud (c.f. the decision of the Privy Council in Kinch v Walcott (1929) A.C. 482, McHarg v Woods Radio Pty Limited [1948] VicLawRp 86; (1948) VLR 496 and Australasian Meat Industry Employees Union v Mudqinberri Station Pty Limited (1986) 65 A.L.R. 683 at 688); or want of authority (as in Shepherd v Robinson (1919) 1 K.B. 474 -a case in which the application was made before the judgment was drawn up. See also Harvey v Phillips [1956] HCA 27; (1956) 95 CLR 235.) But, once judgment has been formally entered, such setting aside is not to be effected by the court or tribunal which entered up the order. (cf. ante pa. 16) The appropriate course for setting it aside is to institute a fresh action before a court of competent jurisdiction. It is against that background of court practices that the present application has to be considered in relation to this Tribunal as a statutory authority.

18. This Tribunal is empowered to review decisions (Section 25(1)). Such reviews may take place in hearings (see Sections 23 and 35). In addition, where the parties have reached agreement following a Preliminary Conference, the Tribunal may make a decision "without holding a hearing" (Section 34(2)). In addition the Tribunal may finalise a matter by dismissing the application "where all the parties to an application before the Tribunal for a review of a decision consent" (Section 42A(D) or where a party "fails either to appear in person or to appear by a representative at a Preliminary Conference ... or at the hearing of the proceeding" (Section 42A(2)). The authority of the Tribunal is exercised through its Members. Persons who purport to exercise powers which they do not have do not exercise any lawful authority (Kretchmer - ante). Their actions and any orders to which those actions give rise are nullities. (ibid.) The Tribunal may finalise matters referred to it in any of the several ways mentioned but only as provided for by the Act. But having exercised its power in any one of those ways its authority over the parties and its power to direct them in their affairs is spent. Such finality comes into full force and effect upon the making by the Tribunal of an order giving effect to a determination.

19. When an order of the Tribunal is competently made, it may nonetheless be deficient. But whether the Tribunal has acted so as to improperly or incorrectly exercise the power conferred upon it, the Tribunal can do no more in relation to that matter. Any deficiency must be rectified by a court of competent jurisdiction.

20. It follows that no decision of the Tribunal can of its own force give validity to a purported exercise of jurisdiction which was without authority. Nor can such a decision deny to the Tribunal an authority properly vested in it or the responsibility of exercising such authority. Case U131 (87 ATC 767) was a case in which, after referring a matter to the Tribunal, the Commissioner came to contend that the Tribunal was without jurisdiction because the request for reference was out of time.

I said (at p.770):

"12. This Tribunal is the creature of statute. It has the jurisdiction conferred by Parliament and only that jurisdiction. In the exercise of the jurisdiction so conferred, it uses the power and authority and discharges the responsibilities specified by the Parliament. Neither the jurisdiction, nor the powers available to be used in the exercise of that jurisdiction, can be enlarged by any opinion or determination of the Tribunal. As Brennan J., now a Justice of the High Court of Australia, but then sitting as President of this Tribunal said in Re Adams and the Tax Agents' Board (1976) 7 A.T.R. 87 AT P. 89:

'An administrative body cannot therefore lawfully exercise authority merely because it is of the opinion that it has the authority. Its opinion is not the charter of its powers and discretions. It derives its powers and discretions from and in accordance with the law. It is the court's judgment and not the administrative body's opinion which defines the extent of (as well as the constitutional support for) its statutory authority.'

His Honour went on to say:

'An administrative body with limited authority is bound, of course, to observe those limits. Although it cannot judicially pronounce upon the limits, its duty not to exceed the authority conferred by law upon it implies a competence to consider the legal limits of that authority, in order that it may appropriately mould its conduct. In discharging its duty, the administrative body will, as part of its function, form an opinion of its own authority. The function of forming such an opinion for the purpose of moulding its conduct is not denied to it merely because the opinion produces no legal effect.'

  1. In my view, recognition of the soundness of the distinctions made by his Honour makes clear the appropriateness of the course adopted in this case. The parties agreed on, and the Tribunal approved of, a proposal that the issue canvassed in the earlier passages of this decision should be first considered. That was not done in order to bring down a definitive and binding determination as to whether there was 'jurisdiction' to consider and determine the substance of the objection. It was done in order to decide whether or not the Tribunal should embark upon a consideration of the issues raised by the objection.
  2. In my view, the question which the Tribunal must of necessity decide is whether to proceed further and consider the application before it; or should refuse to do so.
    1. The Tribunal might decide to proceed with the reference because it holds the view that it has the power to determine it. Such a view may be correctly or incorrectly formed. If a court of appropriate jurisdiction considers the view so formed by the Tribunal to be incorrect and that it is embarking on issues beyond its jurisdiction, the Court may direct the Tribunal to desist from proceeding. If, without such intervention, the Tribunal proceeds and purports to determine the reference but in doing so exceeds its jurisdiction, its error will be open to be corrected by the courts.
    2. Alternatively, the Tribunal may decline to proceed further with the reference. Its decision not to proceed may be founded upon a considered view that in the circumstances it has no jurisdiction or it may reflect a view, which some seem to have, that it is beyond power for the Tribunal to form such a view. But it is not the reasoning, so much as the determination not to proceed, which is important. If by refusing or failing to proceed the Tribunal errs and thereby fails to discharge the responsibilities cast upon it, the courts can compel it to proceed. However, if the Tribunal has correctly recognised the limits of its jurisdiction, the matter will rest there.
    3. On the view I have formed it is quite inappropriate that I should purport to make any order as to the assessment objected to. Accordingly, I merely direct that the application before me be removed from the list of matters awaiting determination before the Tribunal."

In my view, it is appropriate that in this case I should follow that course.

21. I am not persuaded that the Tribunal does have the authority to grant the relief sought by the Applicant. It follows that in my view the Commissioner had no obligation to respond to the request made by the Tribunal for assistance. Nonetheless I am grateful that he should have done so in the spirit of co-operation and assistance commended by the High Court of Australia in Sutton v F.C.T. [(1959) 100 CLR 5181.

  1. Should I be wrong in that view, the way is open for the Applicant to seek a direction from the Federal Court of Australia which would require the Tribunal to reactivate the application. Alternatively, even if I should be correct in the views I have expressed, it also remains open to the Applicant to procure an order of a court of competent jurisdiction setting aside or quashing the order in question.
  2. Having formed that view it is inappropriate that I should embark upon any consideration of factual and legal issues as to whether the Applicant should be bound to the consequences of the action of the signatory in placing his signature to a document when he "completely misunderstood the nature of the document"; or by the action of his agent whereby the Consent to Dismissal was delivered up to the Tribunal on its behalf; or be estopped by reason of the Commissioner's action in giving effect to the arrangement by the issue of amended assessments.

'24. I conclude by directing that the application giving rise to these proceedings shall stand in the records of the Tribunal as having been dismissed.
