Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 284; (1992) 92 ATC 342, (1992) 24 ATR 1085 (21 September 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. VT92/56 and 57

AAT No. 8284

Number of pages - 13

Income Tax

(1992) 92 ATC 342, (1992) 24 ATR 1085

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - derivation of income - contract for the sale of land entered into in May 1987 conditional upon issue of building permit by local council on or before September 1988 - whether income from contract derived in year in which contract executed or year in which contract became unconditional - whether beneficiaries of vendor trust were "presently entitled" to interest which had accrued on contract instalments held by stakeholder during the year of income when contract was still unconditional - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended, secs 25(1) and 97.

Shirlaw v Southern Foundries 1926) Ltd (1939) 2 KB 207

Combe v Swaythling (1947) 1 All ER 838

F.C. of T. v James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; 1953) 88 CLR 492

F.C. of T. v Australian Gas Light Co and Anor 83 ATC 4800

Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 88 ATC 4392

Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 91 ATC 4192

Harmer v F.C. of T. [1991] HCA 51; (1991) 173 CLR 264

Case Z20 92 ATC 208

Dwight v F.C. of T. 92 ATC 4192

HEARING

MELBOURNE

21:9:1992

ORDER

The objection decisions under review are set aside and the objections allowed in full.

DECISION

The taxpayers in this case - Mr and Mrs Rupert Bunny henceforth referred to as "RB" and "MRB") - are husband and wife and beneficiaries of the Lego Trust, the trustee of which - Lego Pty Ltd "Lego") - carried on the business of building construction and property development.

2. On 27 February 1987, Lego as trustee for the Lego Trust, entered into a joint venture agreement "the Venture") with Yarraville Pty Ltd "Yarraville"), Cutup Subdivision Pty Ltd "Cutup"), Huntington Fifty-Eight Pty Ltd "Huntington") a nominee company whose share capital was held equally by Lego, Yarraville, Cutup, Rupert Bunny Pty Ltd "RB Co") adviser/consultant to Huntington, RB guarantor to Lego), MRB, Arthur Streeton "AS") guarantor to Yarraville), Vincent Bunny "VB") guarantor to Cutup). The recital states that Huntington, at the request of Lego, Yarraville and Cutup and their guarantors, has entered into a contract of sale on 10 December, 1986 under which Huntington has contracted to purchase the property known as "Hansen", 4 Maple Grove, Toorak. The purpose of the acquisition was for "rezoning of the property as residential, renovate and demolish in part the existing buildings and to then proceed with a subdivision of the Property with the intention that on completion of the Development, the lots will be sold and all profits derived for and on behalf of the Venture shall be received by Huntington for and on behalf of and distributed to Lego, Yarraville and Cutup respectively as hereinafter provided in this Agreement." "Hansen" is an historic mansion on large grounds and had, until recently, been used as a leprosarium.

3. It seems that some time after the Venture began its operation, AS contemplated converting "Hansen" to his own residence and approached the other co-adventurers with an offer to buy them out. By a written agreement ("the Agreement) entered into in May 1987, Lego and Cutup agreed to sell their respective interests in the Venture to Yarraville, which covenanted to pay to each of Lego and Cutup severally an amount of $725,000 free of interest in three instalments, the first instalment of $5,000 to be paid on or before 8 May 1987, the second instalment of $20,000 on 6 June 1987 and the final instalment of $700,000 on or before 5 July 1988. Included in the Agreement was a term cl. 3) that:-

This agreement is subject to and conditional upon the City of

Prahran issuing on or before 30 September 1987 a Building Permit

in relation to the renovation or works to be effected to the

principal residential building known as "Hansen" situated on the

property, and it is acknowledged and agreed that the Settlement

Date as hereinafter referred to shall be the date, being the

seventh day after the day of issue of the Building Permit as

aforesaid.

Clause 4 deals with the consideration and the method of payment and stated, inter alia: that "the deposit shall be paid to the Trust Account of Ridgeway Clements, Solicitors, who will hold same as stakeholder pending issue of the Building Permit referred to in Clause 3". Both the deposit and interest thereon was to "accrue to the benefit of and be paid to the party entitled to receipt of the Deposit". The clause also contains a provision that Yarraville will enter into a Building Agreement with Lego on or before the Settlement Date pursuant to which Lego will agree to perform building works on Hansen for a fee of $250,000 "in lieu of the percentage fees referred to in the Joint Venture Agreement". The clause also provides that on or before the Settlement Date, Yarraville:-

will reimburse Lego and Cutup their proportionate shares of all Venture

Costs advanced or paid by Lego and Cutup pursuant to the Joint Venture

Agreement

up to and including the Settlement Date.

4. It is common ground that the Prahran City Council did not issue the required building permit until well after the time stipulated in cl 3 above.

5. As a result of an audit, the respondent added some $758,515 to Lego's net income of $35,589 as returned in the 1987 year of income which included the $725,000 in issue before me, the balance - $33,515 - was not pursued at the hearing), concluding that the $725,000.00, although not received until the 1989 year of income, was nevertheless "derived" for tax purposes on the execution of the Agreement, i.e. in May 1987, cl. 3 being a characterised as "a condition subsequent"; alternatively, it was submitted that "the agreement was honoured - the $725,000 was paid pursuant to the May 1987 agreement because it was that agreement that gave rise to the entitlement."

6. It seems that when the "deadline" for Council approval had passed without the required consent having been obtained, whatever contractual rights the parties may have had to avoid the contract were waived and each party soldiered on until the 26 May 1988, when they purportedly entered into a further agreement, extending time for Council approval by twelve months, i.e. from 30 September 1987 to 30 September 1988; I say "purportedly" since this later agreement was not put in evidence. However, its existence is referred to in various "T" documents cf "T 14" and "T 17" p 75). I was not informed what was the consideration for this variation. Indeed, it is fair to say that neither counsel attached any significance to this agreement. In the circumstances, I propose to refer to it merely as "background".

7. Doing the best I can with the evidence, it seems AS, having decided to acquire "Hansen" for his own domestic residence, the original plan for "Hansen" required substantial changes and different approvals from the various relevant authorities. It also seems that the parties assumed that the value of "Hansen" would be higher if an elderly and dilapidated leprosarium could be converted into an exclusive Toorak mansion than if they were to proceed with the Venture under the original agreement.

8. I now turn to the way the $25,000 was dealt with which Lego received for relinquishing its share of the Venture. Thus, the two first instalments of $5,000 and $20,000 were received by Ridgeway Clements on 8 May 1987 and 6 June 1987 respectively, and held in their trust account as stakeholder in accordance with the Agreement. Mr de Wijn, of learned counsel for the applicants, dealt with this aspect from the bar table, claiming that the amount of $25,000 was released on 22 July 1987 "by separate agreement". When Mr Davies, of learned counsel for the respondent, put to the witness that this amount may or may not have been included in the 1987 return), the best the witness could come up with was: "There is a fair chance I would have thought".

9. Since the profits from the proposed development of "Hansen" would be assessable income in the hands of Lego, it was conceded that the consideration for relinquishing Lego's interest in "Hansen" would likewise be assessable income. The only issue before me therefore is: when did Lego and hence the taxpayers) derive the income?

10. RB was called as a witness and deposed that prior to the termination of the Venture, the Trust had commenced demolishing and renovating at "Hansen". Mr Davies, of learned counsel for the respondent, showed the witness a copy extract from the General Journal of the Trust exh 1), showing "period date 30/6/1987", revealing the following entries:-

DR CR

Trade Debtors $333,666

WIP Maple Grove $87864.02

WIP Huntington 242000.00*

Profit on Construction 3802.98

* the amount of $242,000 was accepted as reimbursement under cl 4

of the Agreement.

The journal entry is signed by the member of the firm of accountants who prepared Lego's return and is dated 18 March 1988. The witness agreed that it "is a fair assumption" that the Trust had been reimbursed in such amount pursuant to the Joint Venture Agreement of 27 February 1987 cl 5.3) and, although the witness had no knowledge just when such reimbursement took place, when Mr Davies put it to him that "it was fair to say that it occurred after 30 June 1987", the witness replied: "Yes, it is".

11. The witness was then shown the tax return of the Trust for the 1987 year, the balance sheet of which carries the notation "Trade Debtors - $754,415.72", which he agreed would probably include the $333,666 debited to "trade debtors" in the Journal. The Crown seeks comfort from this admission to the extent that it is argued that these trade debts were included in the 1987 accounts because the Trust was entitled to them pursuant to the Agreement. Mr de Wijn, on the other hand, submits that these amounts constituted "advances" made pursuant to cl 6.1 of the Joint Venture Agreement, which states:-

Lego, Yarraville and Cutup agree that immediately following the

execution hereof ... Lego, Yarraville and Cutup shall each

deposit in the Account an amount of Three hundred and Fifty

Thousand Dollars $350,000.00) to the credit of the Nominee as an

advance from Lego, Yarraville and Cutup respectively to the

Venture to fund initial Venture Costs pending receipt of the

first or further advances under the Development Loan AND shall

thereafter make such further advances and deposit same to the

credit of the Account as are required from time to time pursuant

to Clause 2.2 hereof.

Thus, submitted Mr de Wijn, the amount of $333,666:-

remains as debts due to Lego, they are contributions by way of

loan to the Joint Venture, and it is under the dissolution

agreement there is an assumption - once the conditions have been

satisfied - that AS' company will ensure that these amounts are

repaid, but they are debts due to Lego, either by the Joint

Venture Company, or by AS, once the conditions have been

satisfied.

12. If I understand the Crown's argument correctly, it is submitted that the amount of $725,000 should have been returned on an accruals basis in the same way as the trade debtors - $333,666 - were returned in 1987. I shall come back to this later, although at first blush, it seems to me that this somewhat simplistic view fails to take into account the two different capacities of the Trust, i.e its role qua builder and its one-third stake in the Venture.

13. To complete the evidence, Mr de Wijn called one Anthony Mark Cohen, a partner of KPMG Peat Marwick in charge of corporate advisory services to give evidence of "generally accepted accounting principles"; cf F.C. of T. v James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492, Hooker Rex Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 88 ATC 4392. The witness had previously been instructed on the background of the Venture by Mr May, a partner of Messrs Herbert Geer and Rundle, the applicants' solicitors and had furnished a report on when, in his opinion, the proceeds from the sale of Lego's interest in the Venture would be recognised as revenue (exh "F"). Mr Cohen made a series of assumptions, few of which were given in evidence. Below is his advice given to Mr May (suitably abbreviated) together with the assumptions he made in reaching his conclusion:

(1) Under the joint venture agreement, a number of permits and

approvals were to be obtained including -

an application to rezone the property;

applications to subdivide the property;

applications for demolition and building works to the existing

home and;

applications for various other components of the property as a

whole.

(2) At the time of entering the agreement, it was estimated that the

process of obtaining such permits would take approximately twelve

months. Although the rezoning application was initially believed

to be a formality, the subdivision application, which sought to

create five separate allotments, ran into objection.

(3) Before the subdivision application had been approved, AS offered

to purchase the other venturers' interests in the project subject

to obtaining certain approvals for town planning and building.

(4) Under the Heads of Agreement dated May 1987, the joint venture

was to be terminated and an amount of $725,000 was to be paid to

each of the retiring joint venturers, i.e. Lego and Cutup. This

payment was subject to and conditional upon the City of Prahran

issuing on or before 30 September 1987 the relevant permits and

planning approvals, and was to be paid as follows:

$5,000 on 8 May 1987

$20,000 on 6 June 1987

$700,000 on 5 July 1988 Final Payment)

(5) As AS intended to use a significant proportion of the property

for residential purposes, the building nature of the development

changed, causing significant alterations to the original plans

proposed by the joint venture.

(6) When it appeared unlikely that the necessary permits would be

obtained in time, the relevant parties executed a Variation of

Heads of Agreement dated 26 May 1988. This variation extended

the time permitted for obtaining the required permits to 30

September 1988.

(7) A dispute arose between RB and MRB and the Commissioner of

Taxation over when the $725,000 received by Lego on behalf of the

Lego Trust should have been brought to account as income for tax

purposes. The Commissioner issued amended assessments to both RB

and MRB in respect of the financial year ended 30 June 1987,

incorporating $66,754 in additional tax imposed under Section 223.

Accounting Treatment

We have been asked to consider the relevant accounting aspects of

the case.

There are currently no Australian accounting standards which deal

specifically with the concept of revenue recognition. However,

the Australian Accounting Research Foundation has addressed the

issue in Exposure Draft 51B - Definition and Recognition of

Revenues "ED 51B").

ED 51B defines revenues as "inflows or other enhancements or

savings in outflows of economic benefits or service potential in

the form of increases in assets or reductions in liabilities,

other than those relating to contributions by owners, that result

in an increase in equity during the reporting period". According

to ED 51B, revenue may only be recognised when:

i) it is probable that the inflow etc has occurred; and

ii) the inflow etc can be reliably measured.

ED 51B defines the term "probable" as "that which can reasonably

be expected to occur on the basis of available evidence or logic"

and implies a probability of greater than 50%.

Although the $725,000 to be received by Lego could have been

reliably measured at 30 June 1987, the probability of receiving

the payment was in doubt due to the condition requiring

Yarraville to obtain the necessary permits. The uncertainty

surrounding the final payment was accentuated by the objections

raised over the subdivision applications, the alterations made to

the original applications and the apparent conflict between the

subdivision and building permits. This uncertainty was

highlighted by the length of time which was required to obtain

the permits and finalise the agreement.

International Accounting Standard 18 - Revenue Recognition ("IAS

18") adopts a similar treatment of revenue recognition by

requiring revenue to be postponed where it is "unreasonable to

expect ultimate collection". Under IAS 18 revenue derived from

the sale of goods should not be recognised until:

i) the seller has "transferred to the buyer the significant

risks and rewards of ownership, in that all significant acts

have been completed and the seller retains no...effective

control of the goods transferred to a degree usually

associated with ownership; and

ii) no significant uncertainty exists regarding the

consideration..., the associated costs incurred...or the

extent to which the goods may be returned.

In recognising revenue, IAS 18 takes into account any

"significant acts of performance" that are yet to be completed by

the vendor. It is clear that the condition requiring the

retiring joint venturers to obtain certain permits and planning

approvals gave rise to a degree of uncertainty as to the final

receipt of the contract revenue.

In our view both ED 51B and IAS 18 codify generally accepted

accounting principles at the relevant time.

In our opinion, based on the recommendations outlined in ED 51B

and IAS 18, under generally accepted accounting principles, the

sale proceeds from the property would not have been recognised as

revenue until the year ended 30 June 1989.

14. Turning to the law and construing the Agreement - given the paucity of the evidence - in the light of the surrounding circumstances, it seems to me that we are here dealing with a conditional or "contingent" contract, it being evident that when the parties entered into the Agreement, it was by no means certain that the Council would grant the requisite building permit. Furthermore, the parties must have been conscious of the fact that if the permit were not granted, the proposed purchase price would be well in excess of the market price for the property. Council's approval was thus "conditional" in the sense that on the fulfilment of the condition of Council approval, the obligation to purchase "Hansen" shall come into force, and non-fulfilment of the condition would entitle the parties to treat the contract at an end. This conclusion would admittedly be strengthened if one were to imply a further condition into the contract on the "Oh, of course" principle (cf per MacKinnon L.J. in Shirlaw v Southern Foundries 1926) Ltd (1939) 2 KB 207, 227), such as if, for example, an officious bystander had asked: "but what is to happen if ...?", a careful draftsman might have been tempted to add:-

If for any reason whatever the Council should refuse the grant of

the building permit referred to in cl.3 hereof, this Agreement

shall become null and void and the vendors shall refund to the

purchaser all payments already made under cl. 4 hereof.

15. Alternatively, cl.3 may be read as a contingent obligation in the sense that the contract of sale shall not come into force unless and until the happening of the - as yet uncertain - event, i.e. Council approval, in which case the non-fulfilment of the condition gives no right of action for breach, but simply suspends the obligations of one or both parties. Although in this case nothing turns on the distinction between "conditional" and "contingent", the latter view is, on the facts, to be preferred, having regard to (i) the wording of cl.3, (ii) the fact that the Agreement did not stipulate that time is of the essence, (iii) the first two payments having to be paid to a stakeholder, are thus not recoverable by Yarraville from Lego were the contract to go off (otherwise than by default of the purchaser); cf Combe v Swaythling (1947) 1 All ER 838, (iv) when the first deadline for Council approval had passed, the parties were somewhat "laid back", treating their respective obligations under the contract as suspended and waiting hopefully for the Prahran Council, like Godot, to grant the necessary approval. Indeed, what occurred may be said to constitute a voluntary forbearance to insist on strict adherence to the terms of the Agreement, with result that the Agreement remains unaffected and the condition of Council approval still continues. This view receives some support from the fact that the parties subsequently extended time for fulfilment of the contingent condition from 30 September 1987 to 30 September 1988 what the consideration was is not known), during which time the approval apparently came through since the final payment of $700,000 was made in July 1988.

16. I must therefore respectfully dissent from Mr Davies' submission that cl.3 constitutes a condition subsequent, if only because the vendor's obligation to sell and the purchaser's obligation to buy were made "subject to and conditional upon the City of Prahran issuing ... a Building Permit ...", i.e. a condition which must be fulfilled before any contract is concluded at all.

17. What is the consequence for tax purposes? I had occasion to review at length the issue of "derivation" in Case Z20 92 ATC 208, a case currently on appeal to the Full Federal Court. In that case, the taxpayer sold subdivisional allotments conditional upon registration of the plan of subdivision on or before 30 July 1985, settlement to be effected within fourteen days of the issue of a Certificate of Title for the said land. The plan of subdivision was, in fact, registered on 25 June 1985, but the relevant contracts were not settled until the next financial year. The Commissioner submitted that since all the conditions necessary to give the vendor a valid claim for the purchase price were satisfied eo instanti with the registration of the Plan of Subdivision, i.e. in the 1985 tax year, the income was "derived" in that year. The taxpayer objected, submitting that the income was only "derived" when the contracts were settled, i.e. in the 1986 tax year. After an exhaustive review of the authorities, I concluded that once the condition, i.e. registration, was satisfied, so that the contracts thereafter became unconditional and the subject land ceased to be the vendor's trading stock, leaving him with no dispositive power over the allotments sold and, although the vendor could not sue for the purchase price before the time for completion had arrived, he was absolutely entitled to recover the purchase price through his right to sue for specific performance.

18. Since it was a material fact in that case that the land constituted the vendor's trading stock - a fact not relied on in this case - I have not been persuaded that Case Z20 is strictly relevant to the outcome of this case; I mention it merely because it featured in Mr de Wijn's submissions and because it represented my views on the various authorities I discussed there at length and to which I continue to adhere until such time as the matter has been decided otherwise by the courts. The point was said to be novel and, as in this case, an expert accountant was called who deposed that his considerable research had led him to conclude that "neither the date of an) unconditional contract, nor the date of settlement, are generally accepted as the proper method in Australia of recognising revenues from the sale of real estate. It appears that each method has been adopted".

19. The facts in this case make the problem a little easier since - unlike Case Z20 - I have concluded that we are here dealing with a contract that was still conditional at the relevant time.

20. Mr de Wijn placed much reliance on the decision in F.C. of T. v Australian Gas Light Co and Anor 83 ATC 4800. Since I dealt with the case at length in Case Z20, I do not propose to repeat what I said on that occasion. Suffice it for present purposes if quote from the joint judgment of Bowen C.J., Fisher and Lockhart JJ. on the issue of derivation:-

Many tests have been propounded and many expressions adopted by

the Courts in attempting to state when income is derived. Those

tests have inevitably been conceived in different circumstances

and to determine different facts and issues. The following

examples may be cited. The fees of accountants are derived when

they have matured into recoverable debts: Henderson v F.C. of

T. 70 ATC 4016; [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612. Fees paid in advance for

provision of dancing lessons are not derived until they are

earned: Arthur Murray NSW) Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 1965) 114 CLR

314; [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 14 ATD 98. The income of a trading business is

derived when its stock is sold and a debt is created: J Rowe and

Son Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 71 ATC 4157; [1970] HCA 57; (1971) 124 CLR 421.

Conversely, fees for the price of goods sold are not earned, and

thus not derived, if a further step is required before the

taxpayer is entitled to payment: Rowe's case. The passing of

property in stock in trade does not necessarily signify the

derivation of income if the consequence is merely the creation of

a right to an account rather than entitlement to a debt:

Farnsworth v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 CLR 504; (1949) 9 ATD 33. If a

taxpayer accounts on the basis of cash receipts, the income is

derived when the `gains have during the period of account come

home to the taxpayer in a realized or immediately realizable

form': The Commissioner of Taxes SA) v Executor Trustee and

Agency Company of South Australia Limited. Carden's case) 1938)

[1938] HCA 69; 63 CLR 108 at p 155; [1938] HCA 69; 1938) 5 ATD 98 at p 132 per Dixon J.

These are some examples of the tests which the Courts have used

in determining the time when revenue transactions are brought to

account or income is derived.

Helpful as these tests may be as signposts, each of them has been

conceived in and applied to varied and contrasting circumstances.

As signposts, they indicate that invariably something more than

provision of goods or services by the taxpayer is required. It

is necessary to determine whether the consequence is that a debt

has been created or whether the taxpayer is obliged to take

further steps before becoming entitled to payment. It may often

be possible to reach the proper conclusion by the application of

these tests if the circumstances of the taxpayer are

unexceptional. However, the taxpayers in this case operate under

exceptional circumstances. They contend that these circumstances

dictate the manner in which they bring their revenue to account.

...

The consequence of the exceptional manner in which the taxpayers

in this case) operate is that as at 30 June in each year their

claims against customers for current liabilities for gas supplied

had not matured into recoverable debts. (at pp 4804-4806)

21. The method of accounting adopted by the taxpayers in that case was to exclude in each year the revenue which they anticipated they would receive and were contingently entitled to receive for gas supplied between the last billing and 30 June of each year, but which had neither been "read" nor billed . The Commissioner sought to argue that the taxpayers had done all that was required of them under their obligation to supply gas during that period and that, since the property in the gas supplied had passed "beyond recall" to the customers, the taxpayers should have brought to account as additional "sales" the value or estimated value of the unbilled gas.

22. At the risk of simplification, that decision, for present purposes can be reduced to the simple proposition - leaving aside the various statutory provisions - that the reading of the meters and billing for gas consumed was held to be a condition precedent to the liability on the part of consumers to pay for the gas. Accordingly, as there was no debt or obligation to pay for the unbilled gas in the relevant quarter up to 30 June each year, the taxpayers did not derive that income during the relevant years within the meaning of sec 25(1) of the Tax Act.

23. Having concluded that the instant Agreement was either conditional or "contingent", I am satisfied that the taxpayers did not derive the income until the contract became unconditional. It is of some interest that this "legal" approach appears to reach the same result as the "accounting treatment" deposed to by Mr Cohen.

24. I should add that I have no idea of the actual date when the Prahran Council granted its approval of the building permit since no one bothered to establish it in evidence. All I can extract from various - non-evidentiary - sources is that cl.3 provides that "the Settlement Date shall be the date, being the seventh day after the day of issue of the Building Permit" and that, according to Lego's 1989 return, an amount of $700,000 was returned in that year, the auditor noting that this amount was received on 12 July 1988 "T 19" p 100) - which suggests that approval may have been granted in the first week of July 1988. However, if Council approval had been given, as is possible, in the latter part of June 1988, it may well be that the whole of the consideration should have been returned in the 1988 year rather than in 1989. Be that as it may, as stated earlier, it is common ground that the Prahran City Council did not issue the required building permit until well after the time stipulated in cl 3 above - thus not in the 1987 year of income.

25. There is a further mystery. Since the parties came to a subsequent arrangement that Lego's deposit ($5,000) and first instalment ($20,000) were to be paid by the stakeholder to Lego for work and labour done cf para 8 above), I am intrigued why Lego was "shortchanged" by some $25,000 when it received, on 12 July 1988, unless, of course, it was the balance owing under the Agreement, viz $700,000. However, since no one bothered to clear this up in evidence, I do not propose to proceed on a frolic of my own.

26. In the circumstances, and following the reasoning I adopted in Case Z20 - although it leads to the opposite result - I am satisfied that the Commissioner's decision on the objections cannot succeed.

27. A subsidiary point arose in relation to an amount of $208, being the interest derived in 1987 on the amount of $25,000 held by the stakeholder, which the Commissioner assessed to Lego and thence as to $104 to RB and MRB respectively in that year). It was submitted by Mr de Wijn - if I understood his argument correctly - that since the condition of Council approval had not been fulfilled in the 1987 year, Lego was not, pursuant to sec 97 of the Tax Act, "presently entitled" to the interest on the amount deposited with the stakeholder, and hence the amount did not form part of its assessable income in that year. The point was barely argued apart from references to the decisions in Harmer v F.C. of T. [1991] HCA 51; (1991) 173 CLR 264, and Dwight v F.C. of T. 92 ATC 4192. For good measure, the objections failed to deal with this aspect. However, Mr de Wijn sought to amend the objections at the hearing to include the excision of this component and this was not objected to by Mr Davies; cf Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v F.C. of T.91 ATC 4192. I therefore acceded to said request.

28. Whilst I can well appreciate that for $208, it is hardly worth counsels' time to argue the point, I cannot afford such luxury. Doing the best I can, it seems to me that since the Agreement provided that the deposit (and, presumably, any subsequent instalment) was to "accrue to the benefit of and to be paid to the party entitled to the deposit" - an entitlement which was dependent on the Prahran Council approving the building permit - neither Yarraville nor Lego could be said to have had a vested and indefeasible interest in the accrued interest of $208 held by the stakeholder as at 30 June 1987, with the consequence in this case that neither party could be said to be "presently entitled" to such sum in that year. However, what appeared to have occurred is that pursuant to a later agreement - referred to in para 8 above - the moneys held by the stakeholder (including, semble, accrued interest) were to be paid to Lego for the building works already executed. Thus exh "E" is a file copy letter from, presumably, Ridgeway Clements, dated 22 July 1987, stating:-

Referring to the telephone discussion between Robin Clements and

VB, we enclose cheque for $25,208.25 being the deposit of

$25,000.00 and interest $208.25 payable to Lego Pty Ltd.

The amount of $208 was thus received by Lego in 1988, not qua interest, but for work and labour done, and thus formed part of its assessable income in the 1988 tax year. However, no amount of interest can be said to have been "derived" by Lego in the 1987 year, when it was assessed, and hence there could be no distribution of $208 from Lego to RB and MRB.

29. For the above reasons, the objection decisions under review are set aside and the objections allowed in full. Associate
