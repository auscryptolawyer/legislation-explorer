Teresa Prudzinski and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria [1997] AATA 250 (23 July 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TERESA PRUDZINSKI v. TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF VICTORIA

No. V95/570

AAT

No. 11882A

Number of pages - 6

Taxation



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

G.L. McDONALD (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - cancellation of registration as a tax agent - applicant pleaded guilty to charges of theft - no conviction recorded - failure by agent to lodge personal returns - poor accounting and administrative practices by agent - whether applicant of good fame integrity and character

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.251K, 251QA

Re Su and Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 82 ATC 4284

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 22 July 1997 (hearing), 23 July 1997 (decision)

23:7:1997

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr S. Palmer

Solicitor for the Applicant: Messrs Tetley McNamara

Solicitor for the Respondent: Mr S. McBurney,

Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

G.L. McDONALD

1. This is an application for review of a decision made by the respondent, the Tax Agents' Board ("the Board") on 20 April 1995 which determined to cancel the applicant's registration as a tax agent, under the provisions of s.251K(2)(d) the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Tax Act"), on the basis that she is not a fit and proper person to hold registration. The authority for the Tribunal to review the decision arises under s.251QA of the Tax Act.

2. At the hearing Mr S. Palmer, of counsel, represented the applicant. Mrs Prudzinski gave oral evidence to the Tribunal and called Ms M. Jockel, Mr N. Curcio to give character evidence on her behalf and Ms K. Duguid, her secretary, to give evidence as to the way she conducted the operations of her office. Mr S. McBurney, a solicitor from the Australian Government Solicitor, represented the respondent. No evidence was called on behalf of the respondent. The Tribunal had before it the documents filed for the purposes of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the "T" documents), including supplementary T documents filed by the respondent. Other documentary evidence was tendered during the course of the hearing.

3. Section 251K(2)(d) of the Tax Act is as follows:

" 251 (2) A Board may suspend or cancel the registration of any tax agent upon being satisfied that- . . . (d) if the tax agent is a natural person - the tax agent is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters; . . . ."

4. Mrs Prudzinski had, until her disqualification by the Board, been a tax agent for a period of approximately 17 years. She is qualified as an accountant, and told the Tribunal that the accountancy aspect constituted the majority of the work carried out by her practice. She holds a Bachelor of Business degree in Accounting and a Graduate Diploma in Public Accountancy from the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology. She has practised as an accountant and tax agent, previously in partnership including with a Mr Curcio, but at the time the events of which we are concerned she was practising as a sole practitioner.

5. The Board alleges a number of circumstances against Mrs Prudzinski, including, as a result of a number of complaints filed by former clients, a complaint filed by a former employee, the fact that she had not filed her own tax returns for the tax years ending 1989 to the present on time, and the fact that her husband had signed several income tax returns during a period in which he was neither her nominee under Division 4 of Part VIIA of the Tax Act and at a time when his registration as a tax agent had been cancelled. However, the principal course of concern is one which relates to the circumstances surrounding her plea of guilty to two offences of stealing. The circumstances surrounding those offences involve the deposit of a cheque for $2.3 million into her bank account. According to Mrs Prudzinski, it appears that the deposit came about as the result of her office contacting a client (Mr Belano) advising that an amount of $2300 was outstanding, either in relation to fees to be paid to the Australian Securities Commission ("ASC"), or part fees to be paid to the ASC and part fees to be paid as the result of professional work carried out by Mrs Prudzinski's office. On 15 November 1992 Mr Belano asked his Bank (Macquarie Bank) to draw a cheque for that sum, payable to the ASC.

6. A mistake was made by the Bank in the sum requested and a cheque for $2.3 million was drawn down and forwarded to Mrs Prudzinski's office. The amount, despite it being payable to a third party, was paid into her ANZ Bank account on the basis that the employee of Mrs Prudzinski who made the deposit thought a sum of $2300 was involved. When the ANZ Bank discovered the discrepancy, i.e. that the full amount on the face of the cheque was $2.3 million, the balance of the account was brought up to reflect this sum. On 21 December 1992, the ANZ Bank wrote notifying Mrs Prudzinski of this. She claimed not to have received this letter until 5 January 1993.

7. At about that time (i.e. 21 December 1992) Mrs Prudzinski had paid a salary by cheque drawn on the same account into which the deposit had been made to one of her employees, Ms K. Duguid. Ms Duguid was informed by the Bank that the salary cheque could not be honoured. The employee reported this to Mrs Prudzinski, who contacted the Bank. The Bank informed Mrs Prudzinski that she had cleared funds of $2.3 million in her account, but the Bank was unable to identify the source of the funds to Mrs Prudzinski, despite her request that it do so. On the afternoon of 23 December 1992 Mrs Prudzinski, after further inquiring of the Bank as to the source of the funds and being assured that the funds were cleared into her account, drew three telegraphic transfers on that account:

(a) one to International Automobiles for $1.22 million (a company associated with the applicant's husband)

(b) a second sum of $530,000 to the applicant's account at the Bank of New Zealand. This sum was subsequently dispersed on 24 December by transfer of $100,000 to her business account (T. Prudzinski's and Associates) and by way of what she claimed to be a deposit of $400,000 to a former business partner, Mr Curcio. Mr Curcio told the Tribunal that he returned the funds to Mrs Prudzinski the next day;

(c) the third drawing was for $510,000 to the applicant's account held at the State Bank in Commercial Road, Prahran. Disbursements from the latter amount were $20,000 to be paid to a Mr Higginbotham; $4000 to be paid towards repayment of a visa card debt; $4900 withdrawn and kept by way of cash; and $475,000 (approximately) transferred from this account to another account of the applicant at Citibank in Sydney. From that latter account she attempted to pay various debts.

8. Mrs Prudzinski told the Tribunal that she was unaware who had deposited the funds into her bank account, but she said to the Tribunal that she knew that they were not from funds deposited by a client, because she was not aware of any clients having such large transactions occurring at that time. One possibility could have been that the funds came from the estate of her grandmother in Israel. She said that she had been told some time earlier by her father that her grandmother, who had died in Israel, was a rich woman and that there was a possibility of Mrs Prudzinski receiving an inheritance. Another possibility which she raised was that the money could have been sourced from a commission which was payable to her in the event of transactions, which were being negotiated by a Mr Wells, and involved the buying and selling of prime bank securities, coming to fruition. That, or those, transaction(s) she said involved millions of dollars or hundreds of millions of dollars and, if successful, would have resulted in her receiving a substantial commission in the order of the sum deposited. Exhibit D is a statement prepared by Mr Wells for the purposes of the committal hearing in relation to the criminal charges against Mrs Prudzinski. In that statement Mr Wells says that he worked at Mrs Prudzinski's office as a consultant on a profit sharing basis. He said that he left the accounting practice in April 1991 and that not any of the transactions in which he had been involved had ever been finalised. In the statement he said that had any of the transactions had come to fruition he would have notified Mrs Prudzinski.

9. Mrs Prudzinski said that she had enquired several times at the Bank, over the telephone, to determine the source of the money, but the Bank was only able to tell her that the funds had been cleared into her account. It being near to Christmas and the start of the holiday season Mrs Prudzinski said that she would have had trouble contacting Mr Wells to confirm a deposit being made as the result of the transactions in which she was engaged with him. She told the Tribunal that she felt that she had done everything she could to source the funds, and relied on the Bank when they said that clear funds were transferred into the account standing in her name.

10. Mrs Prudzinski told the Tribunal that she sought to remove the funds from her bank account because, at that time, she was in dispute with her Bank (the ANZ) concerning an amount said to be owed to the Bank by her, or by her and her husband. She said that County Court proceedings had been commenced with respect to that sum and she was afraid that, if she left the funds in her account, the Bank would remove them in payment of what it claimed to be the debt owing. She said that this had occurred on previous occasions.

11. The error must have come to the notice of the bank that had issued the cheque (Macquarie Bank) and injunctions were issued freezing at least those accounts into which the funds had been deposited and not yet dispersed. Mrs Prudzinski told the Tribunal that with respect to the funds that had already been dispersed, she subsequently entered into an agreement with the Macquarie Bank and those funds were repaid over a period of two years. The last of the payments occurred in 1995.

12. Mrs Prudzinski was subsequently charged with two counts of stealing choses in action. She pleaded guilty to those offences in the County Court in December 1996. After considering a detailed plea in mitigation made on her behalf, the Court determined not to record a conviction, but to place her on a good behaviour bond for a period of one year. The Tribunal ruled on 21 May 1997 that it was not precluded from taking into account her plea of guilty to the two charges of stealing, despite the fact that no conviction had been recorded.

13. It was claimed before the County Court and before this Tribunal that Mrs Prudzinski had suffered a series of serious illnesses as well as a number of miscarriages and a difficult birth (occurring in 1992), from the period 1991 through to early 1995, which, at least in part, explained the difficulties which faced her with respect to her practice as a tax agent during the period in question. She told the Tribunal that she had been receiving constant treatment and strong medication which affected her ability to conduct her work. Ms Duguid said that, from after mid-1991, Mrs Prudzinski was rarely in her office but that work, including tax returns for checking, was sent to her at home where she made necessary corrections and returned it for retyping. The work was then returned to Mrs Prudzinski at home for checking and signing. Mrs Prudzinski told the Tribunal that she went to work occasionally after hours and on weekends, but otherwise managed to conduct her work at home, at the offices of her clients or when she was able to do so from hospital. Exhibit A is a current medical report from a consultant psychiatrist, Mr R. Kirkman, dated 21 July 1997. He indicates in that report that her condition has markedly improved and that in his view she suffers from no psychiatric disability which would stand in the way of her carrying out her normal duties as an accountant. The applicant herself told the Tribunal that she had ceased taking all prescribed medication. The Tribunal accepts that there is no medical impairment currently standing in her way from resuming her practice.

14. It was Ms Jockel's evidence that during the relevant period Mrs Prudzinski continued to act for her clients in a professional manner despite Mrs Prudzinski's ill health and it was her continued persistence in discharging her professional duties in such difficult circumstances which resulted in the high admiration which Ms Jockel regarded Mrs Prudzinski. Ms Jockel, who is a partner in a Melbourne legal firm, told the Tribunal that, if Mrs Prudzinski regained her tax agent's licence, she would not only return to have her own tax returns completed by Mrs Prudzinski but would refer other clients to Mrs Prudzinski.

15. Mr Curcio, who was a former partner of the applicant in an accounting practice, also gave evidence as to her professionalism during the period in which he was her partner (between the period 1981 to 1984). He said that during this period she looked after the partnership books, carried out accounting work on behalf of clients and proved to be a competent, punctual and effective partner. After the dissolution of the partnership, he has continued to see her on a social basis and told the Tribunal that he regarded her as a person of integrity.

16. Mrs Prudzinski agreed that some tax returns, lodged under her name, had been signed by her husband, Mr Graham Collard, in the latter part of 1994 at a time when Mr Collard's registration as a tax agent had been cancelled (see T documents, pp.152, 153, 154 and 186). Mrs Prudzinski said that her husband had signed the tax returns in her name, after she had signed a power of attorney (exh C) in his favour. Mrs Prudzinski told the Tribunal that at the time this occurred one of the illnesses caused her to be incapable of signing her name from time to time and it was during these times that her husband signed for her, even though the preparation of the tax returns had been done by her or by someone other than her husband in her office (e.g. Mrs Collard who is her sister-in-law who was an accountant and tax agent).

17. With respect to complaints lodged by other former clients of Mrs Prudzinski, no evidence was led before the Tribunal. The Tribunal has determined not to take those matters into account, nor the matter relating to outstanding wages said to be owing from Mrs Prudzinski to Mr Zabinsky. The latter is clearly a matter of contract, about which there is a dispute between the parties and, as it said during the hearing of this matter, the Tribunal is unable to see how that can be relevant to the determination of whether or not Mrs Prudzinski is a fit and proper person to be registered as a tax agent.

18. The test to be applied with respect to whether or not a person is fit and proper to act in a particular capacity is to be gleaned from the capacity in which that person is seeking to be registered. In this case, acting as a tax agent requires, as was conceded on behalf of the applicant at the hearing of this matter, honesty, integrity and professionalism in her calling as a tax agent. Despite the high regard in which her two character witnesses held Mrs Prudzinski, the Tribunal is unconvinced that a person who has pleaded guilty to an offence of stealing - a plea which involves an acknowledgment of intention to steal - given the magnitude of the sum involved is able to be regarded as retaining the degree of honesty and integrity which would be required of a person dealing with tax returns which may involve other people's money. The Tribunal regards these incidents as being of such a nature that of themselves they justify the removal of her name from the Tax Agents Registry (see Davies J in Re Su and Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 82 ATC 4284 at 4287).

19. The Tribunal is further concerned that Mrs Prudzinski styled what was her office account as a "trust account". She said that it was known to the Bank that this account operated as her current office account. Her counsel sought to draw a distinction between an audited trust account (e.g. a solicitor's trust account or an estate agent's trust account) and an unaudited trust account as if in some way this helped illuminate a distinction between a trust account and a current account. The Tribunal does not accept this distinction. A trust account, whether audited or unaudited, is and carries with it the clear connotation that the money deposited in it is to be held "in trust". Such is not the case with a current account. The fact that Mrs Prudzinski was not able, or did not think it advisable, to clearly distinguish the two is a factor which, in the Tribunal's opinion, reflects on the professionalism of the way in which she conducted her practice. The Tribunal further notes that the cheque from her client was payable to the ASC. It is curious that Mrs Prudzinski should say that the $2300 was, or may have been, in part payment of her fees. If that was the case, one would expect the cheque to be made out to her current account, rather than specifically to the ASC. The Tribunal expresses concern that such a cheque would be paid into an account styled a "trust account" if, in fact, the account into which it was paid was merely a current account.

20. With respect to her non-filing of her tax returns for 1989 and the years following, Mrs Prudzinski told the Tribunal that she had no taxable income in that period. The Tribunal notes that the Commissioner had requested her to file tax returns and it was not until after Magistrates' Court proceedings had been issued against her that the tax returns were eventually filed in 1995. The applicant's reason for not filing her own tax returns on time - namely that she was looking after the affairs of other people, and that she put more priority in dealing with the affairs of her clients than in dealing with her own affairs, and that her illnesses precluded her from completing her tax returns - does not, in the view of the Tribunal, amount to a sufficient reason to excuse the non-filing of the tax returns on time. This matter also reflects adversely on the manner in which the applicant approaches her professional task as a tax agent. As was pointed out in Su's case (supra), registration as a tax agent is intended to engender trust and professionalism not only on the part of the clients with whom the agent deals, but also on the part of officials of the Taxation Department (p.4287). The applicant's failure to attend to her own affairs over a number of years is a matter which interferes with the tax agent's standing vis--vis the Department, and constitutes a further ground which leaves the Tribunal satisfied that her registration should be cancelled.

21. The applicant claimed that she was advised by her solicitor that her husband could do all things in her name if she gave him the power of attorney. Whether or not specific advice was given to her with respect to his ability to sign his name to tax returns filed from her office is, in the view of the Tribunal, doubtful. Mrs Prudzinski acknowledged to the Tribunal that at the time that her husband signed the tax returns on her behalf, she was aware that her husband had been deregistered to act as a tax agent. The Tribunal does not accept that Mrs Prudzinski believed that, by signing a power of attorney in favour of her husband, she could authorise him to sign and file tax returns in her name, as if those tax returns had been signed by her. The Tribunal notes at her counsel's prompting that she gave an undertaking to the Tribunal that should she be allowed to continue as a tax agent she would give an undertaking not to have her husband sign tax returns on her behalf.

22. It was urged on the Tribunal that it should take a similar view to her circumstances concerning the stealing offences as was taken by the County Court. A judge in determining what sentence, if any, should be imposed on a person who has pleaded guilty, or has been found guilty, of a criminal offence involves entirely different considerations from the considerations which must be taken into account by administrators in determining whether or not a person is fit and proper to conduct a particular professional activity. In the instant case the findings of the Tribunal leave it satisfied that there are a number of separate issues which by themselves are sufficient to warrant her cancellation as a tax agent, even more so when they are considered in combination.

23. For the reasons stated, the decision under review is affirmed.
