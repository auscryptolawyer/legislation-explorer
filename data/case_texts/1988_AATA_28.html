Last Updated: 23 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - directions - application that hearing of four matters be delayed until outcome of criminal proceedings pending against important witnesses - application unopposed in three matters -whether deciding matter would constitute a contempt of court -possibility of witness incriminating himself - whether order under s. 35 of AAT Act effective to protect witness - s. 39 of AAT Act - whether reasonable to hear case if witness entitled to refuse to answer questions - relevance of length of any postponement of hearing - hearing to proceed in six months' time

Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986

Taxation (Administration) Act 1953 s. 142K

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 190(b)

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s. 39

R v Edwards and Collie Victorian Court of Criminal Appeal (CCA No. 74/87 & 79/87: 6 July 1987)

O'Donovan v J. M. Vereker & Others Full Court of the Federal Court (No. G86/87: 15 October 1987)

Re Steigers Meat Supply (Aust.) Pty. Ltd. and Australian Meat & Livestock Corporation (No. V83/482: decided on 2 November 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) Nos. VT85/1683, 1687,

) 5093, 5227

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

REASONS FOR DIRECTIONS

  1. The applicant in each of these cases is seeking a direction that the hearing of his or its application to review the objection decision of the respondent not be held until after criminal proceedings pending at present against Mr B. Saunders and Mr G. F. Sheehan have been concluded or terminated. At the joint hearing of the applications for those directions all the applicants were represented by Mr Saunders, who is an accountant; the respondent was represented by Mr G. T. Pagone, of Counsel. Mr Pagone informed me that he was instructed not to oppose the application for the direction in the cases of X Co., Y Co. and Z, as in one the tax had been paid and in the other two the respondent was having difficulty preparing its case.
  2. An affidavit sworn by Mr Saunders was tendered in evidence on the applicants' behalf. In it he stated that he and Mr Sheehan had been charged with conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth and conspiracy to prevent or defeat the execution or enforcement of a law of the Commonwealth, namely the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITA Act"), between 30 June 1977 and 31 December 1983. Committal proceedings had been commenced in the Melbourne Magistrates' Court in May 1986 and, after final submissions had been made by Counsel on 17 December 1987, they had been adjourned to 23 December 1987 when it was expected that the magistrate would decide whether or not to commit them for trial. Mr Saunders deposed that he had been advised by Counsel representing him in those criminal proceedings that, if he were required to give evidence in any taxation appeals that touched on any of his actions during the period covered by the charges against him, he should decline to give evidence in the matter and rely upon the privilege against self-incrimination. He intended to follow that advice and believed that Mr Sheehan had been similarly advised and similarly intended to follow such advice. Finally he deposed that he believed, first, that the statutory registers and accounting records of Y Co. (of which X Co. was a vendor shareholder), of B Co. (of which Z was a vendor shareholder), and of A Co. showed an involvement by Mr Sheehan and himself with each of those companies during the period to which the criminal charges related and, second, that a hearing of the application for review would require a detailed examination of their involvement with those companies during that period.
  3. At the hearing of the applications for the directions Mr Saunders stated that the shares in A Co. had been purchased by the C group of companies, the directors of which were Mr Sheehan and himself. He said that the criminal charges related to the purchase of shares in the company by the C group during the period specified in those charges. That was not disputed by Mr Pagone and I accept that those facts are correct.
  4. Mr Saunders pointed out that A Co. bore the burden of proving that the assessment affirmed by the respondent in his decision on A Co.'s objection was wrong. He said that A Co. could discharge that burden only by calling as witnesses the directors of the C group, including Mr Sheehan and himself. If that occurred, they would decline to give evidence as by doing so they could incriminate themselves. Consequently, if A Co.'s application for review were heard before he and Mr Sheehan could be compelled to give their evidence and could not refuse to answer any questions on the ground that the answers might incriminate them, A Co. would be disadvantaged. He referred to section 39 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") which provides that the Tribunal must ensure that every party to a proceeding before it "is given a reasonable opportunity to present his case" and submitted that A Co. would not have a reasonable opportunity to present its case if he and Mr Sheehan were entitled to refuse to answer questions and did refuse to do so. He suggested also that, if the Tribunal heard A Co.'s application for review while the criminal proceedings remained on foot, it might be in contempt of the Court having charge of those proceedings. He said that matters relevant to the activities of himself and Mr Sheehan during the period covered by the charges would have to be canvassed in the evidence presented to the Tribunal.

5. Finally, Mr Saunders referred to the fact that there were a number of applications for review before the Tribunal at the hearing of which the evidence of himself and Mr Sheehan would' be required. He pointed out that several of them had already been adjourned because of the criminal proceedings in which he and Mr Sheehan were involved. It was expensive for applicants to have to argue for adjournment of their cases individually; so he asked that the directions which the applicants in these proceedings were seeking should be expressed to extend to the proceedings in respect of those other applicants.

  1. The first of the cases in which directions were given postponing the listing of an application for review was Case U115 87 ATC 686. The direction was given by Davies J. as President of the Tribunal on 26 May 1987. At that time the situation in respect of the criminal proceedings involving Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan was that on 21 July 1986 the magistrate had ruled that the prosecution evidence established a prima facie case sufficient to put them on trial for the offences with which they were charged. They had applied to the Federal Court under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1976 for review of the magistrate's decision that a sufficient prima facie case had been established. A ground of that application had been that the magistrate had not considered whether the schemes alleged to constitute the conspiracies had been effective to create allowable deductions under the ITA Act. Jackson J. had held that that was an issue in relation to each of the alleged conspiracies, so that the magistrate should have done so. The Crown had appealed against that decision; one of the questions raised in the appeal was whether the magistrate had been required to consider the question. Subsequently, on 15 October 1987 the Full Court of the Federal Court gave judgment on the appeal; it' decided that it was not an issue in respect of either of the offences with which Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan were charged.
  2. At the time when Davies J. gave the direction in Case U115, the appeal was still pending. The direction given was that at that stage the application should not be set down for hearing because the judgment of the Federal Court on the appeal would determine the scope of the committal proceedings. If the matter thereafter came before the Tribunal the following questions would have to be considered:-
    • (a) the likelihood of witnesses claiming privilege against self-incrimination and the effect on the applicants;
    • (b) the respondent's desire to proceed in the application for review and, if successful, to recover unpaid tax;
    • (c) if the Tribunal were to proceed, whether it would be in contempt of a Court; and
    • (d) a successful prosecution of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan might affect whether the application for review proceeded.

8. The second of the three cases was Case U178 87 ATC 1028. In that case the direction was given by Mrs Balmford, Senior Member, on 18 September 1987 that a date already set for the hearing of the application for review be vacated and that the application not be fixed for hearing prior to the handing down by the Full Court of its judgment on the appeal. In Case U115 the applicant was one of the companies in respect of which Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan were alleged to have conspired. In Case U178 evidence had been given that at that time the case which the prosecution intended to try to make out in order to prove the charges related to transactions involving 45 companies which did not include the applicant in the proceedings with which she was dealing. Also evidence was given that at that time it was not intended to extend the prosecution's case to include transactions involving the applicant. However, Mrs Balmford referred to the breadth of the conspiracy charges, which contained no details limiting them to any specific transactions undertaken by Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan and which related to offences allegedly committed within a period of six and a half years. She pointed out that, when after committal (if it occurred) the indictment was drawn, it might relate to activities in respect of which evidence had not been given at the committal proceedings and that no indemnity had been given to Mr Saunders or Mr Sheehan in respect of those matters. Consequently, she was not persuaded that the facts in respect of the applicant in the proceedings with which she was dealing were sufficiently different from those in Case U115 that she should give different directions.

9. The third case was No. VT85/23. That case came on for hearing in June 1987. The applicant and the respondent both wished the hearing to proceed. At a directions hearing held earlier the applicant had been represented by Mr Saunders; he had informed the Tribunal that he wished the hearing to take place and that he intended to give evidence. At the commencement of the hearing, however, Counsel for the applicant and the respondent were referred by the Tribunal to Case U115 were asked and whether they could give an assurance that the Tribunal would not be in contempt of a Court if it made findings in respect of the facts necessary to enable it to give a decision on the review. After an adjournment the applicant's Counsel informed the Tribunal that there was a danger that the Tribunal would be in contempt of a Court. Accordingly, the Tribunal, with the agreement of both Counsel, decided not to commence the hearing and directed that it not be set down again until the criminal proceedings had been concluded or terminated.

  1. Mr Pagone drew attention to the fact that the directions in Case U115, Case U178 and VT85/23 were all given before the Full Court of the Federal Court handed down its judgment on the appeal. He pointed out that, whereas in Case U115 findings of fact would have had to be made in respect of the activities of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan in respect of one of the 45 companies to which the prosecution case in the committal proceedings had related, A Co. was not one of those companies. Consequently, he submitted, there could not be any question of the Tribunal committing an act of contempt by making findings as to the activities of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan in respect of A Co. and as to the effectiveness or otherwise of those transactions to create allowable deductions.
  2. Mr Pagone submitted further that, even if the Tribunal had to receive evidence and to make findings of fact in relation to matters which would be an issue in the criminal proceedings, it could avoid acting in contempt of the Court having charge of those proceedings by prohibiting, pursuant to section 35 of the AAT Act, the publication or disclosure of evidence given to it. Applications in the Tribunal's Taxation Division were heard in private; so members of the public would not be able to hear the evidence. In the proceedings before the Tribunal the intention of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan would not be in issue, only the effectiveness or otherwise of what they had in fact done. So the Tribunal would not have to make findings of fact in respect of any matter directly in issue in the criminal proceedings.
  3. In answer to Mr Saunders' submission that the applicant would be prejudiced by Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan not giving evidence, he suggested that the applicant would have no need to lead evidence to prove any fact, as all the facts could be established by documentary evidence. Further, Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan would have no right to refuse totally to give evidence. They could refuse to answer questions if the answers might incriminate them, but otherwise they would have to answer. He again pointed to the power of the Tribunal under section 35 of the AAT Act to prevent disclosure of evidence given to it. If an appropriate order were made, he submitted, a witness would be able to give evidence without incriminating himself even though his giving the same evidence without such an order might result in his doing so.
  4. Mr Pagone emphasized the detriment to the respondent where the hearing of an application for review was delayed in a case where the tax had not been paid by a company in the circumstances which the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Act 1982 ("the T(UCT) Act") had been enacted to deal with. The respondent could not proceed against vendor shareholders and promoters until' the liability of the company to pay tax had been finally established. He submitted, therefore, that A Co.'s application for review should be set down for hearing as soon as possible. If at the hearing it became apparent that Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan were entitled not to answer certain questions and if the answers to those questions were vital to the case which A Co. had to establish, that was the time at which the Tribunal should consider whether the hearing should proceed further and, if so, what orders it might be appropriate to make under section 35 of the AAT Act.
  5. Mr Saunders, in reply, questioned whether, even if the Tribunal had made an order under section 35 of the AAT Act prohibiting the disclosure of evidence given by him, at his trial he could lawfully refuse to answer questions put to him about that evidence. He submitted that, as section 190(b) of the ITA Act imposed on an applicant for review the burden of proving that the assessment was incorrect, oral evidence would be required from the directors of the C group of companies to supplement the documentary evidence; consequently A Co. would be prejudiced if he and Mr Sheehan refused to answer questions about the transactions which the applicant claimed created allowable deductions. He pointed out further that, in cases where the respondent relied on section 260 of the ITA Act, the applicant had to establish what its intentions were; that, he said, would result in the Tribunal making findings of fact in respect of matters in issue in the criminal proceedings. He also drew attention to the decision of the Victorian Court of Criminal Appeal in R v Edwards and Collie (CCA No. 74/87 & 79/87 an as yet' unreported judgment delivered on 6 July 1987) which, he said, was at direct variance with the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court given on his and Mr Sheehan's appeal against the decision of Jackson J.
  6. In R v Edwards and Collie the Court of Criminal Appeal refused leave to appeal against a conviction for conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth. The indictment had stated the manner in which the applicants were alleged to have intended to defraud the Commissioner, namely to divert the sales tax liability of wholesale merchants participating in the scheme to an entity or entities which would not and could not pay the sales tax and to conceal the sales tax liability of the entities. At the trial there had been agreement that the scheme had not been effective to cause there to be no liability to pay sales tax. The judge had directed the jury to that effect. He had also directed it that dishonesty had to be proved to establish the offence charged and that the means whereby the Commonwealth was to be deprived of sales tax had to be shown to be dishonest. In the context that meant, he said, that the jury had to be satisfied that the accused had no belief in the legality of the scheme.
  7. On appeal the applicants submitted that the trial judge had not directed the jury adequately as to the time at which they had to be shown to have had no such belief. Each of the three judges who constituted the Court of Criminal Appeal wrote a separate judgment. Each found that the trial judge's directions had been adequate. Each proceeded on the basis that the prosecution had had to prove that the scheme was unlawful. They pointed out that there had been agreement at the trial that the scheme was not effective to prevent a liability to pay sales tax from arising. The scheme had caused that liability to rest on a company which had lacked the funds with which it might have discharged the liability. At the hearing of the appeal, therefore, the parties presumably did not raise the question whether the ineffectiveness of the scheme to prevent any liability arising had needed to be proved. The Court did not have to direct its mind to that question. By contrast it was expressly argued in, and considered and decided by, the Full Court of the Federal Court in Mr Saunders' appeal.

17. The decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court has, I consider, resolved the question, at least for the time being. Further, it is to be noted that Pincus J. at page 6 of his reasons for judgment observed that the magistrate had properly treated as relevant the extent of the confidence of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan in the devices designed to obtain deductions but "had made no error in declining to regard that as the essential question". Fox J. agreed in substance with Pincus J.'s reasons. I am satisfied, therefore, that, if, in deciding an application for review, the Tribunal found that the scheme to which the conspiracy charges related, or any other schemes identical to them that Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan had devised, were or were not effective to create allowable deductions, it would not by doing so commit a contempt of the Court having charge of the criminal proceedings. However, it must be added, that the Tribunal was informed that an appeal had been lodged with the High Court of Australia against the judgment of the Full Court of the Federal Court in O'Donovan v J. M. Vereker & Others (No. G86/87: 15 October 1987), which was handed down on the same day as the judgment in the appeal by Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan, and that a ground of the appeal to the High Court related to the question whether knowledge of the ineffectiveness of a scheme to create allowable deductions was an element of offences such as those with which Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan were charged. At present, however, the Tribunal is bound by the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court and must give its directions on that basis.

18. I am satisfied that, if the Tribunal in dealing with A Co.'s application decides either that a scheme involving the C group created allowable deductions or that it did not do so, it will not thereby decide either directly or indirectly any matter which is an element of either of the offences charged in the criminal proceedings. It was not suggested that there were any other matters in respect which the Tribunal might have to find facts in dealing with A Co.'s application which would be directly or indirectly in issue in the criminal proceedings. I have come to the conclusion, therefore, that the Tribunal would not commit an act of contempt of the Court having charge of those proceedings if it dealt with A Co.'s application.

19. However, in case before the Tribunal deals with the application, the High Court of Australia decides the appeal against the Federal Court's decision in O'Donovan v Vereker and holds that ineffectiveness of a scheme to create allowable deductions is an element in a conspiracy of the type with which Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan are charged, I should, I think, deal with the question whether, by appropriate orders made under section 35 of the AAT Act, the Tribunal can make the findings of fact necessary for it to be able to give a decision on the review without committing a contempt of the Court having charge of the criminal proceedings.

20. That was a matter which had to be addressed by the Tribunal in Re Steigers Meat Supply (Aust.) Pty. Ltd. And Australian Meat & Livestock Corporation (No. V83/482: decided on 2 November 1984). The reasons for the decision in that case have not been published because of an order made under section 35 prohibiting its publication. However, the paragraphs of those reasons which deal with a question similar to that which it is necessary to consider in these proceedings disclose nothing which would prejudice the persons in whose interest the section 35 orders were made. So they can be set out here. They are as follows:-

"30. In support of his submission that the Tribunal would be in contempt of the Supreme Court if it were to investigate and make findings in respect of the facts alleged to constitute the offences with which Mr X and Mr Y are charged in the Supreme Court of Victoria, he cited the case of Watts v Hawke and David Syme & Co. Ltd. [1976] VicRp 77; [1976] VR 707 where the Supreme Court of Victoria held that the Judiciary Committee of the Australian Journalists' Association would be in contempt of court if it investigated and determined questions of fact which were the same as those in issue between the parties at the trial of a civil action in the Supreme Court. However, Kaye J., who presided at the trial, after examining a number of English and Victorian cases where the courts had considered what constituted contempt of court, stated at 715:-

"The principles emerging from the reported cases lead me to the conclusion that a contempt of Court would be committed if a non-curial tribunal were to investigate and to make findings on matters the same as those in issue in a pending civil action, and if such investigations and findings would create a real and definite tendency to prejudice or to embarrass the fair trial of the action.' (emphasis added)

The cases to which His Honour referred were cases in which the conduct complained of had been likely to interfere with the due administration of justice.

  1. In Watts v Hawke His Honour considered a submission that there would be no risk of publication of the findings of the Judiciary Committee because of rule 51(s) of the Constitution and Rules of the Australian Journalists' Association. That rule prohibited the publication of findings of the Judiciary Committee without the authorization of the Federal Council or the Federal Executive of the Association. His Honour rejected that submission because, although the Secretary of the Victorian Branch of the Association had sworn in an affidavit that all the organs of the Association involved in the matter of complaints regarded themselves as bound by the same requirement of non-disclosure, which His Honour assumed to refer to the Branch Committee, the Federal Executive, the Judicial Appeal Committee and the Federal Council of the Association, it did not appear that the Secretary of the Victorian Branch was authorized by their members to swear to those matters on their behalf. Further, quite apart from any possibility of publication by the Association, "the reality associated with the first named defendant's complaint" led him to the conclusion that there were persons connected with or interested in the disciplinary proceedings who might be expected to disclose the outcome of the Judiciary Committee's investigation whatever it might be.
  2. In the course of the proceedings I ruled that evidence would be admitted of facts tending to establish that Mr X and Mr Y had committed offences. I pointed out then that prohibition of publication was within the discretion of the Tribunal by virtue of section 35(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The power to prohibit extends to publication of evidence given before the Tribunal and of matters contained in documents lodged with it or received by it in evidence. Before that ruling was given the Tribunal had in fact already given such directions in these proceedings. The effect of such directions is that, as the evidence given before the Tribunal must necessarily be reviewed in the reasons which the Tribunal gives for its decision, those reasons cannot be published.
  3. Further, there is no person who might be expected to disclose the reasons for the Tribunal's decision in defiance of that prohibition. Section 63 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 renders such wilful defiance of the Tribunal's directions punishable by a fine of $1,000 or imprisonment for three months. There is, therefore, I consider, no risk that the investigation by the Tribunal of the facts alleged to constitute offences by Mr X and Mr Y and the making of findings with regard to those matters for the purpose of reaching a decision in these proceedings will have any real and definite tendency to prejudice or to embarrass the fair trial of either of them in the Supreme. Court of Victoria. Nor is there any such tendency to prejudice or to embarrass the fair trial of a civil action in the Federal Court in which Steigers is an applicant."
  4. I adopt what is written in those paragraphs as applicable in respect of the hearing of A Co.'s application for review. It must be noted, however, that section 35 does not authorise the Tribunal to prohibit disclosure of the decision which is made in any proceedings. Section 142K of the Taxation (Administration) Act 1953, added by the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, requires that the identity of an applicant not be revealed; but it does not prohibit disclosure of the fact of the decision. Consequently, if the decision of the Tribunal were in respect of a matter which was in issue in criminal proceedings before a Court, it is possible that, by making that decision, the Tribunal would be in contempt of that Court.
  5. If the High Court of Australia does take the same view as Jackson J. that in conspiracy proceedings such as are faced by Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan the ineffectiveness of schemes to. create allowable deductions must be proved, the question whether the Tribunal, by deciding A Co.'s application or the application of any other taxpayer, will commit a contempt of court depends, I consider, on the nature of the scheme relied on in the particular case. If it is materially different from the scheme which is the subject of the alleged conspiracies, quite clearly a decision as to its effectiveness is not a decision in respect of a matter in issue in the criminal proceedings. Where, however, it is identical with that scheme, a decision by the Tribunal as to its effectiveness, if made known to the jury at the trial, might create a real and definite tendency to prejudice or to embarrass the fair trial of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan. However, before it could do so, it would be necessary for evidence to be given at the trial to establish that the scheme in respect of which the Tribunal had made its decision was in fact the same as the scheme in the alleged conspiracies. That could not be shown by presenting evidence of statements made or documents tendered in evidence at the Tribunal's hearing, at least not without breach of the Tribunal's order under section 35. But it might be possible for the evidence to be obtained from some other source, e.g. the taxpayer. I am inclined to the view, therefore, that, if the High Court decides that knowledge of the ineffectiveness of the scheme to create allowable deductions is an element of the offences, the Tribunal should not decide any application for review where the applicant relies on the effectiveness of a scheme identical with the scheme in Mr Saunders' and Mr Sheehan's alleged conspiracies. Where, however, an applicant relies upon some other scheme, there is no reason on the ground of possible contempt of Court why the Tribunal should not hear and decide the application.

23. I turn now to the difficulties which result from the fact that A Co., in order to discharge the burden of proving that the assessment is incorrect, may need to adduce evidence from Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan and that the answers to some of the questions which they may be asked may have a potential to incriminate them. The difficulties do not arise out of the fact that Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan are the defendants in criminal proceedings which are already on foot, although, as Mrs Balmford pointed out in Case U178, the two charges which they now face are worded in such broad terms that they encompass far more than the case which the prosecution has elected so far to present at the committal proceedings. Even if, when the indictment is drawn, the breadth of those charges is reduced so that the details of the conspiracies relate only to the 45 companies in respect of which the prosecution case has been presented at the committal proceedings, the difficulties facing the Tribunal will still remain. If, by answering a question, Mr Saunders or Mr Sheehan ' would incriminate himself in respect of any offence, not necessarily either of those with which they have been charged, he would be entitled to demand that he should not be required to answer it.

24. Whatever the outcome of the present criminal proceedings, the difficulties will not be removed except in respect of the two offences with which they are charged at present. If they are convicted, that will be a fair indication that, if they engaged in similar transactions in respect of other. companies, they probably committed similar offences by doing so. So any answers which they gave to questions asked of them in proceedings in the Tribunal in respect of those other transactions might well be of an incriminating nature. If, on the other hand, the present criminal proceedings result in their acquittal, that will not mean that the jury was necessarily satisfied that the transactions were lawful; they might have been acquitted only because the prosecution failed to prove some other element of each of the offences. If they were charged with other similar offences in the future, it might be able to remedy that failure in those proceedings. So an acquittal will not mean that answers which they may give in respect of similar transactions in respect of other companies are necessarily not of an incriminating nature in respect of other similar offences. Consequently, it cannot be expected that the present difficulties will be resolved by the completion of the trial in the present criminal proceedings.

25. So, if applications for review where the applicants need to call Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan as witnesses are not to be heard while answers to questions which will have to be put to Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan may incriminate them, the correctness of the assessments will never be capable of being finally determined while Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan are alive, or at least until they have been tried and either convicted or acquitted of every offence which they may be alleged to have committed. Where the taxpayer has paid the tax that was assessed, he will have his right to have it repaid to him (if his application for review is successful) postponed indefinitely. On the other hand, where the, taxpayer is a company and the T(UCT) Act is applicable, the liability of the vendor shareholders and the promoters will not arise and the public will suffer from the fact that the respondent is unable to recover from the vendor shareholders and the promoters any tax which should be paid by them under that Act.

26. Mr Pagone submitted that the evidence of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan would not be necessary for A Co. to discharge the burden of proving that the assessment was incorrect. The soundness of that assertion can be ascertained only when the matter is put to the test at the hearing. I shall assume for the moment that A Co. would be disadvantaged in trying to establish its case if Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan did not give evidence and answer questions about the transactions involving the C group of companies which were intended to create allowable deductions. In proceedings before any court or tribunal each party must decide for itself how best it should present its case. Not infrequently a party is not able to present its case as it would wish. An important witness may have died or have vanished without trace. He may have gone overseas; in that case the party must decide whether or not it is better to incur large expenditure on paying for him to return to the jurisdiction to give evidence (or on having his evidence taken on commission, if that is possible and acceptable to the court or tribunal) or of presenting a less cogent case.

27. So the situation which results from the possible unwillingness of Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan to give evidence which the applicant considers that it needs in order to establish or strengthen its case, but which they consider will incriminate them, is not particularly unusual. Where such a situation arises, the court or tribunal will generally try to ensure that the party is disadvantaged as little as can reasonably be achieved. In particular it may delay setting the case down for hearing in order to give the party an opportunity to resolve the situation, e.g. by tracing the witness, finding another person who is able to give similar evidence or by taking any other steps that may mitigate it; but, where a situation clearly cannot be resolved or cannot within a reasonable time be mitigated to a greater extent than has already been achieved, it does not permit further postponements. That, I am satisfied is how the Tribunal should deal with cases such as A Co.'s case.

28. Section 39 of the AAT Act requires the Tribunal to give a reasonable opportunity to every party to present his case; what constitutes a reasonable opportunity depends on the particular facts. The Tribunal is not required, nor is it authorised, to postpone indefinitely the hearing of an application where the difficulties faced by a party will not be resolved, or be capable of being mitigated, by delaying the hearing for a reasonable time. I have come to the conclusion, therefore, that, where an applicant needs to rely on the evidence of a witness who cannot give that evidence without incriminating himself and there is no reason to believe that he will be able to do so within a reasonable time, the Tribunal has no alternative but to set the application down for hearing, leaving the applicant to present, the best case he can in the circumstances.

29. I think it desirable to add that, as Mr Pagone pointed out, no person is entitled to refuse totally to give evidence to the Tribunal. He must answer questions unless the answer is one which may incriminate him. Clearly the applicant can call Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan to give evidence and they must do so. If a question is asked of either of them and he may incriminate himself by answering it, he may decline to do so. However, recently, when an application was heard by the Tribunal in the Taxation Division, a witness initially declined to answer questions because the answers to them might have incriminated him; but, after the Tribunal had made orders pursuant to section 35 of the AAT Act not only prohibiting disclosure of the evidence given by the witness but also excluding from the hearing room all persons other than the two Counsel, the witness and the member constituting the Tribunal and after Counsel had acknowledged that they were bound by the orders not to disclose the evidence to those instructing them, the witness agreed to answer all the questions which were put to him and did so. Whether he could have been compelled by the Tribunal to do so in those circumstances it is unnecessary for me to consider. If, as I understand may be the case, Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan were involved in the promotion of the scheme or schemes about which applicants wish them to give evidence, they may well regard themselves as being under some sort of duty to those applicants to give evidence on their behalf. If so, the procedure which was adopted in the recent case would appear to be well adapted to preventing them from suffering any adverse consequences from giving evidence which might incriminate them.

30. Accordingly, I am directing that A Co.'s application be set down for hearing in the normal course on the earliest convenient date after 1 July 1988. So far as other applications are concerned, in a case where the tax has been paid, the taxpayer is usually the only person disadvantaged by postponement of the hearing and no-one else is prejudiced by the postponement. Where, on the other hand, the tax assessed has not been paid, and particularly where the liability of vendor shareholders and promoters under the T(UCT) Act depends on the liability to pay that tax being finally determined, the applications should be set down for hearing in the normal course and not postponed because of the difficulties which the applicants will face because they may need to adduce from Mr Saunders and Mr Sheehan evidence which may incriminate those witnesses.
