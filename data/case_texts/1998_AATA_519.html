1. By notice dated 2 September 1994 the Applicant sought review of a decision by a delegate of the Respondent made 27 July 1994 affirming a prior decision made 27 July 1989 that during the term of his employment at the Australian Defence Force Academy (ADFA) the Applicant was not an eligible employee within the meaning of the Superannuation Act 1976.

2. The academic wing of ADFA is a college of the University of New South Wales (UNSW). As the Applicant's contract of employment was with the UNSW (although to be performed at ADFA) the University was, upon its application, joined as a party to these proceedings.

4. The Applicant's right to review by this Tribunal is pursuant to section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 which states inter alia:

"154. (1) In this section -

`decision' has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975;

`reviewable decision' means a decision of the Commissioner, or a delegate of the Commissioner, under this Act, under the superseded Act or under the regulations made under either of those Acts, and includes a decision of the Superannuation Board, or a delegate of the Superannuation Board (other than a decision under section 141 of the superseded Act);

`Superannuation Board' means the Superannuation Board established by the superseded Act.

(2) A person affected by a reviewable decision who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by notice in writing given to the Commissioner within the period of 30 days after the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision.

(3) ...

(4) Upon receipt of the request, the Commissioner shall reconsider the decision and may confirm or revoke the decision or vary the decision in such manner as he thinks fit.

(5) ...

(6) Applications may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or varied under sub-section (4) and for review of decisions of the Superannuation Board, or delegates of the Superannuation Board, made under section 141 of the superseded Act.

(7) For the purposes of such a review, the Tribunal shall, subject to sub-sections 21(1A) and 23(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, be constituted by a presidential member and 2 non-presidential members, of whom at least one shall be an eligible employee or a pensioner, or by 3 non-presidential members, of whom at least one shall be an eligible employee or a pensioner and at least one shall be a senior non-presidential member."

5. The Applicant's case is that having accepted an offer of employment with the UNSW, he became entitled to the benefits of the "Mobility Provisions" of the Public Service Act 1922 (as amended) and thus, during the currency of his employment with the UNSW, he remained an "eligible employee" as that term is defined in the Superannuation Act 1976 .

6. The so-called "Mobility Provisions" of the Public Service Act 1922 are to be found in Divisions 2 and 3 of Part V of that Act. The following provisions of the said Act are relevant:

"87K (2) Where a person who:

(a) is deemed by subsection 87J (6), 87Y (2) or 87Z (2) to have ceased to be an officer; or

(b) ceases, under subsection 87V (1) or 87ZC (2), to be an officer;

is engaged in eligible public employment on the day after the day at the expiration of which he is so deemed to have ceased, or so ceased, to be an officer, he becomes a person to whom this Division applies on the first-mentioned day."

Section 87C reads inter alia:

"(1) Subject to this section, an officer (including an unattached officer) becomes an officer to whom this Division applies:

(a) on the day on which he becomes engaged in eligible public employment;

...

(4) Subsection (1) does not apply to an officer who becomes engaged in eligible public employment:

(a) if he becomes so engaged by reason of being transferred to the Public Service of the Northern Territory under section 84A;

(b) if the repealed Officers' Rights Declaration Act 1928 applies to him in respect of that employment;

(c) if he becomes, under section 87J, upon becoming so engaged, an eligible person in relation to a declared Commonwealth function; or

(e) if, upon becoming so engaged, he is absent from the Service on leave of absence granted under this Act for the purpose of enabling him to become engaged in that employment, being leave of absence that was granted:

(i) if that employment became eligible public employment after the commencing day by virtue of the making of a regulation for the purposes of section 87 - before the day on which the regulation was made or the day as from which that employment became eligible public employment, whichever was the later day; or

(ii) in any other case - before the commencing day.

(5) Where an officer becomes, by reason of his becoming engaged, or while he is engaged, in particular employment, an officer to whom this Division applies, he continues to be such an officer until he ceases to be engaged in that employment or he ceases to be an officer, whichever first occurs."

And sub-section 87J(3) reads:

"Subject to this section, where the Board notifies the Prime Minister that it is satisfied that officers included in a class of officers are performing duties connected with the performance of a function, the Prime Minister may, by declaration published in the Gazette, declare that function to be a declared Commonwealth function and the duties of officers included in that class of officers to be connected with the performance of that declared Commonwealth function."

In conformity with sub-section 87J(3) the Minister for Finance, for and on behalf of the Prime Minister, published in the Commonwealth Gazette of 11 July 1986 a notice to the effect that the function of the conduct of University degree studies for officers at the University College of UNSW at ADFA was a prescribed function corresponding to a declared Commonwealth function and that employment by the said UNSW at ADFA was employment connected with the performance of prescribed functions.

7. The term "eligible public employment" is defined in subsection 87(1) of the Public Service Act 1922 as:

" `eligible public employment' means:

(e) employment as the holder of a public office; or

(f) employment by a public authority;"

Compare this definition of "eligible Commonwealth employment":

" `eligible Commonwealth employment' means:

(a) employment as the holder of a Commonwealth office; or

(b) employment by a Commonwealth authority;"

Whereas "employment" is defined to mean:

" `employment' means:

(a) employment in a full-time capacity; or

(b) employment in a part-time capacity where:

(i) the person employed does not also perform duties in the Service;

..."

Where paragraph 87(2)(jb) reads:

"A reference in this Part to employment by a Commonwealth authority shall be read as including a reference to:

(jb) employment as a member of the staff of the Australian Defence Force Academy;"

8. The Applicant's claim is that during his employment at ADFA he was an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 . "Eligible employee" is defined in subsection 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 in the following terms:

" `eligible employee' means -

(a) a person who, immediately before the commencement of this Act, was an employee for the purposes of the superseded Act and has continued to be, and is, such an employee;

(b) a person who is a permanent employee;

(c) a person who is a temporary employee and is specified in a direction given under section 11, 12 or 13;

(d) ...

(e) a person -

(i) who is a temporary employee;

(ii) ...

(iii) ...

and who, immediately before the commencement of the employment by reason of which he is such a temporary employee or his appointment to that office, was an eligible employee by virtue of another paragraph of this definition or a previous application of this paragraph;"

"Temporary employee" is defined as:

" `temporary employee' means -

(a) a person who is an employee for the purposes of the Public Service Act; and

(b) any other person employed by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority otherwise than in a permanent capacity,

but does not include -

(c) a part-time employee who is not an approved part-time employee; or

(d) ..."

An "approved part-time employee" is defined as:

" `approved part-time employee' means a part-time employee included in a class of part-time employees declared by the Minister, by notice published in the Gazette, to be an approved class of part-time employees for the purposes of this Act;"

9. The then Minister for Finance made the following declaration for the purposes of sub-section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 , namely that the following employees are a class of part-time employees which is an approved class of part-time employees for the purposes of that Act:

"(a) a person who is an officer for the purposes of the Public Service Act 1922; and

(b) a person employed by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority as defined in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, in a permanent capacity.

Provided that person is permitted by his or her terms and conditions of employment to undertake employment on a part-time basis in a permanent capacity."

Further, by Statutory Rule No 372 of 1986, being the Superannuation (Approved Authorities) Regulations (Amendment), the Minister for Finance declared that the University College, being the college established by the University of New South Wales within the Australian Defence Force Academy, was an "Approved Authority" as that term is defined in sub-section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 .

10. Crucial to the determining of the facts in this matter is the credit of the Applicant. We found the Applicant garrulous and he gave an impression of manufacturing his evidence and of re-interpreting events to what he saw as his advantage at the time. Perhaps the best illustration of this is his evidence to the Tribunal that in the period from 21 August 1986 he had been provided with a secretary at ADFA. In particular, he stated:

"She would come to my room, she would take dictation."

That person, a Ms Bolton, was called. She stated unequivocally that she had not taken dictation from the Applicant and was not his secretary. Her duties at the relevant time were to act as a typing pool for all members of the Department of Economics and Management of the University College at ADFA except for the head of the Department, Professor Kasper, who had his own secretary. Nor did she carry out any other secretarial type duties for members of the Department including the Applicant.

11. In coming to our decision in this matter therefore, we have placed greater reliance upon contemporaneous documents than the self-serving recollections of the Applicant. In contrast to the Applicant, however, we found Professor Kasper a careful and thorough (even pedantic) witness who had a better recall of events than the Applicant. Where versions of events vary, we prefer to accept the evidence of Professor Kasper.

12. The Applicant commenced employment in the Commonwealth Public Service on 15 August 1957. In 1986 he was employed by the Department of Foreign Affairs attached to the Australian Development Assistance Bureau (ADAB) at the level of a Clerical Administrative Officer Class 8. As appears from documents in Exhibit R2, the Applicant was not performing adequately in this position (see page 16 of Exhibit R2) and on or about 20 February 1986 made a claim pursuant to the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 for "stress" (page 60 Exhibit R2).

13. Records in the Applicant's file with the Department of Foreign Affairs reveal that the Applicant was absent from his employment because of "stress" for varying periods commencing 3 April 1986. On 3 November 1986 he returned to his normal employment on a part-time basis, working four hours a day; however, he reverted to full-time sick leave after one week and remained on sick leave from the Department of Foreign Affairs until his retirement at age 65 on 18 March 1987.

14. Prior to these events in early 1986, the Applicant had made the acquaintance of Professor Kasper, the head of the Department of Economics and Management at ADFA. On 4 February 1986 Professor Kasper wrote a minute to the Rector of the University College at ADFA which read inter alia (Exhibit R3 p92):

"1. I have been in contact with a very experienced Canadian academic with an excellent background in management, who will live in Canberra in the next few years. His name is Bruce Fisher, age 63 (personal summary attached).

2. Given our management mission, our (youthful) age structure and our limited experience in management, I plan to draw Bruce Fisher into our Department and will in due course approach you as to how we might frame an offer for a one- or two-year engagement."

15. In accordance with his plans Professor Kasper arranged for the Applicant to give a series of lectures at ADFA. Those lectures were for periods of five hours a week over six weeks and delivered to a small group of honours students.

16. We accept Professor Kasper's evidence that at that time the University College at ADFA was having difficulty in attracting appropriate staff and that the Applicant's curriculum vitae showed that he had experience in lecturing in the appropriate discipline.

17. As a result of discussions between Professor Kasper and the Applicant, it was resolved that the Applicant would be offered a non-tenured position termed a "Visiting Fellow". Remuneration was to be at the Senior Lecturer scale adjusted to take into account the fact that the Applicant would not be undertaking a full teaching load.

18. Negotiations then took place between the University and the Applicant. Those negotiations are set out in a series of letters commencing 2 July 1986 as contained in Exhibit R3 and are dated 2 July 1986, 19 July 1986, 11 August 1986, 13 August 1986, 16 August 1986, 21 August 1986, 22 August 1986, 27 August 1986, 28 August 1986, 30 August 1986, 2 September 1986, 26 November 1986, 3 December 1986, 4 December 1986, 24 December 1986, 8 January 1987, 13 January 1987, 17 March 1987, 18 March 1987, 20 March 1987 and 24 March 1987.

20. The above correspondence has been set out in some detail as it encapsulates the disagreement between the Applicant and the Respondent and the Party Joined. It will also be seen that the Applicant's view of what arrangements he had with the University have varied.

21. At the time the Applicant was negotiating with the University, and indeed, as he says, he was undertaking the duties of a Visiting Fellow in the latter half of 1986, he was of course absent from his employment with ADAB on sick leave because of "stress". On 21 August 1986 he wrote to the Director-General ADAB (Exhibit R1 p5):

"As you know, I have been on sick leave for the greater part of the past five months. The advice I have from my medical advisers is to the effect that I am not fit to work in the Public Service and that to return to duty in the Service before I reach the Public Service retirement age (March 1987) will result in an aggravation of my illness. However, they also advise me that it would be therapeutic for me to have a part-time role in an academic environment where I can work in hours of my own choosing.

I would be grateful if you would authorize me, under your delegation from the Public Service Board, to engage in part-time employment in an academic environment within a university during the currency of my sick leave. I am advised that Section 91 is the relevant part of the Public Service Act for the grant of such leave."

22. On or about 28 August 1986 the Director-General of ADAB replied to the Applicant stating inter alia (Exhibit R1 p10):

"Taking into account advice from the Public Service Board, I am unable to give blanket approval at this stage to your engagement on any remunerative employment during the work periods covered by your paid sick leave from the Bureau. Of course should you wish to be involved in academic activities during those periods on an honorary basis, let alone outside of these periods, I would have no objections and would leave that entirely to your judgement. Naturally we wish you success in finding useful and constructive activity during your sick leave period, within the above-mentioned considerations."

23. The above letter from the Director-General of ADAB to the Applicant elicited the following reply dated 13 October 1986 (Exhibit R1 p12):

"Further to our discussion this afternoon, I now make application for leave to engage in employment which is in the interests of the defence of the Commonwealth. The period for which leave is sought is from the date of approval to 19 March 1987.

...

The University was advised that while I felt gratified by the written and oral invitations to visit the Academy and to plan, direct and develop management programs, I felt uneasy about making a full contribution as a visiting fellow or visiting professor fellow. The University made a further offer to me in a letter dated 11 August 1986 (copy attached). I informed the University that I felt much better and very comfortable with a reduction in my offerings, and that I felt pleased that for my part-time contribution the University would pay me 0.8 of an annual salary of $54,460."

24. No doubt, due to his continued absence from his normal work, the Applicant was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 13 October 1986. On 31 October 1986 the Applicant received a letter regarding his return to duty which read in part (Exhibit R1 p17):

"As you are aware the CMO recommended 4 hours per day of less structured research work without (undue) deadlines. With this in mind, it is proposed to initially locate you with the Audit Unit from Monday 3 November 1986. Your attendance for 4 hours daily should be within `core hours' (i.e. 9.30 am - 12 noon and 2.00 pm - 4.00 pm)."

As stated above, the Applicant's return to duty even on reduced hours lasted one week.

25. Professor Kasper was extensively cross-examined by the Applicant's counsel as to the Applicant's place in the Department of Economics and Management and the arrangements for his employment. As pointed out by Professor Kasper, the actual contractual details between the Applicant and the UNSW were handled by Mr Spooner, the University's Academic Staff Officer who was at the University's Kensington campus. What we do accept, however, is that it was never within Professor Kasper's contemplation that the Applicant would somehow transfer with subsisting entitlements from the Commonwealth Public Service to the University.

26. It was submitted by the Applicant's counsel that the UNSW had a duty towards the Applicant to inform him that ADFA was a body which was subject to the Mobility Provisions of the Public Service Act 1922 . Whether the UNSW had this duty or not, we find that the Applicant was well aware of the Mobility Provisions and was very concerned to manipulate matters so as to take advantage of those provisions. On 6 August 1986 he wrote to Professor Kasper stating inter alia (Exhibit A11):

"There have been more telephone calls from Mr. Spooner:

...

- he is not concerned with my public service situation: he agreed that in accepting the proposed offer I would be gaining on-going employment on the regular staff of the College and mobility provisions would apply - there is no need for him to mention these matters in the offer of appointment."

And on 16 August 1986 the Applicant wrote to the Respondent stating (T8 p22):

"I am grateful to you for speaking to me in relation to my superannuation enquiry and for indicating that, on receiving a written request from me, you would confirm in writing the advice given me regarding my salary for superannuation purposes in the event that I accepted the offer of part-time employment as a member of the staff of the Australian Defence Force Academy.

It is my present intention to accept the offer of part-time employment made in University of New South Wales letters of 2 July 1986 and 11 August 1986 (copies of these letters are attached). Concurrently I would exercise my rights under the mobility provisions of the Public Service Act ensuring that I remain a contributor under the Superannuation Act. A copy of my letter to the University, dated 16 August 1986, is attached."

27. We have no doubt that it was the reply to this letter that led to the Applicant seeking to reinterpret the arrangements between himself and the UNSW. On 29 August 1986 the Respondent wrote to the Applicant stating inter alia (T9 p23):

"The Superannuation (Approved Part-time Employees) Regulations only apply to persons who are permanent part-time employees; they do not apply to either temporary part-time employees or persons employed part-time under contract. It will be necessary to establish that the offer of employment you have received from ADFA is in fact an offer of permanent part-time employment for you to be an `approved part-time employee' for the purposes of the Act, and thereby remain an `eligible employee' for the purposes of the Act."

28. Unfortunately the officer of the UNSW who was responsible for contractual arrangements between the University and the Applicant, Mr Spooner, was too ill to give evidence in these proceedings. In one sense that does not matter as the documents speak for themselves. Significantly, however, in his affidavit (Exhibit P2 para 3) he states:

"I do recall that Mr Fisher was a difficult person to deal with and that it was necessary to, as much as possible, put all of the dealings you had with Mr Fisher in writing as misunderstandings seemed to occur following oral conversations."

29. Mr Spooner was also able to add (Exhibit P2 para 4):

"I had handled many appointments like Mr Fisher's. Visiting appointments were either honorary or remunerated depending upon the needs of the University and the type of person given the appointment. Invariably visiting appointments were on a temporary basis as they were designed to recognise academic contributions to the University and in some cases facilitate academic exchange between the University and the community. I also handled many employees who were transferring their service from the Australian Public Service (`APS') to the University College in Canberra. Inevitably, those matters involved many dealings with the department from which the prospective employee was coming and the matters were never simple. As I recall, the employee concerned usually wanted to transfer across to the University with all of their benefits intact (this was the case whether or not there were mobility provisions) and the department would try and pass over to the University any contingent liabilities for recreation leave, long service leave and so forth. What ensued was a negotiation between the University and the department over what percentage of leave and other liabilities could be assumed by the University and what would remain a liability of the APS. If Mr Fisher had been transferring from AIDAB (sic) I would have expected letters, telephone calls and other contact with AIDAB (sic). As it is, there is no correspondence of that type on the University file. I also have no independent recollection of any contact from AIDAB (sic) or any other part of the APS."

30. As stated above, the Applicant's request to ADAB made by letter dated 13 October 1986 requesting leave in order to engage in employment which was in the interests of the defence of the Commonwealth, a letter which was forwarded on the same day as the Applicant had been examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer as to his fitness for work as a result of his anxiety state (see Exhibit R2 p30). The request of 13 October 1986 was refused. One of the reasons is stated in a letter from the office of the Public Service Board to the Director-General ADAB, namely that the Public Service Board had a policy that other forms of leave should not be granted whilst a member was on sick leave. The Tribunal can only add that if the Applicant was unable to perform the restricted duties of his employment, it is difficult to ascertain how he could enter upon the duties of a Visiting Fellow at ADFA .

31. Having been thwarted in an attempt to transfer to ADFA upon the basis that such transfer was in the interests of the defence of the Commonwealth, the Applicant tried a new tactic. On 4 December 1986 he wrote to the Director-General of ADAB in the following terms (Exhibit R1 p20):

"A number of months ago I advised the Bureau that I had been selected for employment on the staff of the Australian Defence Force Academy. The Academy is now covered by Part IV provisions of the Public Service Act. I accepted the amended offer for permanent part-time employment at the Academy which is for me to make a contribution of 0.8 of a full-time role for a fixed term of three years (subject to annual review). For this part-time contribution the University will pay me 0.8 of an annual salary of $54,460 ...

... it is my current intention to take up the appointment on 5 December 1986 without resigning from the APS. Accordingly I wish to be released from ADAB (under Part IV provisions) on the morning of 5 December so that I may then take up employment on the staff of the Academy."

32. In evidence the Applicant gave an account of how he alleges he took up duty at ADFA on 5 December 1986. He wrote to ADFA on 4 December 1986 stating that he was taking up duty on 5 December. He informed the Acting Head of the Department that he was now officially there and he placed notices on the notice boards of the Academy stating that he had taken up duty. He also announced loudly three times in the hall of the college, that he had taken up duty.

33. The reaction of the Department of Economics and Management to the above is set out in a letter to the Applicant by Dr Gannicott who was the Acting Head of the Department at that time. That letter dated 24 April 1987 reads (Exhibit R3 p29):

"Dear Bruce,

I refer to your letter of April 11 1987. In that letter you claim that you `have, of course, been working in the Department for some considerable time'. If you mean by this that you have been officially employed in the Department then I must reiterate to you the University's view that you took up duty on March 20 1987. I must also point out to you that I took up duty as Acting Head on November 30 1987 (sic), and on no occasion between then and March 20 did I ask or require you to perform any official duty whatsoever.

During this time you have been permitted to continue occupying the office assigned to you by Professor Kasper, when you were employed as a part-time lecturer earlier in 1986. You were allowed to occupy this office, despite the fact that you were not performing official duties, out of sheer goodwill. Given that we had at that time some spare office capacity, and given also our assumption that you were negotiating in good faith with the University to bring about an early entry on duty, I took the view that it would be discourteous and unreasonable to require you to vacate an office which you might quickly reoccupy on official duty.

In the light of the fact that this Department has extended considerable goodwill towards you in the provision of office and telephone facilities, I must make clear to you that I strongly object to your reference to Peter Hall, with its apparent attempt to involve members of the Department in your persistent claim to have taken up duty in December 1986. Your letter is simply incorrect in claiming that `early in December ... Peter Hall officially gave me a very warm and sincere welcome to the Department.' My information is that you spoke to Peter Hall on December 5, telling him that you had taken up duty on that day. Peter merely acknowledged, with thanks, that you had conveyed that information - and his polite acknowledgement was no less than was to be expected, given the continuing courtesy you have received from this Department. In fact, the University does not accept that you took up duty on December 5, and nothing said by Peter Hall or anyone else in this department can reasonably be taken to imply the contrary."

34. The abovementioned correspondence, particularly the last paragraph thereof, also reinforces the view of the Members of this Tribunal that the Applicant is a person who will misrepresent events if he considers that it is to his advantage to do so.

35. Although the Applicant claimed that he was engaged at ADFA full-time as and from 5 December 1986, he was still an employee of the Department of Foreign Affairs on sick leave. In cross-examination he conceded he continued to receive sick pay from the Department of Foreign Affairs until he retired on 18 March 1987 at age 65. A payment made late in these proceedings by the Applicant to the Receiver of Public Monies, allegedly being the amount of salary received by him during the period he claims he was at ADFA, does not alter our view of the facts as they existed at the relevant time.

36. The Applicant retired from the Commonwealth Public Service on 18 March 1987, that is to say the day immediately prior to his 65th birthday. He then completed the formalities and became an employee of the UNSW on 20 March 1987, that is the day immediately after his 65th birthday.

37. The Applicant's stay at ADFA was apparently not a happy or productive one during the 1987 academic year. During the 1987 academic year Professor Kasper received a complaint from students as to the Applicant's competency as a lecturer and at the end of the first year the Applicant's contract with the UNSW was not renewed.

38. Having regard to all the material before us in this matter we find the following facts, namely:

(i) the Applicant and the UNSW in a series of correspondence entered into negotiations respecting a position as a lecturer to be taken up by the Applicant at the University College of the UNSW at ADFA;

(ii) by letter dated 21 August 1986 the Applicant accepted an offer of part-time employment at ADFA, the salary to be paid being 80% of the maximum salary paid to a senior lecturer;

(iii) the date the Applicant was to take up duty was a matter of further agreement between the Applicant and the University;

(iv) at the time the Applicant accepted the offer from the UNSW he was an officer of the Commonwealth Public Service (CPS);

(v) at no time did the Applicant either resign from the CPS or obtain leave of absence from the CPS. He remained as an officer of the CPS until he retired on 18 March 1987, the day before his 65th birthday. His retirement from the CPS was because he had reached the age of 65 years, the normal age of retirement from that service;

(vi) at the time the Applicant accepted the offer of employment from the UNSW he was well aware of the mobility provisions of the Public Service Act;

(vii) the Applicant was not employed by the UNSW until 20 March 1987;

(viii) prior to taking up his employment with the UNSW the Applicant was granted certain courtesies by the University College at ADFA. These courtesies included the use of a room and telephone and access to a pooled typing arrangement. At no time was the Applicant provided with a secretary, as opposed to the provision of a person to type papers and correspondence for him along with other members of the Department of Economics and Management;

(ix) notwithstanding the Applicant's unilateral actions on 5 December 1986, the UNSW did not regard or accept the Applicant as entering into its employ until 20 March 1987. There was no action taken by anyone concerned with the UNSW which in any way indicated an acceptance of the Applicant's actions on 5 December 1986. In particular we reject the allegation that the Applicant was "warmly welcomed into the Department" on that day;

(x) although the Applicant may have done some work at ADFA in 1986, that work (apart from a six week period in early 1986) was in anticipation of his later taking up duty at a date in the future and was on a voluntary basis; and

(xi) at the time the Applicant accepted the offer of employment with the UNSW he well knew that that employment was to be on a part-time basis and was not permanent but for an initial term of one year with a maximum of three years.

39. It may well be that the Applicant will claim that the UNSW breached the contract between them in refusing to accept the Applicant's taking up duty on a date proposed by him - see the letter of 11 August 1986 to the Applicant which states (Exhibit R3 p79):

"... if you could let me know the date on which you would propose to take up duty."

If such an action lies, then the Applicant's remedy is in a forum other than this Tribunal.

40. In our opinion, there was no breach of any contract of employment by the UNSW. The parties had, by letters of 2 July 1986 and 11 August 1986 to the Applicant and his reply of 21 August 1986, reached agreement as to the main terms of the contract by which they were to be bound. That other matters can be the subject of later agreement and incorporation into the contract is recognised in cases such as G R Securities Pty Ltd v Baulkham Hills Private Hospital Pty Ltd 40 NSWLR 631 especially at 634, quoting Sinclair, Scott & Co Ltd v Naughton [1929] HCA 34; 43 CLR 310 at 317, namely:

"... the parties were content to be bound immediately and exclusively by the terms which they had agreed upon whilst expecting to make a further contract in substitution for the first contract, containing, by consent, additional terms".

In this matter the date the Applicant was to take up duty was subject to a further agreement between the parties. The words of the letter of 11 August 1986 to the Applicant do not admit of an unequivocal acquiescence by the UNSW to a proposal by the Applicant but refer to a date "you would propose to take up duty" (Tribunal's emphasis).

41. Consistent with the cases quoted above, it was thus open for the parties to agree to the main terms of the Applicant's contract of employment and then enter into a new contract containing an additional term, namely that there was a mutually agreeable date of commencement. In fact, this is what happened as on 20 March 1987 the Applicant in effect made, and commenced performance of, a new contract with the UNSW containing the additional term that the period of the contract commenced on 20 March 1987.

42. We are also satisfied that any contract between the Applicant and the UNSW was not one of permanent employment.

43. What constitutes permanent employment has been discussed in two decisions of this Tribunal, namely, Montgomery v Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal decision No 2078 of 11 April 1985) and Rumley v Commissioner for Superannuation 11 ALN N1.

44. In re Montgomery supra the Tribunal said at paragraphs 25 and 26:

"25. If there is no reference to the period of time for which an employment by the Crown is to last the inference is that it will last indefinitely but not of course, forever. The right of the Crown or for that matter any employer, to dismiss an employee without cause is well understood in the community subject of course to reasonable notice and statutory rights, if any. But when an employment is described as permanent it is generally either a reference to the normal retiring age, intervening sickness or misconduct excepted, or at least to the expectation of the employee that unless exceptional circumstances supervene he or she may anticipate continuous employment for an indefinite time.

26. If on the other hand an employment is described as temporary it is a reference to some specific period of time or some future event which will obviously result in the termination of the contract. For example, a research assistant engaged to assist a Royal Commission would obviously treat his employment as being bound to conclude when the Commission delivered its report."

The Tribunal then continued at paragraph 34:

"34. The apparent reason for excluding temporary employees from the early retirement benefits conferred by division 2 of Part V is that by the very nature of the terms of a temporary engagement the position or office occupied by the employee will cease to exist either by abolition or otherwise when the contract has run its course. If it is a term of the contract that it will end on the happening of a certain event or at the expiration of a specific period the eligible employee cannot fairly be deemed to have retired involuntarily. But if there were no such term and the date for termination is entirely in the hands of the employer the employment must be regarded as `in a permanent capacity' regardless of the expectations or predictions of either party to the contract or any objective observer as to when termination is most likely to occur."

45. In Criminale and Ors v State Superannuation Board and Anor 22 IR 240 Lee J said at p247, after referring to McClelland v Northern Island General Health Services Board [1957] WLR 594:

"Whether an employee is or is not within the definition is not to be judged merely by reference to whether his employer has stated to him that he is, for instance, employed at an annual rate, payable weekly, or a weekly rate or a fortnightly rate but by reference to whether it is the intention of the parties that his employment shall be permanent in the sense that barring serious economic circumstances and, of course, putting to one side misconduct by the employee, the position is intended to be available indefinitely."

46. The question of what constituted permanent vis-a-vis temporary employment was also considered by von Doussa J in Andersen v Umbakumba Community Council 56 IR 102 at 104. In that case the contract of employment stated:

"Employment hereunder shall commence on 5 April 1993 and shall cease at the close of business on 4 April 1995."

At p106 his Honour said:

"A contract of employment to run throughout a nominated number of days, weeks, or years would be a contract of employment for a specified period of time. If the terms of the contract of employment, instead of identifying in this manner the period of time during which it is to run, provides that it is to run until some future event, the timing of the happening of which is uncertain when the contract is made, the contract will be for an indeterminate period of time.

...

... the cessation date merely records the outer limit of a period beyond which the contract of employment will not run (unless a new Agreement is entered into pursuant to cl 29). Within the period stated in Sch 1 the period of the contract of employment is indeterminate. At any point during the two year period identified by the commencement and cessation dates neither side could know with any certainty when the period of the contract of employment might come to an end."

This case was one alleging unjust dismissal pursuant to the Industrial Relations Act 1988 (Cth). We do not find it helpful in considering the notion of permanent employment as that term is used in the Superannuation Act 1976 .

47. Similarly, we do not regard the fact that the Applicant was held against an establishment position as determinative of whether his employment was permanent or temporary.

48. We find that the Applicant's employment with the Party Joined was temporary employment. The contract specified a period of one year which may or may not have been renewed. There was no undertaking that the Applicant's employment would continue until some nominal retiring age or for an indefinite period but rather a very specific period which in any event would not exceed three years. To put it another way, the Applicant did not have a tenured position but had a contract for a specified period, of which only one year was certain.

49. We also find that the Applicant's employment was part-time. That much is made clear by the correspondence between the parties. The Applicant, at the time he accepted his appointment, was under no illusions about the part-time nature of his employment and his salary was structured to take account of this fact. When the Applicant made an offer to the UNSW to become a full-time employee, he was soundly rebuffed - see the UNSW letter of 27 August 1986 to the Applicant, together with the Applicant's reply of 30 August 1986.

50. There is no evidence before us that the Applicant at any time was an approved part-time employee. The contrary is true as the Minister's Direction of 1 April 1986 refers only to persons employed in a permanent capacity.

51. As the Applicant was at all relevant times both a temporary and a part-time employee of the Party Joined, and not an approved part-time employee within the Direction of the Minister for Finance dated 1 April 1986, he was therefore not an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 and the decision under review is affirmed.
