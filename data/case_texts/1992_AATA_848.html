Administrative Appeals Tribunal

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - default assessment - overseas bank account - allegations of income from Australian criminal activity - evidence – admissibility - natural justice - applicant overseas - admissibility of video conference evidence - evidence requires cross examination as to credit - whether video evidence satisfies requirements of natural justice

Applicant

Date: 3 December 1992

Place: Sydney

REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. The Tribunal delivered a decision on preliminary arguments about the admissibility of video conference evidence on 7 April 1992. A Directions Hearing took place on 20 October 1992 to establish ground rules for the formal hearing.
  2. At that Directions Hearing the respondent submitted first that, since it was alleged that the applicant left Australia illegally, the Tribunal's "indulgence" (to use the term of counsel for the respondent) in granting a video conference hearing to hear evidence of the applicant should be reviewed. Second, that the evidence to be admitted by video conference should be evidence as to which no issue of credit arises. Since the evidence of the applicant manifestly would involve serious testing of credit, and this was implicit in all argument at the earlier Directions Hearing, this submission amounts to an invitation to the Tribunal to reverse its previous finding and to hold that video conference procedures should not be permitted.
  3. On the first submission, that the Tribunal's "indulgence" in granting a video conference hearing should be reviewed, the Tribunal finds against the respondent The respondent sought to have an affidavit of a Federal Police Officer, to the effect that the applicant left Australia illegally, entered into evidence. This evidence was excluded on the basis that it was not relevant. The reason for this was that the Tribunal had already made a decision at the earlier hearing that there was a reasonable apprehension on the part of the applicant that he would be victimised if he returned to Australia to give evidence. The respondent had ample opportunity to put its case on the matter at that hearing. But even if the Tribunal had to consider the issue de novo, the circumstances of the applicant's departure from Australia and any alleged crimes in Australia are not relevant to the issue in front of the Tribunal. This is a tax case not a trial about the applicant's claims to good citizenship and the sole issue is one of the accessibility of the applicant to the Tribunal where there is reasonably perceived impediment to his attendance in person. It is not a question of "indulgence" but one of the Tribunal, fulfilling its obligations under its Act to allow the applicant to make his case and its obligation to provide natural justice or, to use the more evocative American term, procedural fairness to both parties. Key among these grounds is that, even assuming in the respondent's favour that the applicant is guilty of unspeakable crimes and illegal escape from Australia and that his inability to attend the Tribunal in person is a direct result of his own actions, justice demands that he should, so far as it is reasonably practicable, consistent with other procedural fairness imperatives, have the basic civil right to present his evidence to the Tribunal. This Tribunal is not passing criminal judgment, it is deciding only whether the applicant is liable for Australian tax. This justice imperative is given sharp focus in a case, such as the present, where the default assessments are based on not much more than guesstimates which are, in turn, inferences drawn from tenuous connections between flows of cash out of Australia and the applicant, guesstimates which this Tribunal at its earlier hearing seriously weighed the possibility of rejecting because they did not pass the basic threshold of being a valid assessment, and where the onus to rebut these guesstimates is put firmly on the applicant taxpayer.

4. Conclusions about the right to present video conference evidence were reached in the earlier decision after the respondent was given adequate means to make submissions on this subject. The applicant objected to the respondent's argument on the basis that the current hearing was merely to set the practical ground rules of the video conference. The Tribunal agrees with the applicant and sees no basis or adequate reason to vary them now.

  1. The respondent put forward the argument that the Tribunal had the obligation to make the right or preferable decision and not decide solely on the basis of argument put forward by the parties. Davies J in Carpentaria Transport Pty Ltd v FC of T 90 ATC 4590, 4592-3 overturned a Tribunal decision on the basis of a fundamental statutory definition which was not put forward in argument by the parties. While this general point is taken, its limits must be clearly delineated within accepted notions of the Tribunal's role in an adversary system, lf one of the parties fails to argue its case effectively or to put forward argument, how far is the Tribunal physically resourced or able to do the job for them? How far should it over-ride tactical decisions to refrain from taking available points of law or fact? To take just one problem, under s 34(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal is bound to make a decision in accordance with the terms of the parties' agreement.
  2. Counsel for the respondent argued that the Tribunal was not bound by principles of res judicata and could go back and reconsider its earlier decisions. Effective use of scarce resources and maintenance of public faith in a quasi-judicial institution built on legal foundations would indicate that it follow its own considered decisions, particularly those recently made after considered argument of the issues. In fact, being a tribunal based on legal traditions of persuasive precedent, it should follow decisions of other members unless there are good and strong reasons for departing from them. Parties to Tribunal references cannot be allowed to second guess submissions and come back to the Tribunal every time they adduce new evidence or find better reasons. This is affirmed by Pincus J in Bogaards v McMahon [1988] FCA 161; (1988) 80 ALR 342, 348 where he held that the tribunal was not entitled to deal with the application before it because '... the precise issue sought to be brought before the tribunal has been exhausted by its previous orders, and that the tribunal is functus officio as to the necessary legal effect of its orders and has no power to review them." He went on (at 350) to hold that where the Administrative Appeals Tribunal deals directly with a decision under review, its functions are exhausted and it cannot reopen the same issue. The same result follows with consent orders. In my view the decision can be extended to orders made in Directions Hearings. Whether the Administrative Appeals Tribunal is strictly a judicial body in the Constitutional sense, and whether strict res judicata or issue estoppel apply, are not the key issues. Bogaards is authority for the proposition that an exercise of the Tribunal's power creates an estoppel.
  3. Of course, the applicant's fundamental right to put evidence before the Tribunal must be weighed against the respondent's right to test that evidence fully and fairly. These rights are balanced with some care in the earlier decision. And this brings me to the respondent's second submission, that the evidence to be given by video conference should be limited to evidence to which no issue of credit arises. The respondent submitted that procedural fairness could be accorded in this case only by taking evidence on which there is a matter of credit by the personal attendance of the witness in front of the Tribunal. The respondent sought to persuade the Tribunal that the earlier decision of the Tribunal had not decided this point, deciding only the narrower point that the Tribunal could take evidence by video conference.
  4. The major problem with these submissions was that they traversed exactly the same ground as the earlier decision. Counsel for the applicant argued that the issue had not been decided. This argument is rejected. It was the admissibility of the video evidence of the applicant which was directly in issue in the earlier hearing, with all the questions of the likelihood that credibility would be tested that that necessarily implied. The transcript of evidence shows that a different counsel for the respondent, when invited explicitly by the Tribunal to make a submission on the general issue of the non-admissibility of video conference evidence, refrained from doing so. He preferred to rest his case on the argument that the applicant in the case had no reasonable basis for failing to attend the Tribunal in person.
  5. The Tribunal has two competing interests to balance and any decision necessarily involves a choice of evils. The submissions of the respondent focus on the second of those interests only and was not a great deal of help on the more difficult question of reconciling two desirable but competing interests. A number of authorities were cited as to the importance of procedural fairness. Barbaro v Minister of Immigration [1982] FCA 141; (1982) 44 ALR 690,692 and Minister of Immigration v Pochi (1980)44 FLR 41 held that principles of natural justice apply to hearings of the Tribunal. These points were taken up in Fletcher v FC of T (1988) 19 FLR 455, citing earlier authority, which stressed the Tribunal's duty to act with judicial fairness and impartiality, but emphasised the need for flexible adoption of fair procedures which are appropriate and adapted to the circumstances of the particular case (citing Mason J in Koia v West [1985] HCA 81; (1985) 159 CLR 550, 585). The cases emphasise the citizen's right to natural justice and the need for balanced translation of that principle into a modern procedural context.

10. There can be no right more basic to natural justice than the right of each party to put its side of the case. But broad generalizations about natural justice are not very instructive in this case because we are dealing with a novel issue involving new technology which, potentially, gives a new dimension to the ability of the Tribunal to accord procedural fairness to the parties to disputes. In the process the Tribunal must balance the right to a full and free hearing of the applicant's case against the same right for the respondent to test the evidence of the applicant in full and unfettered cross examination. This becomes, unfortunately and to some extent at least, a zero sum game in which this fundamental right of natural justice of the applicant is bought to a degree with compromises by the respondent. If we insist on a full right to cross examine the applicant in person before we can accept his evidence, for all practical purposes on the Tribunal's finding of reasonable apprehension of victimisation if he attends, we exclude the applicant's practical ability to give his evidence to the Tribunal. In the unreported decision of Dahlia Mining Co Ltd and North West Iron Company Limited v Collector of Customs (Federal Court, Unreported 20 December 1991, Judgment No. 833/91) at 25 Davies J stressed that the Tribunal, while not bound by the strict rules of evidence, is bound to provide "to the parties fair and equal opportunity to present a case". He went on to say that the ordinary course of proceedings is by way of oral evidence on disputed matters in the Tribunal. I respectfully endorse this view and emphasise that this present case involves very unusual facts and should not be seen as a general precedent to present evidence to the Tribunal in disputed matters by medium of video conference. The Tribunal was designed from the outset as a more flexible vehicle for resolving disputes, one less hidebound by traditional rules of evidence and formalities if substantive justice could be served by new approaches. The costs and effective practical delivery of mechanisms for the dispensation of justice were obviously high in the minds of the architects of the Tribunal. In the vast majority of cases, certainly where the amount of money or non-monetary values in dispute is sufficient to considerably outweigh the cost saving of electronic means of communication, the normal method of dealing with key matters of disputed evidence will be through normal evidence of witnesses presented and cross examined in person.

  1. It is significant that Davies J stressed equal opportunity to the parties to present their case. The content of such a principle cannot rely on formalistic assertions to the effect that both parties are free to come to the Tribunal when the substance of this right is dented to one or other party in practice. To give proper content to this principle, the Tribunal must have regard to the realities of access in a particular case and to the adequacy of new technology to provide a reasonable approximation for personal attendance to give evidence. In the real world, the content of this principle may not be perfect access to either party but the fairest practical opportunity to present evidence, consistent with the granting of reciprocal rights to the other party. In the final analysis, the Tribunal has reserved two safeguards. First, there will be active supervision of the video evidence session to ensure that fair opportunity is given to both sides. If this must be active Tribunal supervision it is because it becomes too easy for one or other party to exploit the situation by paying lip service to the right of the other to be heard while denying its substance. Second, the Tribunal explicitly reserved the right to the parties to put submissions to the Tribunal on the proper weight to be given this evidence. Nobody pretends this is the easy path or that issues of balance will not be involved. But the exercise of judgments on both matters will be the subject of a Tribunal finding and this will be subject to full review by the Federal Court.
  2. In Garcin v Amerindo Investment Advisors Ltd [1991]4 All ER655,656-7 Morritt J in the UK High Court allowed video conference evidence by a witness in a foreign jurisdiction. Morritt J acknowledged the genera) rule that evidence should be proved orally in open court but used a general discretion in the Supreme Court rules to grant the opportunity for video conference presentation. The Court addressed the problem of the lack of sanctions to compel the witness to answer questions or any sanction to reinforce the oath. The learned judge did not see these as impediments, referring (at 659) to the ability of the applicants, in that event, to renew arguments for the exclusion of applicant's evidence. Significantly, in reaching his decision he took into account the cost and expedition of the procedure adopted. The evidence was from an official in a US investment firm about accounts of the defendants in the action. It did not involve evidence of a party to the litigation.

13. In Australian Postal Commission v Hayes [1989] FCA 176; (1989) 87 ALR 283,289 Wilcox J said:

"Counsel argue that the testing of opposing relevant material by cross-examination is an essential feature of the opportunity to correct or contradict that material; it is not enough that the party against whom the evidence is led has the right to present evidence in reply. Moreover, although counsel accept that there exists some discretion to control cross-examination so as to ensure relevance and to guard against repetition and prolixity, it is said that the right to cross-examine means the right effectively to cross-examine. If directions given by a court or a tribunal have the effect of so fettering cross-examination that a witness' evidence cannot property be tested, procedural fairness has been denied."

That, of course, is the question for analysis and the question which counsel for the respondent did not take up at the appropriate time in the first directions hearing of this matter. The question for analysis is whether a video conference gives opportunity for effective cross examination and this Tribunal finds, prima facie, that it does. Prolonged cross examination can, for all practical purposes, allow the respondent to argue he has not had the chance to property test evidence. It can strip the applicant in this case of any substance to his right to enjoy a fair opportunity to put his side of the case.

Counsel for the respondent reassured the Tribunal that the respondent would not use the procedure in this oppressive way and the Tribunal is happy to accept this assurance. It would not be a happy situation if counsel for a respected independent statutory officer either used his powers or created the perception that those powers would be used as an instrument to deny the substance of procedural fairness to a taxpayer. The Tribunal will be active, during the hearing, in ensuring that the right to test opposing material is not used oppressively and, in the process of evaluating its probative value, will take this into account As our system of courts and tribunals move into the brave new world of new technology and are forced to address the tough decisions which need to be made to provide timely and cost effective and accessible justice to the Australian community they will, as the High Court has already done in its own appellate procedures, adapt their procedures and their rules to suit the changing environment. If Australian judicial institutions (I use this term in the widest sense to cover all courts and tribunals) do not adapt flexibly and creatively to new technologies and pay greater regard to the cost of conducting hearings, they will be replaced, as it has been in many areas already, by other dispute resolution mechanisms which can give new content to concepts of procedural fairness, to the practical needs of clients who seek justice and to delivery of a service at a cost which does not effectivety exclude most taxpayers with disputes. It is ail too easy to pay lip service to the formal notion of procedural fairness and to suffer the effective erosion of its practical content.

14. Of course, the effectiveness of the applicant's rebuttal of the evidence will be assessed in terms of the total context in which that evidence is given. No doubt, in response to the procedural fairness type of argument, the respondent has shown a willingness to be more flexible in the time for the video link. At the Directions Hearing the respondent was talking in terms of booking 1 or 2 hours and reserving some further time which might be necessary. The applicant was talking in terms of requiring something like two days of video conference and of the need to put something up to 50 to 60 documents, sight unseen, to the applicant during the course of cross examination. The applicant reserved liberty to apply to see these documents at a future directions hearing. The means of introducing these documents at the hearing should not, with the availability of overseas agents, prove to be beyond the ingenuity of the parties. There is liberty to apply if they cannot reach an amicable arrangement which fulfils the respondent's purpose.

15. For the record, it should be noted that at the Directions Hearing it was conceded on behalf of the applicant, that the applicant warved rights to secrecy within the terms of the Act so far as it was necessary to give practical implementation to the video conference. The parties agreed that the applicant would arrange and pay for the practical details of the video conference, with one terminal in the central Sydney CBD and the other at a place convenient to the applicant. The respondent should be given ample opportunity to inspect the room to be used and the arrangements with liberty to apply if any issues need to be resolved. The hearing will be held in February 1993 or as soon as practicable thereafter at a mutually advantageous time and there will be a further Directions Hearing before this to settle outstanding issues.
