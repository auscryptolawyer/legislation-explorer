Re Geoffrey Walter Edelsten v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation; Max Christopher Donnelly and Charles Robert Wilcox [1989] FCA 68 (15 March 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: GEOFFREY WALTER EDELSTEN

And: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION; MAX CHRISTOPHER DONNELLY

AND CHARLES ROBERT WILCOX

No. NG 1012 of 1988

FED No. 107

Bankruptcy

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Lockhart(1), Wilcox(1) and Pincus(1) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - Appeal from order annulling bankruptcy arising from presentation of a debtor's petition - Principles governing determination of the question whether presentation of petition constitutes an abuse of process - What constitutes a purpose foreign to the nature of the process - Standard of proof applicable to the finding of foreign purpose - Sufficiency of evidence to support finding of trial judge that debtor sought to reduce the prospect that certain transactions could be set aside as preferences - Observations upon the effect of a concession by the debtor that, in presenting his petition, he sought to choose a particular person as trustee - Need for legislative reform.

Bankruptcy Act 1966 ss55, 115, 118, 120, 122, 154, 156A.

HEARING

SYDNEY

15:3:1989

Appearance for the Appellant: Appellant in Person

Solicitor for the Applicant: Appellant in Person

Counsel for the First

Respondent: Mr G K Downes, QC with

Mr D B McGovern

Solicitors for the First

Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel for the Second

Respondent: No appearance in Court

Solicitors for the Second

Respondent: Isenberg Spedding & Player

Counsel for the Third

Respondent: No appearance in Court

Solicitors for the Third

Respondent: Minter Ellison

ORDER

The appeal be dismissed.

The appellant pay to the first respondent his costs of the appeal.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The question at issue in this appeal is not whether the appellant, Geoffrey Walter Edelsten, should be bankrupt. Rather, the question is whether his bankruptcy should be pursuant to the petition presented by the appellant himself or pursuant to that presented by a creditor of the appellant, Full Point Pty Limited. The first respondent, the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, who claims to be a substantial creditor of the appellant, successfully applied before Burchett J for an order anulling the bankruptcy of the appellant upon his own petition and thereafter his Honour made a sequestration order upon the petition of Full Point. The second respondent, Maxwell Christopher Donnelly, is a registered trustee in bankruptcy who was appointed by the creditors of the appellant as trustee of his estate, in substitution for the trustee selected by the appellant himself. The third respondent, Charles Robert Wilcox, an officer of the Health Insurance Commission, was concerned with other aspects of the litigation before the learned primary Judge but not with the matter which gives rise to this appeal. The first respondent appeared by counsel to resist the appellant's appeal but neither the second nor the third respondent was represented before us.

2. The relevant facts are within a limited compass. On 8 July 1987 Full Point presented to the Court a creditor's petition by which it sought a sequestration order against the estate of the applicant. The petition was based upon an act of bankruptcy said to have been committed by the appellant on 22 May 1987. The creditor's petition was served on 22 August 1987. It was listed for hearing on 23 September 1987. On 21 September 1987 the appellant presented his own petition, pursuant to s.55 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, and, upon its acceptance by the Registrar, he became a bankrupt by force of s.55(3). Section 55 relevantly provides:

"55. (1) Subject to this section, a debtor may

present to the Registrar a petition against himself

accompanied by a statement of his affairs, verified

by affidavit, and a copy of that statement.

(2) The petition and statement of affairs

shall each be in accordance with the prescribed

form.

(3) Where it appears to the Registrar that a

petition presented to him under this section is in

accordance with the prescribed form and that the

statement of affairs accompanying the petition is

also in accordance with the prescribed form or the

Court directs, under sub-section (4), the Registrar

to accept the petition--

(a) the petition shall be accepted by the

Registrar, who shall endorse it

accordingly; and

(b) thereupon, by force of this sub-section,

the debtor becomes a bankrupt by virtue

of the presentation of the petition.

(4) Where it appears to the Registrar that a

petition presented to him under this section, or

the statement of affairs accompanying such a

petition, is not in accordance with the prescribed

form, the Registrar shall not accept the petition

unless the Court, upon reference by the Registrar,

directs him to accept it.

(5) ...

(6) ...

(6A) ...

(7) ...

(8) A person who becomes a bankrupt by force

of this section continues to be a bankrupt until--

(a) he is discharged by force of section 149;

(b) he is discharged by order of the Court;

or

(c) his bankruptcy is annulled under section

74 or 154.

(9) ..."

3. The hearing of the creditor's petition was adjourned on 23 September 1987. On 7 October 1987 the first respondent filed an Application whereby he sought, amongst other orders, an order that the bankruptcy of the applicant pursuant to the debtor's petition be annulled. This Application succeeded, the case for the first respondent being that the debtor's petition constituted an abuse of process. On 10 March 1988 Burchett J made an order under s.154 of the Act annulling the bankruptcy which had arisen out of the presentation of the debtor's petition. Upon the same day he made a sequestration order pursuant to the petition of Full Point, the hearing of that petition having been adjourned pending the disposal of the Application of the Deputy Commissioner.

4. The identity of the petition by virtue of which a sequestration order is made may have important practical consequences. Firstly, the rights of creditors may be affected. Thus s.118 of the Act requires a creditor who has, within six months before the presentation of a petition or after that presentation, received monies as a result of execution having been issued by him or her against the property of the debtor to pay those monies to the trustee in bankruptcy. Section 120 provides, in relation to settlements, that time shall be computed from "the commencement of the bankruptcy". That term is defined by s.5 of the Act so as to refer to the date upon which the bankruptcy is deemed by s.115 to have commenced. Section 115, in turn, provides that the bankruptcy of a person shall relate back to the time of the commission of the earliest act of bankruptcy within a period of six months before the presentation of the particular petition by virtue of which the person became bankrupt. Section 122 avoids preferences given in favour of a creditor within six months before the presentation of the petition upon which the debtor became bankrupt.

5. Secondly, the administration of an estate may be affected, at least in the first instance, by the identity of the petitioner. Where a registered trustee has filed a consent to act as the trustee of the estate of a particular debtor and that debtor becomes bankrupt, that registered trustee becomes the trustee of the estate: see s.156A. It often happens that the petitioner, whether a creditor or the debtor, arranges with a particular registered trustee to give such a consent, the petitioner thus determining the identity of the trustee of the bankrupt estate. This determination may be overruled, either by the Court or by the creditors; but, in most cases, it is not. And, as the Act gives substantial discretions to the trustee, much may turn upon his or her personal characteristics.

6. The concept of abuse of process has been familiar to bankruptcy practitioners for many years. As long ago as 1888 the Queens Bench Division of the High Court of Judicature, in England, held that it was an abuse of process for two debtors, who were neither partners nor joint traders, to file a joint petition: see In re Bond (1888) 21 QBD 17. The Court was concerned with two aspects of the matter: the deprivation of the revenue of a second filing fee and the likelihood that the expenses of a debtor with no assets would be paid out of the estate of the other debtor, who held assets.

7. In Ex parte Painter; In re Painter (1895) 1 QB 85 a submission that the presentation of a bankruptcy petition constituted an abuse of process was rejected on the facts. But the Court affirmed the application to such proceedings of the concept of abuse of process. The test stated by Vaughan Williams J at p 91 was whether the relief sought was "foreign to the bankruptcy laws".

8. In re Betts; Ex parte Official Receiver (1901) 2 KB 39 was a case in which a claim of abuse of process succeeded. Wright J held that, in filing a succession of bankruptcy petitions, the debtor was making use of the bankruptcy law in order to defraud his creditors. His Lordship commented: "I cannot think that such a state of things comes within the bankruptcy law". See also In re Hancock (1904) 1 KB 585, Re Dunn; Ex parte Official Receiver (1949) 1 Ch 680 and In re a Debtor (1967) 1 Ch 590.

9. The High Court of Australia considered the concept of abuse of process, as applicable to bankruptcy proceedings under the present Act, in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 44; (1984) 154 CLR 589. That case has some factual similarities to the present case, a debtor's petition having been filed by the debtor shortly before the appointed hearing of a creditor's petition. At p 598 Gibbs CJ, Murphy, Brennan and Dawson JJ referred to the English authorities mentioned above and went on:

"The principle of those decisions applies

equally, in our opinion, when there is an

abuse of the process provided by s.55,

notwithstanding that it cannot be said that

there has been an abuse of the process of the

court because no judicial order is made when a

registrar accepts the petition under s.55. We

agree with the conclusion stated in In re

Mottee (1977) 29 FLR 406 at p 412 that a

bankruptcy resulting from the acceptance of a

debtor's petiton may be annulled if the

presentation of the petition was an abuse of

the procedure provided by s.55.

In the present case the debtor submitted that

he had not been guilty of any abuse of

process, and that he was entitled to present

his own petition for the purpose, which he

frankly admitted, of preventing the making of

a sequestration order and thereby preventing

his bankruptcy relating back to a time since

when he has disposed of moneys to which the

trustee's title may relate back. He relied on

a dictum of Lord Evershed M.R. in In re Dunn:

'The circumstance that the debtor has

filed his petition in order to protect

himself from evils which he might

otherwise suffer, and not with any

benevolent intention of benefiting his

creditors by securing a fair distribution

of assets amongst them, is no reason why

an order should not be made.'

In re Dunn was the case of a gaming debt,

which was possibly unenforceable, and it is

not entirely clear from the report what were

the 'evils' to which Lord Evershed referred,

unless it was the possibility of having to

fight a doubtful claim. That decision, and

others like Ex parte Painter; In re Painter

and In re Hancock, where the object of the

petition was to avoid the pressure of a

committal order, and In re Mottee, where the

debtor wished to avoid a possible claim by his

wife under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1959

(Cth), may be explained as cases in which 'the

debtor was entitled to use the machinery of

the Bankruptcy Act for his own purpose so as

to shield himself from further liability to

committal or other harassment': see In re a

Debtor. In none of those cases, however, was

the debtor seeking to avoid a liability or

harassment of a kind that resulted from the

operation of the bankruptcy laws. In our

opinion, a distinction must be drawn between

the pursuit of 'an ulterior private purpose'

-- which may not necessarily amount to an

abuse of process -- and a purpose foreign to

the nature of the process in question: ... It

is a purpose foreign to the bankruptcy laws,

and an abuse of process, for a debtor to

present a petition for the purpose of making

it impossible for a creditor to obtain a

sequestration order on a pending petition and

with the further purpose of shortening the

period of relation back, possibly placing

beyond the reach of the trustee property which

would otherwise vest in him."

10. A question has arisen before us as to the import of the last sentence in the passage just quoted. The appellant submits that, by this sentence, their Honours were propounding as the appropriate test that it must appear that the debtor has a combination of two purposes: first, to make it impossible for a creditor to obtain a sequestration order; and, second, to shorten the period of relation back, under s.115 of the Act. In the present case, unlike in Clyne, the effect of a bankruptcy occurring pursuant to the petition of the debtor rather than that of the creditor is not to shorten the period of relation back. Under either alternative the bankruptcy would relate back to 22 May 1987, the date of the only proved act of bankruptcy. Accordingly, the appellant contends that the principle enunciated in Clyne is inapplicable to his case.

11. We think that, in the subject sentence their Honours had in mind a case where both these purposes appeared. But it does not follow that they were of the opinion that a petition may constitute an abuse of process only in such a case. As always, the reasons for judgment must be read in their context. In Clyne the High Court was concerned with a case in which the two purposes referred to were established by the evidence. We read their Honours as saying no more than that, in such a case, the petition will constitute an abuse of process. The adoption by their Honours of the touchstone "purpose foreign to the bankruptcy laws", combined with their use of the English decisions, demonstrates that they saw the facts of the particular case as constituting merely an illustration of a more general principle. In our view, the ratio decidendi of Clyne is that a petition will constitute an abuse of process if it is presented for a purpose, whatever that purpose may be, which is foreign to the bankruptcy laws.

12. The appellant argues that Burchett J erred in detecting, in the present case, such a purpose. His Honour's decision turned upon the question whether, in presenting his own petition, the appellant harboured the purpose of saving from avoidance as preferences certain transactions which occurred early in 1987. If a sequestration order were made on the petition of Full Point, the period of six months prescribed by s.122 would extend back to 8 January 1987, Full Point's petition having been presented on 8 July 1987. If, on the other hand, the bankruptcy stemmed from the presentation of his own petition, on 21 September 1987, the preference period would extend back only to 21 March 1987. Understandably, therefore, some attention was paid at the hearing before Burchett J to the period running from 8 January 1987 to 21 March 1987.

13. The evidence revealed several transactions during this period. They were summarised by his Honour in these terms:

"Between 8 January 1987 and 21 March 1987,

agreements were entered into, and moneys

received and disbursed, by the bankrupt. The

amounts involved were substantial. On 20

February 1987, the bankrupt executed an

agreement involving the transfer of all his

interests in certain medical practices

conducted in what were called the 'existing

Cenrin Centres'. The agreement provided for

the payment of a consideration of $275,000-00

to one of the bankrupt's companies, Davule Pty

Limited. Pursuant to the agreement,

$250,000-00 was in fact paid to Davule Pty

Limited on 23 February 1987. This company was

described by the bankrupt in evidence as his

'alter ego'. He used it as a corporate

vehicle for the conduct of his own affairs.

On the same day that the $250,000-00 was

credited to its account, a cheque was drawn on

the account in the sum of $40,000-00 in favour

of another company. That company, the

bankrupt said, had manufactured examination

tables and other items used in the medical

centres with which he was associated. The

basis on which Davule Pty Limited was the

paymaster in respect of these items is not

entirely clear. The cheque butt describes the

cheque as in repayment of a loan involving

another of the bankrupt's companies, Highaim

Pty Limited; but he, in evidence, suggested

that, on the contrary, the payment may have

been by way of the making of a loan to the

other company. A further payment of $15,000,

made on 13 March 1987, was involved in a

similar ambiguity as to whether it constituted

a loan or a repayment of a loan. The entire

sum of $250,000-00 was disbursed by various

payments within a short period, and

importantly, prior to 21 March 1987.

On 2 March 1987, the bankrupt executed

another agreement by which he agreed to 'cease

and yield up all right title and interest he

may have in any medical practices carried on

by him at Medical Centres conducted by

Superclinics (a company Superclinics Australia

Pty Limited) and to assign and transfer all

such right title and interest to Superclinics

or its nominee.' In consideration, the

company agreed to pay to the bankrupt or his

nominees $100,000-00. On the same day, the

company was directed to make the payment in

favour of Davule Pty Limited, the bank

statements of which show that a cheque for

$100,000-00 was deposited on 3 March 1987 and

wholly withdrawn in various amounts by 6 March

1987. By then, the whole of the previously

mentioned sum of $250,000-00 had also been

withdrawn.

It thus appears from the evidence that at

least $350,000-00 was received, during the

period between 8 January 1987 and 21 March

1987, as the proceeds of agreements made

within the same period by which the bankrupt

turned to account his interests in various

medical practices, and that the whole of those

moneys were disbursed before the end of the

period, to the extent of at least $55,000-00

in what were shown by records controlled by

the bankrupt as loan repayments.

In addition, there is evidence that in January

1987 the bankrupt arranged for the sale to his

wife of furniture having a substantial value.

The price then paid by her was $100,000-00.

Although it appears there may have been some

question as to whether the furniture was owned

by a company, the bankrupt acknowledged that

it had been owned by him prior to the sale to

his wife."

14. Burchett J went on to note evidence that, in March or April 1987, the appellant consulted the late Mr Peter Clyne and that he told Mr Clyne that he was "looking down the barrel of bankruptcy". Upon the objective background evidence that was a realistic assessment of the appellant's position. At a later stage the appellant obtained advice from both a solicitor, who was not called as a witness, and an accountant. The appellant acknowledged in cross-examination that he was told something about preferences being able to be attacked, although he said that he was confused about the matter. He knew the importance of the period of six months before bankruptcy. Against this background his Honour made a finding in these terms:

"It is easy to accept the tenor of the

bankrupt's evidence that he did not have any

clear understanding of the ground on which

transactions prior to bankruptcy might be

attacked by a trustee. But, given the nature

of the large transactions in February and

March, that he discussed the prospect of

bankruptcy perhaps as early as March with Mr.

Clyne and thereafter with accountancy and

legal advisers, and that he arranged to file

his own petition after service upon him of the

creditor's petition, in fact having it filed

only two days before that petition was due to

come before the court, an inference can be

drawn that he had a general understanding that

the further the initiation of his bankruptcy

could be distanced from the transactions in

February and March the less likely it was that

they or some of them might be defeated. He

had, as I have said, received some advice

specifically related to the subject of

preferences, as well as advice concerning the

possible impact of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act

upon the payments extracted by the

Commissioner of Taxation under s.218 of the

Income Tax Assessment Act.

...

Not without considerable hesitation, I have

come to the conclusion that the preponderance

of probabilities favours a finding that when

the bankrupt caused his own petition to be

filed, he did so with the purpose (albeit he

had other purposes also) of reducing the

prospect that earlier transactions entered

into by him could be set aside. To that

extent, I do not accept the bankrupt's denial,

but I make it clear that, though I have not

failed to have regard to the seriousness of

the issue involved, I have no great confidence

about the matter, deciding it on no more than

a persuasion according to the civil onus.

There is, after all, nothing inherently

improbable about the bankrupt's explanation of

his motives, and as Scarman L.J. said in

Goldsmith v Sperrings Ltd (1977) 1 WLR 478 at

p 498: 'It is never easy to determine a man's

purpose.' I have considered, without deriving

assistance from it, evidence tendered by the

applicant of manipulations by the bankrupt to

evade the operation of the s.218 notices:

that evidence has, of course, a bearing on

credit, but the case cannot be proved by an

inference of some kind of transferred

propensity. What has weighed heavily with me

is a consideration of the character and

circumstances of the transactions in February

and March 1987, and the likelihood that they

involved matters of real concern to the bankrupt."

15. The appellant puts two submissions in relation to this finding. Firstly, he submits that, in making a finding upon a bare balance of probabilities, Burchett J failed to apply the proper standard of proof. It is not suggested that his Honour should have applied the criminal standard of proof, that is proof beyond reasonable doubt, but the complaint is made that the primary Judge ignored what was said by Dixon J in Briginshaw v Briginshaw [1938] HCA 34; (1938) 60 CLR 336 at p 361-362:

"The truth is that, when the law requires the

proof of any fact, the tribunal must feel an

actual persuasion of its occurrence or

existence before it can be found. It cannot

be found as a result of a mere mechanical

comparison of probabilities independently of

any belief in its reality. No doubt an

opinion that a state of facts exists may be

held according to indefinite gradations of

certainty; and this has led to attempts to

define exactly the certainty required by the

law for various purposes. Fortunately,

however, at common law no third standard of

persuasion was definitely developed. Except

upon criminal issues to be proved by the

prosecution, it is enough that the affirmative

of an allegation is made out to the reasonable

satisfaction of the tribunal. But reasonable

satisfaction is not a state of mind that is

attained or established independently of the

nature and consequence of the fact or facts to

be proved. The seriousness of an allegation

made, the inherent unlikelihood of an

occurrence of a given description, or the

gravity of the consequences flowing from a

particular finding are considerations which

must affect the answer to the question whether

the issue has been proved to the reasonable

satisfaction of the tribunal."

16. We do not agree with the appellant's criticism of Burchett J's reasoning. Certainly, his Honour revealed that he had hesitated in coming to a conclusion upon the critical issue. Moreover, he said that he had "no great confidence" about the matter. But Burchett J did correctly direct himself as to the appropriate standard of proof: "a persuasion according to the civil onus". This accords with the test stipulated by the Full High Court of Australia in Rejfek v McElroy [1965] HCA 46; (1968) 112 CLR 517 at pp 521-522:

"No matter how grave the fact which is to be

found in a civil case, the mind has only to be

reasonably satisfied and has not with respect

to any matter in issue in such a proceeding to

attain that degree of certainty which is

indispensable to the support of a conviction

upon a criminal charge ..."

17. No doubt, as Dixon J pointed out in Briginshaw, what constitutes reasonable satisfaction cannot be divorced from the nature of the issue to be decided. But Burchett J was well aware of the issue which he had to decide and of its significance.

18. Secondly, the appellant claims that the conclusion reached by Burchett J was an erroneous conclusion in point of fact. For the purpose of making good that submission the appellant referred us to parts of the evidence tendered at the hearing, both oral and documentary. We have considered that material. In doing so we have had the benefit of perusing the written submissions as to the evidence which were presented to Burchett J by counsel for the Deputy Commissioner. We think it unnecessary to say more than that we are satisfied that there was ample material to support the finding made by his Honour. It is not necessary to express any conclusion as to whether any particular transaction or transactions would constitute a preference; but there are certainly matters which warrant investigation in that connection. It is not difficult to see that the appellant might have wished the relevant date under s.122 to be 21 March 1987 rather than 8 January 1987.

19. We should add that the appellant gave evidence that, in deciding to present his own petition, he was motivated by a desire to have a particular person, being a registered trustee, as the trustee of his estate. The appellant said that he had had discussions with this person and that he had obtained from him some indications as to the course which he would be minded to take in respect of some important aspects of the administration of the estate, if he were to be appointed. It need hardly be said that, if any such indication had been given, it would have been improper conduct by a registered trustee. A trustee ought never to compromise the proper exercise of his or her discretion by a prior agreement with the debtor, and it is particularly undesirable that there be any room for the suspicion that the trustee has bargained for appointment by promising lenient treatment of the prospective bankrupt. In the present case we make no finding of misconduct. The trustee concerned has had no opportunity to answer the allegation. But we do comment that the appellant's statement that he was influenced in presenting his petition by a desire to have a particular person as trustee of his estate, who would be "fair", may constitute an independent ground for regarding that petition as being an abuse of process.

20. In his dissenting judgment in Clyne, Deane J drew attention to the desirability of an amendment of the Bankruptcy Act to make special provision for cases where a debtor's petition is presented whilst a creditor's petition is pending against him or her. Burchett J, in the present case, supported that suggestion. Before parting with this appeal we wish to add our voices to the call for a statutory solution to the problem which has emerged in Clyne and in this case. In this computer age there would appear to be no difficulty about the Registrar immediately ascertaining, upon presentation at the counter of a debtor's petition, whether there was pending against that person a creditor's petition. At a time when the resources of the Court are severely pressed it appears to us to be absurd that time should have to be spent upon an inquiry into the purpose underlying the presentation of a debtor's petition. As Burchett J pointed out, purpose is not easily determined. The determination of that question may involve, as it did here, lengthy investigation over several hearing days.

21. Reform of the present law could take any one of a number of forms. The easiest course might be to provide that, if a creditor's petition is pending at the time of the filing of the debtor's petition, the debtor does not then become bankrupt but that the debtor's petition should be set down for hearing at the same time as the creditor's petition. This would give to the presiding Judge a discretion as to the course to be followed.

22. The appeal will be dismissed with costs.
