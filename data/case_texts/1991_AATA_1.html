Re Taxation Appeals [1991] AATA 1; 22 ATR 3001 (3 January 1991)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. WT87/472

AAT No. 6560

Income Tax

[1991] AATA 1; 22 ATR 3001

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - General medical practitioner - Transfer of practice to private company - Service agreement - Third party mortgage - Whether monies borrowed to purchase goodwill of practice - Whether interest expense deductible - Whether Part IVA applies.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 secs 51(1), 177A, 177C, 177D, 177F

Case X53 90 ATC 409

John v FC of T [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 166 CLR 417; 89 ATC 4101

Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542

HEARING

PERTH

3:1:1991

ORDER

I direct that the decision on the objection in respect of the tax year ended 30 June 1985 is affirmed.

DECISION

The sole issue in this case is whether interest payments on money borrowed by the applicant medical company - Kildare Pty Ltd ("Kildare") - to acquire a general medical practice are an allowable deduction in the hands of the company. The applicant tendered a statement of facts set out below. All but fact No. 3 were agreed.

"1. Kildare was incorporated in Western Australia on 22 May 1984.

2. Dr Kildare entered a Trust Deed to form the

Kildare Pty Ltd Superannuation Scheme on 6 July 1984.

3. In September, 1984, Kildare borrowed the

sum of $40,000 from National Australia Bank Ltd, West

Perth, with which it purchased the goodwill of Dr

Kildare's medical practice from Dr Kildare.

4. Dr Kildare practised as a General

Practitioner and his practice had goodwill which was

valued by his accountant, Mr Halbert, on the basis of

one-quarter of gross fees for the year immediately prior

to the sale of the practice. That one-quarter is the

sum of $40,000. "

2. It was part of the arrangement that Dr Kildare enter into a service agreement with the company pursuant to which he agreed to provide his services to the company for a period of three years. The service agreement was not available at the hearing. However, I am satisfied that nothing turns on that.

3. The Commissioner conceded, properly in my view, that the general practice of Dr Kildare had considerable goodwill and quantum was not challenged.

4. Kildare incurred interest payments in the sum of $3,224 upon a loan of $40,000 with the National Australia Bank Ltd, West Perth. The taxpayer alleges that the loan was used to purchase the goodwill of the medical practice of Dr Kildare. The Commissioner, on the other hand, has disallowed the interest expense of $3,224 as a deduction in the 1985 tax year upon the following two alternative bases -

(i) that the sum is not an allowable deduction

because the moneys borrowed were not expended to acquire

an income-producing asset but for the purpose of paying

out a housing loan on the doctor's domestic home, so

that the expenditure was "private" and hence excluded

from deduction pursuant to the exception contained in

sec 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the

Act") (in fairness to Mr Buss, of learned counsel for

the Crown, this issue was not seriously pressed); or

(ii) that the arrangements entered into by the

taxpayer constitutes a "scheme" to which Part IVA of the

Act applies.

5. I propose to deal with each of these submissions seriatim.

Is the interest expense deductible under sec 51(1)?

6. Sec 51(1) of the Act provides -

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they

are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable

income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or producing such

income, shall be allowable deductions except to the

extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital,

or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are

incurred in relation to the gaining or production of

exempt income."

7. Exh B contains part of the application by Kildare to the National Australia Bank Ltd for the loan of $40,000. The purpose of the loan is described as -

"Goodwill created by Dr Kildare when operating in own

name to be purchased by this company for taxation

purposes on suggestion of their accountant which will be

utilised in clearance of housing loan with the Perth

Building Society in names of Dr Kildare and Mrs Kildare."

8. The mechanics by which an amount of $40,000 came into the hands of the company are as follows. Dr Kildare and his wife owed the Perth Building Society $65,000 secured against their home. In September 1984, Dr Kildare approached the National Australia Bank Ltd on behalf of Kildare for a loan of some $40,000 (Exh B). Dr Kildare and his wife then entered into a mortgage contract with the National Australia Savings Bank Ltd for a personal loan in the sum of $26,400. The collective proceeds of these two loans were used to discharge the two mortgages of the Perth Building Society over the private residence of Dr Kildare and his wife. The loan of $40,000 to Kildare was secured by a third party mortgage over the private residence of Dr Kildare. Against this background, where the borrowing was used to re-finance the loan secured against the doctor's domestic home, I was invited by Mr Buss to find that the transaction was indeed entered for a private purpose, with the result that the interest payments thus generated are not deductible. However, the existence of the loan cannot be denied, nor that it was used - however fleetingly - to acquire the practice of Dr Kildare.

9. I am satisfied on the evidence that Kildare took over a thriving medical practice and that the amount ($40,000) was reasonable. On a finding that there was an actual loan to acquire the practice, the interest on the loan moneys is classically an outgoing incurred in producing assessable income, and thus qualifies for deduction under sec 51(1): Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542; John v FC of T [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 166 CLR 417; 89 ATC 4101.

10. Dealing with the law, I was referred to Case X53 90 ATC 409 where the facts were identical with those now before me (even to the point where Mr Halbert was the grey eminence behind the sale), save that the doctor involved in that case was a medical specialist rather than a General Practitioner. Part IVA was relied upon. The case was ultimately decided in favour of the Comissioner on a factual basis; viz that the skill of a medical specialist "is a quality peculiar to the individual ... not susceptible to commercial sale as part of goodwill." (p 413). That finding may be unexceptionable, but the question for tax purposes is, with respect, "what had the company bought?" Put that way, the answer is: "a thriving specialist practice, albeit one which depended upon the skills and abilities of a doctor whose services had been secured by way of a valid service agreement neither too wide in time nor place."

Does Part IVA apply?

11. Part IVA was inserted into the Act by sec 7 of Act No 110 of 1981 and intended as a general anti-avoidance provision to replace the former (by then substantially emasculated) sec 260 of the Act. The new Part applies to transactions entered into on or after 27 May 1981.

12. The term "scheme" is defined broadly by sec 177A(1) as meaning:

"(a) any agreement, arrangement, understanding, promise

or undertaking, whether express or implied and whether

or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by

legal proceedings; and

(b) any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of

action or course of conduct."

13. In order for Part IVA to apply, it must be shown that a taxpayer obtained a "tax benefit", the meaning of which is explained in sec 177C. Suffice it for present purposes to say that a tax benefit includes a deduction being allowable which would not otherwise be allowable in the absence of the scheme. Sec 177D sets out the criteria for ascertaining whether a particular scheme is one to which Part IVA applies. Sec 177F confers upon the Commissioner a discretion to cancel a tax benefit obtained by a taxpayer in connection with a scheme to which the Part applies. That section, so far as relevant, provides -

"177F(1) Where a tax benefit has been obtained, or would

but for this section be obtained, by a taxpayer in

connection with a scheme to which this Part applies, the

Commissioner may -

(a) ....

(b) in the case of a tax benefit that is referable to a

deduction or a part of a deduction being allowable to

the taxpayer in relation to a year of income - determine

that the whole or a part of the deduction or of the part

of the deduction, as the case may be, shall not be

allowable to the taxpayer in relation to that year of income,

and, where the Commissioner makes such a determination,

he shall take such action as he considers necessary to

give effect to that determination.

(2) ....

(3) Where the Commissioner has made a determination

under sub-section (1) in respect of a taxpayer in

relation to a scheme to which this Part applies, the

Commissioner may, in relation to any taxpayer (in this

sub-section referred to as the 'relevant taxpayer') -

(a) if, in the opinion of the Commissioner -

(i) there has been included, or would but for this

sub-section be included, in the assessable income of the

relevant taxpayer of a year of income an amount that

would not have been included or would not be included,

as the case may be, in the assessable income of the

relevant taxpayer of that year of income if the scheme

had not been entered into or carried out; and

(ii) it is fair and reasonable that the amount or

a part of that amount should not be included in the

assessable income of the relevant taxpayer of that year

of income,

determine that that amount or that part of that

amount, as the case may be, should not have been

included or shall not be included, as the case may be,

in the assessable income of the relevant taxpayer of

that year of income; or

(b) if, in the opinion of the Commissioner -

(i) an amount would have been allowed or would be

allowable to the relevant taxpayer as a deduction in

relation to a year of income if the scheme had not been

entered into or carried out, being an amount that was

not allowed or would not, but for this sub-section, be

allowable, as the case may be, as a deduction to the

relevant taxpayer in relation to that year of income;

and

(ii) it is fair and reasonable that that amount or

a part of that amount should be allowable as a deduction

to the relevant taxpayer in relation to that year of income,

determine that that amount or that part, as the case

may be, should have been allowed or shall be allowable,

as the case may be, as a deduction to the relevant

taxpayer in relation to that year of income,

and the Commissioner shall take such action as he

considers necessary to give effect to any such

determination.

(4) Where the Commissioner makes a determination under

sub-section (3) by virtue of which an amount is allowed

as a deduction to a taxpayer in relation to a year of

income, that amount shall be deemed to be so allowed as

a deduction by virtue of such provision of this Act as

the Commissioner determines.

(5) Where, at any time, a taxpayer considers that the

Commissioner ought to make a determination under

sub-section (3) in relation to the taxpayer in relation to a

year of income, the taxpayer may post to or lodge with

the Commissioner a request in writing for the making by

the Commissioner of a determination under that sub-section.

(6) The Commissioner shall consider the request and

serve on the taxpayer, by post or otherwise, a written

notice of his decision on the request.

(7) If the taxpayer is dissatisfied with the

Commissioner's decision on the request, the taxpayer

may, within 60 days after service on the taxpayer of

notice of the decision of the Commissioner, lodge with

the Commissioner an objection in writing against the

decision stating fully and in detail the grounds on

which the taxpayer relies.

(8) The provisions of Division 2 of Part V (other than

section 185) apply in relation to an objection made

under sub-section (7) in like manner as those provisions

apply in relation to an objection against an assessment."

14. Dr Kildare and Mr Halbert both informed the Tribunal that the purpose of incorporating Kildare was to enable Dr Kildare to establish a superannuation scheme which would give him greater tax benefits than if he continued to operate the practice as a sole practitioner. Thus, Kildare, as the employer of Dr Kildare, paid $6,296 as an employer contribution into the Kildare Pty Ltd Superannuation Scheme and claimed that amount as a deduction in the company's 1985 tax return. The allowability of this deduction was not challenged by the Crown, although Mr Buss hinted darkly that I was not to assume that this constituted a concession by the Commissioner that such deduction, "available" as a result of the deliberate decision to incorporate for that purpose, was immune from attack by recourse to Part IVA. On this occasion, I was informed from the bar table, that the Commissioner, having made a determination under sec 177F(1), had nevertheless chosen, as an exercise of his discretion under sec 177F(3), to allow the whole superannuation contribution as a deduction.

15. In the result, the Commissioner chose only to cancel the interest payment on the loan, and to treat it as a tax benefit. This was done by letter dated 12 December 1988 (Exh 5), stating that the Commissioner had made a determination pursuant to sec 177F of the Act in the following terms -

"The establishment of a medical practice company to take

over the medical practice of Dr Kildare in circumstances

which gave rise to an income tax advantage which went

beyond the provision of superannuation benefits is a

'scheme' as defined in sec 177A and there is a 'tax

benefit' in connection with the scheme, pursuant to

sub-section 177C(1), represented by deductions for interest

on money borrowed to purchase goodwill of the practice.

I have concluded, under section 177D, that that part of

the scheme relating to the payment of interest was

entered into for the purpose of enabling Kildare to

obtain a tax benefit and that it therefore constitutes a

scheme to which Part IVA applies.

Accordingly, in terms of paragraph 177F(1)(b) the whole

of the deduction claimed for interest in the return of

Kildare for the year ended 30 June 1985 is not allowable."

16. Notwithstanding the clear and honest admission by Dr Kildare that the incorporation was undertaken for the dual purposes of obtaining the tax deductions of the additional superannuation payments and the interest on the capital borrowing, the Commissioner has chosen to exercise his discretion under Part IVA to ignore the former and to cancel the latter, notwithstanding that both were brought about as a direct and intended consequence of transmuting a self-employed professional into a company. If there is a subtle distinction between the two, I must freely confess that it escapes me, and were it in my power to increase the assessment I would do so, if only because logic ineluctably compels such a result. It seems that the "choice principle", an effective tool to reduce taxation during the lifetime of sec 260, has been transferred from taxpayers to the Commissioner by Part IVA. So be it.

17. The definition of a "scheme" in sec 177A(1) of the Act is extremely wide - it means inter alia, "any action, course of action or course of conduct". It does not require a quantum leap to conclude that the execution by Kildare of a mortgage agreement with the National Australia Bank Ltd is an action or course of conduct which constitutes a "scheme" for purposes of Part IVA. This finding, in turn, requires an examination of the details surrounding the mortgage agreement viewed against the background of the incorporation of the company and its main purpose.

18. The following occurred in cross-examination of Dr Kildare:

"So, in substance, you went to see Mr Halbert and Mr

Halbert suggested it would be a good idea to sell your

practice to a company, and he explained there were

certain taxation benefits? Is that correct?---Yes.

Well, in fact, really, the honest truth is I approached

Mr Halbert because this was in the medical press all the

time about the ability to incorporate to increase your superannuation

And he agreed that, yes, it seemed a good idea? Right?

And he gave you certain advice as to the tax benefits

that might be involved?---Yes.

And you had also heard that matters could be arranged so

that, in effect, you would be able to get a tax

deduction for the interest payable on the loan to pay

the goodwill?---Yes.

And did Mr Halbert tell you that or was that something

you had heard from some of your medical practitioner

colleagues?---I believe that I asked Mr Halbert about

that as well.

And he confirmed that that was something that could be

done, did he?--- Yes.

So there was sort of a twin-benefit, if you like, of the

superannuation deductions, and also the deduction of the

interest on a loan secured over your own house?---Yes."

(tr pp 38-39)

To the extent that Mr Halbert denied all knowledge of the fact that Dr and Mrs Kildare would mortgage their home in order to secure the amount borrowed by the company to acquire the goodwill of the practice I frankly disbelieve him. He had previously incorporated other medical companies, following the same pattern, all involving home loans to finance the purchase of goodwill (tr p 10); cf Case X53 90 ATC 409.

CONCLUSION

19. Mr Buss, in his closing address, identified the following facts as constituting the scheme for purposes of Part IVA which allowed Kildare to claim the interest payment as a deduction-

(i) the incorporation of Kildare;

(ii) the transfer of the goodwill of Dr Kildare's

practice to Kildare;

(iii) the establishment of the superannuation scheme; (iv) that Kildare, Dr
and Mrs Kildare borrowed from the

National Australia Bank;

(v) that the funds borrowed from the National Australia

Bank were used to repay the existing housing loans of Dr

and Mrs Kildare with the Perth Building Society; and

(vi) the moneys borrowed were secured by two mortgages,

one of which (the loan to Kildare) was a third party

mortgage granted by the bank upon the security of the

home of Dr and Mrs Kildare.

20. The evidence discloses that Dr Kildare and his wife became the registered proprietors as joint tenants of the property described as "portion of Canning Location 123 and being Lot 45 on Diagram 67890" on 21 January 1981 (Exh 1). The Certificate of Title records the Perth Building Society as the registered mortgagee of the property, and that the two mortgages with the Society were discharged on 17 October 1984 at 9.11 o'clock and that at precisely the same moment the National Australia Bank Ltd and the National Australia Savings Bank Ltd became the new mortgagees.

21. It is clear that the parties gave effect to the intended use of the moneys as disclosed on the loan application by Kildare with the National Australia Bank Ltd - "goodwill ... to be purchased by Kildare Pty Ltd for taxation purposes on suggestion of their accountant which will be utilised in clearing of housing loan with Perth Building Society in the names of Dr and Mrs Kildare". In short, the evidence does not support the submission of Mr Marks, counsel for the applicant, that the "taxation purposes", referred to in the loan application, was simply the superannuation deduction.

22. I am furthermore satisfied that both Dr Kildare and Mr Halbert were keely alive to the taxation benefits to be derived from entering the mortgage agreement with the National Australia Bank Ltd, as evidenced by the doctor's cross-examination extracted above. It is true that for a fleeting moment in time, some $40,000 found their way into the hands of Kildare. However, put bluntly, the purpose of substituting one mortgagee for another was to create a tax deduction by recourse to an "incestuous" relationship of prohibited degree; cf sec 177D(vi) - "any change in the financial position of any person who has, or has had, any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) with the relevant taxpayer, being a change that has resulted, will result or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme." Such "incest" creates a tax benefit to which Part IVA applies - in this case the conversion of a non-deductible interest payment on a domestic home into a deductible expenditure in the accounts of the company.

23. I have therefore concluded that the Commissioner was correct in determining that the establishment of the medical practice company gave rise to an income tax advantage and constitutes a "scheme" as defined in sec 177A. It follows that the amount of $3,224, represented by a claim for interest on money allegedly borrowed to purchase the goodwill of the practice, the only issue I have been asked to determine, is a "tax benefit" as defined in subsec 177C(1).

24. For the above reasons, the decision of the Commissioner on the objection is affirmed.
