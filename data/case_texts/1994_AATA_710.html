WT93/78-79 and Commissioner of Taxation [1994] AATA 710; (1994) 31 ATR 1272 (21 December 1994)

Last Updated: 18 May 2009

TAXATION - question of whether royalty payments made to applicant by a company pursuant to an agreement regarding goldmining tenements were assessable income or exempt income - Tribunal found that payments in question were “income” and after an analysis of a series of agreement entered into by the applicant found that they were not “exempt income” under Section 23(pa) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 23, 25, 26

Date: 21 December 1994



  1. The applicant applied to this Tribunal pursuant to s14ZZ(a)(i) of the Taxation Administrative Act 1953, for a review of the objection decisions made by the respondent on 30 September 1993 affirming the assessments of $40,783 and $337.537 received by the applicant in relation to each of the years ended 30 June 1991 and 30 June 1992 respectively.
  2. In each instance the amounts assessed represented the applicant’s share of payments made by Goldfan Limited pursuant to an agreement entered into on 5 July 1989 between that company, on the one part, and the applicant and another person, on the other.
  3. The issue is whether the amounts are assessable income under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, (“the Act”) and specifically whether the amounts are income receipts or capital receipts and, if income receipts, whether they are exempt income pursuant to s.23(pa) of the Act. That section, so far as it is relevant to this matter, operates to exempt from assessable income amounts which are income and derived by a bona fide prospector from the sale, transfer or assignment by the person of their rights to mine gold, in a particular area in Australia.
  4. It was agreed between the parties that the applicant and his partner were, for all intents and purposes, bona fide prospectors. It was also agreed that the applicant and his partner did dispose of rights to mine for gold in a particular area of Australia and that the receipts in question arose ultimately, but not necessarily directly, as a consequence of that disposal.
  5. The relevant issue in this respect therefore, was whether the amounts in question were derived from the sale, transfer or assignment of the relevant rights to mine.
  6. The facts, which were not in dispute, were that in 1982 the applicant was the registered owner of certain rights to mine gold (“the mining tenements”), either alone or with his partner (“the prospectors”) and on 15 April 1985 the prospectors entered into an option agreement with Altus Corporation Pty Ltd (“Altus”). For reasons which will become apparent it will be necessary later to refer in detail to the terms of this and other relevant agreements. The consideration payable by Altus to the prospectors for them entering into the option agreement totalled $60,000 which sum would be taken to be part of the consideration of $150,000 for the sale of the mining tenements should the option be exercised. In due course the option was exercised. None of the $150,000 has been assessed, a matter which is not an issue. However, what is in issue relates to a further clause in that agreement which required Altus to pay the prospectors further sums based on $1.00 per tonne of any ore mined from the tenements which was crushed and treated.
  7. On the same day, 15 April 1985, the prospectors entered an agreement, called a Deed of Acknowledgment, with Clackline Refractories Limited (which later changed its name to Herald Resources Ltd)), (“Herald”). This agreement served to acknowledge on the part of the prospectors that Altus had granted option rights to Herald to acquire the subject mining tenements. In this deed Herald agreed, in the event of it exercising its option, to meet the payment obligations which might become due to the prospectors by Altus should the tenements be mined.
  8. Altus exercised its option on 11 June 1986 and contemporaneously transferred the tenements to Herald which became the registered proprietor of the mining tenements, registration ultimately being effected on 4 December 1986. Altus, having already paid, in three instalments, $60,000 to the prospectors, then paid the balance of the acquisition price, $90,000 on 11 June 1986. As mentioned, the taxation treatment of the $150,000 consideration is not in dispute.
  9. Just over a year later, on 26 June 1987, Herald sold the tenements to Goldfan Limited (“Goldfan”) which company some two years after that, on 5 July 1989, entered into two agreements - (i) what is called a Deed of Royalty with the prospectors, whereby in consideration of the prospectors giving up any rights they may have had against Altus, Herald and Goldfan, they accepted an undertaking by Goldfan to pay them equivalently calculated sums as they had negotiated originally with Altus; and (ii), what is called a Deed of Surrender, between Altus and Goldfan. This agreement’s recital indicates that Goldfan had acquired several mining tenements from Herald which Herald had acquired from Altus. A royalty of $2.00 per tonne of crushed and treated ore taken from those tenements was payable as consideration (which, as it happened, included the mining leases Altus acquired from the prospectors). The Deed of Surrender provides that contemporaneously with the sale of these tenements, on 26 June 1987, a -

“.... deed of assumption and consent (was) executed ... between Herald, Goldfan and Altus, (whereby) Herald assigned and Goldfan assumed all of the rights and obligations of Herald to pay the Royalty and by its execution of the said deed Altus consented to the assignment and assumption.” (Clause B of Ex. 3)



  1. The prospectors were not party to this deed, nor apparently was it necessary to have them join in the assignment of rights and obligations in question to enable Goldfan to secure a clear title to the tenements which they intended to mine.
  2. In the 1990/1991 and 1991/1992 years of income the applicant’s share of these payments by Goldfan totalled $40,783 and $337,573 respectively.
  3. At the hearing Mr P Fletcher, for the applicant, contended that if the disputed sums were income, which was not conceded, they were exempt pursuant to s.23(pa). Mr W Martin QC, for the respondent submitted that the amounts were not exempt income under that section. The Tribunal sees this as the only relevant issue. Amounts which are income derived from Australian sources can only escape assessment by also being exempt income and thus outside the purview of s.25(1). Otherwise they are assessable.

Are the Amounts Income?

  1. It seems appropriate firstly to dispose of this question since an affirmative answer leads to a consideration of whether the amounts are exempt income under s.23(pa) of the Act. An answer in the negative leads to a question of whether the amounts, not being income, are assessable income nevertheless under some other provision. In this respect Mr Martin, for the respondent, conceded that the amounts, if not income, were not assessable under Part IIIA of the Act. That being conceded, if the amounts are found not to be of an income nature and therefore not assessable under either s.25(1) or s.26(f) of the Act, consequently they would not be assessable at all.
  2. The sums were received periodically - annually assessed, based on the actual amount of ore mined from the tenements in question, crushed and treated each yearly period ending on 30 June. There is an expectancy that similar sums will be received in future so long as the mine is worked and to this extent the payments would exhibit regularity. The amounts were not fixed nor their annual sum certain and neither did their payment bear any relationship to the amount of consideration paid to transfer title in the tenements to the end remitter of the payments - their receipt was independent of the transfer of the applicant’s interest in the underlying profit-yielding subject, the tenements, although they flowed from the working by a third party of those tenements. These amounts bear the character of periodicity, regularity and recurrence and are not limited by any agreed price nor paid in consideration of the applicant having given up anything. Their quantum is a function of operations carried on by another. It would be going too far to say that the payments received by the applicant were windfall gains - they are clearly connected with a commercial agreement, not paid in any voluntary sense but by operation of the terms of that agreement (the actual terms of the relevant agreements are considered in detail later). The payments bear none of the classic attributes of a capital receipt - they are not made for the transfer of property but as a consequence of the prerequisite of property having been transferred (c/f Edgerton-Warburton v DFCT [1934] HCA 40; (1934) 51 CLR 568), nor do they represent a once-and-for-all payment or instalments of a capital sum. Therefore, the payments, not being of a capital or windfall nature, nor in relation to any private or domestic arrangement, are income. Had the applicant worked the mine himself then he would have received income from that activity - it follows that what he receives as a result of the mine being worked is income - c/f Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) v Meeks [1915] HCA 34; (1915) ALR 244; 19 CLR 568, and Heavy Minerals Pty Ltd v FCT [1966] HCA 60; (1966) 10 AITR 140, 115 CLR 512.

Are the Amounts Exempt Income Under s.23(pa)?

  1. Sub-paragraph 23(pa) of the Act, so far as relevant, exempts income derived by a person from the sale, transfer or assignment by the person of his rights to mine, in a particular area in Australia, for gold ... being a person who is a bona fide prospector. As mentioned, it is an agreed fact that the applicant is a bona fide prospector in this regard.

It is necessary to make a careful analysis of the series of agreements entered into by the applicant to discern the true nature and source of the payments received from Goldfan.

The Option Agreement

  1. This was made on 15 April 1985 between the prospectors, called “the Vendors” in the agreement, and Altus, called “the Purchaser”. It acknowledges that the prospectors are the registered proprietors of certain mining tenements.

Clause 2 of that agreement states:

  1. Offer to Sell

    • (1) Subject to the conditions set forth in this Agreement the Vendor hereby offers to sell the entire right title and interest in and to the Mining Tenements to the Purchaser for the sum of ONE HUNDRED AND FIFTY THOUSAND DOLLARS ($150,000) which shall be reduced by any sums paid under Clause 2(4) (“the Price”).

    • (2) The offer contained in Clause 2(1) may be accepted by the Purchaser at any time in writing during an Option Period as defined in Clause 2(3).

The Deed of Acknowledgment

  1. This also was entered into on 15 April 1985 between Clackline Refractories Limited (which became Herald), called “Clackline” in the agreement, and the prospectors, called “the Proprietor” in the agreement. After a citation warranty clause acknowledging the option agreement with Altus in relation to which option rights have been in turn granted by Altus to Clackline and covenants relating to co-operation of the Proprietors with Clackline (Clauses 1 to 3) the deed provides:
    1. In consideration of Clackline agreeing hereby to hold the Proprietor’s order for payment to the Proprietor monies due and payable by Clackline to Altus in respect of the Tenement to the extent of monies due to the Proprietor pursuant to an agreement of even date between the Proprietor and Altus (“the Option”) the Proprietor covenants with Clackline[1] :

(1) that he will not mortgage charge encumber or otherwise dispose of his right and interest in the Tenements except to Altus or Clackline or with the prior agreement of Clackline;



(2) the he will not terminate the rights of Altus to acquire the Tenement under the terms of the Option or otherwise allow them to be determined or to expire by effluction of time without giving Clackline seven (7) days prior notice in writing which notice shall specify the grounds of the proposed termination determination or expiry;



(3) that he will not revoke repudiate or vary the Option without the prior agreement in writing of Clackline;



(4) during the currency of its option agreement with Altus Clackline shall have possession of the Tenement and the exclusive right to explore the same;



(5) that if Clackline notifies Altus of its acceptance of Altus’ offer to sell the Tenement to Clackline pursuant to its said option agreement with Altus such acceptance shall be deemed to be acceptance by Altus of the Proprietor’s offer to sell the Tenement to Altus pursuant to the Option; and



(6) that he will indemnify and hold Clackline harmless against any breach by him of Clause 4(1), (2), (3) and (4)



  1. This deed shall be binding upon the assigns personal representatives and successors in title of the parties.

  2. Clackline may assign all or any part of its rights hereunder without the consent of the other party. The Proprietor may not assign its rights or obligations hereunder.

  3. Pausing here for a moment, it becomes apparent that the prospectors, having entered an option to sell agreement with Altus contemporaneously agreed that an unrelated party, Clackline (Herald) assumed all the rights against the reference which Altus had and could assign these to anyone of its choosing without reference to the prospectors. It would seem that the prospectors, by entering into this deed, have, so long as Altus or Clackline honour the terms of the option agreement, agreed to any assignment of the option that Clackline (but not Altus) may choose.
  4. The relevant question then is that if Clackline (Herald), pursuant to Clause 6 of the Deed of Acknowledgement, should unilaterally assign its rights under that deed (to exercise the Altus option in its favour and undertake to make all the payments which Altus would have been required to make had it exercised the option), to a third party, whether the prospectors rights against Altus and hence Clackline, would be automatically enforceable against the third party instead. As it turned out, Clackline (Herald) did not assign its interest under the deed, exercised the Altus option to acquire the tenements, paying the required balance of the purchase price. However, Herald, on 26 June 1987, sold the tenements to Goldfan. At this time the only enforceable rights possessed by the prospectors were against Altus or its assignee Herald. It had no agreement with nor automatic right against Goldfan to force it to pay the liability of Altus or Herald under Clause 3(4) of the option agreement, that is, its entitlement to be paid $1.00 per tonne of all ore mined from the tenements, crushed and treated, regardless of who should carry out that process or indeed regardless of who had legal title to the tenements. The prospectors enforceable rights in this respect lie then against either Altus or Herald.

Deed of Royalty

  1. In apparent acknowledgment of this liability Altus and Herald entered into an agreement, called a Deed of Royalty, with Goldfan. This deed serves to have Goldfan assume the responsibility of the payments to the prospectors. The deed further required the prospectors, in consideration of Goldfan agreeing to assume that liability, to give up any rights which they had against Altus or Herald, stemming from the option agreement. That in effect, is the essence of the Deed of Royalty entered into on 5 July 1989 between Goldfan and the prospectors, called the “Beneficiaries” in the deed. The recital to this agreement refers to the sequence of agreements so far mentioned and at paragraph F it states:

“WHEREAS

A ... E



  1. By a Sale Agreement dated the 26th day of June 1987 Herald sold and Goldfan purchased the Tenements amongst other things and by a deed of assumption and consent to even date herewith between Herald Goldfan and Altus, Herald assigned and Goldfan assumed all of the rights and obligation of Herald to pay the Royalty and by its execution of the deed Altus consented to this assignment and assumption.

  2. Pursuant to an option agreement of even date herewith between Altus as grantor and Goldfan as grantee, (“Royalty Option”), Goldfan was granted the option exercisable at any time after the 30th June 1989 to cancel a royalty which was payable to Altus, part of which sum was to be paid directly to the Beneficiaries (prospectors) in satisfaction of the Royalty and it was agreed that Altus and Herald would use their best endeavours to have the Beneficiaries (prospectors) cancel the royalty and in consideration enter into this Deed pursuant to which they are granted a royalty similar in all material respects to the Royalty.

  3. The Beneficiaries (prospectors) have agreed with Goldfan to enter into this Deed on the terms and conditions hereinafter appearing.

NOW THIS DEED WITNESSETH:

In consideration of the Beneficiaries agreeing to surrender all their right and interest in the Royalty to Goldfan and to release Goldfan, Herald and Altus from all actions, claims, demands and liabilities whatsoever arising out of the entitlement of the Beneficiaries to the Royalty, Goldfan shall pay to the Beneficiaries the royalty referred to in Clause 2 thereof and subject to the provisions of Clause 3 hereof, this Deed may be pleaded by Goldfan, Herald or Altus as a bar to any such action, claim, demand or liability relating either directly or indirectly to the Royalty.

Goldfan shall by the 30th day of September in each year produce to the Beneficiaries an audited statement of royalty due to the previous 30th day of June together with payment thereof and the Beneficiaries may have reasonable access upon giving reasonable notice to Goldfan, to Goldfan’s records for the purpose of checking those statements.



3 ... 8 (not relevant)



  1. The practical effect of the Deed of Royalty was for the prospectors to release Altus and Herald from their obligations under the Option Agreement and enter into a fresh agreement, along similar lines to Clause 3(4) of the Option Agreement, with a new party, Goldfan. Does it therefore follow, as Mr Fletcher argues for the applicant, that payments by Goldfan pursuant to its obligations under the Deed of Royalty are “income derived by (the applicant) from the sale, transfer or assignment by the (applicant) of his rights to mine, in a particular area in Australia, for gold ...”. Mr Fletcher submitted that the essence of the payments or their source is found in the agreement to sell the tenements and nothing else.
  2. If the payments had come from either Altus or Herald pursuant to their respective obligations arising under the Option Agreement then we have no doubt that the source of such payments is found in that agreement. But as Goldfan acquired the tenements without having to seek the consent or agreement of the prospectors the source of the payments to them by Goldfan can only be found, in fact, in the Deed of Royalty. Certainly, common sense would lead to the conclusion that indirectly the source may be traced back to the original Altus/prospectors’ Option Agreement, for without that Goldfan would never have been persuaded by Altus or Herald to undertake their liabilities to the prospectors.
  3. To use an analogy, if the applicant now unilaterally assigned his right to the payment from Goldfan then the recipient could hardly claim that this is income from the sale of a mining tenement - it is too remote. So we find that whilst the payments have their origins in an agreement which may have given rise to the derivation of exempt income under s.23(pa), because the amounts were in fact paid pursuant to a quite separate and voluntary agreement entered into by the applicant, those (indirect) payments lose a sufficient nexus to be characterised as income derived from the sale of the tenements. They are not payments for the sale by the prospectors of the tenements because Goldfan bought them from Herald and in this respect the requirements of s.23(pa) are not met.
  4. As to the law, it is out of respect to the two counsel who appeared before the Tribunal, that we go further and deal with their respective submissions although it is not considered necessary given the finding of fact above.
  5. The thrust of Mr Fletcher’s submissions was that the source of the payments received from Goldfan was the original option agreement to sell the tenements - that as the tenements were sold and a condition of that sale was that should the tenements ever be mined then the applicants would receive the amounts conveniently referred to as royalties. Mr Fletcher referred to a decision in the New Zealand Court of Appeal, Inland Revenue Commissioner v Phillips’ Gloeilampenfabrieken [1955] NZLR 868 where Gresson J said, in relation to the source of interest flowing from a debt:

“... ‘source’ does not mean the quarter whence the moneys come, but the originating cause of the payment being made - the quid pro quo which the recipient of the many gave to entitle him to receive payments from time to time; ... “ (883)



  1. In further support of his submission Mr Fletcher referred to a later statement in this judgment by the learned judge:

“It is not sufficient to ascertain the fund out of which the income was in fact paid, which is no more that the reservoir from which it was drawn. It is not whence it was paid, but why it was paid, that is the determining factor. The emphasis is not upon the receipt, but upon the derivation of the income. .... Looking at the real substance of the facts with the eyes of a practical man, it was from the provision of the loan moneys that the income was derived; ... “ (884)



  1. The analogy drawn by Mr Fletcher was that, as a hard matter of fact, c.f. Nathan v FCT [1918] HCA 45; (1918) 25 CLR 183, the payments to the applicant were derived from the sale of the tenements by a bona fide prospector and that too is their source. It seems implicit in this submission that the Tribunal, should it agree, must find that the substance of the agreements, which provide the source of the payments is preferred to their form as discussed above.
  2. In a similar vein Mr Fletcher, by reference to statements by Dixon CJ in Caltex Ltd v FCT [1960] HCA 17; (1959-60) 106 CLR 205 when speaking of a debt substitution by new loans for old, concluded that the whole arrangement amounts to “nothing ... but the novation of a dollar indebtedness, or something equivalent or akin to a novation”; (217 and see Fullagar J at 227). The submission on behalf of the applicant in this regard is that we should view the substitution of Goldfan for Altus as the party responsible for the payments of the so called royalties as, in substance, nothing more than something akin to a novation. This argument contends that the substantial effect of the substitution did not serve to alter the expectancies of the applicant. We would however observe that it may well have affected the ability of the applicant to recover any debt arising should Goldfan be more or less able to meet its obligations than either Altus or Herald. So in that sense there were other considerations arising from the applicant having agreed to give up its rights of action against Altus and Herald.
  3. The thrust of Mr Martin’s submissions on behalf of the respondent was that the tenements were disposed of by the prospectors to Herald pursuant to the assigned Altus option agreement, for a fixed sum of $150,000 and an undertaking that should certain events occur in future then further sums will be paid. But from the date of transfer of the tenements to Herald, it was in a position to deal with them without reference to the prospectors. Should it do so then either it or Altus remained obligated to the prospectors for payment of the further sums contingent upon the future events occurring - that is, the mining, crushing and treatment of the ore. He submitted that because Herald did dispose of the tenements to Goldfan and in due course the prospectors entered into an agreement to relieve Altus and Herald of any further obligations in consideration of a fresh undertaking by Goldfan to make contingency payments to them, those payments arose from a chose in action, not he submitted, from any sale of the tenements by the prospectors to Goldfan as s.23(pa) requires. This chose in action, it was submitted, was a capital asset of the applicant which could be separately dealt with by assignment or surrendered to Goldfan for some other consideration. The chose in action, it was submitted, was not in any sense a right to mine as contemplated in s.23(pa).
  4. Mr Martin pointed out that Herald was able to transfer the tenements to Goldfan without inhibition from the prospectors who had no charge registered against them and that Goldfan, by making payments to the prospectors was not acquiring anything which they did not already have. This, it was submitted, stamped the payments with a revenue character. In support Mr Martin referred extensively to Cliffs International Inc v FCT [1979] HCA 8; (1979) 142 CLR 140, which although concerned with a deduction provision, did concern a promise to pay, based on a tonnage of iron ore mined, in relation to a prior transfer of shares in the company holding the mining tenements, the relevant consideration being a lump sum payment of £200,000. In that case Barwick CJ said:

“But there are several generalizations emerging from the decided cases which, in my opinion, have universal validity in relation to a decision whether a payment made in performance of a promise given as part of the consideration for the acquisition of a capital asset is itself of a capital or revenue nature. These generalizations are to be found in the reasons for the judgment of this court in Edgerton - Warburton v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation. I have no need to repeat or extract them here, or to repeat the citations which are there made. It is sufficient to say that from the statements in that case and the decision to which reference is there made, it clearly and, in my opinion, correctly appears that the fact that payments are made or received in performance of a promise given as part of the consideration for the acquisition of a capital asset does not necessarily mean that the payments are themselves of a capital nature. (148) ....



It is proper to point out, and to do so with emphasis, that by making the recurrent payments the appellant acquired nothing which it did not already have. ... The recurrent payments were not made for the shares though it might properly be said that they were payable as a consequence of the purchase of the shares.” (149)



  1. Mr Martin also further referred to the consenting judgment of Jacobs J where his Honour, found that the payments were of a revenue nature. However, we do not find it necessary to pursue that line of reasoning, simply acknowledging that a convincing case has been established to characterise as revenue/income the payments which the applicant in these proceeding received. The significant point emerging from the decision in Cliffs International, though is that the payments in this case can properly be seen to have their origins or source in the promise to pay undertaken by voluntary agreement between the prospectors and Goldfan, not the promise to pay undertaken between Altus and the prospectors which was extinguished. In this sense the promise is not one sourced in the sale agreement, which it would have been, should the applicant succeed in this matter. Mr Martin further referred to the Full Federal Court decision in Moneymen Pty Ltd v FCT 91 ATC 4019 and the High Court decision in FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199 which concerned rights arising under assignments where what was received was not characterised as having the same source as the matter of substance assigned - in Moneymen milk sale rights were assigned and in Myer it was an interest stream. In both instances the proceeds of the assignment were simply characterised as income flowing from the respective assignment.
  2. The Tribunal has decided for the reasons set out above that the amounts received are assessable income, not being exempt income under s.23(pa) and really that is the end of the matter.
  3. Having decided however to consider comments on Counsels’ further submissions, the Tribunal now also observes that, should it have been necessary, it would be unable to adopt the substance-over-form submission so ably argued by Mr Fletcher. Such a finding would also lead to the same conclusion already reached.
  4. For these reasons the objection decisions under review are affirmed.

[1] It is necessary to point out that the Tribunal was not provided with copies of the sale agreement of 1 June 1985 between Clackline (Herald) and Altus (called the “Option Agreement” in Ex.3) but it becomes apparent from the Deed of Surrender (Ex. 3) mentioned below, that Clackline (Herald) had agreed to pay Altus a “royalty” of $2.00 per tonne or ore mined, crushed and treated, so Altus stood to gain one dollar of royalty after paying the prospectors the covenanted royalty of $1.00 per tonne or ore mined, crushed and treated. Altus therefore preserved to itself the obligation to pay the prospectors even though it had transferred the tenements to Herald. The option agreement between Altus and the prospectors did not require the “royalty” obligation to follow any subsequent transfer of the rights to mine contained in the mining tenements.
