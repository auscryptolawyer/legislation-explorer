Re Ernest Sydney Scott v Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] FCA 486 (23 December 1987)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: ERNEST SYDNEY SCOTT

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. G203 of 1987

Superannuation

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Neaves(1), Beaumont(2) and Gummow(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Commonwealth - deemed invalidity - eligible employee (applicant) purported to resign from employment on medical grounds - applicant also applied for and in receipt of pension under Repatriation Act 1920 - whether applicant should be deemed to have retired on ground of invalidity - nature of discretion of Commissioner for Superannuation.

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.7, 54.

Administrative Appeals Act 1975 ss.43,44.

HEARING

SYDNEY

23:12:1987

Counsel and Solicitors D.H. Murr instructed by for the Applicant: MacDonald Edgerton & Watts

Counsel and Solicitors G. Hosking instructed by for the Respondent Australian Government

Solicitor

ORDER

The appeal be allowed with costs.

The decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal dated 10 April 1987 be set aside; in lieu thereof that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation be set aside with a direction that the Commissioner give an appropriate direction under s.7(2).

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order

36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

This is an application by Ernest Sydney Scott by way of appeal from a decision of the General Administrative Division of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal") given on 10 April 1987 affirming a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") made on 4 March 1983, and subsequently confirmed by the Commissioner, refusing to exercise favourably to Mr Scott the discretion conferred by sub-s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Superannuation Act").

2. Mr Scott, who was born on 18 June 1923, was employed for many years in the Postmaster-General's Department of the Commonwealth of Australia. On 19 May 1960, while so employed, he became a contributor to the Provident Account established under Part V of the Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth) ("the superseded Act"). Upon the establishment of the Australian Telecommunications Commission ("the Commission"), Mr Scott's employment in the Postmaster-General's Department was terminated and he became an officer of the Commission. He continued to be a contributor to the Provident Account until the Superannuation Act came into operation on 1 July 1976. He then became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established by that Act. He resigned from his employment with the Commission with effect from 10 October 1980. His contributory service thus exceeded 20 years. He was, immediately prior to his resignation taking effect, an "eligible employee" within the meaning of that expression in the Superannuation Act.

3. At the date of Mr Scott's resignation, s.183 of the Superannuation Act applied to a person who was or had ceased to be an eligible employee, being a person who was, or at any time had been, a person referred to in par.(a) of the definition of "eligible employee" in sub-s.3(1). That paragraph was in the following terms:

"(a) a person, who, immediately before the

commencement of this Act, was an

employee for the purposes of the

superseded Act and has continued to be,

and is, such an employee."

Section 183 went on to provide that the regulations might make provision for modifying the Superannuation Act, or a provision of the Act specified in the regulations, in the application of the Act or that provision to and in relation to a person to whom s.183 applied, or to or in relation to a prescribed class of persons to whom that section applied. The modifications that might be made included modifications providing for benefits in addition to, or in substitution for, benefits provided for by the Superannuation Act. By the Superannuation (Former Provident Account Contributors) Regulations ("the regulations"), being Statutory Rules 1978 No.227, the Superannuation Act and the provisions thereof specified in the Schedule to the regulations were to apply, subject to the modifications specified in the Schedule, to and in relation to those persons to whom s.183 of the Act applied who

(a) had ceased to be eligible employees,

being eligible employees referred to in

par.(a) of the definition of "eligible

employee" in s.3(1) of the Act;

(b) had not, after so ceasing, again become

eligible employees; and

(c) immediately before 1 July 1976, were

contributors to, or were liable to

contribute to, the Provident Account

established under the superseded Act.

The matter has proceeded on the basis that Mr Scott was such a person.

4. Upon his resignation, Mr Scott became entitled, pursuant to s.80 of the Superannuation Act as modified by the regulations, to a lump sum benefit, payable out of the Superannuation Fund established by the Superannuation Act, of an amount equal to his accumulated contributions. His accumulated contributions amounted to $8,638.43 and that amount was paid to him on 1 December 1980. A short time prior to his resignation from his employment, and in contemplation thereof, Mr Scott applied for a service pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth). A decision was made under s.85 of that Act to grant him a service pension with effect from 20 August 1980. It does not appear when that decision was made.

5. At the relevant time, the Telecommunications Act 1975 (Cth) provided that, if an officer appeared to the Commissioner to be inefficient or incompetent, or unable to discharge or incapable of discharging the duties of his position, the Commission might, inter alia, retire him from the Australian Telecommunications Commission Service (sub-s.56(1)). The procedures necessary for retirement on the ground of invalidity were not taken in relation to Mr Scott and no decision was made by the Commission that he be retired.

6. In order to determine what were the benefits to which Mr Scott would have been entitled under the Superannuation Act as modified by the regulations if he had, on 10 October 1980 (which was a date before the date on which he would have attained his maximum retiring age), ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity, it is necessary to have regard to the following additional matters:

(a) Sub-ss.(3), (3A) and (4) of s.66 and

s.79 of the Superannuation Act had no

application in Mr Scott's case;

(b) Mr Scott's period of prospective

service, as defined in sub-s.3(1) of the

Superannuation Act, was not less than 20

years but was less than 31 years;

(c) Mr Scott had not paid supplementary

contributions;

(d) Mr Scott would not have been entitled to

make an election under s.69 of the

Superannuation Act as his contributory

service exceeded 8 years;

(e) Three times Mr Scott's accumulated basic

contributions would have been greater

than one-half of the amount that was the

amount per annum of his final annual

rate of salary;

(f) Mr Scott had not been a contributor to

the Provident Account established by the

superseded Act on more than one

occasion;

(g) Mr Scott did not fall within s.212, one

of the additional sections inserted by

the regulations; and

(h) As his contributory service exceeded 20

years, no question would have arisen of

reducing on medical grounds the benefits

otherwise payable to him (see ss.66(2)

and 184 of the Superannuation Act).

7. Taking those matters into account, the benefits to which he would have been entitled in the circumstances postulated would have been as follows:

(i) if he did not make an election under

s.68 or s.198 (another of the additional

sections inserted by the regulations) -

to invalidity pension at the rate of 70

per centum of his final annual rate of

salary (shown on Exhibit "A" as

$11,419), that is to say $7,993.30 per

annum (see the modified sub-ss.66(1) and

67(1));

(ii) if he made an election under s.68 - to

invalidity pension at the rate of 50 per

centum of his final annual rate of

salary or $5,709.50 per annum and a lump

sum benefit, payable out of the

Superannuation Fund, equal to his

accumulated contributions or $8,638.43

(see the modified sub-ss.66(1), 68(1),

68(2) and 68(5));

(iii) if he made an election under s.198 - to

a lump sum benefit equal to three times

his accumulated basic contributions or

$25,915.29 (see the modified sub-s.66(1)

and the additional ss.196, 198 and 200

inserted by the regulations).

Payment of the benefits to which Mr Scott would have been entitled, except where the benefit was expressed to be payable out of the Superannuation Fund, would have been made out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund (s.112).

8. If Mr Scott had ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity and had made an election under s.68, he would not have been entitled to make an election under s.198 (sub-s.198(2)). An election under s.68 or s.198 would have had to be made not later than three months after becoming entitled to invalidity benefit under sub-s.66(1).

9. Mr Scott corresponded with the Commissioner in relation to his entitlement under the Superannuation Act and in due course the Commissioner treated that correspondence as being a request for the exercise in Mr Scott's favour of the discretion conferred by sub-s.7(2) of that Act. That sub-section provides:

"Where a person has, before attaining his

maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible

employee otherwise than by reason of death or by

reason of his having been retired, or his

services having been otherwise terminated, on

the ground of physical or mental incapacity to

perform his duties, but the Commissioner is

satisfied that, at the time he ceased to be an

eligible employee, he was, by reason of physical

or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his

duties, the Commissioner may direct that the

person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be

deemed to have been retired on the ground of

that physical or mental incapacity."

10. As has already been mentioned, a delegate of the Commissioner, and the Commissioner on reconsideration, declined to exercise that discretion in Mr Scott's favour. The matter then came before the Tribunal which affirmed the decision though on grounds different from those relied upon by the delegate and the Commissioner.

11. It was accepted in the proceedings before the Tribunal that Mr Scott was a person who had, before attaining his maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of his physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties. The Tribunal was, however, satisfied on the material before it, contrary to the view taken both by the delegate and the Commissioner, that at the time Mr Scott ceased to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his duties. The Commissioner did not challenge that finding.

12. The Tribunal went on to consider whether, the condition precedent to the applicability of sub-s.7(2) being satisfied, it was appropriate that the discretion which that sub-section confers should be exercised in Mr Scott's favour so as to deem him to have been retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity. The Tribunal concluded that it was not appropriate that the discretion be so exercised. In reaching that conclusion the Tribunal adverted to the circumstance that, if the discretion were exercised, an adjustment would have to be made back to the date of the cessation of Mr Scott's employment so that, unless it were fair to treat the employee as having been retired on the ground of invalidity as from the date of the cessation of his employment, the discretion ought not to be exercised in his favour.

13. The Tribunal also adverted to the circumstance that, had he been retired on grounds of invalidity on 10 October 1980, Mr Scott would have been entitled to exercise the right of election conferred by s.68 or s.198. It considered that it would have been to his advantage in 1980 to have made an election under s.198 on the basis that it "would have enabled him to receive a lump sum and also to apply for and obtain a service pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth)". The Tribunal noted that, if it were to exercise the discretion under sub-s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act in Mr Scott's favour, he would have to consider whether, in the present circumstances, he should exercise the right of election under s.68 or s.198 and that it was not clear whether it would be to his benefit to exercise either election. The Tribunal continued:

"In the circumstances, it is no longer possible

to put Mr Scott into the position he would have

been in had he retired on the ground of

invalidity. Since 1980 to date, he has been

receiving a repatriation pension. No decision

that the Commissioner could make and no order of

the Tribunal can affect the sum that Mr Scott

has received under the Repatriation Act 1920

(Cth) and the Veterans' Entitlement Act 1986

(Cth). He has had that benefit and it is a

benefit that cannot be adjusted by a decision

taken under s.7(2) of the Act.

If the s.7(2) discretion is exercised favourably

to Mr Scott, he will be entitled to insist on

payment of arrears of superannuation pension

from October 1980 to the present date and a

continuing pension hereafter. In such event, he

will also be entitled to retain, as a double

benefit, the pension received from 1980 to date

under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth) and the

Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (Cth).

In our opinion, the possibility of this double

benefit makes it unjust to exercise favourably

the s.7(2) discretion. It may be unfortunate

that this is so, but of course one of the

reasons for the early resignation was that Mr

Scott wished to obtain the benefit of a pension

under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth). He has

had that benefit and, having done so, it is now

impossible for the Commissioner or the Tribunal

to reverse the situation."

14. It was submitted on behalf of Mr Scott that, as a matter of law, the Tribunal was not entitled, in exercising the discretion which sub-s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act conferred, to take the above matters into consideration. Each of them was said to be a matter extraneous to the exercise of the discretion.

15. Sub-section 7(2) confers a wide discretion on the Commissioner and, on review, on the Tribunal: Commissioner for Superannuation v. Hastings (1986) 70 ALR 625. As the Full Court there said (at pp 630-1):

"Because the circumstances of individual cases

must differ widely, it is impossible to cast

such a provision in a rigid form; a discretion

must be vested in a suitable person, to be

exercised upon the merits of the individual

case. The industrial and medical history of the

applicant for a pension is undoubtedly material

to the decision to be made. Otherwise, as the

relevant matters are unspecified in the

legislation, it is for the Commissioner to

determine what matters are to be taken into

account and the weight to be given to them

'unconfined except in so far as the subject

matter and the scope and purpose' of s.7(2)

enables a court to say that 'given reasons are

definitely extraneous to any objects the

legislature could have had in view': see the

words of Dixon J. in Water Conservation

Commission v. Browning [1947] HCA 21; (1947) 74 CLR 492 at

505, adopted in R. v. Australian Broadcasting

Tribunal; Ex parte 2HD Pty Ltd (1979) 27 ALR

321 at 325; [1979] HCA 62; 144 CLR 45 at 49. The ultimate

question for the Commissioner is whether it

would be reasonable, having regard to the

interests not only of the applicant for a

pension but of other contributors to the

superannuation fund, to treat the applicant as

being entitled to the same benefits as if he or

she had retired on the ground of invalidity.

The Commissioner's decision is, of course,

itself subject to review by the Administrative

Appeals Tribunal."

16. The function of the Court is not to substitute its own opinion or decision whether the discretion should be exercised in Mr Scott's favour. That matter has been committed by Parliament to the Commissioner and the Tribunal. The only question for the Court is whether the Tribunal, in exercising the power under sub-s.7(2), has gone beyond the proper limits of the discretion.

17. In my opinion, no error of law on the part of the Tribunal has been demonstrated. The Tribunal was not, in my view, confined to considering whether it would have been appropriate in October 1980 to exercise the discretion under sub-s.7(2) in Mr Scott's favour. It was entitled, and, as I think, bound, to consider, as it did, the consequences of exercising the discretion in his favour at the date of its decision, namely 10 April 1987. It is not to the point to say that, had the occasion for the exercise of the discretion arisen in October 1980, it would almost certainly have been exercised in his favour. The question for the Tribunal was whether, in the circumstances as they existed in April 1987, it was appropriate, or just or fair, whichever word one adopts to describe the appropriate test, to treat Mr Scott as though he had been retired on invalidity grounds at the earlier date.

18. In my view, the Tribunal was entitled to have regard to the circumstance that, if Mr Scott were to be deemed to have been retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity, it would be open to him to exercise an election under s.68 or s.198 and to the further circumstance that, unless an election were made under either section, he would be entitled, as from 10 October 1980 and continuing, to an annual pension of 70 per centum of his final annual rate of salary. It was also open to the Tribunal to conclude, in the light of the supervening events, including the circumstance that he had applied for and been granted a service pension under the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth) on the footing that he was not entitled to a pension under the Superannuation Act, that the discretion under sub-s.7(2) should not be exercised in his favour. To exercise the discretion in his favour in those circumstances would have resulted in him being placed in a more advantageous position than a comparable employee who had been retired on invalidity grounds on the same date as Mr Scott's resignation took effect and this, in my view, was a factor which the Tribunal could properly regard as relevant to its decision. To take it into account was not to have regard to a matter extraneous to the object or purpose of sub-s.7(2).

19. It was also submitted on behalf of Mr Scott that the Tribunal, in exercising its discretion, had failed to take into account the circumstances in which he came to resign from his employment and failed to follow the procedures which would, almost inevitably, have resulted in a decision by the Commission under sub-s.56(1) of the Telecommunications Act 1975 (Cth) that he be retired on medical grounds.

20. The Tribunal clearly adverted to those circumstances in that part of its reasons for decision which led it to conclude that the pre-condition to the exercise of the discretion under sub-s.7(2) had been established. The Tribunal said:

"We believe that his failure to ascertain the

proper means of obtaining retirement on the

ground of invalidity resulted from his medical

problems including the anxiety and depression

from which he was then suffering. He felt that

he was unable to continue to work and unable to

cope with the travel and conversations necessary

to fully discuss the matter with Telecom."

21. While it is true that this aspect of the matter was not again expressly referred to, I am not prepared to find that the Tribunal did not have it well in mind as one of the factors to be considered when determining how the discretion under sub-s.7(2) should be exercised.

22. In my opinion, the appeal should be dismissed with costs.

The applicant, Mr. Scott, applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of a decision of the respondent, the Commissioner for Superannuation, made under the Superannuation Act 1976. Before going to the specific provision under which the Commissioner made his decision, some reference should be made to the general scheme of the legislation in its application to persons who cease to be eligible employees. A person may cease to be an eligible employee prior to attaining the maximum retiring age by reason of death or resignation or by dismissal for cause or by reason of his having been retired on the ground of invalidity, i.e., physical or mental incapacity to perform the duties of office. Retirement on the ground of invalidity may occur with or without the approval of the employee after the employee has been medically examined when it is shown, on medical grounds, that the employee is unfit to perform the duties of office (see, e.g., Telecommunications Act 1975 (Cth) s.56(1)(c); Trigger v. Australian Telecommunications Commission [1984] FCA 399; (1984) 4 FCR 242 at p 255). The significance of retirement on the ground of invalidity, as contrasted with retirement on other grounds, is that under the Superannuation Act 1976, different benefits are payable in the case of an employee who retires by reason of age (age retirement benefits) or on the ground of invalidity (invalidity benefits) than in the case of an employee who resigns or is retired early on some other ground (see Davis, The Law of Superannuation in Australia at pp.257-259; 261). As a matter of form, in circumstances to be explained later, Mr. Scott purported to resign from his employment. The present question is whether, as a matter of substance, he should be deemed to have retired on the ground of invalidity, notwithstanding his resignation. In a proper case, the Commissioner is given the power to direct that a person who has resigned from his employment is to be deemed to have been retired on grounds of invalidity. The Commissioner refused to give this direction here and it is this decision which was sought to be reviewed in the Tribunal.

2. The Commissioner's power to give an appropriate direction is conferred by s.7 of the Superannuation Act 1976:

"7. (1) A reference in this Act to the retirement

of an eligible employee on the ground of invalidity

shall be read as a reference to his having been

retired, or his services having been otherwise

terminated, on the ground of physical or mental

incapacity to perform his duties.

(2) Where a person has, before attaining his

maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible

employee otherwise than by reason of death or by

reason of his having been retired, or his services

having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of

physical or mental incapacity to perform his

duties, but the Commissioner is satisfied that, at

the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he

was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity,

unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner may

direct that the person shall, for the purposes of

this Act, be deemed to have been retired on the

ground of that physical or mental incapacity."

3. As has been said, the Commissioner refused to give a direction under s.7(2). The Tribunal affirmed the Commissioner's decision and Mr. Scott now appeals to the Court pursuant to the jurisdiction conferred by s.44(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

4. Mr. Scott was employed as an electroplater by Telecom Australia and its predecessor for more than 30 years. On 19 May 1960, he became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established under the Superannuation Act 1922. On 1 July 1976, the Superannuation Act 1976 (hereafter "the Act") came into operation, superseding the Superannuation Act 1922. Mr. Scott became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act.

5. In June 1979, Mr. Scott contemplated retirement. He wrote to the Commissioner:

"I work for No. 1 Workshop, Unwin Bridge Rd,

St. Peters, Telecom. My Doctor may retire me

on account of ill health, I would like

information regarding pension and provident

due to me, my age is 56 years."

(In fact, the letter was written by Mrs. Scott as Mr. Scott is unable to write a letter. His wife writes correspondence for him which he signs. Subsequent references to letters written by Mr. Scott should be taken against this background.)

7. On 3 October 1980, in circumstances to be elaborated later, Mr. Scott tendered his resignation from Telecom, as from 10 October 1980, on "medical grounds". On the same day, he signed a printed application form distributed by the Commissioner and proposed to be completed for the purposes of the Act by a contributor who "resigns or a contributor who retires and who elects to exercise an option to take a Lump Sum Benefit in lieu of a pension entitlement". Mr. Scott applied for a refund of accumulated contributions and payment of a lump sum benefit. His stated reason for termination of services ("Resignation, Dismissal, Age/Early Age Retirement, Invalidity Retirement, Involuntary Retirement" were mentioned on the form) was "resignation". By notification dated 27 October 1980, a delegate of the Commission, Lesley Spinks, informed the Commissioner that Mr. Scott's resignation had been accepted from 10 October.

8. On 4 May 1981, Mr. Scott wrote to the Commissioner:

"...please inform me, as to when The Provident,

is to paid to me. I retired from Telecom in

October, 1980, and to date have not heard from

Commissioner for Superannuation, therefor

would you kindly let me know, whether there is

a claim form to made out.

9. The Commissioner replied by letter dated 9 June 1981:

"...the letter you received, dated 25 Sep 1979,

referred to the options available had you been

retired on the grounds of invalidity.

2. As your application for benefit stated

that you had resigned and not retired the only

benefit payable is a refund of accumulated

contributions..."

10. On the Commissioner's file copy of this letter, the following note appears:

"I rang Lesley Spinks who confirmed Mr. Scott

had resigned and had advised Telecom he did

not wish to be considered for invalidity as he

was planning to receive a Social Security

pension.

28.7.81"

11. On 17 August 1981, Mr. Scott wrote to Telecom:

"Could you inform me on this matter, I retired

on the grounds of invalidity last October

(1980). All I have received to date, is

accum. Super refund conts, amount $8,638.43.

I have one letter which reads.

(c) On election, the lump sum that would have

been payable under the provisions of the

retired, not resigned..."

12. The Commissioner responded by a letter written in September 1981 in which he confirmed his previous position. By letter dated 5 February 1982, Mr. Scott again wrote to the Commissioner:

"In my confused state of mind, it has taken me

over 12 months to realise this has happened to

me. I thought I had retired on invalidity,

fully expecting what was due to me in

Superannuation, I had previously made

inquiries, as to what I was entitled to, long

before I had retired."

13. On 2 March 1982, the Commissioner responded, informing Mr. Scott that his letter had been accepted as an application under s.7(2) of the Act. Thereafter, the Commissioner sought, and obtained, medical opinions with respect to Mr. Scott's state of health prior to and at the time of his resignation. By letter dated 10 March 1983, the delegate of the Commissioner informed Mr. Scott that:

"...(he is) not satisfied that at the time you

ceased to be an eligible employee by way of

resignation you were by reason of physical or

mental incapacity unfit to perform your duties

and therefore has not deemed you to have been

retired on the ground of physical or mental

incapacity.

3. As a result of this decision there is no

further entitlement to benefit under the

Superannuation Act 1976."

14. By letter dated 20 March 1983, Mr. Scott sought a review of this decision. By s.154 of the Act, jurisdiction to review such a decision is conferred upon the Tribunal.

15. The principal issue before the Tribunal was whether, at the time of cessation of his employment, Mr. Scott was unfit, by reason of physical or mental incapacity, to perform his duties. Having heard medical evidence, the Tribunal concluded that Mr. Scott satisfied this condition of s.7(2). But the Tribunal was also of the view that, because of complications which were perceived to have arisen from the circumstances that Mr. Scott had applied for and was in receipt of a pension under the Repatriation Act 1920, the discretion conferred by s.7(2) should not be exercised in his favour.

16. Before going to the reasoning of the Tribunal on this aspect of the matter, reference should be made to some evidence given to the Tribunal by Mr. Scott as to the circumstances of his signing the application form already mentioned on 3 October 1980. In examination in chief, he said:

"...do you remember signing a resignation form

for Telecom?---I think I did put one in. I

thought I - resign - I thought I retired, you

know.

Where did you sign the document?----When they

sent it to me I think because I could not go

because I could not walk.

Mr. Scott was cross-examined on this evidence:

17. Lesley Spinks was not called to give evidence. The Commissioner called evidence from Mr. J.R. Coles, an officer of Telecom in charge of "Medical Redeployment and Retirements". His evidence in chief included the following reference to Mr. Scott's notification of his intention to resign (it appears that Mr. Coles was not speaking from his personal knowledge):

"What action did Telecom take after they

to discuss the method of resignation.

on the 27th of the lOth and advised that he

wished the resignation to proceed as he would

be receiving a pension from Veterans Affairs.

What action did Telecom then take?----Telecom

proceeded in the normal method of

resignation."

In the course of his cross-examination, Mr. Coles said:

"Do you have any knowledge of the telephone

conversation between (Lesley) Spinks and Mr.

Scott? Did (Lesley) Spinks ever report back

to you?----There is a note on the file, yes.

Do you have that note with you?----Yes, I

Could you - - -? --- Would you like me to

Did (Lesley) Spinks, to your knowledge, point

out any procedure that needed to take

place?----In the normal line of their duties

they would have, yes.

But he was not aware of Mr. Scott's mental

18. The Tribunal found that although he had been a good tradesman, Mr. Scott had reached the end of his effective working life by 1980 when only 57 years of age. (Mr. Scott could have retired by reason of age on 18 June 1983 on attaining 60 years of age.) By a certificate dated 15 August 1980, Dr. R.G. Williams, Mr. Scott's general medical practitioner, expressed the following opinion:

"This is to certify that Mr Ernest Scott 57 yrs

...of 2 Brennan St Yagoona has been under my

care since October 1974.

He suffers from anxiety state or neurosis with

noise intolerance which he blames on the

effects of his war service for four years in

theatres of war in New Guinea and New Britain

during world war 2. He states that this

neurotic state is now getting worse and is

aggravated by taunts of younger members

amongst his workmates, whom he considers are

misbehaving and drinking excessively at work.

He also suffers from mild hypertension and is

on Aldomet and Chlotribe.

He also suffers from painful arthritis and

tendonitis of both feet which is aggravated by

standing. He may have some impaired arterial

circulation in his feet as well.

He appears to have reached the stage where he

will no longer be able to continue his present

job and should retire permanently from it

because of his health, and to have therefore

reached the stage of permanent

unemployability."

19. Dr. Williams confirmed this opinion in evidence to the Tribunal, saying that Mr. Scott had become permanently unfit for work, the main causes being anxiety neurosis and noise intolerance.

20. The Tribunal said (at pp.7-8):

"It is unfortunate that the above report by Dr

Williams was not then communicated to Telecom

and, further, that Mr. Scott did not have a

full discussion with superior officers or

personnel officers at Telecom. We think that,

as Dr Williams said in evidence, Mr Scott had

come to a stage where he found his work

intolerable and we would add to that that he

found travel intolerable. He felt unable to

present himself for work. He remained off

work during August and September 1980 pursuant

to certificates of Dr Williams which were

forwarded to Telecom. He believed, correctly,

that if he retired on the ground of invalidity

he could obtain a lump sum payment under the

Act and that, if he did so, and did not

receive a pension, he could at the same time

apply for and receive a Service pension under

the Repatriation Act 1920 (Cth).

On 3 October 1980, Mr. Scott forwarded a

document of resignation on 'medical grounds'.

We believe that his failure to ascertain the

proper means of obtaining retirement on the

ground of invalidity resulted from his medical

problems including the anxiety and depression

from which he was then suffering. He felt

that he was unable to continue to work and

unable to cope with the travel and

conversations necessary to fully discuss the

matter with Telecom."

21. However, the Tribunal declined to exercise the discretion under s.7(2) for reasons which may be summarised as follows: if the direction were then to be given, it would operate retrospectively to the date of the cessation of employment; unless it is "fair" to treat the employee as having retired on the ground of invalidity, as from the date of cessation of employment, the discretion ought not to be exercised; in 1980, it would have been to Mr. Scott's advantage to exercise his option to receive a lump sum payment under the Act, for to do so then would have permitted him to receive that lump sum and also to obtain (as he did) a service pension under the Repatriation Act; however, at the time the Tribunal dealt with the matter (the hearing was in November 1986; a reserved decision was given in April 1987), the repatriation legislation was different: repatriation pensions are now tested not only against income but also against assets. It followed that it was no longer possible to put Mr. Scott into the position he would have been had he retired on the ground of invalidity and it would be unjust to permit him to receive a "double benefit" in the form of a pension under the Act together with a repatriation pension.

22. The Tribunal said (at p.l2):

"If the s.7(2) discretion is exercised

favourably to Mr Scott, he will be entitled to

insist on payment of arrears of superannuation

pension from October 1980 to the present date

and a continuing pension hereafter. In such

event, he will also be entitled to retain, as

a double benefit, the pension received from

1980 to date under the Repatriation Act 1920

(Cth) and the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986

(Cth).

In our opinion, the possibility of this double

benefit makes it unjust to exercise favourably

the s.7(2) discretion. It may be unfortunate

that this is so, but of course one of the

reasons for the early resignation was that Mr

Scott wished to obtain the benefit of a

pension under the Repatriation Act 192O (Cth).

He has had that benefit and, having done so,

it is now impossible for the Commissioner or

the Tribunal to reverse the situation."

23. On behalf of Mr. Scott, it is submitted that the Tribunal fell into error in failing to exercise the discretion under s.7(2) in Mr. Scott's favour. It is said that the discretion is limited by the subject matter, scope and purpose of the Act; and that the evident object of s.7(2) is to avoid injustice where an employee might be thought to be morally entitled to a pension but not within s.7(1) although effectively forced out of his or her employment for health reasons. It is then argued that the Tribunal wrongly took into account an irrelevant matter, namely, Mr. Scott's entitlement to a repatriation pension; and that the Tribunal erred in law in failing to take into account the circumstances, first, that Mr. Scott had been deprived of a benefit under the Act to which he would have been entitled since 1980 had he followed the appropriate procedures and, secondly, that had Mr. Scott received invalidity benefits under the Act in 1980, he would then have been entitled to his service pension also.

24. The operation of s.7(2) was considered by a Full Court (Woodward, Keely and Wilcox JJ.) in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Hastings (1986) 7O ALR 625. The Court said (at p 630):

"...it is too simplistic to describe s.7(2) as

creating a power intended to be used for the

benefit of persons who comply with its

pre-conditions. Rather, we see it as a

provision designed to avoid injustice in cases

where an employee might be thought to be

morally entitled to a pension, because

effectively forced out of his or her

employment for health reasons, but is not

within sub-s (1). Because the circumstances

of individual cases must differ widely, it is

impossible to cast such a provision in a rigid

form; a discretion must be vested in a

suitable person, to be exercised upon the

merits of the individual case."

25. In Hastings, the Court rejected an argument that the reference to "may" in the phrase "may direct" in this provision imported a duty to act in an appropriate case. It was held that these words were facultative only so that the Commissioner was not bound to make a direction in a case where the precondition, that an employee was unfit to perform his duties, was satisfied. The Court said (at p.631):

"The ultimate question for the Commissioner is

whether it would be reasonable, having regard

to the interests not only of the applicant for

a pension but of other contributors to the

superannuation fund, to treat the applicant as

being entitled to the same benefits as if he

or she had retired on the ground of

invalidity." (Emphasis added)

26. If we were concerned with a private superannuation fund, a balancing exercise of the kind described in this passage could readily be perceived. However, we are dealing here with a statutory public fund. It is true that provision is made by Part III of the Act for the establishment and management of a Superannuation Fund Investment Trust ("the Trust") (Division 1 of Part III) and for the establishment and management of a Superannuation Fund ("the Fund") (Division 2 of Part III). But the relevant invalidity benefits are not payable out of the Trust or the Fund. The benefits are paid out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund (see s.112(2) and State Superannuation Board v. Trade Practices Commission [1982] HCA 72; (1982) 150 CLR 282 at p 308; see also s.159). Since the payment of a benefit to an eligible employee cannot affect the entitlement, or the amount of the entitlement to a benefit in the case of any other employee, it is not apparent how, for the purposes of s.7(2), the interests of other contributors are to be taken into account. If that is so, it is difficult to see how the contemplated balancing of the interests of the applicant, on the one hand, and of other contributors, on the other hand, could be achieved.

27. In Hastings, it was said that s.7(2) was designed to avoid injustice where an employee might be thought to be morally entitled to an invalidity benefit. In our opinion, Mr. Scott has established such a moral entitlement here. In substance, although not in form, Mr. Scott was entitled to invalidity benefits when he ceased employment in October 1980. It is not, and could not be, suggested that his qualification for a service pension would then have disqualified him from invalidity benefits under the Act. The only reason why Mr. Scott did not secure invalidity benefits in October 1980 was his failure to follow the procedures which were appropriate in his case and that failure was adequately explained. In declining to exercise the discretion under s.7(2), the Tribunal relied upon the changes in policy in the means test for service pensions which were introduced after Mr. Scott ceased employment. With all respect, it is difficult to see how such a collateral, supervening consideration could properly be taken into account adversely to Mr. Scott. At the time of ceasing employment, Mr. Scott could have established an entitlement under each legislative scheme provided he had then followed the prescribed formalities. That he failed to do so was no fault of his. There was ample evidence, and the Tribunal found, that, at the time he considered ceasing employment, Mr. Scott was confused with respect to, and did not properly understand, his several entitlements under the Act and under the repatriation legislation. Moreover, once alerted to the problem, Mr. Scott acted reasonably promptly by seeking clarification of his position in May 1981. It is true that more than seven years have now elapsed since Mr. Scott ceased employment. But, having raised the matter within seven months of his ceasing to be employed, the subsequent passage of time, involving, as it apparently did, a change in the means test for service pensions, cannot be attributed to any fault on Mr. Scott's part.

28. In our opinion, a strong moral case for the exercise of the statutory discretion emerges from the following circumstances: first, the finding by the Tribunal of actual invalidity; secondly, the finding by the Tribunal that Mr. Scott was confused in 1980 with respect to his legal position; and thirdly, the absence of any suggestion of unreasonable delay by Mr. Scott in seeking the direction.

29. We accept that the existence of special circumstances may justify a refusal to exercise the discretion given by s.7(2). That is to say, we accept that, even if an employee can demonstrate that he ceased employment on invalidity grounds, a residual discretion remains in the Commissioner to decline to give the statutory direction. If, for instance, Mr. Scott had held an adequate understanding of what was involved in dealing with his several pension entitlements or if he had been guilty of unreasonable delay in seeking invalidity benefits, it may have been proper that no direction be given under s.7(2). But this is not the present case. All of the relevant considerations indicate that the statutory discretion should have been exercised in Mr. Scott's favour. Sub-section 43(1)(c)(i) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 empowered the Tribunal to make a decision setting aside the decision under review and making a decision in substitution for the decision so set aside. Sub-section 44(4) empowers this Court, on the appeal from the Tribunal to make such order as is thought appropriate by reason of its decision. In our view the Commissioner should have been ordered to give an appropriate direction (cf. The Queen v. Anderson; Ex parte Ipec-Air Pty. Ltd. [1965] HCA 27; (1965) 113 CLR 177 per Kitto J. at p 188, Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v. Conyngham (1986) 68 ALR 441 at p 448 per Sheppard J., Beaumont and Burchett JJ. concurring).

30. We would propose that the appeal be allowed with costs; that the decision of the Tribunal be set aside; and that in lieu thereof, it be ordered that the decision of the Commissioner be set aside with a direction that the Commissioner give an appropriate direction under s.7(2).
