Lauri and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 712 (13 September 1991)

Last Updated: 9 July 2008



SUPERANNUATION - Benefit classification certificate conditions relationship to retirement incapacity - benefit calculated on the basis that retirement incapacity linked with incapacity on a benefit classification certificate - employee significantly disabled performing duties - retired on incapacity after motor vehicle accident - whether new symptoms substantially contributed to by BCC condition - whether new symptoms substantially contributed to by condition connected with a BCC condition - accident aggravated spine conditions - symptoms caused by spinal canal stenosis connected with cervical spine and lumbar spine conditions on BCC - decision affirmed



Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 s.37

Superannuation Act, 1976 ss.66(2); 16(4); 154(6)



Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70

Re Rayment and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT Decision No. 5322, 23 August 1989

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT Decision No.411, 30 September 1980 Noted: (1980) 3 ALN N1

Re Davy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 7 ALN N413

REASONS FOR DECISION



13 September 1991 Deputy President B.H. Burns

Member, Mr B.C. Lock

Member, Dr J.T.B. Linn



1. This is an application under s.154 of the Superannuation Act, 1976 ("the Act") for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent made on 15 March 1990, confirming a decision made on 13 March 1987 by another delegate, being a decision:



"pursuant to subsection 66(2) of the Act to be of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Lauri's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate (BCC) that was in force in respect of him immediately before his retirement, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions".



The decision had the effect of denying the applicant a pension under the Act. Rather, he was entitled only to a lump sum benefit.



2. At the hearing of this matter the Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975, s.37. In addition the applicant tendered three exhibits (Exhibits A1-A3) and the respondent ten (Exhibits R4-R13). Three sets of documents were lodged for identification but ultimately not received into evidence. The applicant was represented at the hearing by Mr S. Milazzo of counsel and the respondent by Mr R. Whithear, an officer of the respondent. The applicant gave oral evidence and called two witnesses, Dr R.E. Russell, a consultant physician and the applicant's treating doctor, and Mr B. Rundle, a physiotherapist who had treated the applicant on numerous occasions. The respondent called Mr H. Schaeffer and Mr P. Carney, neurosurgeons who had treated the applicant; Mr B. Cornish, an orthopaedic surgeon who the applicant had consulted; Dr B. Meldrum, psychiatrist, who the applicant had also consulted and Dr L. Liaw, the Commonwealth Medical Officer who examined the applicant for the purposes of his entry into the Superannuation Scheme.



3. The parties agreed before the Tribunal that the sole issue to be decided was the application of sub-s.66(2) of the Act to the applicant. It was agreed that he fulfilled the prerequisites of sub-section (a) and (b). What was in dispute was the application of sub-section (c). Sub-section 66(2) reads as follows:



"(2) Subject to sub-sections (3), (3A) and (4) of this section and section 79, where -



(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;



(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and



(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,



the person is entitled -



(d) where the period of contributory service of the person is not less than 8 years -



(i) if the person does not make an election under section 71 or 72 - to invalidity pension in accordance with section 70 and, where the person has paid supplementary contributions, a lump sum benefit in accordance with that section;

(ii) if the person makes an election under section 71 - to invalidity pension, and a lump sum benefit, in accordance with that section; or



(iii) if the person is entitled to make an election under section 72 and makes such an election - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with that section; or



(e) where the period of contributory service of the person is less than 8 years - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with section 73."



It was agreed that the applicant had less than eight years contributory service.



4. The applicant was born on 6 April 1939. At the time of the hearing he was divorced and living in rented share accommodation. In 1955 the applicant dived off a jetty into shallow water and fractured his cervical spine. At the time he was 16 years of age and still attending school. As a result of this accident he was totally paralysed for approximately three months. After extensive treatment he regained some use of his limbs but was left with left sided hemi-paresis, he dragged his left foot when walking and lacked strength in the left arm and hand.



5. In 1962 the applicant was employed by the Commonwealth Department of Works at the Weapons Research Establishment, Woomera, in the North of South Australia. He worked as a temporary clerk class one at Woomera until 1977. In that year he transferred to the Defence Research Centre (Salisbury) in Adelaide's outer suburbs, where he was employed by the Department of Defence, until his retirement on 12 December 1986. In late 1978 an incident occurred which caused the applicant to be taken from work in an ambulance. He slipped and fell backwards into a chair experiencing severe lumbar pain. He had sustained a prolapsed disc necessitating a lumbar laminectomy which was performed by Dr H. Schaeffer. He was off work for three months on compensation.



6. After the three months away from work the applicant recovered and was able to resume his previous employment. He said that after his recovery he continued to have the same problems relating to his cervical spine as before. He had regular physiotherapy treatment on his lumbar spine and he said that with this treatment he had very little trouble either with his lumbar or cervical spine. Indeed he was able to successfully compete in lawn bowls and snooker for a number of years.



7. In 1983 the applicant was made a permanent employee and became eligible to join the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme set up by the Act. As a requirement of his joining he was examined by Dr L. Liaw, who recommended that a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") be issued. A delegate of the respondent issued a BCC pursuant to s.16(4) of the Act which reads:



"(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, will not continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or conditions is or are specified."



The BCC in respect of the applicant was issued on 28 February 1984 (T6) and specified the following conditions:



"NERVOUS DISORDER

FRACTURED CERVICAL SPINE WITH NERVE DAMAGE

ARTHRITIS

LAMINECTOMY LUMBAR SPINE"



The Tribunal considers that with the exception of nervous disorder, each of the conditions listed is a physical condition that can be defined in terms of some pathology (Re Andreatta and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT Decision No. 6970, 29 May 1991).



8. On 29 November 1985, the applicant was involved in a motor vehicle accident on his way home from work. He said that as he was nearing his home a neighbour backed out of a driveway causing him to swerve and apply the brakes of his vehicle causing rapid deceleration. The collision was a glancing blow causing relatively minor damage to his car and none to that of the other driver. He said he suffered severe pain in his back and neck and that walking was difficult after the accident. He noticed a dramatic deterioration in his mobility and said he had been in constant pain ever since the accident.



9. After the accident on 29 November 1985, the applicant did not return to work. He said he had difficulty walking and the constant pain in his neck and low back made it impossible for him to concentrate. He gave evidence about his job at the Defence Research Centre, and the Tribunal also had the benefit of his duty statement (Exhibit R5) and the job specification for a clerical assistant grade 3 (Exhibit R6). He had been required to spend a portion of his day walking around the workshops which he said he could not do after the accident in 1985 because of pain and because he was less able to walk than before the accident. He had also been required to write reports for various purposes, fill in forms and answer queries on the telephone and in person. He said he could no longer perform those duties as his pain made it impossible to concentrate (pages 40-44 of the transcript).



10. The accident in November 1985 was, said the applicant, a turning point in his physical condition. He said in evidence:



"Q. When you say you noticed it about six months after the 1985 accident, are you suggesting that it wasn't there before? In other words in the first - in that six-month period?

A. No. I am suggesting that it's sort of something that crept up on me. You know, day to day it's very hard to - if you just notice a little bit - sort of think in six months time, gee, I used to be able to do this before.



Q. All right. In the six months before the 1985 accident ... have you noticed the same sort of slowing down, lack of mobility?

A. No, no. I was fully mobile, ...



Q. So you say there was no change in the mobility as far as your legs were concerned in the six-months prior to the accident?

A. No.



Q. Prior to the 1985 accident?

A. No, no.



Q. What about your right arm?

A. No, that was fine.



Q. Right. What about bending, lifting, your ability to do that in the six months prior to the 1985 accident compared with say the six months after the 1985 accident?

A. There is no comparison. I find it very hard to lift things. I can't lift any weights.



Q. What? When were you talking about?

A. After the accident. Before that I could lift fairly large objects around the house.



Q. And bend?

A. Yes, and bend over - - -



Q. And stoop?

A. --- - - - because I was playing bowls and that. So you are bending over all the time.



Q. And what has been your experience or your understanding of that situation? You told us first of all of the six months after the accident, after the 1985 accident?

A. Yes.



Q. From that point on have you noticed any improvement, any worsening of that situation with respect to - I would like you to take them in turn - your legs, your right arm and your mobility in terms of your back, your lifting and - - -?

A. It seems to be - progressively I seem to have less mobility and less control of my legs and I can't lift anything of note anyway.



Q. Has there been any - what about stooping?

A. It's very difficult.



...



Q. Has the pain with respect to those three areas that's the neck, lower back and your legs, has that changed in intensity at all? Has it got greater or less or has it stayed about the same, or what? What is the situation? That's after - you know, we have talked about the six months after the accident, since then?

A. If anything it's got a little bit worse. it hasn't improved any."



There was no reason not to believe that the applicant did indeed suffer a marked deterioration in his condition after the motor vehicle accident in 1985. We found the applicant to be an impressive and credible witness whose evidence we accept.



11. Sub-section 66(2)(c) of the Act requires the Commissioner, and on review this Tribunal, to form an opinion about "the incapacity that was the ground for his retirement". A CMO, Dr Long who recommended retirement on invalidity grounds in a report dated 19 June 1986 (T15), specified the condition leading to incapacity as "low back and neck pain" and its contribution as "100%". It does not appear, from the determination made in relation to the applicant under the Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act, 1979 s.9 (Exhibit R12) nor from the notice of separation notifying the applicant of his retirement (T19) what the retirement incapacity actually was. The Commissioner held the view that incapacity in this context "means inability to perform the functions of an office by reason of prevailing physical or mental symptoms" and quoted the decision in Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1, Re Davy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 7 ALN N413 and Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller (infra), as authority for this proposition. The duties the applicant was unable to perform were those of a a clerical assistant grade one at the Defence Research Establishment at Salisbury.



12. We were referred to the Tribunal's decisions in Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70 and Re Rayment and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT Decision No. 5322, 23 August 1989 especially pages 16 to 20, as support for the proposition that the Commissioner could form an opinion as to the condition which caused the retirement incapacity but that the incapacity issue was fixed by the person retiring the employee. In Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT Decision No. 411, 30 September 1980, noted at (1980) 3 ALN N1. The Tribunal said at p.14:



"The phrase 'incapacity which was the ground for ... retirement' is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. it would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between 'incapacity' and a 'condition' is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions."



We would respectfully adopt the Tribunal's reasoning in that case. Pain is not itself an incapacity, it may cause incapacity, as it has done in this case, but the pain is not the incapacity which was the grounds for retirement.



13. In Cocks v Commissioner for Superannuation (1990) 21 ALD 297, Davies J, at pages 305-306 proceeded on the basis that it was proper for the Commissioner to form his own opinion of what the retirement incapacity was. (See also Beaumont J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153; 63 ALR 237 at page 160 (FCR); 244 (ALR). Incapacity is the inability to do something. A person may have a certain capacity for an activity and as long as they do only so much of that activity as they have capacity for, there is no incapacity for that activity, for example light duties. If, as in this case, a medical condition reduces capacity, so that there is no capacity to do an activity the person could previously do, it is that inability, not its cause which is the incapacity. In this case the applicant does not have the capacity to fulfill the duties of a clerical assistant grade one because he lacks the mobility required and he lacks the ability to concentrate sufficiently to perform those duties.



14. Sub-section 66(2)(c) requires the Tribunal to form an opinion as to whether one or more of the conditions specified on the applicant's BCC:



- caused the incapacity which was the grounds for retirement or,



- substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the grounds for retirement or,



- was connected to the condition which caused the retirement incapacity or,



- was connected to a condition or conditions which substantially contributed to the retirement incapacity.



At the hearing, Mr Whithear for the respondent, very properly made a number of significant concessions. He conceded that the BCC conditions "nervous disorder" and "arthritis" were not relevant to the applicant's retirement. He further conceded that the remaining BCC conditions "fractured cervical spine with nerve damage" and "laminectomy lumbar spine" could not be said to be the sole cause of the incapacity, nor was any condition connected with them the cause. Consequently the questions for the Tribunal can be stated:



Did the conditions "fractured cervical spine with nerve damage" and/or "laminectomy lumbar spine" substantially contribute to the applicant's retirement incapacity, or,



Did a condition connected with those conditions substantially contribute to the applicant's retirement incapacity.



If either is found to be the case, then sub-s.66(2)(c) of the Act will apply to determine the applicant's entitlement.



15. The applicant contended that his retirement incapacity was caused wholly by an acute flexion-extension injury with associated pain and disability to his spine incurred in the accident on 29 November 1985. He argued that, at the time of his retirement on 12 December 1986 the pain from this injury persisted, bringing about his incapacity and therefore his retirement. He relied on the opinion of Dr Russell who in evidence said at p.56 of the transcript:



"Q. ... How would you respond to the proposition in relation to Mr Lauri's case specifically, that his fractured cervical spine with nerve damage predisposed him to suffering symptoms as a result of his extension injury or flexion injury in 1985?

A. Well, as I say, it is difficult, I think, to take one side or the other but my impression with that type of injury to a previously damaged spine, that the question of whether you've had previous damage or not is almost irrelevant. It will happen if it is going to happen just due to the impact. And I don't think there is much in an abnormal spine like his that can be said to facilitate injury. It might be the sort of commonsense view but I don't really feel that there's enough evidence to say that he would have been free from injury if he'd had not a previous injury.



Q. Yes. I take it from that that you are unable to say that - well, you wouldn't agree with the proposition that Mr Lauri suffered the injuries he suffered in 1985 because of his pre-existing condition?

A. No. I don't think that that is a necessary related factor to the subsequent injury."



He went on to say at p.66, that the loss of capacity which forced the applicant to give up work was caused entirely by the 1985 accident.



16. The applicant also called Mr B. Rundle who had been his physiotherapist between 1978 and 1990. He told the Tribunal of a dramatic loss of movement in the applicant's spine after the 1985 accident. At p.130 of the transcript he said:



"Q. All right, and then as you continued to treat him what happened?

A. Well, at that stage, we'd - he'd gone backwards from my notes that his range of movement had diminished approximately to about 20 per cent of - - -



Q. What was it before?

A. Well, at one stage we had a note here that it was up to approximately 60 per cent, so at that stage we had obviously measured him as having lost 40 per cent of his active range.



THE D.PRESIDENT: When was it 60 per cent?

A. That was prior to the motor accident - prior to the 29th of the 11th, yes."



It was the applicant's case that the injuries arising from the motor vehicle accident were solely responsible for the incapacity which led to his retirement. Though he had a severely injured cervical spine and had had surgical intervention in his lumbar spine, he had the capacity to do the work he had been doing up to the 29 November 1985. The incapacity for that work, he said, was due only to the effects of the motor vehicle accident. In suffering those injuries he put it that his old injuries, specified on his BCC were irrelevant.



17. The respondent submitted that the condition causing the applicant's retirement incapacity was substantially contributed to by his BCC conditions. Either they predisposed him to further injury or it was those very conditions which caused the incapacity when accelerated or exacerbated by the trauma suffered in the accident. It was further submitted that he had suffered an acute strain injury, such an injury was substantially contributed to by the condition of his spine and was therefore connected to conditions specified in the BCC.



18. Mr Schaeffer, the neurosurgeon who performed the applicant's lumbar laminectomy gave evidence that he did not consider the 1985 accident to have had more than a minor effect as he said at p.90 of the transcript:



"... he has some vulnerability as a result of his pre accident conditions, but as I have already pointed out, as I see it, the vehicle accident was a low velocity vehicle accident causing sort of minimal damage to the car and I find it difficult to believe that any major problem would have ensued for that reason.



Q. All right, so, whatever Mr Lauri's degree of vulnerability was, in the events that actually occurred, you don't believe that that vulnerability gave rise to any significant disability in the long term?

A. Not significant disability. As I've already pointed out, I accept that if he experienced some symptoms in the neck region they may have been for a bit longer because of the vulnerabilities created by the pre existing condition. And the same could apply to the low back region but I am not very happy about the low back region because although I accept that he may have experienced some symptoms there after the car accident, after all his low back was not sort of forcibly moved to any significant extent in the car accident, as I know it. ..."



It was his opinion that the applicant's complaints were genuine but should be attributed to a psychological rather than a physical cause.



19. Dr Meldrum, a psychiatrist who had treated the applicant, gave evidence and two of his reports to the respondent (Exhibits R7 and R8) were received into evidence. Dr Meldrum was not prepared to make any diagnosis linking the applicant's pain with a psychiatric cause. He went no further than to state it as a possibility. Having regard to the opinion of Dr Meldrum and the fact that Mr Schaeffer is not qualified to diagnose a psychiatric cause for the applicant's symptoms, we are not satisfied that the applicant's symptoms have a psychiatric basis.



20. The submission that the applicant was suffering an aggravation or acceleration of his original low back condition was supported by Mr Cornish, an orthopaedic surgeon who said at page 111 of the transcript:



"Q. Could you tell the tribunal what Mr Lauri's condition was as far as his lumbar spine at that examination on 18 February 1988?

A. His symptoms were not confined to his lower back. He reported that he was feeling - he reckoned his feet were more clumsy. He tended to stumble and fall. He said that he had actually been having physiotherapy three times a week but I don't for the duration - what duration. ... And claimed there was increasing back pain and the focus was on the back and legs. There's no record of him - I haven't written down any observations on or his complaints on his neck.



Q. Right. Did you make a diagnosis at that time on 18 February 88, as far as his back is concerned?

A. ... Well, I thought that he was suffering largely from a continuation of his original problem with this uncertain contribution from the vehicular accident and there was a CT examination cf the lumbar spine performed 28.1.86 which showed, in general terms, evidence of narrowing of the canal."



and at p.113 he said:



"MR WHITHEAR: You talked about an uncertain contribution by the motor vehicle accident. Has the more recent examinations shed any further light on that contribution or is it still uncertain in your opinion? A. Looking at it from the time of that last examination back over what would have been four years - three years from my contact - the overwhelming presentation clinically is of his longstanding quadriplegic affects."



He said the accident of November 1985 had accounted for much of the applicant's pain by disturbing his existing conditions. In his opinion there was not a new condition present, simply a degeneration of the applicant's old lumbar spine injury. As he put it at p.118:



"I think the degenerative state has been given a shunt or an acceleration as a result [of the accident]."



21. The other submission of the respondent was that the accident of 29 November 1985 had given rise to a new condition, but one connected to conditions specified on the BCC. This was supported in relation to the applicant's new injury by the evidence of the neurosurgeon, Mr Carney, who said of the injury sustained in the accident of 29 November 1985 (at p.186 of the transcript):



"... Mr Lauri gave me a history of progressive spastic quadric paresis.



Q. If I could you stop you there, could you explain what you mean by that?

A. He gave me a history that all of his limbs became progressively weakened and stiffened over a period of time from the injury.



Q. Is this something different to the spinal cord injury ... [sustained in 1955]?

A. No. I am sorry - is it different?



Yes?---It is a spinal cord injury but it is not the spinal cord injury of 1955.



Q. Right. How does it differ from the 1955 injury?

A. Well, it's a different date to be begin with.



Yes?---Secondly, progressive symptoms are not an acute injury. They are brought on by some process which affects the spinal cord and produces chronic, slowly progressive damage.



Q. Would the 1955 injury have also produced progressive damage?

A. I had no history of that and it's unlikely."



His opinion differs from that of Dr Russell in that he attributes the applicant's pain to spinal injury rather than musculo-ligamentous injury.



22. In his report to the respondent dated 28 August 1989 (T38) Mr Carney stated:



"I consider that previous injury to the cervical spine and spinal cord rendered Mr Lauri vulnerable to the effects of the accident, even though the damage to his car was not extensive.



... I consider that the condition of cervical spine fracture with nerve damage has very substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground of retirement. I consider this injury was compounded in the motor vehicle accident of 1985 by further damage to the spinal canal resulting in a progressive deterioration of spinal cord function due to spinal canal stenosis."



It is clear that Mr Carney considered the applicant predisposed to the condition he says resulted from the accident, that is, spinal canal stenosis. This accords with a proposition put by Dr Russell in a report to the respondent dated 30 November 1989 (T33) in which he said:



"In Mr Lauri's case, because of pre-existing damage to his spinal cord with subsequent muscle atrophy, damage may have been more readily produced by the accident in November 1985."



Though in fairness, it must be pointed out that Dr Russell has since resiled from this position. Mr Carney said that spinal canal stenosis, or a narrowing of the spinal canal, was almost certainly a degenerative process which would have occurred even if the applicant had not had the motor vehicle accident in 1985.



23. We accept the evidence of Mr Cornish in relation to the applicant's lumbar spine. We find that the pain the applicant now suffers and the incapacity which results is caused by an aggravation of the condition of his lumbar spine, on which a laminectomy operation was performed in 1978.



24. We also accept the evidence of Mr Carney in relation to the applicant's cervical spine. We find that the applicant's neck pain and resulting incapacity is substantially contributed to by spinal canal stenosis. In our view, Mr Schaeffer's opinion was based on his perception that the applicant's pain had a largely non-organic basis. As to the applicant's psychiatric condition, we have accepted the evidence of Dr Meldrum. We prefer the evidence of Mr Carney to that of Mr Schaeffer. Accordingly, we find the applicant suffered an acceleration of his spinal canal stenosis caused by the motor vehicle accident on 24 November 1985.



25. It was the result of this acceleration of spinal canal stenosis which led to the applicant's retirement incapacity. Leaving aside for the moment the question of whether the B.C.C. conditions "fractured cervical spine with nerve damage" or "laminectomy lumbar spine" could be said to substantially contribute, there is no doubt given the evidence which the Tribunal has accepted that the applicant's spinal canal stenosis is connected with both of these conditions.



26. Sub-section 66(2)(c) does not define the term "connected with". However, the Full Court of the Federal Court has interpreted this sub-section on a number of occasions. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Perrett [1989] FCA 348; (1989) 23 FCR 425; 89 ALR 385. At p.433 (FCR); 393 (ALR), in a passage approved by the Full Court in Perrett v Commissioner for Superannuation VG196, Jenkinson J said:



"In my opinion, the expression 'connected with' in s.66(2)(c) does comprehend, on the proper construction of the paragraph, something short of the relationship between cause and effect. The choice of that expression by the draftsman strongly suggests that something more comprehensive than a causal relationsip was to be indicated, for there is no shortage of stock expressions for denoting a causal relationship in the phrase book of legislative drafting, and 'connected with' is not one of them. I respectfully agree with the opinion Beaumont J expressed in Miller's case that 'connected with' is intended to have in s.66(2)(c) its ordinary meaning, and that that meaning comprehends 'those cases where a link or an association exists between two medical conditions'. In my opinion such a link or association might be constituted by a higher incidence, in a community, of a medical condition among those of the community in whom another medical condition is known to exist than among the rest of that community."



In Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (1989) 22 FCR 413; 86 ALR 239 the Full Court, at pp.420-421 (FCR); 246-247 (ALR) interpreting a previous Full Court decision in Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller (supra), insisted that, having regard to the purpose of the legislation, a connection which operates to deprive a Commonwealth employee of part of their benefit must be "substantial".

27. The Tribunal is satisfied that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was substantially contributed to by the condition of spinal canal stenosis. On the material before the Tribunal, we find that the applicant's spinal canal stenosis has a substantial connection with the BCC conditions specified as "fractured cervical spine with nerve damage" and "laminectomy lumbar spine".



28. In any event, the Tribunal is also of the view and so finds, on the evidence of Mr Cornish that the two above mentioned BCC conditions substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement.



29. The decision under review, that sub-s.66(2)(c) applies to the applicant in calculating his superannuation benefit is affirmed.
