REASONS FOR DECISION

4 December 1987 Deputy President CJ Bannon QC

The basic questions in these proceedings are whether or not the income of a superannuation fund established by the first taxpayer was exempt from taxation pursuant to s23F of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 (Cth.) ("the Act") and whether or not contributions by the first taxpayer to the superannuation fund were deductible pursuant to S82AAC of the Act.

Objections to assessments upon the first applicant for the taxation years ending 30 June 1977 to 1982 inclusive and upon the second applicant for the taxation year ending 30 June 1978 to 30 June 1983 were heard together by consent. Proceedings upon an objection to the first taxpayer's assessment for the year ending 30 June 1976 were stricken from the list by consent, in accordance with an arrangement between the parties.

The first taxpayer is a company which operates a restaurant business. The second taxpayer is a Superannuation Fund created by the first taxpayer for its employees, the Trustee of which is the managing director of the first taxpayer. The Deed (Exhibit C) was executed on 28 June 1977. It is not alleged that the Deed is a sham.

The managing director of the first taxpayer gave evidence that he called a meeting of the company's then employees, including his wife and himself. At that meeting, the accountant spoke and explained why the fund was being established and that the employees were not required to contribute. The managing director spoke and said the company's contributions to the fund depended on whether or not it made a profit. He told the staff they would be members of the fund. The unexecuted Deed was placed on a table in their sight. Nobody raised any objections.

The managing director said the Deed was subsequently executed by the company and himself as the Trustee. The company forthwith contributed $29,643 to the fund on 30 June 1977. Bank statements showed subsequent contributions to the fund (Exhibit D).

Six sample notices to members of the fund as to their interests in the fund became Exhibit E. These were all dated 30 October 1981.

The first taxpayer changed its accountant following a Taxation Department investigation in 1980. Sometime around March 1983, the new accountant collected certain records from the old accountant, among them certain application forms to join the superannuation fund (Exhibit F) signed by thirty five persons and mostly undated. Also collected were two journals dealing with the superannuation fund and the interests of alleged members (Exhibit H).

Following a review of the application forms by the Taxation Department, it was decided to allow deductions for superannuation contributions to the first taxpayer in respect of the financial years ending 1981 and 1982 to the extent of $9,863 and $17,697 respectively for certain of the employees whose rights were considered to be fully secured in those years; in accordance with a schedule attached to a letter sent by the Department to the new accountant dated 31 October 1983 (Exhibit G). Amended assessments issued took into account these figures, and no dispute arises before me concerning these allowances. Concerning the previous years and other employees, the taxpayers' argument is that their interests in the fund were fully secured.

The managing director of the first taxpayer gave evidence. Copies of the income tax returns of the second taxpayer for the years ending 1978 to 1983 inclusive were in evidence (Exhibit 1). Concerning entries therein relating to persons claimed to be members of the fund, he said that figures in the columns headed "forfeited benefits" related for the most part to persons who were not entitled to benefits, because they had been members of the fund for less than five years when their employment terminated. One person was not entitled because he was dismissed after being found to be engaged in theft. Another waiter was dismissed after a complaint by a customer as to his conduct.

While a Trustee invested with discretionary power is not required to observe the rules of natural justice, he is bound "to take an informed view of whether or not to exercise his discretion, and not to act irresponsibly, capriciously or wantonly." Jacobs Law of Trusts in Australia 5th ed, para1609. It appears doubtful that this rule was adhered to in the case of the dismissed waiter.

The Trust Deed (Exhibit C), in the clause headed Paragraph Nine, deals with benefits on termination of service. Paragraph 9.2 provides a discretion to pay the whole or part of the amount standing to the credit of a member of the fund who resigns prior to achieving entitlement after five years service. It appears most of the alleged members did not have the discretion exercised in their favour, the most notable exception being a long-standing employee who received $3,700, although his claimed membership of the fund was less than five years.

Paragraph Three of the Trust Deed is in the following terms:

"Every employee of a constituent company nominated by the directors thereof and who has completed an application for membership in the form approved by the company and the trustee undertaking to be bound by the provisions of this deed and has lodged such application with the trustees may be accepted into membership of the fund. The terms and conditions of membership shall be as set forth in this deed and the application for membership."

Paragraph Eight deals with maintenance of records which are to be available to members. It also provides for annual notification to each member of the amount of his interest. I am not satisfied this was done with the majority of the alleged members. I am not satisfied that the records of the fund were available to the alleged members or that they were even informed of their existence or whereabouts.

It was submitted by counsel for the taxpayers that although there was no compliance with the requirements of Paragraph 3 of the Trust Deed, at least prior to 1983, the episode at which the first accountant and the managing director addressed fifteen then employees before execution of the Trust Deed amounted to estoppel by conduct so that the Trustee of the fund was precluded from denying that the employees were members of the fund. I am unable to accept this. There is no evidence that any of them altered their position as a result of the representations made to them. There is no evidence that any subsequent employees were ever informed of the representations, or that the fifteen employees addressed were all continuing employees at the times the 35 application forms were signed.

My impression is that until the Taxation Department investigation, the managing director of the first taxpayer treated the fund largely as an adjunct to the funds of that taxpayer, making a few comparatively small payments, but not treating it as separate trust property and not informing the alleged members of their rights. With the assistance of the new accountant, these defects appear to have been overcome.

However, the fact remains, in my opinion, that the rights of the members of the fund, or the claimed members, were not fully secured within the meaning of ss 23F(2)(d) and S2AAC of the Act during the years in issue. The law on this subject is set out in the judgment of Taylor J in Driclad Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 91; (1963) 121 CLR 45 at 58,59 and that of the Full Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v The Northern Timber and Hardware Co Pty Limited [1960] HCA 93; (1960) 103 CLR 650,657. There is no need to repeat the observations of the High Court. I am satisfied the rights of the employees to receive payments were not secured to them during the years involved as required by law. The details of the fund, the Trust Deed, and their interests in the fund were not made clear to the employees. The fund operated largely as a discretionary fund of the first taxpayer. An important element in securing the rights of employees is informing them of their rights and this was not done. But for the concession made by the Taxation Department regarding the financial years ending 1981 and 1982, the same observation would apply to the whole of the claims in those years. In view of the amendment of those assessments I need not comment any further.

No penalties for late payment have been imposed. The objections to the assessments in dispute are disallowed.
