HEARING

ADELAIDE

26:11:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that:

1. The decision dated 17 September 1990 made pursuant to s.3(3) of the Superannuation Act, 1976 is affirmed.

2. The decision dated 3 April 1991, made pursuant to s.140(2) of the Superannuation Act, 1976 be set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the applicant will be allowed a further period of 28 days from the date of this decision in which to repay the sum of $44,737.75, being the amount of the lump sum benefit which the applicant was previously entitled to under the provisions of the Act.

DECISION

The Tribunal has before it two applications for review of reviewable decisions made by the respondent. The two reviewable decisions are properly before the Tribunal having been reconsidered and confirmed by the respondent pursuant to s154 of the Superannuation Act, 1976 ("the Act").

2. The first decision made on 17 September 1990 (T3, file A) was made pursuant to s.3(3) of the Act which states:

"3. (3) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and,

immediately after so ceasing, again becomes an eligible employee, he

shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed not to have so ceased."

The delegate of the Commissioner declined to deem the applicant not to have ceased to be an eligible employee for the purpose of the Act on his ceasing employment with the Department of Social Security ("the DSS") on the 30th April 1990. On reconsideration, the delegate after reviewing all the circumstances concluded: "I am of the opinion that, following the cessation of his status as an eligible employee on 30 April 1990, Mr Marshall, at the present time, has not again become an eligible employee for the purpose of the Act. I have hence confirmed the decision upon reconsideration ... " (T2, file A) (emphasis added).

The applicant contends that his "resignation" was never formally accepted therefore he had not "resigned" - he remained an "eligible employee" and his entitlements under the Act should continue.

3. The second decision made on 3 April 1991 (T3, File B) was made pursuant to s.140(2) of the Act and in it the delegate allowed the applicant a period of 14 days from receipt of notification of acceptance of his late election in which to repay a sum of $44,737.75, being the amount of the lump sum benefit which the applicant was previously entitled to under the provisions of the Act.

4. The applicant seeks review of this decision on the grounds that the period of 14 days is too short and that in the special circumstances applying, he should be allowed 3 years to 1 May 1994, in which to pay the Commissioner the lump sum of $44,737.75.

5. By agreement between the parties, both decisions are to be reviewed together.

6. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 relating to each decision (the "T" documents - file A and file B) and received in evidence exhibits (A1-A8) and (R1-R10) which were tendered by the parties. Oral evidence was given before the Tribunal by the applicant, by Ms J. Baulderstone, Personnel Manager, Area South, Department of Social Security ("DSS") and by Mr I. Cameron, Assistant Area Deputy Manager, DSS. Evidence by telephone from Brisbane was taken from Mr P. O'Leary, former Director, DSS, Adelaide.

7. The applicant was represented by Mr Downs of counsel and the respondent by Ms Munoz, an officer in the employ of the respondent.

8. The Tribunal makes the following findings of fact. The applicant was born on 12 August 1947. He was employed as a Clerk Class 1 in the then Department of Social Services and on 20 July 1965 became a permanent employee. He commenced as a contributor under the Superannuation Act, 1922 ("the superseded Act") on 10 December 1965 by virtue of his employment. Since, at the date of commencement of the Act on 1 July 1976, he was an "employee" and a contributor for the purposes of the superseded Act, he became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Act. In a letter dated 12 April 1990 he submitted his resignation from his employment with the DSS effective from 30 April 1990. His application for a refund of his superannuation contributions, signed on 27 April 1990, was received by the Retirement Benefits Office ("the RBO") on 11 May 1990, and payment of the refund was approved on 15 May 1990.

9. During his career with the DSS, the applicant received various promotions and at the date of his resignation, he was the programme manager, Control Recovery and Review Section of the Southern Region of the DSS in Adelaide. He had previously been manager of the Glenelg Branch of the DSS. He was well regarded by his supervisors and his immediate supervisor, Mr Ian Cameron, gave evidence to the Tribunal to this effect. Apart from 2 years National Service during which time he saw active service in Vietnam, the applicant has served all of his public service career in the DSS. At this stage it will be useful to set down the sequence of events as found by the Tribunal, surrounding the resignation and its sequelae.

On 12 April 1990, the applicant submitted a written resignation

with effect from 30 April 1990 (T5/30, file A). The letter of

resignation was endorsed by Ms J.Baulderstone, Personnel Manager, DSS

with the words "Original accepted by the Director 19/4" and she told

the Tribunal that Mr Cameron told her the Director had accepted it.

Later in evidence before the Tribunal, Mr P. O'Leary said that he did

not formally accept the resignation but merely passed it on to Mr

Cameron with words to the effect "What do you know about this?". He

said also that Mr Cameron indicated to him that he (Cameron) had

discussed the matter with the applicant. The Tribunal's findings

regarding whether or not the applicant's resignation was accepted,

appear later in these reasons.

In this letter of resignation the applicant asked of his

superannuation entitlements: "In relation to my superannuation payout,

I would like written confirmation from Central Office advising my

entitlement (both gross and net) under the present scheme and my

entitlement under the new scheme." and alongside this in the margin

someone has written "applied for refund (therefore) this does not

apply."

On 27 April 1990 the applicant signed an Australian Government

Retirement Benefits Office form "Resignation Benefit Application" and

on p 4 of that application, the applicant "ticked" the box marked

"Refund of Contributions" and signed the form after the words "I Bruce

Antony Marshall hereby apply for a refund of accumulated contributions

under s.80 of the Superannuation Act, 1976." On the same page of the

application there was provision for an alternative, namely,

"Preservation of Superannuation rights" (T6/35, file A). This

alternative was not sought by the applicant at that time.

On 30 April 1990, the applicant ceased work at the DSS. On the same

day he handed over keys to the office car and had a farewell drink with

some staff (transcript p 146).

On 8 May 1990 (approximately), the applicant received a payment for

long service leave, leave loading and recreation leave (transcript pp

59-60).

On 15 May 1990 approval for the payment of the Refund of Contributions

was made in the office of the RBO.

On 25 May 1990, the applicant received from the RBO a cheque for

$44,315.81, being the amount of his lump sum entitlement of $44,737.75,

less tax. By this time the applicant was reconsidering his position.

He had had discussions with Mr Cameron with no particular outcome. He

had almost made up his mind that he wanted to resume work with DSS. He

enquired as to what he should do with the cheque and was advised to

bank it and wait for a request to refund it.

On 29 June 1990 applicant seeks to rescind his resignation. This

letter (T7/36, file A) is endorsed by Mr Cameron - "Given the contents

of minute 89/7205 of 20/11/90 I approve the withdrawal of Mr Marshall's

resignation. I believe the decision to be in the best interests of

departmental efficiency. Mr Marshall will resume duty on 9/7/90 with

Change Management in Corporate Services." At about the same time the

applicant applied to the respondent to be deemed not to have ceased to

be an eligible employee under s.3(3) of the Act.

On 1 July 1990 the Superannuation Act, 1990 ("the 1990 Act") came into

effect.

On 3 July 1990 the applicant handed to the Personnel Section, the DSS,

Adelaide, a bank cheque for $44,315.81 (T6/30, file B).

On 9 July 1990 applicant resumes duty in the Corporate Services

Section Area South in the DSS at the same level of salary and

responsibilities that he held prior to resignation. The period he was

absent viz 1 May 1990 to 7 July 1990 was to be accounted for by use of

Recreation Leave and or Long Service Leave. He has continued to work

in this capacity from that date onwards and each fortnight 5% of his

salary is deducted for superannuation purposes. The evidence in the

documents before the Tribunal is that this money has been credited to

the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme (PSS) established under the

1990 Act, and not to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS), the

scheme established under the Act.

On 25 July 1990 the RBO advised that the legal opinion they were

acting on held that the applicant could not retrospectively withdraw

his resignation and that as a lengthy period had elapsed before

recommencement, s.3(3) of the Act would not apply. The RBO refused to

accept the cheque refunding the money at this stage (T3/27 and T15/57,

file A).

10. The Tribunal now turns to consider the applicant's submissions concerning his resignation which are relevant when reviewing the decision made under s.3(3). The applicant submitted that his resignation was ineffective because it had not been accepted by those in authority in the DSS, in accordance with the Departmental guidelines for further resignation of officers (A3); alternatively, if the resignation was accepted, then the applicant had withdrawn his resignation and the DSS had accepted the withdrawal. The applicant submitted that the Continuous Record of Personnel Amendment Form (A6) and a letter from Ms Trudy Brennan of Personnel Services, advising the Department of Finance that the applicant's resignation was withdrawn and he was deemed to be on recreation and long service leave for the period between his resignation and recommencement (A7), indicated that his employment with the DSS had never ceased.

11. The applicant submitted that his resignation was ineffective because it had not been formally accepted in accordance with procedures set out in the Guidelines Relating to the Resignation of Officers and Employees from the DSS Personnel Management Manual, Vol. 7 (A3). The guidelines provided for a resignation to be in writing, addressed to the officer nominated to accept resignations, and should give reasonable (two weeks) notice. The Tribunal finds that the applicant's resignation was in writing and addressed to Mr O'Leary, the State Director of the DSS, was dated 12 April 1990, and gave notice of resignation on 30 April 1990 (T5/30, file A):

"I wish to tender my resignation effective from close of business

Monday, April 30th, 1990.

The principal reason for my resignation is a desire to free myself from

the shackles of bureaucracy and to develop my entrepreneurial skills.

I would have felt much more comfortable about my future had a

redundancy package been offered, but the absence of such will no doubt

drive me to greater endeavour.

Please arrange for the Personnel Section to process my termination

payment (Recreation Leave, Leave Bonus and Long Service Leave

entitlements) and credit it to my Credit Union account with C.P.S.

In relation to my superannuation payout, I would like written

confirmation from Central Office advising my entitlement (both gross

and net) under the present scheme and my entitlement under the new

scheme.

It is not without some regret that I leave the Department.

I wish you well in the future.

Regards,

Bruce

Signed Bruce Marshall"

The Tribunal finds that Mr O'Leary was an officer nominated to accept resignations, as were Mr Cameron and Ms Baulderstone (R3).

12. The guidelines further provided that the resignation be accepted by a person with the authority to accept resignations, and that once formally accepted, the resignation was binding on both parties and could not be withdrawn or varied. The applicant submitted that Mr O'Leary had not accepted his resignation despite the notation on it by Ms Baulderstone that it had been so accepted (T5/30, file A).

13. Mr O'Leary told the Tribunal that he did not accept or reject the applicant's resignation and that he passed the letter on to other officers with the delegation to accept resignations. Mr Cameron said that the practice in the DSS was that the person to whom the letter was addressed, accepted the resignation. He said that Mr O'Leary was the highest officer in the SA office of the DSS; next were the deputy directors and below them the area managers, one of whom was MrCameron. Below him, was the applicant as the manager of a regional office. In his opinion, the officer who would have accepted the applicant's resignation would have been at or above his level of seniority. Mr Cameron recalled a telephone conversation from Mr O'Leary when they discussed the applicant's resignation, and Mr Cameron believed that Mr O'Leary was going to accept the resignation. Mr Cameron could not recall telling Ms Baulderstone that Mr O'Leary had accepted the resignation, but thought it was "probably what did happen" (transcript p 204). The Tribunal accepts the evidence of Mr Cameron and Ms Baulderstone as a more accurate account of what transpired, than that given by Mr O'Leary. We find that the resignation of the applicant from the Commonwealth Public Service was accepted by Mr O'Leary.

14. The applicant submitted that he should have been counselled before the resignation was accepted, as provided in the guidelines. Mr Cameron gave evidence that he had discussed alternative possibilities of the applicant taking leave instead of resigning, and had also discussed the viability of the applicant leaving to take up consulting work, but that the applicant's mind was made up. The Tribunal accepts this evidence and finds that the applicant was counselled prior to the resignation being accepted by Mr O'Leary.

15. The guidelines have the status of policy only - they are not statutory instruments. The Public Service Act, 1922, ("the Public Service Act") and the Regulations and Determinations made thereunder, and the General Conditions of Service Australian Government Employment Award 1985, ODNC No. 1831, 1984 ("the Award") do not address voluntary resignation. The Tribunal accepts that the guidelines were appropriate to use in the applicant's case, and finds that they were followed.

16. The Tribunal is of the view that the following requirements need to be met for a valid notice to terminate an employment contract:

1) clear specifications, either oral or written of the intent to

resign and of when the termination is to occur: Morton Suntour Fabrics

v. Shaw (1967) 2 ITR 84;

2) receipt by the person to whom it is directed: Riordan v. War

Office (1959) 1 WLR 1046, 1054-1055; and

3) an unconditional notice, or conditions that are satisfied: McGrane

v. Ryan (1965) AILR 83. The Tribunal finds that these requirements

were met and on these alone, policy guidelines aside, the applicant

gave a valid notice to the DSS to terminate his employment contract.

17. The Tribunal accepts that a notice of termination of a contract is a unilateral right, and does not require the consent of the other party to the contract as was pointed out by Gray J. in Birrell v. Australian National Airlines Commission (1984) 5 FCR 447, at 457:

"The giving of notice of termination of a contract, in accordance with

the terms of that contract, is a unilateral right. Its exercise does

not depend in any way on the acceptance or rejection of the notice by

the other party to the contract. The giving of such a notice operates

to determine the contract by effluxion of the period of notice."

The effect of the applicant's notice was therefore to bring his employment contract to an end when the notice expired on 30 April 1990, and the Tribunal so finds.

18. The Tribunal finds that sometime within the first week after leaving his employment, the applicant considered that he had made a mistake and communicated this to Mr Cameron on 4 May 1990. On 25 May he contacted Mr Reiger, the Deputy Director of Corporate Services, and told him that he had "made the wrong decision and wanted to return to work" (transcript p 81). He then had several discussions with Mr O'Leary about returning to the DSS. On 29 June 1990, the applicant wrote to Mr Cameron and advised him that he would like to rescind his resignation and substitute the time off work with "recreation leave, furlough and leave without pay" (T7/36, file A). Mr Cameron, on the same day, noted on the letter that he had approved the withdrawal of the applicant's resignation. The applicant returned to the DSS on 9 July 1990.

19. The applicant submitted that his resignation was withdrawn when he went to speak with Mr Cameron on 4 May 1990, and further, following discussions with the DSS personnel section, his letter of 29 June 1990, withdrawing his resignation, was accepted by Mr Cameron on the same day. The applicant contended that the effect of the acceptance of this withdrawal was to reinstate the original contract of employment between the applicant and the DSS.

20. The applicant further submitted that common law principles applied to this situation as there was no authority under the Public Service Act or other statutory instruments for a withdrawal of resignation. He cited an article by G.J. McCarry, "Termination of Employment Contracts by Notice" (1986) 60 ALJ 78, which referred to withdrawal of resignations and discussed Emery v. Commonwealth of Australia [1963] VicRp 79; (1963) 5 FLR 209 in support of a proposition that withdrawal of a resignation once accepted reinstated the original contract of employment.

21. The facts before the Tribunal are that the applicant's resignation was accepted, he left the DSS, and he asked for it to be withdrawn after the expiry of the period specified in the notice (i.e. after 30 April 1990). Emery, however, concerned the retrenchment of a Commonwealth employee, and subsequent withdrawal of the retrenchment before the notice became effective. The employee did not consent to this withdrawal and the issue for decision was whether he could still be considered retrenched, having declined to accept the withdrawal of the retrenchment. Pape J. was asked to consider the effect on the contract of employment if the employee had accepted the withdrawal. He considered a number of conflicting authorities and said that his preferred view was that the original contract would continue. This view did not constitute the 'ratio' of Emery but, in any event, that case is clearly distinguishable on the facts. The Tribunal is not persuaded by the applicant's submission and is of the view that the applicant's employment, in fact, ended at the end of the period of notice on 30 April 1990, when he left the DSS.

22. The applicant made an additional submission that the employment contract could somehow be created retrospectively, after the applicant's written withdrawal was accepted by Mr Cameron on 29 June 1990 (T7/36, file A). The Tribunal knows of no authority for such a proposition and is of the view that the contract could not be so created. The Tribunal notes that reinstatements of employees who have left employment under the original contract of employment are provided for by statute in specific circumstances. The applicant's situation is not one of these.

23. The applicant further submitted that he should be allowed to withdraw his resignation because he was in a highly emotional state when he resigned. He said that he had conflicts with Mr Cameron over a transfer to another position, his wife was expecting a baby, and his mother required nursing home care. The authority for this proposition is Martin v. Yeoman Aggregates Ltd (1983) ICR 134, where the director of the employer company was involved in a heated argument with an employee. He told the employee that he had been dismissed. Five minutes later the director had calmed down and retracted the dismissal, but the employee insisted that he had been dismissed and pursued statutory remedies for unjust dismissal. The Employment Appeal Tribunal held that it was possible to withdraw the words of dismissal immediately the director realised that he had acted in anger.

24. In the present case, the Tribunal finds that the applicant had given much consideration to his resignation over some time. He had discussed it with Ms Baulderstone and Mr Cameron. His situation is more like that of the plaintiff in Birrell's case (supra) where Gray J. confined Martin's case (supra) to words spoken in the heat of the moment, and found that the plaintiff took a considered step of submitting his notice of resignation in writing, after a weekend of deliberation. The Tribunal found the applicant's evidence unconvincing as to his emotional state at the time of resignation. In our view, his resignation was a considered and planned course of action which was not taken in the heat of the moment. We find that the applicant's emotional state at the time of his resignation was not such as attract the principles enunciated in Martin (supra). The Tribunal finds that at the time the applicant left his employment with the DSS, he fully intended to do so with the intention of establishing a business advising people about their pension rights. The Tribunal therefore finds that, although the applicant may have been somewhat upset by his situation, his resignation was still effective.

25. The applicant further submitted that Turner v. Australasian Coal and Shale Employees' Federation and Anor [1984] FCA 275; (1984) 55 ALR 635, was authority to support the contract of employment existing after the employment relationship between the applicant and the DSS had ended. Turner's case (supra) concerned an employee who, although hired by a colliery, was not given any work, and sought to become a member of the Federation. The court held that until the colliery finally gave him notice that the contract was at an end, there was a contract of employment in existence. The present case is one of cessation of a contract of employment by agreement, and consequent re-employment, and Turner's case (supra) is clearly distinguishable.

26. Having reached the conclusion that the contract of employment had come to an end, we find that the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee for the purposes of s.3(3) of the Act.

27. The Tribunal now turns to whether or not the applicant again became an eligible employee for the purposes of s.3(3) of the Act. "Eligible employee" is relevantly defined by s.3(1) of the Act as follows:

"3. (1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears -

...

'eligible employee' means -

(a) ...;

(b) a person who is a permanent employee;

(c) a person who is a temporary employee and is specified in a

direction given under section 11, 12 or 13;

(d) ...;

(e) a person -

(i) who is a temporary employee;

(ii) ...; or

(iii) ...;

and who, immediately before the commencement of the employment

by reason of which he is such a temporary employee or his

appointment to that office, was an eligible employee by virtue

of another paragraph of this definition or a previous

application of this paragraph;

...

'permanent employee' means -

(a) a person who is an officer for the purposes of the Public

Service Act; and

(b) any other person employed by the Commonwealth or by an approved

authority in a permanent capacity,

but does not include a part-time employee who is not an approved

part-time employee;

...

'temporary employee' means -

(a) a person who is an employee for the purposes of the Public

Service Act; and

(b) any other person employed by the Commonwealth or by an approved

authority otherwise than in a permanent capacity,

but does not include -

(c) a part-time employee who is not an approved part-time employee;

or

(d) a person who is engaged or appointed for employment outside

Australia only, not being a person who is included in a class

of persons approved by the Minister, in writing, for the

purposes of this definition;"

28. A "permanent employee" under s.3(1) of the Act is either an officer for the purposes of the Public Service Act, or someone employed permanently. In 1965 the applicant had been appointed to the Public Service under s.42(1). His appointment was confirmed in February 1966 (R2, folio 18). "Officer" for the purposes of the Public Service Act, is defined as "a person appointed ....to the service ... but does not include an employee": s.7(1) officers are appointed to the Public Service under s.42 and reappointed under s.47B(1). There is no evidence before the Tribunal that the applicant has been re-appointed to the Public Service, and the Tribunal finds that he is not an officer for the purposes of the Public Service Act. The Tribunal concurs with the legal opinion from the Office of General Counsel at the Attorney-General's Department, that the applicant could under s.47B(1) be re-appointed with effect from 9 July 1990 (T19/76, file A).

29. The respondent appeared to concede that the applicant was ostensibly employed permanently after his return to work on 9 July 1990 and this would bring him within the status of "any other person employed by the Commonwealth in a permanent capacity" for s.3(1)(b) of the Act. However, the Tribunal notes that the only way that the applicant can be employed is under the Public Service Act. Employees can be engaged by the Secretary of a Department (Division 10) and s.26(1) provides for the Secretary to delegate the appropriate powers. The applicant could therefore be engaged by a delegate of the Secretary as an employee. However, no evidence of this was placed before the Tribunal. For the above reasons, the Tribunal is unable to find that the applicant is a permanent employee under the provisions of s.3(1) of the Act. The same situation arises with respect to whether it can be said that the applicant is a "temporary employee" for the purposes of the definition of "eligible employee" contained in s.3(1) of the Act. The Tribunal is of the view, for the same reasons outlined above, that the applicant cannot be said to be a temporary employee. See also paragraph 18. of these reasons.

30. The Tribunal is of the view that the agreement to the applicant's withdrawal of his resignation by Mr Cameron (T7/36, file A) is not an agreement to create another contract of employment with the applicant. As referred to above, there was no evidence before the Tribunal to show that any appointment or engagement of the applicant had been made by the relevant delegate of the Secretary of the DSS, or by the Public Service Board under s.47B(1) of the Public Service Act. The DSS purported to employ the applicant but failed to exercise any of the clear statutory powers referred to above. Its failure to do so can lead only to a result similar to that in Chapman v. Australian Federal Police Commissioner (1983) 76 FLR 428. There it was held that under the statute there was no power to appoint by contract, and the Police Commissioner could not enter into such an arrangement, despite the appearance of all the "trappings of offer and acceptance" in discussions between the Commissioner and the plaintiff, a prospective appointee. Similary, this Tribunal finds that under the Public Service Act, the DSS had no power merely to accept the applicant's withdrawal of his resignation and re-employ him as if there had been a common law contract of employment because the said Act provided specifically for appointment and employment.

31. It appears from an examination of the correspondence in the "T" documents that nothing has been done to re-appoint Mr Marshall as an officer of the Commonwealth Public Service. The Tribunal accepts that under s.47B(1) of the Public Service Act, a delegate of the Public Service Commissioner can reappoint Mr Marshall to the service with effect from the date Mr Marshall recommenced duty in the DSS, viz 9 July 1990. There is no evidence before the Tribunal, or indeed before the respondent, that this has been done. The applicant's evidence before the Tribunal on this matter was as follows (transcript p 147):

"Q. ... Just another matter. In terms of - have you been

reappointed?

A. Not to my knowledge.

Q. So your employment at the moment is a physical employment in the

sense that you have been with the department since 9 July 1990 but

there has been no appointment of a retrospective nature at all?

A. No. Allan Williams again has told me that all he has to do, all the

personnel officer has to do is sign an instrument of reappointment and

I would have been reappointed. I believe that is what the case is but

---

Q. Have you been making - I mean, you are being paid, no doubt?

A. Yes, I have been paid. I have had the same Australian government

service number on my pay slip. They have been withholding the same

amount to the superannuation each fortnight. They have been

withholding the same amount to the ---

Q. What, as before ---?

A. Yes.

Q. Before you left your employment?

A. Yes."

leave purposes is held by the Secretary only.

Mike Kennedy advised that it might be prudent to hold off the process

until the legal action has been finalised, - and the above delegation

is only held by the Secretary. (Signed) 19/12

AAT hearing still pending (Signed) 7/4/92."

From that, the Tribunal must conclude that no re-appointment has been made. We note from the "T" documents that the applicant "applied to become a member of the PSS Scheme until this issue is resolved" (T3/24, file B). The Tribunal finds that the applicant does not meet the description of "eligible employee" and accordingly, has not again become an eligible employee pursuant to s.3(3) of the Act.

33. If the Tribunal has erred, and the applicant could be found to have again become an eligible employee, we now consider whether this took place "immediately after so ceasing" for the purposes of s.3(3) of the Act. The meaning of "immediately after so ceasing" was considered by the Full Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 28 ALR 293. The provision operated to deem continuity of a person's status as an eligible employee, provided the gap in time was not unreasonably large (per Lockhart J., pp 314-315):

"Mr Phillips QC contended that sub-s (3) has no application to the

present case, where the respondent never ceased to be a permanent

employee, because of the principle as to a day being an indivisible

unit of time. He contended that sub-s (3) applies to cases where a

person ceases to be an eligible employee and again becomes one,

provided he does so 'immediately' thereafter. He contended that

'immediately' does not necessarily mean instanter; but rather has a

meaning which may vary from case to case, sometimes allowing a gap in

time of hours, days or weeks. In sub-s (3) it has the meaning of

'within a reasonably short space of time' in all the circumstances. In

support of his contention, Mr Phillips QC referred to the definition of

the words 'immediately' and 'forthwith' in Strouds Judicial Dictionary

and Butterworth's Words and Phrases Legally Defined; Permanent Trustee

Co of New South Wales Ltd v. d'Apice [1968] HCA 29; (1986) ALR 437; 118 CLR 105, and

Dorsman v. Nichol (1978) 20 ALR 231.

Mr Phillips QC contended that sub-s (3) is a deeming provision merely

used for more abundant caution to put beyond doubt a particular

construction that otherwise might be uncertain. He relied on St Aubyn

v. Attorney-General [1951] UKHL 3; (1952) AC 15, per Lord Radcliffe at 53 and Hunter

Douglas Australia Pty Ltd v. Perma Blinds [1970] HCA 63; (1969) ALR 676; 122 CLR 49,

per Windeyer J. at 65-7.

A deeming provision may be included in an interpretation section in a

Statute for the purpose of extending the meaning of a defined term to

something it would not mean in ordinary parlance: see St Aubyn v.

Attorney-General, supra, and Hunter Douglas Australia Pty Ltd v. Perma

Blinds, supra, at the pages mentioned earlier. In my opinion this is

the purpose of sub-s (3). It is intended to ensure that a person does

not lose his status as an eligible employee merely because he ceases to

be one, and later becomes one again, provided the gap in time is not

unreasonably large. It is designed to cover all cases where a person

has a particular position or holds a particular office and ceases to do

so, but 'immediately' thereafter takes another position or office, in

each case being a person who satisfies the statutory denotation of a

'permanent employee'. It matters not whether the interval of time is a

second, a day, or a week, provided the person again becomes an eligible

employee 'immediately' after ceasing to be one. ...

It follows that if the respondent had elected to be appointed to the

Public Service, he would have ceased to be an eligible employee upon

the abolition of his position or office and immediately thereafter

again become an eligible employee upon his being appointed to the

Public Service, and therefore deemed not to have ceased to be an

eligible employee."

This approach was adopted by the Tribunal in Re Rattray and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 2 ALD 979, where the applicant's office was legislatively abolished and one day elapsed between this and his commencement with Telecom. He had been appointed to the position with Telecom some two weeks beforehand. The Tribunal held that the break was very short, and that the applicant had commenced his new employment "immediately" after ceasing his other employment. In Re Rowe and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 216, the applicant's position as a full-time member of the Repatriation Review Tribunal was abolished and he was appointed as a part-time member of the Veterans' Review Board. After numerous delays, he was appointed some 71days later as a full-time member. The Tribunal said that he had not become an eligible employee "immediately after" ceasing to be one because the gap of 71 days between full-time employment was too great to be regarded as short. In Re Wood and Commissioner for Superannuation (1989) 9 AAR 528, the Tribunal found that the applicant had again become an "eligible employee" immediately after ceasing to be one after a break of one working week. The Tribunal considered in that case that the applicant's stated intention to engage in a full-time consultancy was not genuine; he had arranged to take up another offer of employment before his resignation and this was complete except for a formal written offer. At the date of resignation, he had intended at all times to go straight on to this new employment well before he left his former employment or took any steps towards establishing his consultancy.

34. The Tribunal finds that the applicant intended to resign on 30 April 1990, and that his employment from 9 July 1990 "as if" he were an eligible employee does not make him one. In any event, even were he to have been reappointed on 9 July 1990, this period between resignation and reappointment is too long to constitute "immediately after" for the purposes of s.3(3) of the Act. This section is (as the Full Federal Court said in Bayley supra) to avoid the problem of a person who, as a permanent employee, relinquishes a particular office, and "immediately" after takes up another position or office as a permanent employee. The present case in no way would allow for a construction of "immediately after so ceasing" with respect to the applicant's re-employment by the DSS, and accordingly we find that s.3(3) does not apply to the applicant.

35. The decision of the respondent that s.3(3) of the Act does not apply is affirmed on the basis that the applicant has not "again become an eligible employee".

36. We now turn to consider the second decision under review in which the respondent allowed a period of 14 days from receipt of notification of acceptance of the applicant's late election in which to repay the sum of $44,737.75, being the lump sum benefit which the applicant was previously entitled to under the provisions of the Act.

37. The Tribunal considers at the outset that the refusal of the RBO in July 1990 to accept the applicant's cheque refunding the lump sum was inappropriate at that time. The RBO was aware that the applicant had resumed his Public Service career and should, in our view, have accepted the cheque and put in train the necessary steps to help the applicant take advantage of the "preservation" provisions of the Act. We appreciate that we reach this conclusion with the benefit of hindsight but there is a wealth of experience in these matters in the RBO, and it seems to us not unreasonable to expect that the course of action we suggest should have been implemented. We note that with the help of the RBO these steps were put in train some months later.

38. The Tribunal makes the following findings of fact:

On 26 July 1990 the bank cheque payable to "RPM Retirement Benefit

Office" for $44,315.81 was returned to the applicant by the Personnel

Section, DSS, with advice to the effect that he should invest it.

On 3 August 1990 the applicant signed a contract to purchase a town

house unit at Foundry Street, Goodwood for $150,000. Settlement on

this contract was completed on 28 September 1990 and the purchase price

was provided by means of a first mortgage for $80,000 and the balance

viz $76,010.90 in cash from the applicant's own resources but including

the $44,737.75 refund of superannuation contributions.

On 17 September 1990 the respondent advised the State Personnel

Officer of the DSS that s.3(3) of the Act could not be held to apply on

the grounds that "a nine week break between cessation of employment and

reinstatement is substantial enough for the 'immediacy' element in Mr

Marshall's case to be non existent". In this same letter, the

respondent invited the applicant (through the Personnel Officer, DSS)

to lodge a late election to preserve his benefits under the Act and to

submit reasons why the Commissioner should, pursuant to s.157(1), allow

the late election to be recognised.

On 1 October 1990 the applicant applied to preserve all of his

superannuation rights under the Act (T10/42, file A) and in a letter

dated 12October 1990 addressed to the respondent, he set out his

reasons (T12/49, file A).

At or about the same time, the applicant sought a reconsideration of the decision made under s.3(3) and asked for a statement of reasons under s.13 Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act, 1977. These were provided but resulted in a delayed consideration of the request for late election to preserve the benefits. The respondent sought the advice of the Crown Solicitor on the resignation aspect of the applicant's case and on 21 November 1990, advised the applicant that he would consider the late preservation application independently of the reconsideration issue.

On 18 April 1991 the respondent advised the applicant by letter that

his late election to preserve his benefits would be accepted. He was

requested to refund the lump sum of $44,737.75 within 14 days of the

receipt of the letter. The Tribunal notes that in making this

decision, the respondent was unaware of the fact that the applicant had

invested the money in real estate. The readiness on the part of the

applicant to refund the money on 3 July 1990 may have indicated to the

respondent that money was held in readily available form.

39. Section 140(2) states:

"140 (2) Where -

(a) a person referred to in subsection 137(1) has been paid -

(i) in a case where the person is a person to whom paragraph (a)

of that subsection applies - an amount by way of lump sum

benefit to which the person has become entitled under section

80 of Part VIA; or

(ii)...

(b) after that amount was, or those amounts were, paid, the person

makes an election under section 137,

the election does not have effect unless an amount equal to that

amount or to the aggregate of those amounts, as the case requires,

is paid to the Commissioner within 7 days after the date of the

election or within such further period as the Commissioner, in

special circumstances allows." (emphasis added)

The Commissioner allowed a period of 14 days from the receipt of the letter dated 18 April 1991 but did not say what special circumstances existed for doing so. In this matter, the recognition of the late election had to be decided and it was not so decided until 9 April 1991. In reaching his decision that the late election under s.137(1) should be allowed, the Commissioner concluded (T3/25, file B):

"11. It is noted that Mr Marshall requested written details of his

superannuation entitlement under both CSS and PSS in the text of

his letter of resignation. It appears that the Department of

Social Security did nothing in connection with this request as the

note 'applied for refund therefore this does not apply' is

hand-written in the margin next to the request.

12. Under these circumstances it would appear that Mr Marshall did

not receive any additional information that would have altered his

understanding of the options available to him as presented on the

Form S2R. Consequently grounds exist for consideration of his late

election under subsection 157(1) of the Act."

The election was signed on 1 October 1990 and 7 days had clearly passed. The Commissioner apparently considered these to be circumstances special enough to justify payment of the money "within 14 days of receipt of notification of acceptance of his late election" (T3/25, file B). On the assumption that the money was readily available, we consider that was a proper decision.

On 29 April 1991 the applicant advised the respondent that the money

had been invested in realty and was not readily available. There

followed numerous letters between the parties that finally led to an

appeal being lodged with this Tribunal for review of the decision under

s.3(3). The applicant in the meantime had approached the Ombudsman and

copies of correspondence between the parties were sent to that

official. All of this added to the delay in finalising the issue. In

a letter dated 1 May 1991, the applicant sought an extension of time

for 3 years, presumably to 1 May 1994 to repay the sum involved.

On 16 July 1991 the respondent advised the applicant that "As the

period ..... allowed for repayment has elapsed your election to

preserve ..... is deemed to be ineffective".

On 16 September 1991 the applicant lodged his appeal with this

Tribunal against the decision that s.3(3) could not apply to him.

40. It soon became apparent that if this matter was to be settled properly, both the s.3(3) decision and the decision under s.140(2) of the respondent in allowing the applicant 14 days after receipt of notification of acceptance of his late election to repay the sum in question, must be considered together. However, the latter decision had not been reconsidered. The applicant sought the reconsideration on 17 December 1991. On 5 May 1992, a delegate of the respondent reconsidered the decision and confirmed it (T2/2, file B).

41. The reconsideration delegate concluded "I have not been able to identify any circumstances in his case which would justify me granting him a further period of any duration beyond the period of 14 days allowed by a delegate in his decision of 3 April 1991".

In reaching this conclusion, the delegate undertook a very thorough examination of the phrase "special circumstances" and of the decisions of the Federal Court and of this Tribunal on that phrase. He referred to: Re Beadle and Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; (1984) 1 AAR 362, Re Anderson and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1985) 8 ALN N271, Re Brisley and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1985) 8 ALN N257, Re Game and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (S90/42 of 1990), which were decisions of the Tribunal, and to Beadle and Others v. Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; (1985) 60 ALR 225, a decision of the Full Federal Court.

42. At paragraph 15 of his reconsideration, the delegate summarised these decisions as follows:

"15. In summary, I consider that the following general principles in

relation to the expression can be adduced from the decisions of the AAT

and the Federal Court as referred to above:

.it is not possible to lay down precise limits or rules as to what

constitutes 'special circumstances'. All of the circumstances of the

individual case must be looked at in determining whether special

circumstances exist;

.the discretion must be exercised in a way that is consistent with the

ends or objects of the legislation;

.the period of time allowed in the enactment is relevant, and was

considered sufficient by the legislature in the normal case;

.'special circumstances' warranting an extension of time must include

events which would render the period allowed as unfair or

inappropriate. Relevant factors here include the extent of the

applicant's knowledge of the relevant provisions, the adequacy of the

information conveyed to the applicant, the diligence of the applicant

and whether the circumstances were of the applicant's own making.

.in the case of lengthy delay, weighty facts would be required to

establish special circumstances."

With respect we endorse that summary.

43. The Tribunal finds that the applicant had other real estate in South Australia in which he estimated he had an equity of between approximately $62,000 and $100,000 depending on the market. The Foundry Street unit is rented out at $180.00 per week and has an equity on the above figures of $72,000 approximately.

44. We find that the applicant contributed to the difficulty in which he now finds himself by investing the money in realty when he was still pursuing his rights under the Act, on the basis that he had not resigned. Such a belief would surely imply that once settled, the money must be paid back. But should this mistake cost him the accumulated benefits of 25 years contributory service when, if properly handled in the first place, it would not have occurred. We think not.

45. In a recent decision, Re Vichie and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority V91/827, 24 July 1992 (unreported), the Tribunal said:

"The phrase 'special circumstances' was considered by the Tribunal in

the context of the Social Security Act 1947 in Re Ivovic and

Director-General of Social Services [1981] AATA 57; 3 ALN N95. The Tribunal said at

(N96-97):

"Whilst it would be unwise, if not impossible, to attempt to lay down

any precise delineation of what may amount to 'special circumstances'

for the purpose of s.115(4A) (cf Re Norman (1886) 16 QBD 673 at 677 per

Lopes L.J.), the use of the word 'special' is, we think, intended to

allow the decision-maker the fullest opportunity to consider the

particular circumstances of each case."

In Re Beadle and Director-General of Social Security 6 ALD 1 at 3 the Tribunal said:

"An expression such as 'special circumstances' is by its very nature

incapable of precise or exhaustive definition. The qualifying

adjective looks to circumstances that are unusual, uncommon or

exceptional. Whether circumstances answer any of these descriptions

must depend upon the context in which they occur. For it is the

context which allows one to say that the circumstances in one case are

markedly different from the usual run of cases. This is not to say

that the circumstances must be unique but they must have a particular

quality of unusualness that permits them to be described as special."

On appeal [1984] AATA 176; (60 ALR 225) the Full Federal Court in dismissing the appeal said at 228:

"As we have seen the Director-General in acting under s.102(1) is

concerned with the period between the date a claimant became eligible

and the time when the claim was lodged. The legislature has indicated

that six months latitude is sufficient in the normal case. The

Director-General has power to fix a longer period in special

circumstances. Presumably in this context special circumstances must

include events which would render the six months unfair or

inappropriate. For example, where the delay beyond six months was due

to the claimant's being misled by a departmental officer or was due to

the negligence of a third party it might be thought the normal six

months would be inappropriate; that special circumstances had been

shown which warranted a longer period. More difficult would be

questions of ignorance, illiteracy, isolation, illness and the like.

It would depend upon the circumstances of the particular case whether

these constituted special circumstances. We do not think it is

possible to lay down precise limits or precise rules. The matter is

one for the Director-General bearing in mind the purpose for which the

power is given. The phrase 'special circumstances', although lacking

precision, is sufficiently understood in our view not to require

judicial gloss." (emphasis added)

46. In the context of s.140(2) of the Act, it is necessary to consider the period provided by the legislation for repayment of the lump sum and to consider whether the particular facts of the matter have the element of "special" as to justify any extension.

47. The Tribunal is of the opinion, having regard to the circumstances of this particular case, that there are circumstances which are unusual enough to be called special viz:

. The incorrect impression conveyed to the applicant by DSS that

his resignation could be rescinded, after he had left his

employment.

. The "employment" of the applicant by the DSS in the same or a

similar position as that which he occupied on resignation.

. The attempt by the applicant to refund the lump sum as early as

3 July 1990.

. The refusal of the respondent to accept the refund.

. The lack of confirmation given to the applicant as to his rights

under the CSS and PSS as he requested in his letter of

resignation.

48. It does seem to the Tribunal that the respondent has gone out of the way to assist the applicant to retain the benefit of his past service. Firstly, in inviting the late election to preserve the benefit. Secondly, when in the reconsideration of the decision under s.3(3) of the Act, it made its "observations" (T2/17 - file A) of the present position of the preservation application and finally in the reconsideration of the decision under s.140(2) of the Act when it did not vary the original decision.

49. The original decision-maker was not aware that the money had been invested in real estate and the 14 days allowed may have seemed appropriate. The Tribunal now has all the facts before it. In Freeman v. Secretary, Department of Social Security [1988] FCA 294; 15 ALD 671, 673 Davies J. said:

"In Jebb's case, I approved the general approach of the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal '.... to regard the administrative

decision-making process as a continuum and to look upon the

Tribunal's function as a part of that continuum so that, within the

limits of the reconsideration of the decision under review, the

Tribunal considers the applicant's entitlement from the date of

application or other proper commencing date to the date of the

Tribunal's decision."

50. The Tribunal is charged with making the correct and preferable decision on the facts as they are found in evidence before it - Re Drake and the Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60. The preferable decision is that the applicant be given adequate time to raise the money and pay it back to the Commissioner. Fourteen days would, in our view, be unfair and inappropriate. So indeed, would be the 3 years (now less than 2 years) requested by the applicant. The applicant has sufficient assets upon which to raise the necessary funds by way of bridging finance. Such matters take time, valuations must be obtained and loans approved. It is not unreasonable to expect that these matters can be arranged within 28 days.

51. Accordingly, the decision of this Tribunal is that the decision of the respondent made pursuant to s.140(2) will be set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the applicant will be allowed a further period of 28 days from the date of this decision in which to repay the sum of $44,737.75, being the amount of the lump sum benefit which the applicant was previously entitled to under the provisions of the Act.

52. As previously indicated, the decision of this Tribunal regarding the respondent's decision made pursuant to s.3(3) is that it is affirmed.
