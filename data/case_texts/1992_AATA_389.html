Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 389; (1992) 24 ATR 1205 (23 December 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

RE: TAXATION APPEALS

No. NT92/29

AAT No. 8459

Number of pages - 14

Income Tax

[1992] AATA 389; (1992) 24 ATR 1205

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B J McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - undisclosed interest income - unexplained deposits - failure to disclose consultancy fees - alleged proceeds of gambling winnings - whether amounts constituted assessable income - whether additional tax correctly imposed

Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) s 226(2)

Income Tax Ruling 2517

HEARING

SYDNEY

23:12:1992

ORDER

1. The Tribunal varies the objection decisions under review by altering the method of calculating additional tax to an application of a per annum rate of 14.026 per cent and a culpability component of 50 per cent.

2. In all other respects the objection decisions are affirmed.

DECISION

As a result of information held within the Australian Taxation Office, enquiries were made of the applicant in relation to undisclosed interest income. As he failed to supply the information requested, his affairs were then subject to an audit. At the conclusion of the audit, the commissioner included in the applicant's assessable income further amounts of $83,462, $47,055, $8,063, $39,425 and $14,613 in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1986 respectively. Additional tax in terms of sub-section 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act was also assessed but was subsequently reduced to amounts of $75,914, $39,369, $4,780, $19,885 and $5,959 for each of the respective years.

2. As a result of negotiations, certain agreed positions emerged. The applicant agreed that the amounts of interest omitted from each of his returns and which were subsequently shown to have been derived by him were assessable income. The understatements of interest were quite gross. In 1982, the amount disclosed was $116, whereas the final amount established was $7,419-97. In 1983, the figures were $124 and $2,603-65, in 1984 $12 and $7,359-14, in 1985 $81 and $18,818-38. The first explanation given for failure to disclose this interest emerged from a letter from the accountants he instructed in 1987 to assist him. On his instruction, they said -

"In the course of obtaining the most favourable returns and in

administering his investment port folio the taxpayer

transferred large amounts of his capital from one account to

another, from interest to non-interest bearing accounts, from

regular cheque accounts to commercial bill accounts, and the

like (appendix 'B'). These movements, while carried out for

purely commercial and general rationalisation reasons, had the

unintended effect of clouding the waters as to what interest

was derived and from what source or sources."

However when I asked him to give an explanation for failure to disclose interest, he said in evidence that he did not disclose it as he thought there would be a query as to the origin of the capital. He said, "if you start declaring interest investigations could start".

3. The amounts of interest were derived from deposits which were in the applicant's name. There is no suggestion that fictitious names were used in relation to these accounts.

4. The first matter in issue concerns unexplained deposits of $8,089 and $3,704 in the 1985 and 1986 years. The applicant had failed to disclose consultancy fees paid to him by the service company of a firm of solicitors, totalling $7,600. He maintained during the audit, and in evidence before me, that the unexplained deposits included these sums and that there had been double counting. No further evidence was put before me to substantiate this allegation. Against this was evidence from an officer of the Australian Taxation Office, who swore that all the amounts which were particularised, and which were undoubtedly consultancy fees, had been vouched. Where any of these fees coincided with any sums comprising the deposits they were excluded from the list of unidentified deposits. I have no reason to reject this evidence, particularly as no detailed evidence was tendered by the applicant on this subject. In my view he has not discharged his onus of proving that the two sums of $8,089 and $3,704 should not have been included as part of his assessable income in the 1985 and 1986 years.

5. The principal matter in issue concerned three deposits, two of $52,000 and $23,000 in the 1982 year and one of $41,528-24 in the 1983 year. The applicant alleged that they were the proceeds of gambling winnings and were therefore not assessable income.

6. In evidence before me, he said that he had met a man, whom I will call M1, at the St George Leagues Club late in 1980. The meeting developed into a friendship. The applicant believed that M1 worked in the United States of America in a computer environment. He said that when his friend was in Sydney, he would call him and they would have lunch together. Early in January 1981, he said the discussion turned to the possibility of gambling and, in particular, to winning at blackjack in casinos. M1 told him that he had a system of his own devising and that he was confident that he could increase a gambling stake ten-fold. The applicant said in evidence that he was dubious at first but later accepted the boast as a challenge.

7. It was arranged that at a subsequent lunch he should give M1 $10,000. He said that he had received this money as a present from his father in 1977 prior to his death. Although the applicant was continually investing in fixed interest securities and moved investments around to obtain a higher rate, he affirmed that the $10,000 had remained in the form of cash in a tin at his home between 1977 and 1981. He said that he gave the money to M1 but did not receive any receipt. It was arranged that M1 should take the money, convert it into United States currency, and gamble with it on the applicant's behalf.

8. The applicant said that he did not hear from M1 again until March 1981 when he returned to Australia. He told the applicant over another lunch that he had won a total of US$65,000 and had converted it to Australian currency which he thereupon presented to the applicant in cash. The applicant produced a bank statement from the Rural Bank of New South Wales, confirming a deposit of $68,061-60 on 13 March 1981. On 10 April 1981, there is a debit entry of $65,000 which the applicant said represents an amount which he placed on deposit from the gambling proceeds. The applicant said that the odd cash amount which constituted the deposit, including coins, came directly from M1.

9. In 1983, the applicant said that he received a telephone call from M1 to say that he would be again coming to Australia on a flight and that he wished to see the applicant. He telephoned him at work and they arranged to meet at a restaurant. At that luncheon, M1 told him that he was having troubles with his family and that he was having problems with his own concentration. He did not want to continue gambling. He told the applicant that he had won more money on his behalf to the equivalent of US$40,000. The applicant said that he had converted this into Australian currency and gave it to him at the luncheon. On 1 February 1983, an amount of $41,528-24 was deposited by the applicant in his account with the Commonwealth Savings Bank. The entry appears on page 6 of a series of statements to which reference will later be made. He said that this amount, including the coins, represented the cash paid to him by M1 at the luncheon.

10. The applicant gave evidence that since that day he has not seen M1 again. He said he had received a few telephone calls from him at work on a social basis. The applicant said he asked M1 how his luck was going but that his friend was "close chested about his other activities". The applicant did not know whether M1 gambled for himself or even whether he performed this service for any other person. Originally he told investigators, when this version of events came to light, that his personal file was in his car which had been stolen from a car park at a railway station and that consequently he did not have the home address and telephone number of M1. He told me that he had never telephoned M1 who, he said, "wanted to be a free agent". Later, however, he said that M1 had moved on a number of occasions between 1984 and 1986 and that it was not possible to keep up with him. He told me in evidence that he had not mentioned the existence of M1 or the related transactions to any members of his family as they disapproved of gambling. He had not even mentioned M1 to his family as a friend without mentioning the gambling transactions. He had not mentioned the arrangements to any other friend at work or elsewhere.

11. The evidence which the applicant gave to me concerning the transactions with M1 was not forthcoming at the beginning of the audit and was not brought forth until quite some time after investigations had started. The applicant said that his reason for this was because his family would disapprove. He gave no explanation for this reason, having regard to the confidential nature of investigations. Later, he told me that he objected to the way the investigator had originally contacted him on the telephone. He said "there was no official letter so they were not going to get the full straight facts". He did not explain why he took this view as he acknowledged he had examined the investigator's identity card and that he, the applicant, had confirmed the appointment for the visit with the investigator by telephoning the investigator's office. The third explanation he gave to me in evidence was that he did not mention M1 because he wished to protect him. When it was put to him that M1 was not under investigation, he said "you don't disclose everything in the first instance".

12. The original standard query letter was sent to the applicant on 11 April 1986. The applicant replied on 15 April 1986 saying that his car had been stolen while it contained a file of personal papers and that he was unable to obtain copies of statements from financial institutions as he did not have sufficient details. He then asked the Commissioner to supply him with account numbers, so that copies of statements could be obtained. The applicant did not deny in evidence that the purpose of the letter was to find out what the Commissioner knew about his investments.

13. The audit commenced on 1 December 1986 and the initial interview took place on 15 December 1986. On that occasion the applicant did not disclose the existence of the Commonwealth Savings Bank account where the $41,528 had been deposited, a term deposit with the State Bank, which was funded in part by the $68,061 he had received from M1, nor the account with Macquarie Investment Management Limited which had its origins in the same source. None of the accounts, in other words, which came into being as a result of the alleged gambling proceeds was disclosed at the first interview.

14. On 5 February 1987, the investigator interviewed the applicant again and referred him to a commercial bill investment of $155,000. The applicant told him that this came from a lump sum termination payment and from the proceeds of the sale of property, and that the remainder came from "cash held at home from personal savings". In fact these sums were later identified by him as the proceeds of gambling. The investigator noted at the interview that the applicant had mentioned the possibility of establishing that the money came from punting on horses. Although the subject of gambling had come up, no mention was made of the existence of M1.

15. On 6 April 1987 the applicant appointed a well known firm of chartered accountants as his agent. The letter of appointment stated that his approach was made "with the intention of making a full and complete statement to the Commissioner". In fact it is clear from the long letter and lengthy schedules attached to the accountants' letter that he did not tell them of the M1 transactions. The schedules attached to the letter (which the applicant (incredibly) said he had not seen) state that $52,000, which was deposited on 9 July 1981, represented savings accumulated over a number of years and retained by the applicant at home. The sum was later identified by the applicant as the first of the M1 dividends.

16. The letter from the accountants contained bank statements from the Commonwealth Savings Bank. There were 9 pages in the series, but page 6 was missing. This covered the period from 13 January to 17 May 1983. A comparison between the closing balance on page 5 and the opening balance on page 7 shows that the balance of the account had increased by $26,934-08 between 13 January and 17 May 1983. It was subsequently established that most of this came from the deposit of $41,528-24 made on 1 February 1983, to which I have previously referred. This was the second M1 dividend.

17. The accountants were asked on 7 separate occasions to supply a copy of page 6 of that bank statement. It was not supplied until some 4 months later on 2 November 1987. The applicant said it had taken a few weeks to get the statement from the bank. No explanation was given for the 4 months' delay.

18. Four days after the missing page was supplied, namely, on 6 November 1987, the alleged existence of M1 came to light for the first time. On 6 November 1987 the investigator telephoned the accountants to arrange a time for a final interview. He was told that the applicant "was to get some information from a friend in America regarding the deposits of $75,000 and $41,528. This information would show the money was not income in the hands of the applicant". The accountant promised to ring the investigator in a few days' time. On 11 November 1987, the accountant told the investigator of his instructions concerning the gambling wins.

19. On 25 November 1987 the investigator asked the applicant for more details which were given in sketchy form. According to a file note made by the investigator at the time, the applicant hesitated for a long time to tell the investigator M1's name. At the investigator's initiative, details were then sought in relation to M1 from the Immigration Department.

20. Evidence was given by an officer of the respondent who had the carriage of this appeal, concerning the way in which further information regarding M1 emerged. At a preliminary conference on 24 February 1992, the applicant was asked to give M1's exact address. He gave the name of a small village in California. In answer to the presiding member's question at that conference he said that he had attempted to contact M1 "daily". When asked whether he had the telephone accounts, he said that he called from public telephone boxes. He added that he intended to go to the United States to find M1 and bring him back to testify on his behalf. This evidence is inconsistent with his evidence that he did not have M1's telephone number and that he had not telephoned him because he "wanted to be a free agent".

21. At the next directions hearing on 16 April 1992 the applicant provided a name and address, although no telephone number was made available. He was directed to provide this telephone number and a proof of evidence from the proposed witness within 4 weeks. Neither piece of information was ever forthcoming. On 20 May 1992, he told the officer that he did not have the proof of evidence but that M1 was shortly coming to Australia and would arrive some time prior to 30 May. He was advised by the officer that there were no Immigration records indicating entry into Australia under the name of M1. He then said that he had found out that the name of his friend under which searches had been made was not correct but that his true name was similar and was either M2 or M3.

22. Two days after the last conversation with the officer when he indicated that M1 would be in Australia in a few days time, he again telephoned to say that his friend had died in a car accident on his way to the airport where he was to have boarded a plane to come to Australia. He said that his friend was in the United States Air Force and was travelling in an Air Force plane. This is why his name had not been recorded by Immigration. He said that his previous visits to Australia had been on a United States Air Force plane as a passenger. This reference to the US Air Force plane was the first reference of that kind. In explanation, the applicant said in evidence that he had not been asked before what means of transport his friend had used to visit Australia.

23. Enquiries made by the Australian Taxation Office resulted in evidence given before me and show that the Royal Australian Air Force could not establish that bomber aircraft of the USAF visited Williamtown (the airport nominated by the applicant) between April and July 1981. A Wing Commander at the same base confirmed to an investigator, that no USAF aircraft were due to visit Australia in May 1992. In fact there had been only 2 landings in the whole of 1992. The first was of a fighter plane which came from an aircraft carrier and the other was another fighter plane from Korea. Neither of these aircraft had facilities for carrying passengers. The applicant had said during the course of earlier interviews that M1 was an Australian citizen who was working in the United States with a green card. In answer to my questions, he said that M1 had a family in Sydney but he had been unable to contact them. The Australian Taxation Office, acting through its agents, established in evidence before me that there were no records of American tax returns in the name of M1, M2 or M3. Nor was there any record of any social security number being issued to any person under any of those names. Through his liaison with the Australian Federal Police, the Commissioner established that there were no fatal road accidents in or about the area in California nominated by the applicant in May 1992 involving any person with the names of M1, M2 or M3.

24. Throughout the proceedings, the applicant gave me the impression that his story changed at every crisis point during the investigation. I was not impressed with the way in which he gave his evidence, finding him evasive and less than frank. He has followed a pattern of concealment throughout the investigation, giving the story upon which he ultimately relied, only piece by piece as and when he thought he could see an appropriate opportunity. His stories concerning suppression of documents, the loss of his personal file and the loss of page 6 of the bank's statement, are quite unbelievable. Indeed without any shred of corroborative evidence to support his whole story concerning M1 and the gambling transactions, the whole version of the origin of the deposits becomes, in my view, quite unbelievable. He has had every opportunity to offer evidence of a probative, convincing nature and has failed to take advantage of any of them. Ultimately, all I am left with is his unconvincing word, uncorroborated in any detail by any external source. I do not accept his evidence. I believe that at the end of the hearing, the situation concerning the deposits of $52,000, $23,000 and $41,528.24 remains the same as it was at the beginning. They are unexplained deposits. The respondent is entitled to include them as part of the applicant's assessable income. The applicant has not discharged the onus of showing that the assessments, so amended, are excessive.

25. There remains the question of penalties. The existing ones imposed in the amended assessments in 1988 contain a per annum component of 20 per cent and a culpability component of 60 per cent. I see no reason why the per annum component should not reflect the normal practice of the respondent. The higher rate was originally imposed to reflect market rates and to compensate the respondent for the loss of revenue over a period. The rate for 1987/88 was 14.026 per cent. Nothing has been put before me which would indicate that a higher rate would be appropriate. I propose to amend the assessments, therefore, to reflect this.

26. The culpability component, in my view, should be imposed at the highest level applicable under Income Tax Ruling 2517. The conduct of the applicant has amounted to deliberate evasion. It is true that the fixed interest deposit accounts were in the applicant's own name. However, no attempt was made to disclose all of them even after the investigation had begun. The amounts of the discrepancies between interest declared and interest earned are very large. The size of the discrepancy itself is an aggravating factor.

27. The applicant has had relevant commercial and practical experience. In evidence, he said that although he had not gained formal qualifications as an accountant, he was known by that profession colloquially. He has worked for a number of companies as financial manager and financial controller. His level of cooperation has been low, although the applicant insisted to the contrary. The view I take is that each piece of relevant information was played by him as a card in a game. The full hand of cards was not shown to the investigator at the commencement of the audit. The fact that the applicant instructed chartered accountants to assemble his case and to present it on his behalf must be viewed as some form of cooperation. However the fact that he deliberately avoided instructing his accountants in matters which he subsequently put forward detract from the level of cooperation which could genuinely be called reasonable.

28. The failure to disclose consultancy fees is inexcusable and no excuse was offered.

29. In all the circumstances the additional tax imposed should be assessed at the rate reflecting a per annum rate generally imposed by the Commissioner from time to time in the respective assessment years. To that should be added additional tax calculated at 50 per cent by way of culpability component. In all other respects the objection decisions are affirmed.
