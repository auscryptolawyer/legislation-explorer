Re Taxation Appeals [1990] AATA 63; 21 ATR 3323 (19 March 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT88/2101 and 2206

AAT No. 5789

Income Tax

[1990] AATA 63; 21 ATR 3323

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Australian business controlled by non-residents s 136 - notional taxable income - assessment pursuant to s 166 - Further assessment pursuant to ss 136 and 169 - whether second assessment amends, replaces or "extinguishes" first assessment - whether Commissioner bound to elect one method of assessment or may issue collateral assessments in respect of same income for a particular financial year - whether mischief of double jeopardy

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s 37

Income Tax Assessment Act ss 31C, 136, 166, 169, 204, 207

Income Tax Rates Act 1986 ss 17, 23, 46(7)

Batagol v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243

FJ Bloemen Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4096

Booth v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4049

Cadbury-Fry-Pascall Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 31; (1944) 70 CLR 362

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Ltd 80 ATC 4371

Hoare v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4042

Lever Bros Pty Ltd v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Commissioner [1948] HCA 25; (1948) 77 CLR 78

Richardson v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 67; (1931-1932) 48 CLR 192

Winter v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4655

HEARING

SYDNEY

19:3:1990

ORDER

(1) The Tribunal continues to have jurisdiction

to consider the applications.

(2) The matters are stood over to 26 April with a

view to fixing a date for hearing.

DECISION

This was a directions hearing called to consider whether the Tribunal continued to have jurisdiction to review the objection decisions originally sought to be reviewed, in the light of the following subsequent circumstances.

2. Assessments were originally issued for 1979 and 1981. They were endorsed "assessed in accordance with s 136 of the Act". An objection to the earlier assessment was allowed in part. An objection to the second assessment was disallowed in full. Application was then made to this Tribunal to review both objection decisions.

3. At the relevant time, s 136 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") was in the following terms -

"s 136 Where any business carried on in Australia -

(a) is controlled principally by non-residents;

(b) is carried on by a company a majority of

the shares in which is held by or on

behalf of non-residents; or

(c) is carried on by a company which holds or

on behalf of which other persons hold a

majority of the shares in a non-resident

company,

and it appears to the Commissioner that the

business produces either no taxable income or less

than the amount of taxable income which might be

expected to arise from that business, the person

carrying on the business in Australia shall,

notwithstanding any other provision of this Act, be

liable to pay income tax on a taxable income of

such amount of the total receipts (whether cash or

credit) of the business as the Commissioner

determines."

4. Section 136 was quite different in its operation from what may be termed the ordinary assessment sections of the Act. Whereas the latter addressed questions of what income should be included and what deductions should be excluded in arriving at taxable income, the former simply empowered the Commissioner to take a proportion of receipts in arriving at taxable income.

5. When this amount is so calculated, the assessment is then issued pursuant to s 169 of the Act, rather than the ordinary assessment s 166. The difference was referred to by Williams J. in Lever Bros Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1948] HCA 25; (1948) 77 CLR 78 at 81 in the following terms -

"The assessments in the second class were of a

different nature. An assessment can only be made

under s 136 where the business carried on in

Australia is a business which is controlled

principally by non-residents or is carried on by a

company having the characteristics therein

mentioned, and it appears to the Commissioner that

the business produces either no taxable income or

less than the amount of taxable income which might

be expected to arise from that business. It is

therefore an assessment based not on the actual

income derived from the business during the year of

income but on a notional income. The section

provides that the person carrying on the business

in Australia shall, notwithstanding any other

provision of the Act, be liable to pay income tax

on a taxation income of such amount of the total

receipts (whether cash or credit) of the business

as the Commissioner determines. An assessment of

the tax payable on this notional taxable income is

not made under s 166, but under s 169. It is an

original assessment made on a different basis

altogether to an ordinary assessment and under a

different section. It is not in any sense an

amendment of an ordinary assessment."

6. From the Commissioner's point of view, the effect of s 136 was considerably weakened by a decision of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Limited 80 ATC 4371. The Court there held that control did not refer to shareholder control, but to actual day-to-day management control. Apparently as a result of that decision, the section was repealed in 1982 and replaced by Division 13 of the Act, dealing with international agreements and determination of source of foreign associated income.

7. As this present application moved closer to a hearing, the respondent reconsidered his position. Presumably he was concerned that the same weaknesses which led to the downfall of the s 136 assessment against Commonwealth Aluminium Corporation Limited would be found to exist in the assessments against the present applicant. In any event, the respondent wrote to the applicant on 27 October 1989 in the following terms (omitting formal parts) -

"(Applicant's name) AAT REFERENCE NOS NT 88/2101 and

2206

Reference is made to the above taxpayer's 1979 and

1981 assessments which are the subject of the

current reference to the AAT.

As shown on the notices of assessment and on

"adjustment sheets" attached thereto, the

assessments were issued pursuant to s 136 of the

Income Tax Assessment Act. Under s 136, the

Commissioner is authorised to determine a notional

taxable income of so much of "the total receipts of

the business". Authority to issue assessments

based on s 136 is contained in s 169. The

assessments, therefore, are separate and distinct

from any assessments based on the Commissioner's

determination of taxable income under the "ordinary

provisions" of the Act, ie, by reference to

assessable income and allowable deductions. The

latter assessments are authorised under s 166 of

the Act.

The distinction between s 166 and s 169 assessments

was clearly recognised in Lever Bros Pty Ltd v F.C.

of T. [1948] HCA 25; (1948) 77 CLR 78, 4 AITR 133.

The question arises as to whether or not it is open

to argue s 31C or any of the ordinary provisions of

the Act in the context of a s 169 assessment. It

is felt that the answer is not free from doubt. In

the present references to the AAT, the notices of

objection and the Commissioner's statement under

s 37 of the AAT Act both put forward alternative

arguments relying on the ordinary provisions. The

Commissioner still wishes to argue s 31C and the

ordinary provisions as an alternative to s 136.

In view of the above, original 1979 and 1981

assessments are to be issued to the company. The

assessments, will be issued under s 166 and be

based on the ordinary provisions of the Act.

Please note, however, that no effort will be made

to seek payment of the tax due on these assessments

unless the assessments which were issued pursuant

to s 136 are amended to show a nil taxable income."

8. The reference to s 31C, is a reference to one of the reasons given by the respondent in the statement of reasons provided pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act. The first reason, relied upon s 136. The second reason was : "alternatively, s 31C of the Act has application in relation to the specified adjustments and reflects the 'arm's length' prices of the commodities and freight mentioned above". Partly in order to ground an argument under s 31C, and as foreshadowed in the above letter, subsequent assessments were issued for the 1979 and 1981 years under the ordinary assessment provision of s 166. The assessments were for exactly the same amount of money. On their face, they do not purport to be amended assessments. At the directions hearing, I was furnished with copies of notices of objection to these later assessments. There was no evidence whether the objections have yet been dealt with. Certainly objection decisions in relation to the later assessments are not presently before the Tribunal.

9. The applicant submitted that this Tribunal has no jurisdiction to continue with a review of the original objection decisions. It was submitted that the issue of the later assessments had the consequence of setting aside the earlier assessments which, it was said, ceased to have any operation or effect. It was submitted that as a result, the present proceedings before this Tribunal in respect of the objections against the initial assessments no longer concerned subsisting valid assessments. It followed from this, in counsel's submission, that the jurisdiction of the Tribunal had ceased together with the operation of the assessments.

10. Following the decision in Lever Bros, it was conceded that where the Commissioner issues an assessment in the normal way in reliance on s 166, he is therefore permitted to issue a further assessment based on the provisions of ss 136 and 169. It was further conceded that such an assessment would be an original assessment and not an amended assessment. However, counsel submitted that such a later assessment would have the effect of substituting the taxable income and tax assessed by the new assessment for the taxable income and tax liability arising from the first assessment. From that, counsel argued that the second assessment would be an original assessment, not an amended assessment, and that it would for all purposes replace the first assessment. Counsel for the respondent put to me that the applicant would need to go so far as to say that the later assessment extinguished the earlier one. In his reply, counsel for the applicant did not resile from this expression in stating his submission. He seeks to show therefore that the earlier assessment is rendered void and of no effect (that is, extinguished) by the later assessment.

11. In support of his submission, he relied heavily upon a passage in the judgement of Williams J. in Lever Bros in the following terms, at page 82 -

"It is therefore clear that when the notional

taxable income determined under s 136 is

substituted for the actual taxable income, Part

IIIA is concerned with that notional taxable

income, and the company becomes liable to pay

further tax on that portion of that income which,

less all allowable deductions, is not distributed

as dividends. There is no specific section of the

Act which entitles a taxpayer, who is first

assessed under an ordinary assessment and pays tax

and is then re-assessed under s 136, to set off the

amount he has paid on the first assessment against

his liability under the second assessment. But it

seems to me that, when the Commissioner determines

the amount of the notional taxable income under

s 136, this amount must be substituted for the

actual taxable income. The assessment of the

taxpayer on his notional taxable income under

s 169, as the Board said, replaces the assessment

of the taxpayer on his actual taxable income under

s 166, and the moneys paid under the earlier

assessment must be refunded or set off against his

liability under the new assessment."

12. Apart from the above dicta, counsel for the applicant pointed to a number of reasons why it should be concluded that the later assessments had the effect of setting aside and replacing the previous assessments. In support of this part of his argument, he submitted that the Act operates on the basis that there may subsist at any given point of time only one valid notice of assessment which will state the taxable income of the taxpayer for the year of income and will state the amount of tax payable in respect of that taxable income. If it were otherwise, he suggested, (inter alia) that the following consequences would follow -

(a) the tax stated to be payable by both assessments

would be due and payable by the taxpayer

(b) the assessment might specify different taxable

incomes. He said that it was merely coincidence

that both assessments in the present case specified

the same taxable income

(c) the taxpayer would have to pursue separate

objection and appeal proceedings in relation to the

assessments. One might proceed in the Tribunal,

the other in the Federal Court and

(d) whilst the tax assessed remained unpaid (as is the

case here) s 207 of the Act had the consequence

that 2 lots of additional tax would accrue at the

rate of 20% per annum.

13. An assessment in the sense of a mere calculation, it was submitted had no legal effect but when notice was served, s 204 had the consequence of bringing into existence a debt which was due and payable. Assessment is a process, counsel for the applicant submitted, which is definitive of the taxpayer's liability to tax. Its essential character is the creation of tax liability in a specific amount. Having regard to the legal consequences which flow from a fully executed assessment, the Act must contemplate that there can be only one assessment, and therefore only one amount of tax made due and payable. He drew some support for his description of the critical role of assessments from the judgment of Kitto J. in Batagol v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243 at 252 -

"When the group of sections which includes s 170 is

examined, it becomes, I think, quite clear that

this is the concept which "assessment" in s 170 is

intended to express. The making of assessments by

the Commissioner is the subject of provisions in ss

166, 167, 168 and 169, and from these it is clear

that assessment is a process which by force of the

Act is definitive of the amount of the taxpayer's

liability, though subject of course to review and

appeal. Another section, s 171, oddly enough by

its very infelicity of expression shows that a

notice of assessment is essential to the existence

of an assessment. It speaks of "any assessment

issued". Then the next section, s 172, by

describing an amendment in the taxpayer's favour as

one by which his liability is reduced, emphasises

the essential character of an assessment as the

creation of a tax liability of specific amount.

See also the proviso to s 185."

14. Counsel also made reference to certain isolated sections of the Act, such as ss 17 and 46(7), and s 23 of the Income Tax Rates Act 1986 which, he submitted, clearly contemplated that a taxpayer could have only one taxable income in respect of a single year of income.

15. The difficulty with the applicant's argument, as I see it, is that it is not specifically supported by statute. The propositions that counsel advance are nowhere to be found explicitly in the Act. He must rely firstly upon the passage from the judgment of Williams J. to which I have referred, and to the interpretation to be put upon those observations. The balance of his argument is based on inferences which he draws from certain sections and on what is sometimes referred to as the "mischief rule" in statutory interpretation. In my view, the material on which he relies can not support such a heavy burden as the radical propositions to which he has adverted.

16. Williams J. was dealing with a situation that is the reverse of the present situation. The taxpayer had received a normal assessment and subsequently received a s 136 assessment for a different amount. I read His Honour's judgment to mean simply that the calculation of the amount due in the later assessment replaces the amount calculated in the earlier assessment. It does not follow from this that the earlier assessment is "extinguished". Indeed, His Honour had previously expressed a view to the contrary and reaffirmed that view in Lever Bros at page 81 in the following terms -

"I adhere to the opinion expressed in

Cadbury-Fry-Pascall Pty Limited v Federal

Commissioner of Taxation (1) that s 170(3) must be

read distributively with respect to every separate

assessment which the Commissioner is authorized to

make under the Act, and that it does not prevent

him issuing separate assessments although the

effect of the later assessment may be to increase

the liability of the taxpayer under the earlier

assessment. In my opinion, therefore, there is

nothing in s 170(3) which prevented the

Commissioner making the six assessments under

s 136."

17. Indeed, it might be said that His Honour expressly rejected the very argument that counsel for the applicant put to me. It seems to me that the following passage at page 83 is susceptible of such an interpretation -

"It was also contended that the rights of the

Commissioner to assess the appellant under s 166

and under s 169 pursuant to a determination under

s 136 were alternative and that, since the

Commissioner had elected to assess the appellant on

its actual taxable income he could not subsequently

assess the appellant on its notional taxable

income. But there is no express provision in the

Act which compels the Commissioner so to elect and

there is nothing in the policy of the Act from

which I can draw such an implication. The Act

gives the Commissioner a right to assess under

s 169 pursuant to a determination under s 136 which

depends upon the taxpayer having certain

characteristics, and this appears to me to be a

right which is separate and distinct from his

ordinary right to assess under s 166. It is a

means of exacting from such a taxpayer additional

tax to that for which he would be liable on an

ordinary assessment."

18. Williams J. was a Member of the Full Bench of the High Court in Cadbury-Fry-Pascall Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 31; (1944) 70 CLR 362. The views that he there expressed, and reaffirmed in Lever Bros, are to be found at pages 393 and 394 -

"In addition to the two assessments already

mentioned, the respondent had previously assessed

the appellant for income tax upon its taxable

income under s 17 of the Act, so that the

assessment of 25th June 1941 was the second and

that of 5th February 1942 the third assessment of

the appellant. The first assessment was made under

the provisions of s 166 of the Act. It was the

ordinary assessment of the taxable income of the

appellant computed in accordance with Part III.,

Divs 2 and 3, of the Act, and, as it was accepted

and paid, the appellant is entitled to the benefit

of s 170(3) in respect of that assessment. But

s 170(3) must, in my opinion, be read

distributively with respect to every separate

assessment which the respondent is authorised to

make under the Act. If, as the appellant

contended, he can only make one assessment of a

company under the Act, the assessments of 25th June

1941 and 5 February 1942 would both be avoided by

the sub-section, but the assessments provided for

by ss 104 and 105 are separate and distinct

assessments from the ordinary assessment of a

company on its taxable income. The liability of a

private company to pay tax under these sections

depends upon an event which does not arise until

nine months after the close of the year of income,

and they provide for assessments on a completely

different basis to that on which an ordinary

assessment is made. The real question is whether

the respondent, having made an assessment under

either s 104 or s 105, can later make another

assessment under the other section increasing the

liability of the taxpayer.

But the authority to assess conferred upon the

respondent by s 105 is expressly stated to be in

addition to the authority conferred upon him by

s 104. As s 170(3) does not prevent the respondent

issuing separate assessments, he can assess under

each section, although the effect of the later

assessment may be to increase the liability of the

taxpayer under the earlier assessment. Further, I

agree with the Chief Justice that the respondent

was authorized to issue the two assessments by

s 169 of the Principal Act. In the present case,

the respondent, having made an assessment under

s 105, called his subsequent assessment under s 104

an amended assessment, whereas it was in truth an

original assessment. But this is an irregularity

which would be covered by s 175."

19. In my opinion, the authorities support the proposition advanced by counsel for the respondent, namely that the Commissioner is not bound to elect, but may issue collateral assessments in respect of the same income for a particular year. He is not bound to elect between one method of assessment or the other. Both assessments remain valid until one or other has been extinguished by payment or by lawful order of a competent tribunal or court. Each is an alternative statement of a legally created debt to the Commonwealth.

20. This view of the law will not have the effect, as counsel for the applicant submitted, that a taxpayer would be put in double jeopardy. He could not be liable for payment of both assessments. In Richardson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 67; (1931-1932) 48 CLR 192, Starke J., at first instance, said at page 197: The Income Tax Acts do not authorize the Commissioner to take income tax twice over in respect of the same source for the same period of time". The validity of this assumption was echoed by Dixon J. in the appeal at page 207 -

"Again, the case is not one in which a given state

of facts exposes two persons in the alternative to

a liability at the option of another; it is not a

case in which a public officer has an election

between 2 courses so that he can not pursue both at

once."

21. Similar views have been expressed in Booth v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4049 at 4077, Winter v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4655 at 4659 and Hoare v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4042 at 4049 the last passage summarises the observation made in the earlier cases-

"That contention is rejected. The substantive issue

raised in Winter's case was determined by Fox J.

when the application for an order of review in that

matter came on for hearing; see Winter v D.F.C. of

T. 87 ATC 4655. For present purposes, the facts of

the present case and the facts of Winter's case are

similar. The issue was stated by Fox J. at pp

4657-4658 :

'The present case was put simply and solely

on the basis that there were mutually

inconsistent assessments, the

inconsistency being between the assessment

of Mr Winter on the one hand, and the

assessment, taken together, of the

companies to which I have referred, on the

other.'

His Honour then dealt with the facts in some detail

and the submission on behalf of Winter and then

said at pp 4659-4660 :

'In my opinion this submission fails.

There is a clear distinction between

assessment on alternative bases and

failure to properly assess at all, as

Barwick C.J. recognised later in his

judgment in Bailey (see at ATC p 4098; CLR

p 218). The Commissioner may assess

different taxpayers in respect of the same

income on alternative bases, although he

can only collect tax on one basis: See

Richardson v F.C. of T. (1931-1932) 48 CLR

192 at pp 205-207 and 212. In Booth v F.C.

of T. 86 ATC 4049; (1986) 81 FLR 346,

McGarvie J. sitting at first instance, said

at ATC p 4077; FLR p 359 :

'I do not think an implication

arises from the Act that the

Commissioner may not issue

inconsistent assessments to two

taxpayers in respect of the same

income.'

and he cited Richardson's case. The case

went on appeal (see 86 ATC 4612), but this

point was not dealt with. See also D.F.C. of

T. v Jonrich Pty Ltd and Ors 86 ATC 4560 at p

4575. It follows that the existence of

two assessments in respect of the same

income does not deprive either assessment

of its protection under s 177(1): See D.F.C.

of T. v Crowl 87 ATC 4001 at p 4004; and

Tupicoff v F.C. of T. 84 ATC 4851; (1984) 4

FCR 505 at ATC p 4863; FCR p 523.'

I accept the judgment of Fox J. and find there is no

substance in the taxpayer's contention based on

mutually inconsistent assessments. There is no

suggestion that the Commissioner is seeking to

enforce the assessment against the company. None

of the other matters of a substantive issue with

respect to the proposed application for an order of

review are sufficient to justify the granting of an

extention of time."

22. It is true, as counsel for the applicant pointed out, that in all of the above cases, the varying assessments were against different parties. However, in all cases the different assessments were in respect of the one parcel of income earned in a particular financial year. The cases, in my view, remain authority for the proposition that only one imposition of tax is recoverable in respect of that parcel of income. The fact that the assessments are raised against different parties does not, in my view, derogate from this principle. The mischief of double jeopardy which counsel for the applicant feared might arise in the case of multiple assessments can not, in my opinion, manifest itself in the light of these dicta.

23. Furthermore, it seems to me that in FJ Bloemen Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4096 at 4099 there is positive authority for the proposition that multiple assessments, be they original assessments or amended assessments, are possible and are legally valid. They should be viewed distributively until one is upheld in a final manner. At page 4099 Enderby J. said -

"Although I was superficially attracted to the

concept of a subsuming or merging or repealing

effect that would be produced on the original

assessment by the amended assessment, I have come

to the firm opinion that the decision of the Board

on this question was correct. The argument to the

contrary really lacks substance (see F.C. of T. v

Miller [1946] HCA 23; (1946) 73 CLR 93 at p 101 quoted in Lombardo

v F.C. of T. 79 ATC 4542). The scheme of the Act is

unfortunate, when it can operate or be made to

operate as it has operated in this case, but it is

an unfortunate consequence of what is made very

clear by the relevant sections. The mischief

deserves legislative attention. It is not

necessary for me to say any more than that, in my

opinion, the scheme of the Act permits more than

one assessment to be on foot and have a possibly

limited effect at any point in time. It is not

necessary to attempt here to define the possible

limitations, but the original assessment certainly

continues to survive for the purpose of giving

jurisdiction to a Board of Review. In my opinion,

the provision to sec 185 and 173 and the general

scheme of the Act and the language used in F.C. of T.

v Offshore Oil NL 80 ATC 4457; F.C. of T. v Mantle

Traders Pty Ltd 80 ATC 4588 and McAndrew v F.C. of T.

[1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263 at p 269, point overwhelmingly to

that conclusion. The Board was correct in its

conclusion."

24. I have therefore come to the conclusion that the jurisdiction of this Tribunal is not displaced by the issue of the subsequent assessments. The original objection decisions remain the subject of the present application. I will put these applications in the general call-over list on 26 April to enable both parties to consider their position in the meantime. If the matter is to proceed to a hearing, a date can then be fixed at the call-over.
