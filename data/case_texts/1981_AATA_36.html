Re G Hollands and Commissioner of Police Re G Hollands and Registrar of Gun Licences [1981] AATA 36 (22 May 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: G. HOLLANDS

And: COMMISSIONER FOR POLICE

Re: G. HOLLANDS

And: REGISTRAR OF GUN LICENCES

No. A81/2

Gun and pistol licences

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Gun and pistol licences - applications for grant - objection by Police - refusal of applications by Registrar of Gun Licences - application for review - applicant a member of pistol club - failure to take reasonable precautions for safe keeping of firearms - adequate security arrangements subsequently made - licences granted

Law Reform - suggested amendments as to form of application, power to impose conditions and relevant criteria for grant of licence

Gun Licence Ordinance 1937 s.6, s.7, s.10, s.12, s.14, s.20

HEARING

CANBERRA

22:5:1981

ORDER

1. The Tribunal sets aside the decision of Detective Senior Constable G.S. Hobart, a member of the Police Force thereto authorised in writing by the Commissioner of Police, certifying that he objects to the grant of a pistol licence and two gun licences to the applicant and in substitution for that decision the Tribunal decides that no such certification should be made.

2. The Tribunal also sets aside the decision of the Registrar of Gun Licences refusing to grant a pistol licence and two gun licences to the applicant and remits each application to the Registrar:

(i) with the direction that the application for a pistol licence be granted; and

(ii)with the recommendation that subject to the existing gun licences in favour of Mr C.I. Rees being relinquished, the two applications for gun licences should be granted.

3. The Tribunal reserves liberty to apply.

DECISION

1. On 8 September 1980 the applicant applied to the Registrar of Gun Licences, Canberra, for two gun licences and one pistol licence in respect of the undermentioned weapons:-

. Sturn Ruger .22 calibre semi-automatic carbine rifle No. 113-48244;

. Winchester .243 calibre bolt action rifle No. G150455

. Smith & Wesson .357 calibre revolver No. 7K90620.

2. The applicant had previously held licences in respect of these weapons, the licence in respect of the Sturn Ruger rifle having expired on 21 July 1980; the licence for the Smith & Wesson revolver having expired on 26 April 1980; and the licence for the Winchester firearm being still then current and being due to expire on 17 October 1980. Although technically the application with respect to the Winchester rifle would therefore appear to have been an application for renewal of an existing licence, both parties proceeded on the basis that all three applications were applications for the grant of new licences, and I propose to deal with the applications accordingly.

3. Pursuant to s.6(2) of the Gun Licence Ordinance 1937, the Registrar of Gun Licences submitted the three applications to the Commissioner of Police who, through an authorised member of the Police Force, Detective Senior Constable G.S. Hobart, certified in writing on 22 September 1980 that he objected to the grant of the pistol licence and the two gun licences to the applicant.

4. On 5 December 1980, the Registrar forwarded to the applicant a notice in writing informing him of the objection made by Detective Hobart and, as required by s.6(2) of the Ordinance informed the applicant that his licences could not be granted.

5. The applicant duly applied to this Tribunal for review of the decision of the Registrar of Gun Licences refusing his applications. However, at the hearing it became apparent that essentially the applicant was challenging the decision of Detective Hobart to object to the grant of the three licences. By consent, a further application seeking review of Detective Hobart's decision, as well as that of the Registrar, was lodged instanter pursuant to s.7P(2)(a) of the Ordinance, and the time within which to lodge that application was extended.

6. It appears from the statement lodged by Detective Hobart in accordance with s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, that the purpose for which the applicant sought the grant of a pistol licence was so that he could engage in sporting activities conducted by pistol clubs; whilst the gun licences were sought to enable the applicant to engage in the shooting of 'rabbits kangaroos and other vermin'. Having regard to the fact that on separate occasions in September 1978 and February 1979 the applicant's premises had been broken into, firearms had been stolen, and had been used in subsequent criminal offences, Detective Hobart did not consider that the applicant should be the holder of gun and pistol licences. Detective Hobart gave the following reasons for his decision:-

'MR HOLLANDS did not satisfy me that he was willing to take adequate precautions to prevent the theft of the guns on another occasion, although the facilities of the pistol clubs armoury are at his disposal. From the information available to me, MR HOLLANDS took no steps to adequately secure the firearms when they were returned to him following the first theft and he does not intend taking added precautions.

PUBLIC INTEREST

I do not believe that it is in the public interest to permit these guns to be in the possession of MR HOLLANDS. The protection of persons and their property in the Australian Capital Territory, is the task of the Australian Federal Police. In my opinion, due to the lack of responsibility of MR HOLLANDS, a number of persons in this Territory were the victims of serious offences involving firearms which were the property of the appellant.'

7. Mr J. Purnell, who appeared on behalf of the applicant, submitted that the applicant was a fit and proper person to hold gun and pistol licences under the Ordinance; that he was a person of good fame and character; that he had now taken adequate steps to ensure the safe keeping of any firearms in his possession; and that there was no proper basis, therefore, for Detective Hobart's objection.

8. The relevant provisions of s.6 of the Ordinance dealing with the grant of gun and pistol licences, so far as relevant, are as follows:-

'6.(1) Subject to this section, the Registrar may, as he thinks fit, in pursuance of applications made in accordance with Form A in the Schedule _

(a) grant a gun licence to any person who satisfies him that he is not less than sixteen years of age; and

(b) grant a pistol licence to any person who satisfies him that he is not less than eighteen years of age,

and renew any such licence.

(2) The Registrar shall submit to the Commissioner of Police all applications for licences under this Section, and, if the Commissioner or a member of the Police Force thereto authorized in writing by him certifies that he objects to the grant of a licence to any applicant, the Registrar shall not grant a licence to that applicant but shall forward to that applicant a notice in writing informing him of the objection.'

A power expressed in rather similar terms for the Police to object to an application for renewal of a licence is contained in s.14. The form of application in Form A in the Schedule throws little light on what matters may be relevant for consideration by the Registrar in dealing with an application for grant or renewal. The form requires no more than that the applicant must supply details of his age, his full name and address and details relating to the gun or pistol to be licenced. There is no requirement to specify in the application the purpose for which the licence is required or to give any other background information concerning the applicant.

9. These provisions may be contrasted with s.10 of the Ordinance concerning applications for gun dealers licences, where the grant of an application for such a licence is expressly conditioned upon the Registrar being satisfied that 'the applicant is a fit and proper person to carry on the trade of a gun dealer' (see s.10(3)). They may also be contrasted with s.7F which is one of a series of sections inserted in the Ordinance in 1979 with respect to the approval of officers or employees of security organisations to use, carry or be in possession of a pistol. The considerations to which the Registrar must have regard in deciding whether or not to approve a person for the purposes of the Ordinance pursuant to that section are more specific. They include a consideration of the person's mental and physical condition in so far as it may impair his ability to possess or use a pistol with safety; whether the person is an inebriate or an habitual criminal; or whether he 'has at any time, whether within Australia or elsewhere, been convicted of an offence in such circumstances that he is not a fit person to be registered as an approved person' (see s.7F(2)(d)).

10. As will be observed, however, s.6 of the Ordinance is silent as to the considerations which should guide the exercise of the discretionary powers conferred on the Registrar and the Commissioner of Police respectively. It does not follow, therefore, that the powers are arbitrary or completely unlimited: cf Water Conservation and Irrigation Commission (NSW) v Browning [1947] HCA 21; (1947) 74 CLR 492 at 505 per Dixon J. They are powers which may appropriately be exercised 'for the purpose of attaining the object and securing the purpose' of the legislation, 'such object and purpose being ascertained by an examination of the terms' of the legislation: per Latham CJ in Shrimpton v Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 CLR 613 at 620. Within the limits of the considerations which the statute by implication makes relevant, the discretionary powers are nevertheless very wide: cf R v Murphy; Ex parte Clift (1980) Qd.R. 1 at 8.

11. The Ordinance makes it clear that the use or possession of a lethal firearm or weapon is to be subject to proper controls in the public interest. The minimum age laid down by s.6 of the Ordinance for the grant of a pistol licence (18 years) compared with that for a gun licence (16 years) suggests a need for greater maturity in the possession of a pistol and no doubt reflects the principle that handguns, because of their capacity for concealment on a person, are intrinsically more dangerous than rifles. The fact that the Registrar and the Commissioner of Police are each recognised as having functions to perform in the licencing of guns and pistols gives some indication of the importance and of the nature of those functions.

12. Plainly, in my view, the Commissioner of Police with his overall responsibility for keeping the peace in this Territory, has a special concern under the Ordinance to ensure that firearms do not fall into the hands of criminal elements in the community, either directly by the granting of licences to persons, for example, with a criminal record or with known criminal associations, or indirectly, by the granting or renewal of licences to persons who have demonstrated a lack of responsibility in the safe keeping of weapons in their possession. In this latter regard it is an offence under the Ordinance for a person to fail to take 'all reasonable precautions' to ensure the safe keeping of a gun or pistol in his possession (see s.20). I should not be understood as suggesting that such considerations are outside the concern of the Registrar as well; but they are especially relevant to the Police.

13. So far as the Registrar is concerned the only express limitation upon his broad discretion as to the grant or refu3 l of a licence is imposed by s.6(2) of the Ordinance in the case where an objection is lodged by the Police. As was explained by Kerr J. in The Queen v Registrar of Gun Licences: Ex parte Barabas (1966) 9 FLR 229 at 232:-

'The position, therefore, is that under sub-s (2) of s.6 the discretion of the registrar is limited in one case; he is not permitted to grant a licence where the Commissioner of Police, or the person authorized in writing certifies that he objects to the grant of the licence. But if nothing is said by the Commissioner, or by the authorized member of the police force, under s.6, then the discretion of the registrar, in relation to the grant or issue of a gun or pistol licence, remains unimpaired. He does not have to issue the licence merely because there is no police objection.'

14. Whilst the Ordinance provides very little guidance as to the purposes for which gun licences may be granted, it does recognise that there are purposes for which it is proper that members of the public may be licenced or approved to have pistols in their possession. Officers or employees of security organisations to whom section 7F of the Ordinance applies are one class of persons who, subject to the provisions of the Ordinance, may be so approved. Members of a pistol club, (which to be approved pursuant to s.7 of the Ordinance must be a body corporate whose objects include the promotion of the sport of pistol shooting) constitute another class of persons who may legitimately seek to be licenced to have pistols in their possession. There are no doubt other classes of persons in the community who may have a similar entitlement; but I do not need to consider that question further for the purposes of this case. The applicant falls within one of the classes of persons which the Ordinance recognises by implication as entitled to apply for a pistol licence, namely members of an approved pistol club.

15. I turn now to the evidence which was given in these proceedings. The applicant did not dispute that on two separate occasions in September 1978 and February 1979 the premises which he was then renting were broken into and that weapons in respect of which he held a licence were stolen from the premises. On the first occasion a back window of the house was forced and a number of firearms were stolen including two rifles, three pistols and a conversion set, which is a pistol with interchangeable barrels and trigger weights used for target shooting. A rifle belonging to his brother was also stolen. The pistols included the Smith & Wesson .357 calibre revolver which is the subject of an application for a further licence in these proceedings. The applicant said that at that stage the rifles and pistols were kept in a wardrobe in the bedroom and that there was no lock on the wardrobe door. The pistols were kept towards the back of the wardrobe with cameras and other effects in the front half of the wardrobe.

16. Subsequently, two men were arrested and charged with an offence in relation to the use of the revolver in a public place. The applicant told me that one of the men, named Danilo Sordini, was convicted and fined $500.00. Sordini, he said, had been introduced to him by the woman with whom he (i.e. the applicant) was then living in a de facto relationship. Sordini had called at the premises prior to September 1978 to have a look at the Smith & Wesson revolver saying that he was interested in buying it. Sordini had told the applicant that he was a member of the Sporting Shooters Pistol Club. The applicant had taken down the details, but had subsequently discovered that Sordini was not a member of the Club and had 'left it at that'. After the conclusion of the Court proceedings, the Smith & Wesson revolver was returned to the applicant. As a result of this incident he had asked his de facto wife to leave because 'we put her as being at blame for having these people steal the guns, being her friends'.

17. The Police Officer who had investigated the theft of the firearms had informed the applicant that he would be charged with an offence. At that stage, the applicant said that he did not know what offence he had committed. He had been advised by the Police, however, that his safe keeping of the weapons was not good enough. In cross-examination by Mr M.H. Peedom who appeared on behalf of both respondents, he agreed that his security arrangements were very inadequate. He had accordingly made some inquiries by calling at the premises of engineering companies, scrap metal yards and the like about building or buying a safe in which to keep his pistols. He had received quotes ranging from approximately $140.00 to over $200.00 from different engineering companies to make a safe for him but he was hoping not to have to spend more than $100.00. In the meantime, he had taken no positive steps to improve the safe keeping of the weapons at his premises because, as he told me, he did not think that it could happen twice.

18. As it eventuated, the applicant was quite mistaken in this belief. In February 1979 the premises were again broken into. On this occasion, three pistols including again the Smith & Wesson .357 calibre revolver, plus 350 rounds of ammunition of assorted calibres were stolen. Entry to the premises was gained by breaking the glass in a bedroom window situated at the rear of the house. Notwithstanding that there were other articles in the house including some money and rifles, no other items were stolen. The Smith & Wesson revolver was subsequently used in an armed robbery during which a man was wounded. The revolver was later recovered from the waters of Lake Burley Griffin and has been in the custody of the Police ever since.

19. At the subsequent criminal trial, the applicant recognised the accused person as a man who had visited his residence. Detective Hobart indicated in his s.37 statement that the applicant had said that the accused had visited his residence on a number of occasions to attend parties. The applicant, however, denied on oath before me that this was so, claiming that he had recognised the accused as a person who had been to his premises on only one occasion, and that for no longer than two to three minutes, to speak to a man named Jim Petersen who was residing at the premises at that time. The accused was not a friend or associate of the applicant.

20. Shortly after the second offence, the applicant had purchased a copy of the Gun Licence Ordinance and had ascertained that it was an offence to fail to take all reasonable precautions to ensure safe keeping of a gun or pistol in his possession. On this occasion he had felt sure that he would be charged with an offence, and was surprised when this did not happen.

21. Notwithstanding the fact that on two occasions his premises had been broken into and weapons had been stolen which were subsequently used in a criminal manner, the applicant took no further steps to ensure the safe keeping of the firearms in his possession. He explained that as the premises in which he lived were rented he was concerned that he might lose his bond money of $300.00 if he had drilled large holes to put locks on the doors and the cupboards. In answer to a question by Mr Peedom, he said that he could not have stored his weapons in the Pistol Club armoury, because the Club did not have one.

22. Nevertheless, in January 1980, when he went overseas for two months, the applicant had left an air pistol and two rifles which he then owned with a licensed dealer for safe keeping. Apparently this arrangement continued until May 1980 when the applicant moved into his present property, of which he is the Crown Lessee.

23. It appears that by about August 1980 the applicant had completed a number of security arrangements at his new premises to ensure as far as possible the safe keeping of any firearms in his possession. He told me that in March 1980 he had purchased a small safe about 50 cms high by approximately 40 cms deep and 30-40 cms wide for the purpose of securing his hand guns. He had built extra timber supports between the floor joists and had bolted the safe to the floor with coach bolts through the floor going upwards into the safe with the nuts on the inside of the safe. The coach bolts, he said, have rounded heads and cannot be shifted with a spanner. The safe itself was enclosed within a wardrobe fitted with a Yale latch-type door lock which requires a key to open it. The cupboard, he said, was for storage of rifles and the safe, of course, was for the storage of pistols.

24. The applicant said that he had put lengths of dowling timber around all of the sliding windows in the house to prevent the windows from being opened. He had also replaced the glass panel beside the front door with 10mm thick one-way glass. He installed deadlocks on the front and back door and had acquired two German Shepherd dogs who roam the enclosed backyard.

25. He said that the measures which he had now taken were more than those taken by the majority of Pistol Club members in the A.C.T. He said that club members had discussed the strict requirements introduced in New South Wales about two years ago requiring pistol club members to keep pistols locked in a safe or security cabinet. However, only a few of the members did anything about buying a safe or constructing one.

26. I note in this regard that Regulation No. 29 of 1978 made under the Firearms and Dangerous Weapons Act 1973 (NSW) provides that a pistol licence issued (inter alia) to a member of an approved pistol club in New South Wales may be endorsed with the condition set out in Part 1A of Schedule 4 of the Firearms and Dangerous Weapons Regulations. That condition is as follows:-

'The licensee shall, if he has the pistol in his possession at the place where he ordinarily resides, keep it locked in a safe or security cabinet of a type approved by the Commissioner.'

27. Finally the applicant said that he had no criminal convictions. He denied that he was in the habit of showing his guns to friends on social occasions. He said that in fact he led a very quiet social life and that during the three and a half years he had lived at his previous rented premises there had been only one party. He said that he was proud of his guns but only in the sense that they were excellent quality guns _ not in the sense that he was proud of being able to own guns.

28. Character evidence was given by the applicant's current de facto wife and by Mr C.I. Rees, who was a personal friend of the applicant and a fellow member of the Canberra Pistol Club. Mr Rees said that the applicant was a well respected member of the Club; that he had been a committee member of the Club in the past; that he was very careful in his handling of firearms; and that he did not abuse them or neglect them in any way. Mr Rees told me that the Canberra Pistol Club did not have an armoury on the firing range as that would be unsafe. The Club's armourers were both, in fact, licenced dealers who had safes and burglar alarm systems for the purposes of their licenced dealerships. He said that there would be very few members of the Club who had taken similar measures to those undertaken by the applicant in order to ensure the safe keeping of firearms. Out of approximately 120 members of the Club there would, in his opinion, be only another half dozen members who had a safe apart from the two armourers. Mr Rees said that the normal safe keeping measures undertaken by Club members were to ensure that their firearms were locked away in some cupboard or container so that they were out of sight. Ammunition, he said, would be stored separately.

29. At the conclusion of the hearing I requested that Detective Hobart should inspect the applicant's premises with a view to submitting a report to the Tribunal as to the adequacy or otherwise of the safeguards which the applicant has now taken to ensure the safe keeping of any firearms in his possession. I subsequently received a report from Detective Hobart, a copy of which was made available to the applicant for comment if so desired. The report confirmed that the security arrangements were as explained in evidence by the applicant. Based on his inspection Detective Hobart concluded that 'it is my opinion that these precautions represent adequate safeguards to preventing theft of the firearms'.

30. Mr Purnell for the applicant submitted that on the evidence before me I should find that the applicant was a person of good fame and character and that he was a fit and proper person to hold the gun and pistol licences. Mr Peedom on the other hand submitted that the applicant's failure to make any adequate security arrangements for the weapons despite the two thefts and the warning by the Police demonstrated an irresponsible attitude on his part which rendered him unfit to be the holder of a licence. There was no evidence, he submitted, of any contrition on the part of the applicant.

The Tribunal's Findings

31. On the evidence before me I am satisfied that Detective Hobart was fully entitled to feel considerable disquiet regarding the steps taken by the applicant to ensure the safe keeping of the guns and pistols in his possession. The failure of the applicant to take any remedial steps after the first occasion when his premises were entered and one of the stolen weapons was used in the course of a criminal offence is a cause for some concern. In my view, he should have heeded the advice of the Police on that occasion that his security arrangements were inadequate. In retrospect, his belief that the same thing could not happen again appears somewhat naive, as knowledge of places where weapons may be readily accessible might be expected to spread amongst those who have a nefarious interest in such matters. The failure to take any steps after the second occasion is, in my view, more disturbing. On that occasion, the stolen revolver was used in the course of an armed holdup and a member of the public was wounded. If the applicant had any doubts about the necessity to take 'all reasonable precautions' to ensure the safekeeping of the weapons in his possession, they should have been dispelled by this second theft from his premises. Nevertheless, it was still many months before he made any more secure arrangements for his firearms and then only when he went overseas.

32. However, allowing that the security arrangements whilst the applicant resided in his rented accommodation were inadequate, I am satisfied that the applicant has now instituted at considerable expense and trouble security arrangements which as Detective Hobart acknowledges are 'adequate safeguards to prevent the theft of firearms'. The fact that the applicant has taken these measures which would appear to go even further than the stringent requirements of the recent New South Wales regulations to which I referred, demonstrates a responsible attitude on his part and satisfies me that whatever may have been his attitude in the past, he has now accepted his obligation as reflected by s.20 of the Ordinance to take all reasonable precautions to ensure the safe keeping of firearms in his possession.

33. The applicant has no criminal record and there is no evidence to suggest that he is not a person of good fame and character. I have had the benefit of observing the applicant during the proceedings before the Tribunal and I am reinforced in the conclusion to which I have come in this matter by the favourable impression which I have formed of him during the hearing. Exercising as I do for this purpose the powers of Detective Hobart under s.6(2) of the Ordinance, (see s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975), I am not satisfied that this is a proper case in which to object to the grant of the licences for which the applicant has applied.

34. I would accordingly set aside the decision of Detective Hobart certifying that he objects to the grant of those licences and in substitution for that decision I decide that no such certification should be made.

35. I have considered the further question whether there are any circumstances, including those which I have already discussed, which would justify the Registrar, in the exercise of the separate discretion conferred upon him by s.6(1) of the Ordinance, in refusing to grant the relevant licences; but I am not satisfied that there are. So far as the application for a pistol licence is concerned, the Smith & Wesson revolver the subject of that application is at present unlicensed. I therefore set aside the decision of the Registrar to refuse to grant the pistol licence and remit the matter back to the Registrar with the direction that the applicant's application for a pistol licence be granted.

36. There is a problem which emerged late in the hearing with respect to the two applications for gun licences. I was informed by Mr Peedom that both the Sturn Ruger and Winchester rifles are currently subject to a licence in favour of Mr Rees and that having regard to the provisions of s.6(3) of the Ordinance, the Registrar could, on that ground alone, refuse to grant a licence to the applicant for those same guns. Section 6(3) provides that:-

'6(3) A gun licence may be granted in respect of a particular gun or a particular class of gun specified in the licence.'

Mr Peedom also suggested, however, that the problem may be surmountable if Mr Rees were to relinquish his current licences.

37. These submissions raise matters which were not fully canvassed before me. I have concluded therefore that the proper course for me to adopt with respect to the two applications for gun licences is to set aside the Registrar's decision refusing to grant those licences and remit the matter back to him with the recommendation that, subject to the existing gun licences in favour of Mr C.I. Rees being relinquished, the applicant's applications for the two gun licences should be granted. In case there should be any unexpected problems in this regard, I reserve liberty to apply.

38. I wish to add a few comments by way of conclusion. The adequacy of and lack of uniformity in State and Territorial laws controlling the possession and use of firearms is a question that according to newspaper reports, is currently engaging the attention of Federal and State Ministers responsible for Police affairs. It is a question that is also attracting academic discussion (see, for example, the recent publication _ 'Firearms and Violence in Australian Life: An Examination of Gun Ownership and Use in Australia' by Professor Richard Harding). The present case demonstrates, in my view, a number of inadequacies in the Gun Licence Ordinance 1937 to which it may be timely for me to refer:

(i) The form of application for a gun or pistol licence (Form A) is somewhat sparse in the detail which it requires. In my view an applicant should at least be required to specify the purpose for which the gun or pistol is sought and the measures proposed for ensuring the safekeeping of the firearm and any ammunition.

(ii) A specific power for the Registrar to impose appropriate conditions at the time of granting or renewing a licence, such as conditions restricting the use to which the firearm may be 0u0 an imposing requirements as to the safekeeping of the firearm and ammunition, seems to be desirable.

(iii) Whilst I am not suggesting that the various offence provisions in sections 18 to 24 inclusive should be altered, the important obligations which certain of those provisions inferentially impose upon licence holders may appropriately be reflected as conditions of the grant of the licence. The evidence in the present case discloses that the applicant who had been a member of a pistol club for some years, was unaware, until after the second theft from his premises, of the precise obligations as to safe keeping of firearms which the Ordinance imposed. The incorporation of those obligations as conditions of a licence may ensure a greater awareness of them and may allow the Registrar a measure of administrative control in case of breach by way of cancellation or suspension of a licence (cf s.12 of the Ordinance). This administrative control which could be subject to external review (cf s.7P), may be more appropriate in some cases than a prosecution.

(iv) Finally, as the Ordinance specifies criteria by reference to which other forms of licences or approvals may be granted (cf ss.7F(2) and 10(3)), and as the requirement that an applicant for a gun or pistol licence must be a 'fit and proper person' to hold such a licence appears, quite properly in my view, to be fundamental to the administration of the gun and pistol licencing provisions, it may be more satisfactory if that requirement, and any other appropriate requirements (cf s.7F(2)), were specified in the Ordinance rather than being left to implication.
