Jeffery and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 622 (3 September 1993)

Jeffery and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 622 (3 September 1993)

Last Updated: 26 August 2008



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - deferred benefit - election outside prescribed period - discretion to recognise election - whether discretion should be exercised - duty on applicant to inquire - failure of respondent to inform - whether acceptable explanation of delay - decision set aside



AAT Decision No 8964



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. A92/71

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: WILLIAM ATHEY JEFFERY



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION



Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Dr D J Grimes, Senior Member

Dr D B Travers, (Member)

Mr N J Attwood, (Member)



Date : 3 September 1993



Place : Canberra



Decision : The decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the election made by the applicant on 26 May, 1989 under section s 137(1) of the Act, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section.



The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the applicant's entitlement in light of this decision.



(Sgd) D J Grimes

...................................................

Dr D J Grimes

SENIOR MEMBER

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. A92/71

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: WILLIAM ATHEY JEFFERY



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION





1. This is an application to review a decision of 17 July 1990 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation (the Commissioner). The decision, made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), was that the Commissioner declined to direct that an election made by the applicant on 26 May 1989, under sub-section 137(1) should be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act. This decision was affirmed on 7 April 1992 by a Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation under section 154 of the Act.



2. At the hearing of this application, the applicant was represented by Mr R Refshauge and the respondent by Ms T Munoz. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975, together with exhibits tendered by the parties during the hearing. The Tribunal heard oral evidence from the applicant, Mr Anatol Sdanowytsch and Mr John Wadey and for the respondent, Mr Barry Vincent.



3. The benefit that the applicant seeks is entitled `deferred benefits' as provided for by s 136 of the Act. This benefit is more commonly referred to as a preservation of superannuation rights. The deferred benefit which the applicant seeks, pursuant to sub-section 137(1) of the Act, is dependent upon an election being made within 21 days of the applicant ceasing to be an eligible employee or within one month of the event. The relevant parts of s137 are as follows:

"s137(1) A person who -



(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply, and upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or



(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;



may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect by notice in writing to the Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the person.



(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.



(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -



(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of the employer; and



(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."



4. Section 157 sub-section (1) contains a discretionary power, vested in the Commissioner for Superannuation, to recognise an election made after the prescribed time in certain circumstances. These circumstances are as follows:

"s157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."



5. The applicant's evidence was that he was born 20 September 1924, joined the public service in National Mapping in 1964 and became an eligible employee on 20 July 1966 which entitled him to be accepted into the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. Prior to entering the public service he had been in the army and qualified as an army surveyor. His school education was to intermediate level.



6. The applicant's first position in the public service was as a Technical Officer Grade 1, eventually reaching the level of Senior Technical Officer. After initially working in army records he transferred to doing field surveys for mapping control, working in the field for periods of 12 to 13 weeks and then spending 3 weeks in the office compiling results. The further he was promoted the more field work he did. He gave evidence he did little administrative or personnel work and had virtually no contact with the superannuation office.



7. In 1977 the applicant said he was dissatisfied with his job in general, and in particular with a re-organisation which was occurring in National Mapping. At this time he had purchased and paid for a property of ten acres at Jugiong and he decided to leave the public service and try making a living at Jugiong. If he succeeded he would stay out of the service but if not he would return to the service. He knew there would be no trouble with re-employment if he returned. In evidence the applicant conceded that the decision taken by him to resign, leave the public service and move to private business was a reasonably well contemplated decision.



8. In November 1977 he submitted his resignation to the National Mapping Office at Morisett House, Queanbeyan and eventually left work after the Christmas break.

The applicant's letter of resignation was written with the assistance of Mr Anatol Sdanowytsch, his immediate supervisor. Mr Sdanowytsch frequently helped the applicant with letters and wording official documents as this was not the applicant's strong point.



9. The application for registration was submitted to Mr John Wadey, the personnel officer, who, it was the applicant's evidence, was somewhat surprised at his resignation but initiated the necessary procedures. According to the applicant no mention was made of superannuation.



10. The applicant gave evidence that at the time of his resignation he believed that he was only entitled to a return of his superannuation contributions. He said that although superannuation was not discussed very often amongst field staff there had been several resignations amongst the survey staff and those he had spoken to had opted for a refund on the understanding this was the only option available to them.



11. After resignation the applicant received a letter, dated 13 January 1988, sent to his Jugiong address where he then resided, giving details of his entitlements on retirement and enclosing Form S2A with a request he complete it and return it.



12. The applicant's memory was that he filled out Form S2A and returned it by post to head office in Queanbeyan. He signed it at Jugiong and it was not witnessed then but he believes must have been witnessed by a Miss Simms on receipt at the office.



13. The section of the form where the retiree is asked to delete the options not applicable to him, had the option to elect for preservation of superannuation rights deleted. The applicant said he had no memory of doing this and had some doubts whether he had done it because his practise was to use a ruler or straight edge to draw such lines on forms and this had been done free hand.



14. In his request for a reconsideration of the decision of the Assistant Commissioner not to recognise the applicant's request for a late election to preserve superannuation rights, the applicant stated:

"When I made my decision to resign from the public service I was still fit and active and it was my intention to try to make a living from working a small farmlet at Jugiong which I purchased in 1976 and from doing odd jobs on other farms in the area. This property was not subject to a mortgage and my decision to resign was not motivated by a desire to get hold of my superannuation contributions".



The applicant reiterated to the Tribunal that it was not his intention to be refunded his superannuation benefits so that he could fund a private venture, rather that he opted for this as it was the only course of action he believed to be available to him.



15. After leaving the public service the applicant worked as a barman at Jugiong, cropped his land and used his skills to prepare maps and enlarged photographs of properties for local farmers for a fee.



16. The money he received as repayment of contributions he put into his and his wife's joint cheque account. He had no need to use it as he had no mortgage on his farm and earned a sufficient livelihood.



17. In 1980, having tired of travelling backwards and forwards from Jugiong to Canberra, where his wife resided, his old job seemed more attractive and he rejoined the public service in National Mapping in 1980. He was initially employed as a Technical Assistant and then went successfully for a promotion to Senior Technical Officer Grade 1. In 1989, on reaching the statutory retiring age of 65, he retired.



18. The applicant gave evidence that neither at the time of his retirement in 1978 nor on his return to the public service in 1980 was he informed of the possibility of preservation of benefits nor was he given any information on superannuation. The applicant said that had he known of the option to preserve he would have done so as he did not need the money and thought he may return to the National Mapping Office. It was his evidence that he first became aware of the option to preserve benefits early in 1989 when he was discussing his pending retirement with a colleague, Mr Jack Lamb.



19. Following this advice the applicant then went to the Superannuation Board and spoke to a Mr Ross Arnold who assisted him by writing a letter applying for a late election which the applicant copied and submitted.



20. Mr Anatol Sdanowytsch, formerly the applicant's immediate superior, gave evidence that he assisted the applicant in writing his resignation letter and confirmed he knew nothing about the option of preservation at the time.



21. During the hearing the applicant was shown documents from the Superannuation Board issued in 1978 and then yearly from 1984 which notified the applicant of his superannuation contributions and referred to preservation of benefits on the back. He conceded he received these notices but could not remember the references to preservation. It was his belief that the form would not have alerted him to the three choices in regards to his superannuation contributions as he would have believed the reference to preservation referred to existing contributors and not to past contributors who had resigned and rejoined.



22. Mr J Wadey who was the Administration Officer for the Division of National Mapping at the time of the applicant's resignation gave oral evidence. He said that in cases like the resignation of the applicant of which there were four or five per year he saw the role of his section as a reactive one, responding to the requests of the resignee and only on complex issues would central office be contacted. Exit interviews were not normally conducted and in the case of the applicant who was on leave before resignation, an interview was even less likely.



23. In regards to a resignee being informed of the option to preserve their superannuation benefits, Mr Wadey confirmed to the Tribunal that which is encapsulated in a letter written by Mr Wadey to the Commissioner for Superannuation, dated 13 August 1990:

"I cannot recall at any time during my period of duty with Natmap being aware of the option to contributors to the Scheme of being able to preserve superannuation rights after resignation. The option would therefore not be conveyed by my section to Natmap officers. I would suggest, equally, that few, if any, Natmap officers would have been aware of the preservation option or, in fact, very much of the detailed workings of the Scheme - relying instead on the experts in the Central Officer Personnel Section to look after their interests."



24. Mr Barry Vincent who was in charge of induction courses for new recruits to the department gave evidence that superannuation information in such courses at the time the applicant rejoined the service in 1980 only concerned the availability of documents from the Superannuation Board. He confirmed that there was no superannuation manual in the branch nor anyone with expertise in the subject.



25. The issue for the Tribunal to consider is; whether it is desirable, in all the circumstances of the present case, pursuant to section 157(1) of the Act, that the applicant be permitted to elect for preservation of his superannuation rights, notwithstanding that the time prescribed by section 137(1) of the Act has passed?



26. When deciding the question of whether or not to exercise the discretion under s 157(1) this Tribunal has recourse to the principles laid down in a number of recent Administrative Appeals Tribunal decisions. (See Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT 8553, 26 February, 1993) and Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (14 AAR 456)) The Tribunal considers that s 157(1) grants a discretionary power in wide terms and that it is not for this Tribunal to impose an arbitrary limitation on the power not expressed in the words of the statute. The Tribunal is also mindful that the power is not unrestricted in its terms as it specifies that the Commissioner must be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised ...", and that a wide discretion is curtailed by the general purpose and policy for which the discretion is granted. The Tribunal in Re Lander considered that the discretion conferred by s 157(1) was, to some extent, limited by the use of the word "desirable". In Liddle it was said that the word "desirable" strongly suggests a positive connotation, that is something "worthy of achievement" rather then "merely advisable". Mr Refshauge, on behalf of the applicant, submitted that what is desirable in this instance is that the matter be decided in the interests of justice. Further it was argued that the interests of justice would be served by granting the applicant's request to make a late election for preservation of his superannuation benefits.



27. The statute is explicit that elections made under the superannuation fund must be made within the time limit stipulated. In the case of a superannuation fund preservation election, the time period is relatively short, being 1 month before resignation or 21 days after resignation. The Tribunal in Re Lander found that, prima facie, time limits are not to be ignored and therefore the discretion to grant a late election, pursuant to s 157(1), was tantamount to a right to apply for an extension of time. Proceeding on that understanding, the Tribunal in Re Lander drew upon the principles considered by the courts when dealing with applications under the Defence Forces Death and Retirement Benefits Act and Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act, in particular, the principles enunciated by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Limited & Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at pages 319-320. It was made clear in Re Lander that a person who seeks the exercise of the Commissioner's discretion in their favour must satisfy the Commissioner that the discretion should be so exercised.



28. Neither the applicant nor respondent took issue with the applicant bearing the practical onus of satisfying this Tribunal that the merits of the applicant's case warrant a favourable exercise of the discretion, however the applicant submitted that the respondent bears a similar evidentiary burden, that being; a duty to positively inform the applicant of all options available to him upon resignation in regards to his superannuation benefits. It was also submitted that the principles relating to application for extensions of time to lodge an appeal, as enunciated in Hunter Valley and applied in Re Lander and others, were not applicable to the exercise of a discretion pursuant to s 157(1). The applicant suggested that an application for an extension of time deals with the removal of procedural barriers and enables applicants to bring proceedings outside the time limited by the statute under which the reviewable decision was made, whereas section 157(1) presumes that an election has been made and then stipulates that the decision maker consider all the circumstances of the case before determining whether it is desirable to give effect to the late election. The applicant paid considerable attention to authorities dealing with legislative intent and drew the Tribunals attention to, in particular, a judgment of the High Court of Australia in FAI General Insurance Company Limited v Southern Cross Exploration N.L. ( Vol [1988] HCA 13; 165 CLR 268), where Wilson J., commenting upon the power to extend time pursuant to the Rules of the Supreme Court of New South Wales, said:

"The plain meaning of these words is very wide. The court may extend "any time" fixed by "any...order" and may do so as well after as before the time expires and even though the application to extend is not made until after the time is expired....The discretion so conferred is not readily to be limited by judicial fiat. The fact that it manifestly is a power to be exercised with caution and, in the case of conditional orders, with due regard to the public policy centred in the finality of litigation does not warrant an arbitrary limitation of power itself, not expressed in the words of the rule ...."



29. The Tribunal will rely on the principles applied by Wilcox J. in Hunter Valley and the Tribunal in Re Lander as tools helpful to the exercise of the discretion and not as principles to be applied rigorously. These principles are summarised as follows:

"... (i) prima facie, proceedings commenced outside the prescribed period will not be entertained. An extension of time will be granted, however, if it is proper to do so;



(ii) it is relevant whether the applicant rested on his rights or took action to make the decision-maker aware that the decision was being contested;



(iii) any prejudice to the respondent that would be caused by granting the extension of time is relevant;



(iv) any wider prejudice to the general public in terms of disruption to established practices is relevant;



(v) the merits of the substantial application are relevant; and



(vi) fairness of granting the extension of time as between the applicant and other persons in a like position is relevant ..."



30. These principles offer the Tribunal a guide to the exercise of the discretion, enabling the Tribunal to make a decision which is just and equitable between the parties. In considering these principles and their relevance to this application the Tribunal recognises in recent cases a broader approach to the exercise of the discretion to grant an extension of time. In A'Hearn v Comcare (unreported,16 April 1993), Hill J. said:

"... I should say that Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd was at pains to make clear that he was not seeking to set out principles of law governing the exercise of discretion to extend time. Care must be taken by the Tribunal to ensure that there is not a slavish adherence to the matters which are referred to in that judgement, which are listed merely as matters for guidance ..."



He noted later that:

"... [A]t the end of the day the discretion of a court or tribunal given power to extend time will be a wide one, to be exercised as the justice of the case requires ..."



A similar approach has been adopted in the decision of Pulitano and Telstra Corporation Ltd. (N93/233, 27 July 1993), in which it was said that it is for the Tribunal to "do what is just and equitable between the parties" when exercising its discretion to grant or refuse an extension of time.



31. In considering this application for the recognition of a late election to preserve superannuation benefits, the Tribunal notes that superannuation legislation is beneficial legislation, in that it is an Act which makes provision for an Superannuation Scheme for persons employed by the Commonwealth, and therefore it is open to the Tribunal to construe the Act liberally if the language of the section allows. It is not, in the Tribunal's opinion, appropriate that a narrow approach be adopted in the exercise of the discretion to recognise the late election; the discretion should be exercised so as to reflect what is just and equitable between the parties on the facts of the individual case.



32. The Tribunal in Re Lander considered each of the principles enunciated by Wilcox J. in Hunter Valley and found that it was not desirable to recognise Mrs Lander's late election. The Tribunal in that instance were particularly influenced by the fact that Mrs Lander was located in Canberra, that her rights had been carefully explained to her by a personnel officer and that she had the personal ability and the opportunity to make inquiries. The Tribunal in the decision of Papadopolous and Commissioner for Superannuation (A91/217, 29 June 1993), applied a similar approach and also found that it was not desirable to allow the applicant to make a late election. In this application, the Tribunal considers it relevant to focus on the explanation of delay given by the applicant and whether it is fair and equitable in the circumstances to recognise the late election, a consideration of the prejudice caused to the respondent, an examination of the merits of the application and a consideration of any other matters relevant to the proceedings raised by either party.



33. It was the applicant's evidence that, upon resignation, he did not know of the right to preserve superannuation benefits, and in ignorance of the availability of this option he completed the form S2A, requesting a refund of his contributions. Prior to the completion of the form the applicant underwent an exit interview, at which no information on superannuation entitlements was provided and therefore failed to disabuse him of his belief. It was the evidence of Mr Vincent that a Superannuation manual was not available at the Queanbeyan office of National Mapping. Throughout the hearing it became apparent to the Tribunal that information regarding superannuation, at the time of the applicant's resignation, was passed informally by word of mouth, and that the applicant was advised by other employees and his section head that the only course of action available to him was to request a refund.



34. The Tribunal is not satisfied that specific information on superannuation was made available to the applicant and therefore it is appropriate to ascertain whether the applicant made any inquiries of his personnel officer or the office of the Commissioner, bearing in mind the opportunities afforded to the applicant to inquire. The applicant was not informed by his personnel officer, Mr Wadey, of the options available to him. At the relevant period, the applicant was working at National Mapping, Morisset House Queanbeyan, and isolated from the Department of which National Mapping was a section. It was his evidence that he was not aware that the personnel section of his department was located at the AMP Building, Canberra, and that when he was completing the Form S 2A, which was sent to the applicant whilst he resided at Jugiong, he did not make inquiries as to the options available to him.



35. Of relevance to the Tribunal in its deliberation is the applicant's evidence that much of his employment was concerned with the practical aspects of field trips and surveying rather then with administrative matters. The applicant was, during the relevant period, a middle ranking public servant with limited experience in administration or personnel matters. It was his belief that the form S2A was a multi purpose form and that the section titled `preservation' was only available to retirees. It is evident to the Tribunal that the applicant received no personal communication to inform him that his assumption was incorrect, and his ability to inquire must therefore be assessed in this context. The applicant approached the form S2A and his resignation with the mindset that a refund of contributions was the only option available. The Tribunal notes that the applicant was not alone in this misunderstanding as the plethora of late election cases before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal indicate, however in this instance the applicant, primarily a field worker who was geographically separated from his Department's personnel section, left the employ of National mapping with no knowledge of his right to preserve his entitlements. The Tribunal is of the opinion that the Form S2A, contrary to the respondent's submission, was ambiguous, and was not sufficient to alter the applicant's belief that the only available option was to claim a refund.



36. When the applicant rejoined the public service in 1980 and the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme, having been absent for little over one year, he did not undergo an induction course and was not provided with details of the rights of contributors incorporating instructions about how a contributor who resigned could elect to retain his superannuation benefits, as was the situation in the matter of Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT 8609, 19 March 1993). The Tribunal in that instance found that it was reasonable to expect the applicant to make inquiries about his position at the time of his earlier resignation, and decided that the continued delay in making inquiries made it not desirable to allow Mr Secourable to make a late election. These facts distinguish re Secourable from the present case before this Tribunal.



37. For these reasons the Tribunal finds that at the date of resignation the applicant was not fully informed of his rights, nor was he negligent in failing to inquire and inform himself of those rights. In this respect the Tribunal is of the opinion that the respondent ought to have taken reasonable steps to advise the applicant adequately of his rights.



38. In the years 1987 and 1988 the applicant did however receive and read the annual information statements for contributors which, the respondent submitted, gave him ample opportunity to inquire about his superannuation rights on resignation. The applicant informed the Tribunal that he did not read the back of the statements because, as far as he was concerned, the only information that was of relevance to him were the details of his contribution and interest accumulation, as it was his belief that he had made an election and that a reconsideration of that election was not available to him. The Tribunal finds that the mention in general terms on the reverse side of a pamphlet in 1987 and 1988 that an option to preserve rights on resignation existed at that time was not sufficient to put the applicant on notice that he should inquire about his rights in 1978 and 1979.



39. When the applicant before this Tribunal was made aware of those rights in 1989 he acted promptly in making further injuries and applied for a late election to preserve his superannuation rights. This leads the Tribunal to the consideration of prejudice caused to the respondent by the application for late election being 11 years after the applicant's resignation. The Tribunal in re Lander opined that the length of delay is material in considering the extent of the prejudice, as "[t]he longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and exercising a late election, the more difficult it will be to establish that facts and degree of knowledge available to the applicant on resignation".



40. In many of the cases over which the Tribunal has jurisdiction (for example compensation) it is often difficult to piece together a coherent case if there are significant gaps in the evidence. These gaps may occur, for example, if the respondent is of the belief that a matter is finalised and there is no need to collect medical and other evidence to assess and eventually contest the applicant's claim. Equally a witness may be difficult to locate and their memory of the issue or incident may be less then accurate. These considerations have not hindered the applicant's or respondent's case in the matter before the Tribunal. There have been no significant gaps in the evidence as all relevant documentation was before the Tribunal and persons involved in the applicant's resignation and relevant to the proceedings appeared before the Tribunal.



41. The respondent indicated that recognition of the applicant's late election would prejudice the funds stability and its ability to invest, however the respondent did not adduce particular evidence of a persuasive nature as to considerations of wider prejudice to the general public, or to the fund in terms of disruption of general practices or financial disadvantage to the fund. The approach to legislative interpretation that the Tribunal has outlined indicates that it is not for the Tribunal to fetter an applicant's legislative entitlements on the basis of future applications and the impact that they may have on the fund. Such future considerations are not sufficient to disentitle this applicant.



42. The Tribunal has found it helpful to consider recent decisions in similar cases but concludes that the exercise of the discretion pursuant to s 157(1) warrants that each case must be considered in light of its own circumstances. For the reasons given above the Tribunal finds it is desirable that the late election be recognised and treated as if it has been made within the period prescribed in s 137 of the Act.



43. The decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the election made by the applicant on 26 May 1989 under section s 137(1) of the Act, be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section.



The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the applicant's entitlement in light of this decision.
