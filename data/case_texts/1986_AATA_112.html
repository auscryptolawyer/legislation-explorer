Re Adele Laura Carr and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 112 (23 April 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ADELE LAURA CARR

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A83/45

AAT No. 2642

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

E. Smith (Senior Member)

Dr D.J. Howell (Member)

Mrs J.H. McClintock (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - applicant a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1976 who had been accepted into Provident Account under Superannuation Act 1922 - alleged history of anxiety and depression - in fact due to marriage, family and religious problems - deemed benefit classification certificate - schwannoma in right eye and removal of right optic nerve and other physical problems arising later - subsequent retirement on account of anxiety and depressive reactions - unrelated to earlier episode - benefit entitlement - conflict of medical evidence between treating psychiatrist and Senior Commonwealth Medical Officer.

Words and Phrases: "connected with"

Superannuation Act 1922, s.5

Superannuation Act 1976, s.16, 66 and 184

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller (G.39 of 1985) - not yet reported, decision given 28 November 1985.

HEARING

CANBERRA

23:4:1986

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the Commissioner for Superannuation for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the applicant's entitlements under the Superannuation Act 1976 are to be determined under sub-section (1) of section 66 of that Act.

Liberty to apply is reserved to both parties.

DECISION

The applicant has sought review of the decision of the respondent, confirming the decision taken by his delegate on 18 November 1977, that the benefit entitlement of the applicant on her ceasing to be an eligible employee on 1 September 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

2. In so deciding, the Commissioner stated that he was satisfied that the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor under Part III of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act") in 1973 by reason of the condition "anxiety-depression". The applicant was, immediately before the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976 on 1 July 1976, a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act and a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1976 on 1 July 1976, and the Commissioner was of the view that under s.184(2) of the latter Act there was deemed to be in force in respect of her on that date a benefit classification certificate specifying the condition "anxiety-depression". The respondent was further of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for retirement of the applicant was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the condition anxiety-depression or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with that condition. In coming to the last-mentioned conclusion, the respondent relied on the letter dated 19 January 1979 from the Director, N.S.W. Division, Department of Health (T.4/91), which stated, inter alia, that " . . . There is definite evidence that Mrs Carr did suffer from a nervous disorder that required medical attention before her appointment in 1971" and went on to say that "The Commonwealth Medical Officer is still of the opinion that Mrs Carr's retirement was due to a pre-appointment condition, namely Anxiety-Depression which could have been aggravated by the above neurosurgical and gynaecological condition". The reference to "the above neurosurgical and gynaecological condition" was a reference to treatment by a Neurosurgeon and a Gynaecologist subsequent to her appointment and discussed below.

4. In addition to the documents lodged in pursuance of s. 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (identified by the respective T numbers assigned to them in the accompanying Index) and other documentary evidence received by the Tribunal, oral evidence was given by the applicant, her husband, Dr Phillip Greenberg (a psychiatrist), Dr Brian Burke (a psychiatrist), Dr G.W. de Meyrick ( a Senior Commonwealth Medical Officer) and Dr Y. Keshishian ( a Commonwealth Medical Officer).

5. It is desirable at the outset to outline the history of the applicant's service with the Commonwealth. She first joined the Department of Navy in February 1967, under the name of Adele Laura Judson (her then married name). At that stage there was no question concerning her health and she was accepted into the superannuation fund. However, on 15 March 1971, her husband left her and her small child, Laura (aged 4 years), and she suffered the combined difficulties and trauma of the marriage breakup and being left to support a young child. There was also serious family and religious problems arising from her relationship with Mr Carr. On 6 August 1971 she resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service with effect from 26 August 1971. Her reasons for resignation were stated as hassles she was having with divorce proceedings and her need to find a position closer to home to look after her child; she also said that she was "under a bit of pressure at work". She re-applied for temporary employment with the Commonwealth and became a permanent officer again in May 1972. In May or June 1971 she had consulted Dr Burke, to whom she had been referred by her general practitioner. She saw Dr Burke about once a month and stated that she wished to discuss her problems with him. Her problems, she said, stemmed from the fact that in June 1971 she had formed a relationship with her present husband (Brian Hugh Carr) and the formation of that relationship created substantial difficulties for her on religious and moral grounds. She was a Roman Catholic as were all her family. Mrs Carr was herself, as were her parents, born in Italy. Her family's opposition to the situation meant she had no-one with whom she could discuss her problems. She had therefore turned to Dr Burke.

6. On 14 October 1971 she was medically examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer without reference to the fact that she had consulted Dr Burke and it was initially recommended that she be accepted for the Superannuation Fund (T.4/21) as against the Provident Account. However, on 16 November 1971 the Commonwealth Medical Officer, become aware of a certificate from Dr Burke given for the purpose of Mr Carr's absence from work on 6 October 1971 (T.4/23), which referred to "an unsuccessful suicidal gesture" by the applicant. She was then accepted for temporary employment only and "appealed" against that decision (T.4/24). The applicant produced a further certificate from Dr Burke, dated 15 February 1972, which stated that she had been consulting him regarding domestic and marital problems rather than seeking psychiatric treatment as such. He stated that she was quite fit and capable of carrying out any of the positions for which she had applied in the Public Service. She was seen on 2 March 1972 (T.4/28) by a Commonwealth Medical Officer who noted that, while "she looks very reasonable and her reports are satisfactory", she should have a psychiatric report before final decision. Following that, she was seen by Dr. H. Phillip Greenberg. Dr Greenberg's report, dated 18 April 1972, stated that in the process of working out her marital difficulties, with the help of her psychiatrist, she had been prescribed some Valium, some 5 mgm and that, on one occasion being particularly distressed by insomnia, she had taken 3 tablets. As a result her de facto husband (Mr Carr) had become alarmed and this had resulted in a certificate being issued (to cover his absence from work) by Dr Burke stating that she had made a "suicidal gesture". Dr Greenberg stated that, at the interview, the applicant appeared to be a stable and intelligent woman who evinced no indications of psychiatric disorder. He concluded that he considered that she was not suffering from any psychiatric disorder and accepted that at no time did she make an attempt to commit suicide. Accordingly, he considered that she was quite fit for permanent employment in the Public Service and had reasonable prospects of rendering satisfactory service up to the normal retiring age.

7. On 20 April 1972 the Director of Health through Dr Keshishian recommended that she contribute to the Provident Account but could, after a reasonable period of employment, say 4 years, apply for transfer to the Superannuation Fund. Accordingly, she was accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account (T.4/33). There followed a period until late 1975 when the applicant served in the Commonwealth Public Service satisfactorily, gaining various promotions without any health problems of any significance at all. However, in late 1975 symptoms appeared on what was ultimately found to be a tumour (a schwannoma) affecting the right optic nerve.

8. She was operated upon by Dr Grant on 6 January 1976 to remove the schwannoma. She was aware at the time that a tumour of that kind in that place put her at risk of either total blindness, if the tumour affected both optic nerves, or death if the tumour could not be completely removed. Fortunately, it appeared that the tumour was confined to the one eye and was in fact successfully removed. But her right eye and right eye socket were significantly deformed by the operation and the effect of the removal of the right optic nerve. The Tribunal observed that that was so.

9. On her discharge from hospital on 26 January 1976, she was suffering from a bronchial infection and in March had a severe bout of that bronchitis. The bronchitis persisted to the end of May.

10. The applicant's general practitioner (Dr Clark) considered that she was not fit for duty and she was examined by a number of Commonwealth Medical Officers and other doctors. In the result retirement was not recommended. The applicant returned to work on 13 September 1976 for two weeks and then was granted sick leave. She was seen by Dr Innes Brodziak, a specialist physician, at the Commonwealth's request, who noted that she "had experienced a severe disability in the loss of her eyesight in one eye and also had considerable domestic worry and is still in a nervous state but has a real disability in a gynaecological condition for which . . . . she has been advised operation". There was reference also to the applicant's mother's severe illness. On 11 October 1976 Dr Clark wrote to her employer stating that her health had not improved as had been expected and he would not pronounce her fit for any occupation on a full-time basis until her current problems had been settled and her health generally showed an up-hill trent following adequate convalescence (T.4/48).

11. On 1 December 1976 Dr Quinn of the Department of Health examined the various medical reports and concluded that the applicant should not be retired (T.4/54). On 20 January 1977 a Commonwealth Medical Officer reported that she should continue with sick leave but indicated that she could not be retired unless she was visually permanently incapacitated. Gynaecological treatment of the applicant followed and on 22 August 1977 various other reports (by Dr Grant, Dr Thong and Dr Burke) were noted. It was stated that Dr Burke had suggested she be superannuated due to anxiety, depressive reaction. The Commonwealth Medical Officer's view stated:

"In summary 1) Schwannoma - no evidence of

recurrence - prognosis uncertain but she is

obviously convinced that it will recur. 2) Gynae

disorder - this could be cured by hysterectomy but

there could be psychological reactions in her case.

3) Anxiety Depression - this was definitely pre

appointment and is the main reason why she will not

work.

I feel she should be retired due to anxiety

depression which has been aggravated by the

Schwannoma. She could be reviewed in 12/12 when it

is possible that she may be fit to be re-instated."

Dr Burke had provided a report on 2 August 1977 (T.4/67). In part, the report said:

"Her condition has not changed significantly since

my last report and as she appears to be almost

certain to be faced by the continuing worries about

the possibility of future further deterioration in

her eyesight as well as on-going serious

gynaecological difficulties, I cannot see much

prospect of her recovering from her anxiety and

depressive reactions to the point where she will be

employable within the forseeable future".

12. In the result, a recommendation was made by the Commonwealth Medical Officer (T.4/61) that the applicant be retired on grounds of anxiety neurosis. The recommendation noted that she was "on Provident a/c this diagnosis", and suggested she be reviewed again in 12 months, "as it is quite possible that her condition could be such that (she) would be fit to resume work".

13. On 29 August 1977 a report of retirement examination was made and the finding was that the applicant suffered from "depression/anxiety neurosis-exacerbation". This condition was stated to be "pre-employment - causing her to be classified as fit for Provident only". On 3 September 1977 Dr Lilley of the Department of Health recommended as follows (T.4/68):

"I concur. Mrs Carr has a benefit classification

for anxiety/depression. Anxiety over the removal of

the right optic nerve tumour has exacerbated her

pre-existing condition".

(In fact, there was no benefit classification certificate in force at that stage, though one was, in appropriate cases, deemed to be in force by virtue of s. 184 of the Act in its then form).

14. On 29 September 1977 the applicant was retired by a delegate of the Public Service Board (T.4/69). In a letter to her dated 28 October 1977 (T.4/72), she was advised by the respondent that, as she was a contributor to the Provident Account when the new superannuation scheme was introduced on 1 July 1976 there was, under the Superannuation Act 1976, deemed to be in force in respect of her a Benefit Classification Certificate specifying the physical (or mental) conditions which resulted in her original acceptance "as a contributor to the Provident Account rather than the Pension Scheme". Pending a decision as to whether the Commissioner was of the opinion that her retirement on invalidity grounds was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a condition specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate or by a condition connected with such a condition, the amount of her pension was advised. It was stated that she would be advised further "in regard to the conditions specified on your Benefit Classification Certificate". Such a certificate was not drawn up and signed until 21 November 1977 and it specified "anxiety/depression" as the condition by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act. A delegate of the Commissioner determined on 18 November 1977 that the "disabilities" that caused her retirement and the disability at acceptance were "related" and that section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 applied accordingly (T4/74).

15. The applicant was, on 5 December 1977, notified of the certificate and of the decision. She requested a section 154 review, and on 26 February 1979 a reconsideration affirmed the decision. The only additional medical report considered seems to have been the report of the Commonwealth Medical Officer at T4/91 that he was still of the opinion that the applicant's retirement was due to a pre-appointment condition, namely, anxiety/ depression, which could have been aggravated by the neurosurgical/gynaecological condition.

16. The applicant gave evidence as to her matrimonial problems as outlined above and stated that the reasons for her resignation in August 1971 was due to the hassles she was having with the proceedings for divorce, a need for her to find a position closer to home and the pressures she felt at work. She detailed the problems she had arising from her religious beliefs, her family's attitude and the new relationship she had formed with Mr Carr. She explained the alleged suicide gesture in October 1971 as involving the taking of 3 valium 5 mgm tablets in order to obtain "a couple of hours sleep". Although Mr Carr appears, perhaps due to his lack of knowledge of the consequences of the taking of the few tablets, to have taken some alarm at this occurrence, leading to his seeking a certificate from Dr Burke's receptionist (he did not speak to Dr Burke himself), we do not regard this incident as either a suicide attempt or a suicidal gesture. Indeed, Mrs Carr stated, and we accept her evidence on this point, that the three tablets did not really send her to sleep.

17. About August of 1975 the applicant said that she began to get violent headaches at the front of her head and ultimately was referred to a neurologist, Dr John Polgar, and, as she put it, "went from his rooms directly to the hospital" where Dr Grant performed a craniotomy. Asked about her feelings mentally, she said:

"I was scared. I was worried. I was very afraid."

At the hospital she had a craniotomy to remove the schwannoma and after the operation felt "shaky, unsure, uncertain". She said also that she started developing bronchitis, and did not seem to be able to shake it off and her general practitioner put her off work.

18. She also gave evidence that she had then developed gynaecological problems which caused considerable haemorrhaging and said that she felt mentally "Wrung out". She was in a lot of discomfort and pain and felt frustrated about being unable to return to work. She denied that she saw any doctors who had to do with the speciality of psychiatry in 1976. She saw Dr Burke again in 1977. She said:

"I felt I was not coping adequately with the

aftermath of the craniotomy and knowing Dr Burke

from previously I knew I could go back to him and

trust him and get back on top."

She said that she saw Dr Burke with that end in mind on perhaps 6 occasions in 1977.

19. It is clear enough from the evidence that the applicant was extremely distressed in 1971. There was every reason for her to be, given her background, her religious problems, the break-up of her marriage and her new association with her present husband. The respondent sought to add to that, however, problems she had had in her earlier employment with the Department of the Navy concerning her inability to accept discipline, particularly in relation to the making of excessive private telephone calls and adverse reports by her superiors (T.4/17). While accepting that the applicant may not in all respects have been an ideal employee, this does not, in our opinion, add much, if anything, to the medical evidence before us as to the medical condition she was suffering from at the time of her retirement, or, indeed, earlier. It should be noted that the complaints related largely to the period when she was extremely distressed in 1971.

20. The valium incident we find quite unconvincing. While at first sight, and taking all that was before the respondent at face value, there may appear to be some credibility in it, upon examination of the incident in the light of the evidence of Mr Carr and Dr Burke, we are persuaded that there was none. It provides no basis for reaching a conclusion that the applicant suffered from a mental disorder. Perhaps Dr Burke was unwise to have issued the certificate to Mr Carr in respect of Mr Carr's absence from employment, but be that as it may we are convinced that the taking of three 5 mgm valium tablets cannot even be seriously regarded as a suicidal gesture, far less a suicide attempt, and that Mr Carr's "alarm" at the event was misplaced: the applicant's evidence was that the tablets did not even put her to sleep. So much of the respondent's case as depended on that incident must fail. We quote the following passages from Dr Burke's evidence in chief on the subject. We do not think it necessary to do more:

"Mr Higgins: That is the document which refers to

Mrs Judson suffered from a depressive illness, made

an unsuccessful suicidal gesture on Monday, 4

October; do you see that? --- Yes.

Now, firstly, can you tell the tribunal what you

can recollect about the circumstances in which you

wrote that certificate? --- Yes, I happily can

remember that very clearly. I think I received a

telephone call, or my secretary did, from Mr Carr.

I was extremely busy at the time. He told her that

he - - -

Let us make it clear. This is something your

secretary reported to you? --- Broke into an

interview which I was - the consultation I had with

someone else at the time, told me that Mr Carr

believed that his wife had taken an overdose of

Valium and he felt and appeared to her to be

extremely agitated. I saw him very briefly, I

think, for a moment when he came in and I can only

agree with her reading of his condition or his

feelings. He was concerned as to what the effects

of it would be, that he should stay at home with

her at least for that day, perhaps for two or three

days depending on what might eventuate and I wrote

that certificate out in consequence to give him

time off from the Navy."

The Doctor's notes were then obtained and Mr Higgins went on:

"Doctor, I asked you I think whether you had

received any communication from Mrs Carr herself

following the communication from her husband in

October 1971. Having perused your file, are you

able to say? --- No, in fact I did not see her and

what clearly happened was that the - that her

husband - well, Mr Carr, was unaware of the

potential that Valium might have in terms of danger

and dashed into my surgery to tell me, or rang my

secretary - actually I do not have that - that is

not clearly - that point is not clearly made. I

did not see Mrs Carr and she did not tell me that,

no, it was her husband. He was concerned."

Dr Howell also asked Dr Burke concerning this certificate:

". . . . In hindsight, would you have used those terms

now?

Dr Burke answered:

"No, . . . . the information gave (came) from Mr Carr

not from Mrs Carr, and it is clearly fallacious in

terms of - I do not even know that it is untrue,

but it is medically not valid."

21. Dr Greenberg, a psychiatrist of considerable standing, said the applicant was not suffering from any psychiatric disorder or anything that might be described as a nervous, emotional or mental illness when he saw her in April 1972. He said that he had turned his mind to whether the applicant might have been suffering from a neurotic illness of any description and he had concluded she was not. He said she had "reacted to marital stress in a way in which a large proportion of the population would, and I did not think that one needed to postulate any abnormality on her part to have so reacted, to have needed to talk to her doctor and to have taken mild sedatives".

22. Dr Burke was the key witness for the applicant. He, like Dr Greenberg, is a specialist psychiatrist of considerable standing. He has practised his speciality since 1957. He said, in answer to questions by Mr Higgins, that there had been a series of consultations - at monthly intervals - with the applicant for at least six months. (It should be noted that in cross-examination he extended this period to some ten months). He described the cause of her depression in the following terms:

"Simply that she was in a great deal of conflict

regarding her total family and her religious

situation. The family, as I said, the father in

particular, (was) mistakenly opposed to what was

happening - she had the problem of her husband

having formed another liaison, as I recall, and of

course her own Catholic upbringing which was a very

strict one created a great deal of conflict for

her."

He went on, in answer to further questions put by Mr Higgins, as follows:

"Now, did you form any view about whether the

symptoms of depressive illness which you noted,

were indicative of any chronic or underlying

condition? --- No.

When you say no, did you form the view there was

such a condition or that there was not such a

condition? --- I am sorry, there was no reason for

me to believe that she had suffered from any

depressive illness, or depressive symptoms prior to

my seeing her at that time.

And did you regard the onset of these symptoms in

the circumstances, as you understood them, to be

indicative of any deeper disorder? --- No.

How did you regard it then? What was the

explanation of it? --- I felt that - well, I

considered that I was seeing a patient who had been

well-adjusted throughout her life, but because of

the situations which I have referred to had

developed, as we all can, a psychoneurotic illness,

that being a depressive illness.

Is the formation of such depressive symptoms then

to be regarded as a normal consequence of the sort

of pressures that you understood Mrs Judson to be

under, or was it an abnormal reaction? --- I

thought it could be regarded as normal.

Now, as you understand it, that depressive illness

continued to require your attention for some six

months or thereabouts? --- That is correct.

At the conclusion of that period of time, what did

you consider to be Mrs Judson's condition in

relation to that psychiatric illness? --- She

appeared to have resolved the problem to the point

where she was free of symptoms.

And if we could then have regard to her situation

as at that time, would you have regarded her then

as a person suffering from any medical condition?

--- No."

Dr Burke added that the applicant had not consulted him again until 1976, when the other physical problems referred to earlier had developed.

23. In relation to those later problems, Dr Burke, having been referred to Dr de Meyrick's view that the applicant was a neurotic person because her reactions and the time to recover from the stressful reactions, were out of proportion to the stress created, said "I think the stress she suffered was sufficient to explain the problems which arose". He was then asked "And their duration?" and answered "Yes".

24. Dr Burke was asked whether the applicant's condition was "an underlying chronic condition". His answer was:

"I think we are perhaps using some of those words

loosely. Anyone with a significant level of

neurotic illness without there being quite serious

practical problems underlying the symptoms and

might well see a psychiatrist once a week and I

have many such patients for a period of, say, ten

years. And when I use the word chronic I - looking

at the period that was involved in that kind of way

I would have referred to that as a chronic anxiety

condition or depressive illness."

He said that was a different case to Mrs Carr.

25. We are satisfied that Dr Burke's basic evidence was not shaken by Mr Peters, for the respondent, who sought to establish that the applicant exhibited personality traits adding up to a personality disorder, and we do not consider it necessary to go into any further details on that aspect. It should be noted that Dr Burke did agree, in answer to questions from Mr Peters, that one picture that would be justified from the 1976 facts would be a repetition of the same kind as occurred in 1971, a maladaptive reaction to stress; but he added in answer to further questions:

"It is an erotic (a neurotic) ego defence mechanism

that she was again displaying? --- Not if we use

neurotic again now. It was certainly an ego

defence mechanism and in that she was emotionally

disturbed it could be labelled neurotic - regarded

as neurotic. But on the other hand mind you,

people under a great deal of stress can suffer a

variety of symptoms which can only be regarded as

normal.

The symptoms can only be regarded as dormant? ---

Normal, I am sorry."

26. Dr Burke, in re-examination, expressed the following opinions to Mr Higgins:

"First of all you drew a distinction as I understand

it between a person who had an obsessive

personality and a person who had an obsessive

personality disorder? --- Yes.

Could I invite you just to indicate whether the

obsessive personality is a character trait shared

by a percentage of the population, if so how many

would you estimate? --- Yes. And in fact it may be

an extremely useful one. Where a person for

example has to attend carefully to a job situation

or any other aspect of life, they will do it with

conscientiousness which has - which can only be

regarded as admirable.

And would you accept a proposition which suggested

to you that a person with such a personality would

be unlikely to attain their normal retiring age due

to the normal stresses of a work environment? ---

No.

Would you reject that? --- Yes.

And did you see anything in the reactions which Mrs

Carr had - having regard to the matters my learned

friend put to you - do you still adhere to the

opinion you put before? --- Yes."

27. Dr Keshishian, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, of some 18 years' standing, had examined the applicant (then Mrs Judson) in 1971, but had no independent recollection of his examination of her. He said he had not got any history of anything that could prevent him from approving her for superannuation, so he had approved her; but "later on, I must have got hold of a certificate from Dr Burke, the psychiatrist, so, on the basis of that certificate, I changed my decision". He wrote, "Reject - fit temporary employment only". Dr Burke's certificate referred to was the certificate of 6 October 1971, stating that after the applicant's "unsuccessful suicide gesture" on 4 October 1971 it was advisable that her de facto husband remain at home with her for 6-8 October (inclusive). Dr Keshishian said that he thought there must have been some depressive condition underlying; then he had received Dr Burke's second report of 15 February 1972 saying the applicant had consulted him concerning "domestic and marital problems rather than seeking psychiatric treatment as such" and stating that in his opinion she could carry out any of the positions she had applied for in the Public Service. Dr Keshishian said he could not have recommended her for the Superannuation Fund because in Dr Burke's original certificate he had stated that she was "suffering from a depressive illness" and had been under his care since 4 June 1971. Dr Keshishian referred to paragraph 100(b) of the Guidelines for Medical Officers in connection with examinations of candidates for admission to the Public Service and the benefits of the Superannuation Act. He stated that all he had before him at that time was a report from a psychiatrist (Dr Burke) saying that the applicant was suffering from depressive illness. He had understood the reference to "unsuccessful suicide attempt" to imply that she had attempted to commit suicide - that she had tried to take her own life. He conceded that he could see nothing wrong with her mental health when he examined her. He agreed that a suicide gesture was different from an attempt to commit suicide. He could not recall asking the applicant anything about this document. He also conceded that the words "suffering from a depressive illness" could imply a variety of conditions, including a feeling of depression and anxiety following a separation from one's spouse.

28. Dr Keshishian said that even if Dr Greenberg had specifically stated that the applicant was not suffering from any underlying, abnormal condition and that she was a perfectly normal woman, suffering from the effects of depression which any normal person in her situation would be expected to suffer, he would still have put her "on Provident Fund", because her personal psychiatrist had been treating her for depression and had stated in his original certificate that he had been treating her for that condition for some six months. He also stated that he had taken account of the report from the Department of the Navy (presumably the report of 12 October 1971), stating that "towards the end of her service her behaviour became erratic", and attaching a copy of Dr Burke's certificate of 6 October 1971. He conceded that people could be strong-willed and irritable, and react unfavourably to discipline, but said that "sometimes we take the whole picture to get if there is a history of depression and that the personality itself - that is how we work it out sometimes, we put one to one and say, well, it is two".

29. Dr de Meyrick, a Senior Medical Officer in the Department of Health, had signed the medical officer's report on 29 August 1977 (T.4/68) recommending retirement on the grounds of invalidity. He explained the inclusion of the comment in the report that the condition of Depression/Anxiety neurosis-exacerbation (the grounds for retirement as he found them) "was pre-employment" as follows:

"Well, first, there would have been on the result of

the first examination the information I was given

then that she - Mrs Carr was on provident, and the

reason for being on provident was the anxiety

depression - or depression anxiety - I forget which

order it came in, and then that seemed to be

reinforced, in my mind, by the events that followed

that. First of all there was the rather prolonged

period, I thought, for a person to attend a

specialist psychiatrist for depression, which, as

you will probably realize, with neurosis, they are

perfectly normal in most ways, but, under stress,

act abnormally. Well, I do agree. I think anyone

who has marital disorder or discord must be

depressed, but the average person, normal person,

can cope with it, may see a psychiatrist, but not

require to see a psychiatrist for 7, 8, 9 months.

Admittedly, it might not have been for treatment,

but she was still seeing a psychiatrist. So that,

first of all, gave me the impression that she -

sorry, Mrs Carr was suffering from a neurosis. In

addition, we had the report from her department

saying that Mrs Carr did suffer with, I think, hot

tempered - or some such word, which, once more, can

be a normal reaction. But to be refused

re-employment, I felt was rather a little above the

normal. Then we have the gynaecological problems,

which were distressing, there is no doubt about

that; I must be - thank God, I would not know

personally, but they must be distressing. But her

own doctor said that they, in themselves, would not

be sufficient, physically, to stop her working.

Then we have the neurologist who said that she did

have limitation of vision - and it is her monocular

vision - and that could cause some trouble as far

as work, but the ophthalmic surgeon said that there

was no reason why she should not continue at work.

And then we have Dr Brodziak's reports about the

physical state, so - by that I mean that she has

had a series of physical conditions, but has had an

abnormal reaction to each of these. Where the

average person gets over them and continues, Mrs

Carr, due to my interpretation of the neurosis -

anxiety neurosis, was not able to overcome those

conditions herself and required medical treatment

for it."

30. He went on to say, when asked whether he was still of the opinion that the applicant's retirement was due to a pre-appointment condition, namely, anxiety depression which could have been aggravated by the neurosurgical and gynaecological condition:

"Well, as I mentioned, with the - we all suffer

stress, and we all suffer reaction to it, but in my

interpretation of a neurosis, is the person that

cannot overcome these ones without assistance; not

on one occasion, but on regular occasions. And I

felt that each of these, the gynaecological

problem, where she had the unfortunate menstrual

disorder, that was sufficient to - sorry, that she

reacted in a very severe way to that stress. And

the same thing, with losing the sight of one eye,

she reacted quite normally in a distressing manner,

but did not recover in the time that one would

expect her to recover."

31. In cross-examination, Dr de Meyrick conceded he was not a psychiatrist; but he said he based his opinion on 20 years of general practice. He said he would debate the fact that a psychiatrist with equally lengthy experience might be better qualified to express a view whether the reaction of the applicant was normal or abnormal. He said he had the history of events "which fit in very clearly with a typical neurosis". Dr de Meyrick went further and said that, if he got a psychiatric opinion that at the time there was no evidence of any neurosis, that did not mean there was not one. He admitted he saw no signs of anxiety or depression in the applicant but said that he had her history and relied on his understanding of the history. The following exchange between Mr Higgins and the Doctor followed:

"Again, I take it that if Dr Burke was to say that

in his view the reaction was quite normal, you

would simply just not accept that. Is that right -

Well, we have to have Dr Burke saying it is not

normal.

I appreciate that, but I am asking you to make an

assumption? - Well, once more I can only go by my

20 years in general practice.

. . . . . . . . . . . .

So, you will not make that assumption, is that what

you are saying? - You are assuming that Dr Burke

will say what I . . . . .

Yes, doctor, I certainly am? - . . . . . is complety

wrong.

Yes, that is exactly what I am putting to you. You

have got it right? - . . . . You hope Dr Burke to say

that.

Yes? - Right, well, I still go by my reaction.

. . . . . . . . . . . .

Yes. So you are saying that notwithstanding

anything anyone else may say . . . . ? - Oh, no, I am

not saying that.

. . . . . this woman suffers from a neurosis? - Yes."

32. We understand Dr de Meyrick's point of view, but we are unable to accept it. On the evidence before him, it no doubt appeared to him a reasonable proposition that the applicant probably suffered from a neurosis. But he did not have all the facts that the Tribunal has, particularly Dr Burke's full views as put in evidence explaining his certificate and his opinions; nor do we accept the suggestion that Dr de Meyrick's views as to the relative merits of the opinions of psychiatrists of high standing and his own opinions, particularly a treating psychiatrist who had seen the applicant on many occasions. We must accept the former as having the greater weight unless counter-balanced by others of similar standing. No such evidence was forthcoming.

33. In the result, we find that the applicant, when retired in September 1977, was suffering from anxiety and depressive reactions, but that that was not a "pre-employment" condition. The incapacity which was the ground for her retirement was the result of her severe period of misfortunes commencing with the symptoms of the tumour on or in her right optic nerve, the worry associated with that problem, the craniotomy to remove the tumour, the consequent effect on her appearance, the chronic bronchitis which developed, and the subsequent gynaecological problems. We find that the incapacity which was the ground for her retirement was not caused by or substantially contributed to by any physical or mental condition from which she was suffering before she rejoined the Public Service in 1972 and became a contributor to the Provident Account or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition. In this regard, we respectfully agree with and adopt the views of Davies J., and Pincus J. in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller ((G.39 of 1985) not yet reported, decision given 28 November 1985) as to the meaning of "connected with" in the context of the provisions of the Superannuation Act. In particular, we adopt the view stated by Davies J. where he said:

"In the context, I would read the words 'connected

with' in ss.16(10) (b) and 66(2) (c) as dealing with

the circumstance where the immediate or proximate

cause of the incapacity leading to the retirement

was a medical condition other than the condition

existing at the time the employee became an

eligible employee but where, nevertheless, there is

a chain of causation such that the medical

condition existing at the time when the employee

became an eligible employee can be said, through a

chain of events, to have caused or substantially

contributed to the incapacity causing the

retirement."

34. In particular, we find that the applicant was not suffering from any underlying personality disorder in 1971-1972. What she was suffering from, and this was amply confirmed by Dr Burke, was the effects of her marriage break-up, the relationship with her now husband and the consequent family and religious problems that were worrying her. It was not in truth a medical disorder at all. This may give rise to some difficulty in identifying the condition by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the superseded Act for the purposes of s. 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 in its form at the relevant time. The reason for the Board's action probably can best be described as being a suspicion of there having been a history of anxiety and depression; our findings dispel that suspicion.

35. We may add that we would find difficulty in accepting any proposition - which we do not understand to have been put - that, because the Superannuation Board had mistakenly found that a person was suffering from a particular physical or mental "condition" and had placed the person in the Provident Account under s. 5 of the superseded Act at a time before 1 July 1976 for that reason, that person could, after that date, be deprived of their superannuation rights on the ground that there was a connection between the incapacity that resulted in their retirement and the "condition" that the Board had mistakenly accepted existed, even given the existence of s. 16(6), (7) and (8) enabling persons to seek revocation or variation of benefit classification certificates and the fact that the person retired before the 1978 amendments of the Superannuation Act, in particular the addition of s. 184(7) (c), became effective. But be that as it may, we find in this case that there was not any such connection between the two sets of circumstances that were relied on by the respondent; they were unrelated circumstances, each with distinct and unrelated causes.

36. The decision under review will be set aside and in lieu thereof the matter remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the applicant's entitlements under the Superannuation Act 1976 are to be determined under s. 66(1).
