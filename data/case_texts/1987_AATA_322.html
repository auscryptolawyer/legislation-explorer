Re Taxation Appeals [1987] AATA 322 (9 September 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. WT86/4480 and WT87/111-112

AAT No. 4095

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income tax - Allowable deduction - Timeshare agreement - Payment of a single lump sum - Whether payment a revenue outgoing - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1983, sec. 51(1).

Vallambrosa Rubber Co Ltd v. Farmer (1910) 5 TC 529

F.C. of T. v. South Australian Battery Makers Pty Ltd 78 ATC 4412

Creer v. F.C. of T. 85 ATC 4104; 86 ATC 4318

HEARING

PERTH

9:9:1987

ORDER

Pursuant to subsection 43(1)(a) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal affirms the objection decision in relation to the assessment for the years ended 30 June 1983, 1984 and 1985.

DECISION

The point in this application is a short one and there is no authority upon it. The applicant is a "timeshare" purchaser who bought a number of "Intervals" in a holiday resort as an investment. These "Intervals" were acquired pursuant to several agreements, each purporting to create a licence. The agreements expressly provide that no freehold interest is created, hence there is no remainder over in fee simple at the end of the term The applicant insisted on appearing for himself and, due to a misunderstanding between the two batsmen at a preliminary conference, no proper evidence was adduced which enables me to comprehend the basis upon which this timeshare scheme has been devised or the method used at arriving at the cost per "Interval". (For what assistance it may be, each licence agreement contains a holograph figure, described as the "Annual Fee" and arrives at the "Total Sum" by multiplying the "Annual Fee" by 40). In the circumstances it is hardly surprising that my attempt to classify the juristic rights and fiscal consequences which arise from these various timeshare agreements is very much a matter of first impression.

2. In essence, the applicant paid $17,395 in 1983 and $57,500 in 1984 for the right to occupy two specified Units (Nos. 1 and 11) at specified times each year for 40 years at a Western Australian holiday resort complex established as a timesharing enterprise ("Butlin Camp Cove"). These two amounts (together with a loss forward of $47,413 in 1985) were claimed as deductions in each of the three years and duly disallowed.

3. For purposes of this application, "timesharing" refers to a contractual right to occupy a Unit at the resort for a specified "Interval" each year for a period of 40 years in consideration of a pre-paid lump sum. A "Unit" refers to a holiday home within the resort; an "Interval" is defined to mean a period of seven days from Friday to Friday; "Fixed Time" refers to a specified week or weeks.

4. The brochure for the resort was made an exhibit. It offers "the choice of 40 luxury six-bed holiday homes from just $2500 for one week's share - each year for 40 years".

5. The agreement (so far as relevant) provides as follows:

Cl. 1.1 (a) ... the right to use and occupy the Unit described

in Item 2 of the Schedule for the Intervals or

number of Intervals specified in Item 3 of the

Schedule from the Commencement Date specified in

Item 6 of the Schedule. The Interval or Intervals

shall be as provided in Item 3 of the Schedule;

(b) The right to use all the Licensor's fixtures

furniture and fittings now or hereafter upon or

used in connection with the Unit described by

number in Item 2 of the Schedule, and;

(c) The right to use the Common Leisure Facilities

provided from time to time by the Licensor;

(d) The rights to use as aforesaid for a period

commencing on the Commencement Date referred to in

Item 6 of the Schedule and ending on the 30th day

of June, 2023.

2. The conditions upon which the above License and rights

are granted are as follows:

(a) The License and By-Laws, or any amendments thereto,

shall be subject to the terms of the Trust Deed in

all respects and the Trust Deed shall be binding on

the parties hereto as if they were parties to that

Deed.

(b) The Licensee shall pay to the Licensor the amount

set out in item 4 of the Schedule (hereinafter

called "the total sum"). The total sum shall be

paid by the Licensee on the date of payment

specified in Item 4 of the Schedule.

(c) The Licensee shall be discharged and released from

any further liability to pay the annual fee as

aforesaid to the Licensor upon payment to the

Licensor of the total sum.

5. The rights hereby conferred shall rest in contract

only and shall not create in or confer upon the Licensee

any tenancy or any estate or interest whatsoever in or over

the said premises and the rights of the Licensee shall be

those of a licensee only and do not comprise or include any

further or other rights.

8. The Licensor HEREBY COVENANTS with the licensee as

follows:

(a) The Licensee shall have the right to assign or

shall be obliged to notify the Licensor of such

assignment and obtain its written acknowledgement

of the same.

(b) The Licensor shall operate the Butlin Cove Resort

for the benefit of all Licensees and maintain the

resort in good condition.

11. The Licensee undertakes not to lodge a Caveat in

respect of this License over the premises.

12(b) The Licensor shall have the right to elect to

terminate this License prior to 30th September, 1983 upon

giving the Licensee and Trustee 14 days prior notice in

writing of its intention so to do. Upon the expiration of

the notice as aforesaid the Trustee shall repay monies held

on trust for the Licensee pursuant to the Trust Deed

together with accrued interest thereon and this License

shall terminate without prejudice to the rights of the

parties hereunder up to the date of termination. (This

provision is only contained in the 1983 agreements).

13. The Licensee may elect by 30 days prior notice in

writing to the Licensor to surrender this License at any

time after the expiration of Five years from the

Commencement Date. In such event the Licensor shall pay to

the Licensee that proportion of the Total Sum for the

unexpired residue of the term of the License as bears to

the term hereof (calculated on the basis of an equal

payment for each Year of the term of the License) within

One Hundred and Eighty days of receipt of written notice

from the Licensee to that effect.

6. In the events that occurred, the applicant made the two payments previously referred to, which entitled him to the use of the two specified Units for certain specified weeks for 40 years. The 40 year period was not negotiable, in the sense that the development was (semble) was created around the assumption that "Intervals" would only be sold for 40 years and no lesser term.

7. The applicant deposed that he entered into these various licence agreements with the intention of deriving income from the rentals of the two Units and I have no hesitation in accepting his evidence. In the result, I am satisfied that there is no private component involved in any of these timeshare agreements. Indeed, in a letter dated 20 January 1984, the licensor offered to rent Unit 1 for all the nine intervals acquired by the applicant for two years (1984 and 1985) at a weekly rent of $350 each.

8. To complete the factual narrative, in the absence of any explanatory evidence from the applicant due to the aforementioned misunderstanding at the crease, the Crown called an officer of the Perth Taxation Office who had had some discussions with the developer of Butlin Camp Cove as a result of which some (largely illegible) handwritten calculations where made an exhibit, presumably for the purpose of seeking to establish how the weekly rental figures for the 40 year period had been calculated (was it on a present value basis or the present value of the future payments; was there a "discount" factor for the lump sum payment in advance and/or is there a "capital" component in the lump sum payments?). For those seeking greater enlightenment I can do no better than to quote the witness' evidence:

"Perhaps we can go through, may it please the Tribunal,

page 128 of the "T" documents. I think we need not refer

to any other documents. Starting out, there is all the

capital costs; the cost of the land, the buildings and the

bits and pieces around it. It comes up to a total cost to

the developer of 2.1 million. Then he has got sales

values and he just gives some alternatives, I think,

saying, 'if I get 3000 for the 30 units for 18 weeks at

3000 that would generate 1.6 million. And if I get 3500,

it will generate 1.8 million' and so on. He works all

that out, works out two streams and then gives himself, as

a result of that, a budget and net profit of either 1.5

million or 2.1 million . That is as it was explained to

me.

Then, in fact, at different times - looking through those

pages, it changes - whether you go back in time earlier in

time or later in time and naturally as things unfold over

the course of the development. So that appears to be how

the figure was calculated for the cost of one interval?

--- That is as I was given to understand."

9. The Crown's principal argument was that the outgoing was of a capital nature, relying on such well known cases as Vallambrosa Rubber Co Ltd v. Farmer (1910) 5 TC 529 and F.C. of T. v. Creer 86 ATC 4318. These cases illustrate the agonising difficulties courts not infrequently experience when compelled by the Tax Act to characterise an expenditure as either "capital", of a "capital nature" or "private or domestic". If one thing emerges from these authorities it is the undoubted fact that there is no Rosetta Stone which provides a ready translation of these fiscal hieroglyphics, and such "explanatory" phrases as "procuring a right of a permanent character", "creating a lasting advantage", "once and for all" and such variants as "enduring benefit" are about as helpful in separating "capital" from "revenue" as mouthing "yin" and "yan" to explain the mysteries of acupuncture. I can do no better than to quote from BP Australia Ltd v. F.C. of T. [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386 where their Lordships of the Privy Council said:

"The solution to the problem is not to be found by any

rigid test or description. It has to be derived from many

aspects of the whole set of circumstances some of which

may point in one direction, some in the other. One

consideration may point so clearly that it dominates other

and vaguer indications in the contrary direction. It is a

commonsense appreciation of all the guiding features which

must provide the ultimate answer. Although the categories

of capital and income expenditure are distinct and easily

ascertainable in obvious cases that lie far from the

boundary, the line of distinction is often hard to draw in

borderline cases; and conflicting considerations may

produce a situation where the answer turns on questions of

emphasis and degree. The answer 'depends on what the

expenditure is calculated to effect from a practical and

business point of view, rather than upon the juristic

classification of the legal rights, if any secured,

employed or exhausted in the process' (per Dixon J. in

Hallstrom's Case [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at p 648). As each

new case comes to be argued, felicitous phrases from

earlier judgements are used in argument by one side and

the other. But those phrases are not the deciding factor,

nor are they of unlimited application. They merely

crystallize particular factors which may incline the scale

in a particular case after a balance of all the

considerations has been taken." (at p 397).

10. In musical terms, "timesharing" lies somewhere between the black notes of the laws of real property and the white notes of the law of personality, hence the dissonance when compelled to play, not with the notes, but with the cracks between them. It is also apparent that my diffidence in this area of the law is shared by the draftsman of the instant "Holiday License (sic) Agreement". After going to great lengths to exclude a tenancy and a legal estate in land, and restricting the timeshare owner to his rights in contract, in other words, to rights in personam, the agreement demands an undertaking by the licensor not to lodge a caveat (cl. 11). Left to my own devices, I would have thought that what was granted here constitutes a discontinuous lease for a period of 40 years and that such lease constitutes a legal estate in land (cf Smallwood v. Sheppards (1885) 2QB 627). In the instant case, I am however spared the necessity of applying the often subtle tests used in determining whether the occupation of property creates a license or a lease, and/or whether it was the intention of the parties to create the relationship of landlord and tenant. In Booker v. Palmer (1942) 2 All ER 674, Lord Green MR put it succinctly: "Whether or not parties intend to create as between themselves the relationship of landlord and tenant, under which an estate is created in the tenant and certain material obligations arise by implication of law must, in the last resort, be a question of intention." (at p 676), and whilst it does not necessarily follow that a document described as "licence" is merely on that account to be regarded as amounting only to a licence in law (cf Addiscombe Garden Estates Ltd v. Crabbe (1958) 1 QB 513), no good reason is advanced in this case why I should go behind the express stipulation by the parties that the relationship between them is that of licensor and licensee, although I am satisfied that the better view is that - at least in respect of what is referred to as "Fixed Time" - the agreement constitutes a tenancy agreement. My reluctance to go behind the agreement is strengthened by the fact that I am satisfied that nothing turns on the distinction for tax purposes.

11. I now turn to the applicant's argument. An opinion by Mr David Malcolm QC was tendered which, it would appear, was obtained by the developer of the timeshare resort in April 1985. It deals, inter alia, with the assessability - if any - of the lump sum payment paid by licensees. The following question was put to counsel and received the appended reply:

"Q. If the outgoing constituted by the payment of the

licence fees is incurred in gaining or producing

assessable income, or is incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or producing such

income, could it be properly treated by the Commissioner

of Taxation as a capital payment and, therefore, not

deductible by the licensee?

A. My answer to the question is 'No'. Given that it is

established that the licence fee was incurred in gaining

or producing assessable income or necessarily incurred in

carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or

producing such income, the licence fee is prima facie

deductible: F.C. of T. v. South Australian Battery Makers Pty

Ltd [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 at 655 per Gibbs C.J. A payment

described as 'rent' or its equivalent may be nonetheless

of a capital nature. This could not be so in the present

case because no capital asset is acquired by the licensee

but merely rights of use and occupation: the case is

clearly distinguished from such cases as Poole v. F.C. of T.

[1970] HCA 25; (1970) 122 CLR 427, 438 per Walsh J. and Cooper v. F.C. of T.

[1957] HCA 72; (1957 97 CLR 397, 405 per Kitto J. In my opinion, given

that the circumstances under which the licence fees were

incurred made them prima facie deductible, it would not be

proper for the Commissioner of Taxation to treat the

payment as a capital payment not deductible under s.51 of

the Act: cf Creer v. F.C. of T. (1984) ATC 4103. I do not

know of any other reason which would entitle the

Commissioner to disallow the deduction once it were found

that the licence fee was incurred in gaining or producing

assessable income or was incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or producing income.

12. At the outset, I should point out in fairness to Mr Malcolm, that his opinion was given before Creer went on appeal to the Federal Court (86 ATC 4318), which reversed the decision at first instance. On the other hand, learned counsel for the Crown submitted that the decision in Creer had little bearing on the facts of this case. I agree with that submission. The passage referred to by Mr Malcolm in the South Australian Battery Makers case (supra) reads as follows:

"... the present case cannot be resolved simply by applying

these tests. The real problem in the case is not to

determine the character of the advantage sought, once it

has been identified, but to decide what was the advantage

sought by the taxpayer by making the payments. If the

only advantage sought was the right to possession under

the lease - and what was called 'rent' really answered

that description - clearly the outgoings were entirely of

a revenue nature. If on the other hand one advantage

sought by the outgoings was the acquisition of a capital

asset (the land and buildings), the fact that the payments

were called 'rent, and were made periodically, would not

necessarily prevent them from being in part outgoings of a

capital nature.

It is clear that by making the payments the taxpayer

itself did not acquire any interest in the land or

buildings except that of a lessee. The only binding

arrangements between the taxpayer and the Trust was that

embodied in the lease, under which the taxpayer had the

rights and interests of a lessee and nothing more. The

taxpayer was not granted any option to purchase, and it

had no right to enforce the option granted to (a another

wholly owned subsidiary)." (at p 655).

13. The above quotation is unexceptionable in the context of the facts of that case. I have no doubt that in the instant case, if the so-called licence fee had been payable as an annual recurrent payment for the duration of the licence, each such recurrent payment would be an allowable deduction, year by year. But these are not the facts. If, however, Mr Malcolm is of the opinion that because these licence fees involve no capital asset they can therefore not be outgoings of capital, I must respectfully disagree. It is necessary to distinguish between a "once-and-for-all" lump sum payment which secures the right to future income, and the character that payment would have if spread over the number of years for which the income is secured. It by no means follows that the character of the one determines that of the other.

14. In the end, I have concluded that this case does indeed stand or fall on the issue whether or not the lump sum payment is a capital payment. In the circumstances, whether the claim is advanced under the first or second limb of sec. 51(1) matters not.

15. At the outset, it should be made clear that what is involved here is not a "prepayment" of any kind. The whole timeshare development is so structured as to insist on an "up front" payment of the once-and-for-all kind. A "prepayment" assumes that a payment is not due until some later date but is paid before it becomes due. Applying that series of well-worn cases invariably used to determine whether an outgoing is on revenue or capital account, I commence my enquiry with Vallambrosa Rubber Co Ltd v. Farmer 1910 5 TC 529, where, Lord Dunedin said:

"... in a rough way I think it is not a bad criterion of what

is capital expenditure as against what is income

expenditure to say that capital expenditure is a thing

that is going to be spent once and for all, and income

expenditure is a thing that is going to recur every year."

(at p 536).

This statement has been frequently repeated with approval (see for example the BP Australia case (supra) at p 399). This was expanded upon by Viscount Cave in Atherton v. British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd (1925) 10 TC 155 where his Lordship, after noting that the famous test propounded by Lord Dunedin was not intended to be decisive in every case, went on to point out that:

"... when an expenditure is made, not only once and for

all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset

or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade, I

think that there is very good reason (in the absence of

special circumstances leading to an opposite conclusion)

for treating such an expenditure as properly attributable

not to revenue but to capital. For this view there is

already considerable authority." (at p 192-193).

In Sun Newspapers Ltd v. F.C. of T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337, it was held that money spent in eliminating a rival newspaper was an outgoing of capital. Dixon J. noted:

"But the idea of recurrence and the idea of endurance or

continuance over a duration of time both depend on degree

and comparison. As to the first it has been said it is

not a question of recurring every year or every accounting

period; but "the real test is between expenditure which is

made to meet a continuous demand, as opposed to an

expenditure which is made once for all" (per Rowlatt J.,

Ounsworth v. Vickers Ltd. (1915) 3 KB 273. By this I

understand that the expenditure is to be considered of a

revenue nature if its purpose brings it within the very

wide class of things which in the aggregate form the

constant demand which must be answered out of the returns

of a trade or its circulating capital and that actual

recurrence of the specific thing need not take place or be

expected as likely. Thus in Anglo-Persian Oil Co. Ltd v.

Dale (1932) 1 KB 124, the establishment and reorganization

of agencies formed part of the class of things making the

continuous or constant demand for expenditure, but the

given transaction was of a magnitude and precise

description unlikely again to be encountered. Recurrence

is not a test, it is no more than a consideration the

weight of which depends upon the nature of the

expenditure." (at p 362).

Later his Honour added:

"There are, I think three matters to be considered, (a) the

character of the advantage sought, and in this its lasting

qualities may play a part, (b) the manner in which it is

to be used, relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and under

the former head recurrence may play its part, and (c) the

means adopted to obtain it; that is, by providing a

periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment

for periods commensurate with the payment or by making a

final provision or payment so as to secure future use or

enjoyment." (at p 363).

16. It was submitted on behalf of the Crown that the duration of the time share agreement in this case pointed strongly in the direction of "capital" and reliance was placed on the BP Australia case (supra). In that case, it will be recalled, BP Australia Ltd. sought to obtain exclusive outlets for its products by entering into agreements with a number of service station owners whereby it promised to defray certain expenditures in consideration "tieing" these outlets to its products. The issue was once again whether such an outgoing was on revenue account. On appeal to the Privy Council, their Lordships asked themselves:

"What additional indication is given by the actual length

of the agreements? That must be a question of degree.

Had the agreements been only for two or three year periods

that fact would have pointed to recurrent revenue

expenditure. Had they been for twenty years, that fact

would have pointed to a non-recurring payment of a capital

nature. Length of time, though theoretically not a

deciding factor, does in practice shed a light on the

nature of the advantage sought. The longer the duration

of the agreements, the greater the indication that a

structural solution was being sought. In this case the

periods varied between three and fifteen years, but the

average appears to be something just under five years and

the predominant number of agreements was for a five-year

period. The case was argued before their Lordships, as in

the Courts below, on the footing that five years was the

length of the tie and neither side sought to make any

differentiation because a few of the ties were very much

longer. That length of time appears to be neutral, and in

itself indicates neither capital expenditure nor revenue

by its mere length. It therefore does not add effectively

to the argument either way. The question must be decided

by other weights in the balance." (at pp 399-400)."

17. In the result, I have concluded that the outgoing in the present application constitutes in each instance a "once-and-for-all" payment for a single licence (albeit for a discontinuous right of occupation) for a period which - applying common knowledge - extends, give or take a year, over the useful life of the development and improvements. It follows that the licence fee is an affair of capital and excluded from deduction by sec. 51(1) of the Tax Act.

18. If I am wrong in my conclusion that the fee is wholly an affair of capital, it may well be that it contains a revenue component. I do not propose to give a "declaratory opinion" whether or not such a component can constitute an allowable deduction, year by year, when, on one view, the actual expenditure was "incurred" in a previous year since I have no basis for making a meaningful apportionment which could assist an apeals tribunal should this matter go further.

19. I feel bound to add that Mr Pullin, of learned counsel for the Crown did his utmost to put both sides of the argument, mindful that the applicant was unrepresented and clearly out of his depth. I am grateful for his assistance. 20. I would affirm the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection.
