Vaughan and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 46 (27 April 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL





Superannuation - benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force - invalidity retirement on ground of condition specified in certificate -application for review of decision confirming benefit entitlement under section 66(2) of Superannuation Act 1976 - whether incapacity caused or substantially contributed to by specified condition.

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 16(4), 66(1), 66(2), 154, 184







DECISION AND REASONS

Re: RONALD WILLIAM VAUGHAN

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

AAT Decision No 180

No: 78/12023

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Mr V.J. Skermer (Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

Date: 27 April 1979

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

No: 78/12023

Re: RONALD WILLIAM VAUGHAN

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The decision of the Tribunal is that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made pursuant to s. 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 whereby he confirmed a decision taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 24 August 1977 that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 22 July 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 should be set aside, and in substitution therefor the Tribunal decides that his entitlement should be determined under s.66(l) of that Act.

DATED this 27th day of April, 1979.

(R.K. TODD)

Senior Member

(V.J. SKERMER)

Member

(DR M. GLICK)

Member

REASONS FOR DECISION

In this application the applicant seeks review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 24 August 1977, and confirmed upon re-consideration under s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 22 July 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s.66(2) of the Act. The decision was based upon the conclusion that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant was caused or was substantially contributed to by a condition, namely hypertension, specified in a benefit classification certificate which was deemed by s.184(2) of the Act to have been issued and which was still in force immediately before the retirement of the applicant, or by a condition connected with such a condition.

The statutory provisions here under consideration are quite complicated, but need not be here set out. They have been explained in previous decisions of the Tribunal, to which reference may be made: See for instance Re: D.E. Harrison and The Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 78/12024).

There is no doubt, and it was not in dispute at the hearing, that the applicant fell within the provisions of s.66(2)(a) and (b) of the Act. Likewise it was not in dispute that for the purposes of s.184(2) of the Act there was deemed to be in force, on 1 July 1976, in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner under s.16(4) specifying the condition hypertension as the physical condition by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant, in August 1964, as a contributor under Part III of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act"). The only question therefore is whether in terms of s.66(2)(c) of the Act, the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement in July 1977 was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the condition hypertension, or by a physical or mental condition connected with hypertension.

The applicant was born in 1920. He came to Australia from England in 1960, having already been engaged to work for Trans-Australia Airlines. He commenced as an aircraft mechanic and later passed examinations in order to become qualified as a licensed aircraft mechanical engineer. He continued in this capacity until 1975.

In August 1964 the applicant was medically examined with a view to his acceptance as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of the superseded Act. In the questionnaire which the applicant completed he acknowledged that he had "blood pressure", but he had never been treated for it. At the examination a reading of 180/110 was taken. It was recommended that the applicant be accepted for the Provident Account only, the reasons stated being "Hypertension with family history of coronary thrombosis". The statement "family history" must clearly be disregarded for the purposes of considering the reason for rejection.

The applicant did after this go to his own doctor and thereafter was under treatment for high blood pressure. There is some doubt in our minds as to whether the applicant's hypertension gave him any trouble during his employment. He himself claimed that he had not lost any working time as a result of it, but a medical certificate given him by his own doctor in October 1972 suggests the contrary.

In 1975 the applicant developed a major leg vein thrombosis. This gave him pain throughout the time that he was on the long service leave that followed, and in the course of which he travelled in the United Kingdom and Europe. It troubled him particularly while walking. He had a heart attack while staying in Nice, and having been treated there and in the United Kingdom he returned to Australia at the end of his leave. He went on to office work for about six months and then went back on to tarmac work. The work of despatching and receiving aircraft involved physical activity and he had angina pectoris about twice a day, for which he took anginine tablets. Then in June 1977 he had a further more severe attack of pain just after he finished work. He went home and felt very sick, with vomiting. He was in the Royal Melbourne Hospital for six days. On discharge he went home, and then consulted Dr Parle, the then chief medical officer of TAA, who recommended his retirement on medical grounds, his report stating:

"He had a heart attack in 1975. Since then he has had frequent anginal attacks."

He retired accordingly on 22 July 1977. Since then he has not worked save for a period of some eight months in 1978 when he did some clerical work on a temporary or relieving basis. He has continued to have anginal attacks, dependent on the level of his activity.

There was some evidence that the applicant had had some sort of anginal pains prior to 1975. In 1970 he had pains while playing golf. The pains worried him and he had a week off work. The applicant was adamant that it was "a sort of muscular pain". A certificate given by his own doctor at this time was tendered in evidence and stated that he was unfit for work due to "chest pain". In the absence of the ability to question the doctor about the matter the certificate is not of much use. The same comment may be made of the other certificate referred to earlier. Earlier still, in 1962, the applicant had had chest pains and kidney stone troubles and was seen at the Royal Melbourne Hospital by highly qualified medical practitioners. He was found to have mild hypertension, with a reading of 160/105. Dr Gandevia wrote to the applicant's own doctor and told him that the applicant's hypertension was relatively mild, and that he was an extremely tense and rather worried character who had not yet settled down in Australia.

In November 1978, the applicant was examined by Dr A.C. Newell, a consultant physician, who was called to give evidence in support of his application. Dr Newell found that there was no clinical evidence of heart disease although this was obvious in the electrocardiogram which showed that he had had an inferior myocardial infarction at some time in the past. His blood pressure readings were 170/110 at the initial supine reading, and the final seated reading was 150/100. There was no evidence in retinal arteries or leg vessels, or in any other way that can be detected, of hypertensive heart disease. The hypertension, dating from 1960, had not as far as he could tell influenced other organs. An electrocardiogram demonstrated not the changes of hypertension but the changes of myocardial infarction. These conclusions were confirmed by Dr Newell's inspection of the Royal Melbourne Hospital file. When he was admitted on 22 June 1977 his blood pressure was (in the casualty report) 170/110, and an electrocardiogram showed changes related to the 1975 attack. The electrocardiogram lacked what Dr Newell previously referred to as evidence of long-standing severe hypertension. There were no changes characteristic of that condition. He thought it unreasonable to conclude that the applicant's retirement, on the ground of invalidity, from the employment of TAA was caused by hypertension. He retired because he had had a heart attack and because his heart attack was followed by persisting angina which was limiting his walking.

Hypertension, Dr Newell said, was known to be one of the risk factors in the production of myocardial infarction, and was so in relation to the applicant because he had a long history of labile hypertension which means that in his case it was a risk factor, a risk factor which to Dr Newell's mind was not as important as the genetic factors. The probable operative factors relating to the infarction in 1975 were that both of his parents had had heart attacks, that he was overweight and that he had had a deep vein thrombosis shortly before. There was, it was said, evidence to suggest that increased coagulability of blood occurs under these conditions, favouring thrombosis in other sites. Dr Newell was then asked the following questions by the applicant's counsel:

"Q. Given that hypertension may in a general sense be a risk factor in relation to coronary disease, in relation to this specific occlusion which we presume occurred in October 1975, is it necessary to postulate hypertension as a part of that particular process? -- A. No, except that it is just one of several risk factors.

Q. As a risk factor it may or may not have contributed? --A. I mean, on the statistics. You cannot deny that it is a risk factor on the statistical grounds but I would say in this particular case it is a less important risk factor than the more obvious ones of obesity and a positive family history.

Q. And possibly this deep venous thrombosis? -- A. Yes."

Under cross-examination Dr Newell adhered to his view that hypertension, while being a risk factor in the case of the applicant, was less important than his obesity and his family history, but he agreed that it was perhaps third in importance after those in producing a heart attack. He also said that obesity would be a factor in his condition of hypertension. Asked whether the family history of heart disease would be of significance to the condition of hypertension he said: "You tend to lump the cardiovascular system together in relation to a genetic background. If we search the records we may find that his parents were hypertensive as well as having had heart attacks."

Dr Newell also explained that the impact of hypertension varies from patient to patient according to whether the greatest impact is on the major vessels on the cerebral circulation, on the renal circulation or on the retinal blood vessels. Sometimes after many years of "benign" hypertension there is no major involvement of other systems. The most common result of hypertension would be considerable enlargement of the left ventricle of the heart which can lead to failure of the left ventricle with symptoms of cardiac asthma. Hypertension may present as disease of the cerebral arteries resulting in a stroke. In some cases angina will be present as a manifestation of atheroma in the coronary arteries. Dr Newell found none of these direct sequelae, even after 18 years of hypertension, indicating that his blood pressure was tending to be high only when he was examined. In this way he was protected from the possible consequences of hypertension.

Finally, Dr Newell was asked by the Tribunal what was in his opinion the relationship between the hypertension and the leg thrombosis which occurred in 1975, and he responded as follows:

"Q. Was there any factor of relevance there? -- A. No. In my view ... the leg vein thrombosis was related to an overweight man who was doing heavy work squatting. He was a tarmac worker changing wheels and things like that on aircraft. It would have happened whether he was hypertensive or not.

Q. Could the leg thrombosis have been a factor in relation to the heart attack overseas? -- A. Yes. Some say that it indicates a tendency for the blood to coagulate (easily). There was quite a short interval there, because the leg vein thrombosis was just before he went on leave and the heart attack was while he was touring France shortly afterwards."

Dr Parle also gave evidence. In the event he was in fact called by the applicant's counsel. He recalled the applicant quite well as an individual. He said that when he recommended retirement, in the terms already set out, he had considered hypertension and that the conclusion he had arrived at was that his heart attack in 1975 and the subsequent anginal attacks rendered him permanently unfit for work. The previous history of hypertension, the records of the Royal Melbourne Hospital and the medical history previously referred to were put to him and his response was to agree that they did not suggest to him a picture of severe hypertension. He said that there were various things he had to think about and that he was convinced that hypertension was probably a very minor part of his pattern of health, and that he retired him purely and simply on his anginal attacks. Asked if he saw any necessary connection between the heart attack that he was presumed to have had in 1975 and the hypertension which it was known had existed to some extent over the years, he replied: "No. I do not think it played any part at all." Later, he said: "There was only one main disability, or one disability. That was the fact that he had had a heart attack or coronary infarction and that he was subject to angina. This man's work as a licensed aircraft engineer involved shift-work and was quite stressful and it was just not the type of work he should be doing. Given that he had those disabilities that was sufficient ground for his retirement to be recommended, and hypertension was not one of the disabilities."

Dr Parle's assessment of the applicant was all the more telling because it had the backing of some knowledge of the man: "I did a lot of travelling during my years with TAA and I got to know the licensed aircraft maintenance engineers on the tarmac almost personally and that was what I meant when I said I knew him well. I knew him from that."

The only evidence called by the Commissioner was that of Dr Hale, a consulting cardiologist. He was asked whether, given a situation where a person has a condition of hypertension for at least 11 years and is treated prior to a heart attack, he could express an opinion as to whether, on those bald facts, the hypertension would be a significant factor causing the heart attack. When Dr Hale answered in effect that he could only speak of probabilities, he was then asked whether the probability would be that the hypertension was a significant factor in causing the heart attack. The answer was that there was an increased likelihood of his having a heart attack because of the presence of the high blood pressure, and in the questions and answers that followed the probabilities and the risk factors were discussed, but with the exception of a reference to the applicant's blood pressure reading of 180/110 in 1964, Dr Hale was not further referred to the applicant's medical history or records, and of course he had not examined the applicant. In cross-examination by Mr Ashley for the applicant, Dr Hale expressed the view that most incidents of myocardial infarction occur at rest, or at least were at random within the 24 hour period, and that it did not make much difference what the blood pressure is at the actual time of the occurrence. It is no criticism of Dr Hale to say, as Mr Ashley for the applicant submitted, that his evidence did not touch the actual situation of the applicant.

We have set out the medical evidence in some detail. It is of critical importance in deciding the question before us, but none of it was before the Commissioner when he made his decision. Apart from Dr Parle's original recommendation, there was also before the Commissioner a further notation which Dr Parle had made upon request and which read: "Note: He was assessed for Provident Account only because of hypertension. Family history of coronary thrombosis is not in itself a reason. Retired because of coronary thrombosis - not hypertension -with anginal attacks." This having been put before a Senior Medical Officer of the Department of Health the latter reported:

"The disability which caused Mr Vaughan's retirement namely "heart attack and anginal attacks" was caused by the condition hypertension specified in the benefit classification certificate.

In the Medical textbook Principles and Practice of Medicine 19th ed. p. 376 under the heading Essential Hypertension 'Approximately 50% of persons die in congestive failure, 20% of cerebral vascular accidents and 30% of other causes, one of the most important being myocardial infarction'."

The reliance thus placed upon statistical data was criticised with much force by counsel for the applicant. It was submitted, correctly in our opinion, that it would be erroneous to determine the entitlement of the applicant by reference to such data independently of evidence of the actual condition of the applicant. At the hearing of this application clear evidence was given of such condition at the relevant times, and we are required by the Act to direct our attention to the condition, physical or mental, of the applicant. We therefore now proceed to consider the issue before us accordingly.

For s.66(2) to apply, in the proved circumstances, one at least of four questions must be answered affirmatively:

(i) Were the heart attack and anginal attacks caused by the hypertension?

(ii) Were they substantially contributed to by the hypertension?

(iii) Were they caused by a condition connected with the hypertension?

(iv) Were they substantially contributed to by a condition connected with hypertension?

As to (i) there was no foundation whatsoever in the evidence to support an affirmative answer. There was no doubt on the evidence that the heart condition was the incapacity which was the ground of the applicant's retirement, but the evidence was that this was not caused by the hypertension. Mr Clark for the Commissioner effectively conceded this, although it should be noted that the Senior Medical Officer who advised the Commissioner proceeded upon this hypothesis alone.

As to (ii), we find that there was likewise no evidence to support an affirmative answer to the question.

When we turn to (iii) and (iv) the matter is a little more complex. To found these, there must have been a condition causing (in the case of (iii)) or substantially contributing to (in the case of (iv)) the heart attack, and that condition must have been connected with hypertension. The only such conditions offered in argument as qualifying for such a description were obesity and a family history of heart conditions. It was suggested that "connected with" does not in the context connote a causal connection but connotes only that the condition be related to or associated with, in this case, hypertension. For present purposes this analysis could, without any commitment for future cases, be accepted up to a point, but the connection would we consider have to be at least a real or demonstrable connection and not simply a casual connection, as for instance by the condition in question being simply one of a number of conditions that are related to one aspect of human physiology. The fact that a person has hypertension is a reflection of the general cardiovascular tree, but if he subsequently develops a coronary thrombosis it is difficult to say what precipitated that particular attack, and at what stage the generalised disease became more specific, so as to narrow it down to the heart muscle. This need not be pursued very far in this case, however, for of the two conditions that were offered as constituting conditions connected with the hypertension, the following findings appear quite clear:

(i) A "family history of heart conditions" is not a condition at all, as was virtually conceded;

(ii) As to obesity, the evidence of this was really non-existent insofar as establishing the necessary connection of it with hypertension is concerned. There was some evidence of a connection between obesity and the heart attack, but this is not relevant for the present purpose. We thus find that on the broadest connotation of the words "connected with", no such connection has been demonstrated.

Having heard evidence of the state of health of this particular man, we find that evidence compelling, to the extent that it displaces any consideration of the matter based upon statistical data of the kind which has been mentioned.

Accordingly, the decision of the Tribunal must be that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made pursuant to s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 whereby he confirmed a decision taken on his behalf by his Delegate on 24 August 1977 that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 22 July 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 should be set aside, and in substitution therefor the decision of the Tribunal should be that his entitlement should be determined under s.66(l) of that Act.
