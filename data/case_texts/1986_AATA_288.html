Re Taxation Appeals (No 6) Aat [1986] AATA 288 (2 October 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS (No. 6)

AAT No. 2905

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

K.P. Brady (Senior Member)

J.E. Stewart (Senior Member)

D.J. Trowse (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - consultant physician - overseas travelling expenses

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 6(1), 51(1).

Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1935] HCA 81; (1935-1936) 54 CLR 295

Ronpibon Tin N.L. and Tongkah Compound N.L. v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1948-1949) 78 CLR 47

F.C. of T. v. Finn [1960] HCA 69; (1959-1960) 103 CLR 165

F.C. of T. v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1970-1971) 125 CLR 494

F.C. of T. v. Maddalena 71 ATC 4161

F.C. of T. v. Highfield 82 ATC 4463

F.C. of T. v. Klan 85 ATC 4060

HEARING

CANBERRA

2:10:1986

DECISION

The question for decision in this reference is whether an amount of $2,290 claimed in respect of overseas travelling expenses in the year of income ended 30th June, 1980, is an allowable deduction under the provisions of s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

2. Upon the Commissioner's disallowance of the claim, and the objection against the disallowance, the decision on the objection was, at the request of the taxpayer applicant (herein called "the applicant"), referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board of Review concluded its hearing of the matter before 1st July, 1986, and reserved its decision. Under the provisions of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, the functions of the Taxation Boards of Review were transferred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal with effect from 1st July, 1986. As a consequence, the decision in the instant case, together with reasons in writing for the decision, are given pursuant to the provisions of s. 43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

3. At the hearing of the reference before the Board of Review, the applicant represented himself and gave evidence by way of affirmation. He did not call any witnesses. The Commissioner of Taxation (the Commissioner) was represented by one of his officers. No witnesses were called to give evidence on behalf of the Commissioner.

4. The applicant graduated in Australia in 1971 as a Bachelor of Medicine and Surgery. By the end of that year he had completed his compulsory pre-registration year as an intern in one of Australia's leading public hospitals and was granted full registration with the appropriate State Medical Board. By 1973 he had decided on a career path that would lead to his becoming a consultant physician in internal medicine. With that objective in mind, he proceeded to the United Kingdom (U.K.) in about 1973 where he worked and studied for some 15 months. In recognition of his work and studies there, he was awarded membership of the Royal College of Physicians (U.K.) in 1974.

5. It seems that in the latter part of that year he returned to Australia and that, early in 1975, he was appointed as Medical Registrar at the hospital where he had undertaken his internship. During the period 1975 to 1979 inclusive, he continued to be employed as Medical Registrar at the same hospital and worked in most of its general medical units as well as spending varying terms in its medical sub-specialities of Renal medicine, Neurology, Cardiology, Intensive Care, Haematology and Gastroenterology. During that period he also spent some six months gaining additional experience at a repatriation hospital. In recognition of his U.K. qualification and his subsequent Australian work and experience, which also included research work, he was admitted to full membership of the Royal Australasian College of Physicians early in 1979. The applicant continued as Medical Registrar of the same hospital until his employment there was terminated some four weeks prior to his proceeding to the U.K. with his wife and children in February 1980.

6. The applicant's second trip to the U.K. was undertaken in furtherance of his determination to become a highly qualified and experienced physician. The trip was also undertaken on the advice of his colleagues, some of whom held senior positions in the medical world in which the applicant worked. Both the applicant and his colleagues shared the view that the further experience to be gained in the U.K., which was to include advanced research into various aspects of internal medicine at various medical institutions, would enhance the applicant's experience and reputation as a physician, which, in due course, would enable him to compete on more favourable terms than less qualified or less experienced colleagues for a share of the market from which his future income would be derived.

7. The applicant spent some 12 months in the U.K. and returned to Australia early in 1981. While in the U.K. the applicant worked and participated in research programmes on lines already indicated. He also prepared reports on various specialised subjects, some of which were published in a number of learned medical journals. No income was derived from these publications. It seems that on his return to Australia the applicant commenced to practice as a consultant physician, from which income was derived and, in addition, he derived income as a clinical assistant at his former employer hospital.

8. In his return of income for the year in issue, i.e. the year ended 30th June, 1980, the applicant returned income from salary derived (as already indicated) as a medical registrar from 1st July, 1979, until shortly before his departure to the U.K. He also returned income from rent and interest. His claim for deductions, giving rise to the question in issue, may be summarised as follows:

Air fares to U.K. $ 884

Passport. 25

Travel insurance. 132

U.K. accommodation, meals and

electricity (40% of total cost

of $3,122 attributed to applicant). 1,249

------

Total: $2,290

------

------

The applicant did not claim as a deduction any part of the additional travelling expenses which related to his wife and family. The Commissioner's representative conceded that the whole of the amount of $2,290 was incurred in the year of income in issue and we have no reason to doubt the validity of that concession.

9. As already indicated, the question at issue is whether the above amount of $2,290 is an allowable deduction under s. 51(1), which reads as follows:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in

gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily

incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or

producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the

extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a

capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to

the gaining or production of exempt income."

10. In his letter of objection against the disallowance of his claim, the applicant relied upon what have often been referred to as the two positive limbs of s. 51(1). That is to say, he contended in the alternative that, if he should properly be regarded as an employee in the year in issue, the expenditure in dispute was a loss or outgoing which was "incurred in gaining or producing (his) assessable income" (s. 51(1)) or, if he in fact carried on business as a consulting physician in that year, the expenditure was a loss or outgoing "incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income" (s. 51(1)). The applicant also contended in his letter of objection that the expenditure was not a loss or outgoing of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature.

11. The applicant's evidence and submissions before the Board were concerned principally with making good the proposition that he carried on business in the year in issue as a consulting physician and that the expenditure, not being of a capital, private or domestic nature, was properly a deductible outgoing in terms of the second limb of s. 51(1). In support of that proposition, the applicant submitted that his career as a consulting physician really commenced in 1974 when he was admitted as a member of the Royal College of Physicians (U.K.) or, failing that, in 1979, when he was admitted to full membership of the Royal Australasian College of Physicians. It seems that the latter qualification satisfied all of the Commonwealth Department of Health requirements concerning specialist status, which would have enabled him to charge fees as a self-employed specialist from that time.

12. It is undoubtedly true that the applicant derived assessable income from the pursuit of his profession in the period 1974 to 1979 inclusive, and also during the 1980 year of income in issue. It is also no doubt true that the experience gained, and the additional qualifications obtained in that period, enhanced his status and reputation as a physician and that, as a consequence, his income earning potential by 1979 was greater than it might otherwise have been. However, on the evidence, it is quite clear that, whatever his legal entitlement might have been to have commenced practising on his own account as a consultant physician after 1979, he did not in fact do so until after his return to Australia in 1981. Therefore, in our opinion, the applicant's claim under the second limb of s. 51(1) must fail because the expenditure was not in fact "incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing" assessable income of the year in issue, nor was it, in the light of the authorities, related in any relevant way to the gaining of income in the year, or years, subsequent to that year. In connection with these later years, it is observed that, while the case of F.C. of T.v. Maddalena, 71 ATC 4161, was concerned with a claim for a deduction by an employee, the following words of Menzies J. at p. 4163 are nevertheless considered to be apposite to the circumstances of the instant case -

"It (the expenditure) would come at a point too soon to be properly

regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income"

of the 1981 year in which the applicant first derived income as a selfemployed person.

13. The decision of the Supreme Court of Victoria in the case of F.C. of T. v. Klan, 85 ATC 4060, is also considered to be supportive of our conclusions in this matter, even though in that case the Court was concerned with the first limb of s. 51(1) in relation to claims by an employee. It was held, inter alia, in that case (per headnote of ATC report) that "the perception of a connection between expenditure and the future earning of assessable income is not by itself sufficient to justify a deduction". As pointed out by Ormiston J. at p. 4068 of the ATC report:

"It is one thing to assert that it is unnecessary that the outgoing

need produce, as a matter of causation, assessable income in the

year in which the outgoing was incurred. It is quite another to say

that expenditure incurred which is entirely unrelated to existing

activities which produce assessable income can come within the

section (s. 51) because its object and long-term effect is better to

equip the taxpayer to earn income in the future."

14. However, lest it be thought that the decision of Lee J. in the New South Wales Supreme Court case of F.C. of T. v. Highfield, 82 ATC 4463, is supportive of the applicant's claim in the instant case, we refer briefly to that case. That case involved a dentist who leased his general practice and travelled overseas for the purpose of undertaking a full-time post graduate degree in periodontics. Some time thereafter, he sold his practice to the dentist who had been leasing it and then gained additional clinical experience in periodontics before obtaining his specialist registration and eventually setting up practice as a specialist. It was held by Mr. Justice Lee that the taxpayer's intention in undertaking a higher degree was to use the knowledge which he had obtained in the advancement of his existing general practice and that there was, therefore, the necessary connection between the expenditure and the gaining of assessable income so as to justify a deduction under the second limb of s. 51(1).

15. However, an important difference between Highfield and the present case is that, in the former case, the taxpayer, a self-employed person, had an established practice prior to incurring the overseas travelling expenditure there in issue, which was incurred for the purpose of increasing his earnings from that practice. There was, therefore, unlike the position in the instant case, a relevant connection in Highfield between the outgoings and the gaining of future assessable income.

16. We turn now to consider briefly the applicant's claims in terms of the first limb of s. 51(1). The word "business" in s. 6(1) of the Assessment Act is defined as including "any profession, trade, employment, vocation or calling but does not include occupation as an employee". Therefore, whilst the applicant was carrying on a "profession" during the year in issue, he did not on the evidence, and in terms of the word "business" as defined, carry on business in that year. He was an employee between 1st July, 1979, and the time at which he ceased to be employed prior to travelling overseas. On the evidence, there was clearly no connection between the expenditure in issue and the income derived as an employee in that period, or to the income from whatever source, that was derived by him subsequent to his return to Australia in 1981. The authorities have established that the words "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" (first limb of s. 51(1)) mean in the course of producing such income and that, for the expenditure to form an allowable deduction, it must be incidental and relevant to gaining or producing the assessable income (see Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1935] HCA 81; (1935-1936) 54 CLR 295; Ronpibon Tin NL and Tongkah Compound NL v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1948-1949) 78 CLR 47) On the evidence, it is quite clear that the expenditure in issue was not incurred in the course of gaining the applicant's income as an employee in the year of income in issue, nor was it incidental and relevant to that end. As indicated earlier, it did not satisfy that criteria in respect of income derived by the applicant after his return to Australia in 1981.

17. Two commonly quoted cases or the deductibility of expenditure of the nature here in issue are F.C. of T. v. Finn [1960] HCA 69; (1959-1960) 103 CLR 165 and F.C. of T. v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1970-1971) 125 CLR 494 However, in both those cases there was a continuity of employment that extended throughout and beyond the years in issue, and it is apparent that considerable importance was placed by the High Court in both cases on the fact that the expenditure could be related in a relevant way to that employment. Unfortunately for the applicant, that feature is not present in his case, with the consequence that, in our opinion, his claim must also fail under the first limb of s. 51(1).

18. In any event, the evidence clearly shows that the expenditure in issue was incurred at the applicant's own volition and was essentially of a private character. By the operation of the exclusory clause in s. 51(1), his claims under both limbs of that section must fail, in our opinion, because the expenditure was of a "private" nature.

19. For the above reasons, we propose to affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection under review.
