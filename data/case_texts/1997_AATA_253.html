CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - whether expenses incurred in seeking to prevent dismissal from employment are allowable deductions - whether Commissioner of Taxation instructing the Australian Government Solicitor breaches the secrecy provisions of s16 of the Tax Act - whether tax records of an employee of the Tax Office conveyed to the personnel section breaches the secrecy provisions of s16 - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 16, 51(1).



FCT v Maddalena 71 ATC 4161

Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v FCT 80 ATC 4542

REASONS FOR DECISION

24 July 1997 Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)



1. This matter proceeded on statement of agreed facts which (in slightly abbreviated form) are set out below.



  1. The Applicant is a Commonwealth Public Servant in the employ of the Australian Taxation Office (“ATO”).

  2. In January 1995, the ATO’s Special Audit section was informed by the State police that the Applicant was under investigation for fraudulent dealings. On the 21st of March 1995, some 20 criminal charges were laid against each of the Applicant and his wife, alleging fraud in connection with the sale of certain art works [the Applicant’s wife owns and operates an art gallery. Although the respondent originally maintained that the business was operated jointly by the Applicant and his wife, at the hearing before me it was conceded that the business was solely operated by the wife. This concession has, of course, no bearing on the criminal proceedings]. The trial of those charges is still pending in the County Court.

  3. In February 1996 the Applicant and his wife made application for an indefinite stay of the trials on the basis that they could not afford legal representation and would therefore be unable to obtain a fair trial. The Applicant and his wife appeared in the County Court in March, June and July 1996 in connection with that application before it was heard between 13-17 September 1996. No indefinite stay of those proceedings has been granted. Several minor charges arising from the same incidents have remained pending in the Magistrates Court where they are periodically adjourned.

  4. On the 28th of March 1995 and as a result of the criminal charges against him the Applicant was suspended from his employment with the Australian Taxation Office. That suspension has continued until the present.

  5. The Applicant’s suspension from his employment was suspension without pay. The Applicant successfully applied for reinstatement of his full pay on the grounds of financial hardship in April 1995. Since that time the Applicant has been obliged to continue to demonstrate that financial hardship still exists on a periodic basis in order for his full pay to be continued.

  6. In February 1995 the Respondent commenced an audit of the income tax returns of the Applicant, the Applicant’s wife and the Applicant’s daughter for the income tax years 1992 to 1995 inclusive. That audit included a consideration of matters relating to the sale of paintings, including those sales the subject of the criminal charges against the Applicant and the Applicant’s wife.

  7. At the conclusion of the taxation audit referred to in paragraph 6 Amended Assessments for both the Applicant and the Applicant’s wife were issued on 22 December 1995 for each of the income years 1992 to 1995 inclusive (‘the Amended Assessments’).

  8. The Amended Assessments relating to the Applicant’s wife were based on findings that the Applicant’s wife had carried on a business of dealing in art works during the years covered by the Amended Assessments and the quantum of the profits from that business were estimated under s167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The Amended Assessments in respect of the Applicant accepted the Applicant’s contention that he had not been involved in the business of buying and selling art works during the years covered by the Amended Assessments, and [the Amended Assessments] related solely to his entitlement to claim a dependant spouse rebate during those years.

  9. The Applicant and his wife respectively lodged objections to each of the respective Amended Assessments on the 24th of January 1996. Those objections were disallowed on the 12th of September 1996. The Applicant and his wife respectively lodged applications for review of the objection decisions in respect of the Amended Assessments in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on the 20th of September 1996 and those proceedings are still pending in the Tribunal.

  10. On 21 March 1995 a personnel enquiry into the Applicant was initiated by the Australian Taxation Office.

  11. On 14 July 1995 the Applicant was charged by the Police under the Crimes Act (Cth) with one count of imposition in relation to a medical certificate dated 5 March 1991. Since that time this charge has been regularly adjourned along with the other charges remaining in the Magistrates Court and there is no indication when it might proceed to a hearing. [I understand that this charge has recently been withdrawn].

  12. On 13 October 1995 the Applicant lodged a complaint with the Australian Federal Police about the actions of staff of the Australian Taxation Office connected with the audit and the personnel enquiry, alleging breaches of the secrecy provisions of s16 of the ITAA by those staff in the use of audit information and documents and misuse of the information gathering powers under the ITAA.

  13. On 2 January 1996 the Applicant lodged a complaint with the Australian Taxation Office about the actions of staff connected with the audit and the personnel enquiry, alleging breaches of the secrecy provisions of s16 of the ITAA by both staff in the use of audit information and documents and misuse of the information gathering powers under the ITAA.

  14. On 7 May 1996 the Applicant filed an application in the Federal Court of Australia under the Administrative Decision (Judicial Review) (AD(JR)) Act seeking judicial review of the decision of the Australian Taxation Office not to act upon the Applicant’s complaint referred to in paragraph 13. That application for review is still before the Federal Court.

  15. On 14 November 1996 the Applicant filed an application in the Federal Court of Australia under the AD(JR) Act seeking a review of the decision of the Australian Federal Police not to act upon the complaint referred to in paragraph 12. That application for review is still before the Federal Court. [The respondent’s attempt to seek to have that application dismissed or set aside on the ground that there was no “reviewable decision” identified in the application which qualified pursuant to s3(1) of the AD(JR) Act was dismissed].

An assessment for the 1995 income year was issued to the Applicant on 27 November 1995 on the basis of the information contained in his 1995 Income Tax Return which allowed all claimed deductions. That position was not affected by the Amended Assessment for the 1995 income year issued on the 22nd of December 1995.

SUPPLEMENTARY STATEMENT OF AGREED FACTS



  1. By a letter to the Respondent dated 10 March 1997 the Applicant sought to include in the Objection to the Assessment for the 1995 income year a further claim for deduction for an additional expense of $100.00 being the cost of purchasing two editions of a publication known as Miller’s Antique Guide.

  2. The Miller’s Antique Guide was purchased in the 1995 tax year and shows the auction prices of art works on the international market.

  3. By letter dated 19 March 1997 the Respondent disallowed any deduction for the further claimed expense of $100.00 to the Applicant for the 1995 income year.



2. Subsequent negotiations between the parties led to revised claims in relation to the amounts previously objected to. The objected amounts were adjusted to the following:



YEAR AMOUNT

1995 $1,669

1996 $2,156



3. As I understand the claims they relate to the deductibility of three species of expenditure: (1) legal costs - including advice - incurred in defending the various criminal charges arising out of the sale of art works, said to involve the Applicant and his wife, charges which are unrelated to his employment (save, arguably, the claim said to involve a fraudulent use of a medical certificate, a charge since withdrawn); (2) costs incurred in seeking a “Dietrich” order, that is, seeking a stay of the criminal proceedings on the basis that the Applicant was unable to obtain Legal Aid; and (3) costs incurred in “establishing” that the Commissioner acted in breach of s16 of the ITTA, a quest which involved several applications to the courts in an endeavour to challenge decisions made by the Federal Police and the Tax Office. To the extent that incidental expenses have been incurred in defending the various tax audits undertaken by the respondent, the parties have arrived at an agreed apportionment of such expenditure and such expenses are not in issue before me.



4. At the hearing of his references from the decisions of the respondent disallowing his objections against the amended assessments for the 1995 and 1996 tax years, the Applicant appeared in person; the respondent was represented by Mr C of counsel, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor (I have deliberately refrained from naming counsel to avoid the risk that his name may identify the State in which the events have occurred, and hence risk identifying the taxpayer). Apart from tendering numerous exhibits (all of which were objected to by counsel on the grounds of relevance), the Applicant did not give evidence, albeit he was rigorously cross-examined.



5. The Applicant took a preliminary objection, to the effect that Mr C’s appearance was in breach of the secrecy provision of the ITTA. It was said that the ATO instructing “outside” advisers, such as the Australian Government Solicitor, who in turn brief counsel, somehow offends against s16 of the ITTA.



6. The argument, if I understand it at all, is as involved as it is confused. It runs something like this: Counsel, not being an “officer” of the ATO as defined by the ITTA, has been given confidential information, which is expressly prohibited by that Act. Taken in steps, the submission runs something like this:



Section 16(1) defines “officer” to mean:



“a person who is or has been appointed or employed by the Commonwealth ... and who by reason of that appointment or employment ... may acquire information or has acquired information respecting the affairs of any other person, disclosed or obtained under the provisions of this Act or of any previous law of the Commonwealth relating to income tax;



Subsection 16(1A) provides;



“For purposes of this section, a person who, although not appointed or employed by the Commonwealth, performs services for the Commonwealth shall be taken to be employed by the Commonwealth.”



Subsection 16(2) provides:



“Subject to this section, an officer shall not either directly or indirectly, except in the performance of any duty as an officer ... divulge or communicate to any person any information respecting the affairs of another person acquired by the officer as mentioned in the definition of ‘officer’ in subsection (1).”



Subsection 16(4) provides:



“Nothing in this section shall be deemed to prohibit the Commissioner ... from communicating any information to:



(a) any person performing, in pursuance of any appointment or employment by the Commonwealth, any duty arising under any Act administered by the Commissioner of Taxation for the purpose of enabling that person to carry out any such duty;”



7. I was requested by the Applicant to refer the matter to the Federal Court for a ruling. This I declined to do as I consider the whole argument to be misconceived. I am satisfied that the Commissioner is empowered to retain the Australian Government Solicitor (AGS) or any other practising solicitor, for the purpose of proceedings before this Tribunal, and that AGS is in turn empowered to instruct counsel without contravening s16. This matter received some attention by Lockhart J in Consolidated Press Holding Ltd & Ors v FCT & Anor 95 ATC 4231, where his Honour stated (at pp 4236-7):



“Some attention was devoted in argument to the position of AGS and counsel instructed by it to act for the Commissioner in litigation or in an advisory capacity. Whatever may have been the position before the introduction of sub-s. (1A) to s.16 in 1985, solicitors and counsel retained by the Commissioner must now be taken to be employed by the Commonwealth for the purposes of s. 16 because they perform services for the Commonwealth. They are brought under the umbrella of secrecy imposed by s. 16.”



8. For the sake of completeness, I will deal briefly with one of the respondent’s submissions, to the effect that in any event, s15 of the Taxation Administration Act provides, inter alia, that a Deputy Commissioner may be represented by a barrister or solicitor in any action or other proceeding under, or arising out of, a taxation law in which the Deputy Commissioner is a party. From this it is said to follow that the section authorises the Commissioner of Taxation to be legally represented by counsel.



9. Subsection 15(1)provides that:



“In any action ... or other proceeding under, or arising out of, a taxation law instituted by or on behalf of the Deputy Commissioner ... to which the Deputy Commissioner ... is a party or in which the Deputy Commissioner intervenes or seeks to intervene, the Deputy Commissioner ... may appear personally or may be represented by -



(a) a person enrolled as a barrister, solicitor, barrister and solicitor or legal practitioner of a federal court or of the Supreme Court of a State or Territory;”



(b) ...



10. With respect, I have not been persuaded by this submission, which would be more persuasive if either the section read: “In any action arising out of a taxation law in which the Commissioner etc is a party, he may appear personally or may be represented by etc”, or, alternatively, by the interposition of “or” in s15(1) [“... or to which the Commissioner is a party”]. However, on a literal reading of the section, I find it does no more than enable the Commissioner to appear personally or be represented in cases where the proceedings are “instituted by or on behalf of” the Commissioner, or in which he “intervenes or seeks to intervene”.



11. Whilst it may be said that the words “to intervene” or “seek to intervene” refer to the right of a person having a legally recognised interest in the outcome of proceedings to come in and demand to be heard and assert his claim and insist on his interest being pressed home to judgment, I am not convinced that s15(1), standing alone, would be sufficient to entitle the Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner to be represented in proceedings referred to in s15(1) by “outside” counsel.



12. Be that as it may, it is for the Tribunal to determine who may and who may not appear in proceedings before it. Section 32 of the AAT Act provides that “at the hearing of a proceeding before the Tribunal, a party to the proceeding may appear in person or may be represented by some other person. I hold that that section overrides the more restrictive provision of the Tax Administration Act.



13. In any event, even assuming that the secrecy provision of the ITTA prevents taxation records being made available to “outside” counsel, I do not believe that I have the competence to deny counsel the right of audience on a submission that his instructions were illegally obtained. A taxpayer who believes himself thus aggrieved must seek his remedy elsewhere.



14. The Applicant mounted collateral attack from a side flank: it is said that the transmission of confidential information obtained by an officer employed by the ATO in the course of his/her employment is confined and restricted to the administration of the ITTA, and, insofar as confidential information was supplied for a purposes of different legislation, s16 has no application, and hence the disclosure of confidential tax information to the personnel section of the ATO is prohibited.



15. What I understand the gravamen of this complaint to be is that the use, for example, of information by the Special Audit Manager in a personnel inquiry concerning the possession by the Applicant of blank medical certificates involves a breach of secrecy because this information was said to have come into the officer’s possession as a result of a notice served on the State Police pursuant to s264 of the ITTA. [Some blank medical certificates were found in the Applicant’s briefcase during the execution of a search warrant carried out on his person and his property]. From this, it was said to follow that the use by the Auditor of confidential and personal information concerning the income tax audits of the Applicant, his wife and his daughter for purposes of conducting a personal evaluation of the Applicant’s leave entitlements under the Public Service Regulations were unlawful.



16. That the above view of the relevant legislation constitutes a breach of secrecy receives some support in a letter from Ms Joanna Mullins, Director, Policy, Commonwealth Ombudsman (Exhibit G), sent to her counterpart in the instant capital city. Her advice turns on the phrase “administered by” where it appears in s5 of the Public Service Act (Cth). According to this opinion:



“The phrase ‘administered by’ has a precise technical meaning. Ministers of State administer Acts through their Departments. According to Administrative Arrangements Orders, the Public Service Act is administered by the Prime Minister through the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet. The fact that Secretaries, such as the Commissioner of Taxation, exercise powers under the Public Service Act does not mean that those Secretaries ‘administer’ the Public Service Act in the technical sense.”



17. Ms Mullins concluded that the effect of s5 of the Public Service Act is that officers of the ATO may only lawfully pass on information to other such officers in order:



“to enable them to carry out duties under tax legislation (ie legislation ‘administered by’ the Commissioner of Taxation”) [Ms Mullins’underlining].



18. I do not propose to deal with the correctness or otherwise of Ms Mullins’s opinion on the effect of s5 of the Public Service Act. She believes that the fact that the Commissioner is passing on “this sort of information” raises serious public concerns. Be that as it may, the correctness or otherwise of that practice falls outside my consideration, which is solely concerned with the deductibility of certain expenditures incurred by this taxpayer.



19. As will already have become clear, the audit into the Applicant’s income tax affairs and those of his wife and daughter were initiated as early as February 1995 following “information received” from the State police. What particularly aroused the ATO’s interest was the finding by the police of a number of unused blank medical certificates in the Applicant’s possession. Armed with this information, the ATO conducted an enquiry to determine whether the Applicant had relied on false medical certificates for the purpose of sick leave. It seems that this enquiry involved looking at the dates the Applicant had attended art auctions to see whether these dates coincided with any absences from work on “medical” grounds. This information was, according to the Applicant, gathered from “outside” sources in reliance on s263 of the ITTA (“The Commissioner, or any officer authorized by him in that behalf, shall have full and free access to all ... books, documents and other papers for any purpose of the Act ...” [my emphasis]. It seems that the respondent was satisfied that, on at least one occasion, the Applicant had taken sick leave in reliance on a forged medical certificate. This, in turn, caused the Applicant to be charged later that month with one count of “false representation” (that is, imposing on the Commonwealth by an untrue representation with a view to obtain a benefit or advantage; see s29A Crimes Act (Cth)). I was given to understand that this charge was recently withdrawn.



20. Pausing here, the ATO’s investigation into how these blank medical certificates came into the Applicant’s possession and/or what use he may have made of them was investigated by an officer of the ATO, and involved a visit to a medical centre where the Applicant’s confidential medical records were made available to the officer. It was said that this involves a breach of the provisions of s263. To meet this attack, the respondent asserted that no breach of the section was involved because the officer who investigated this allegation at the medical centre concerned “did not represent himself as being there in the capacity as an auditor” armed with a warrant to obtain the Applicant’s medical records, but had merely used his “s 263 authority” as a means of identifying himself.



21. Whilst not strictly necessary for my decision, I suspect there is a fly in the ointment of this argument. A s263 authority does not operate merely as a passport, identifying the authorised officer. The section is clear: once an officer is authorised to enter a building for purposes of inspecting books, documents and other papers, he may legally enter the premises for purposes of that inspection. That authority can only be withdrawn if, “on being requested by the occupier ... for proof of authority, the officer does not produce an authority in writing signed by the Commissioner etc”. On that view, it is no defence to say that what occurred here did not involve the use of the Commissioner’s powers under s263.



22. Again, whilst not relevant to my review, I am satisfied that it is a misuse of a s263 warrant to obtain documents for the purpose of providing grist to the mill of a criminal prosecution unconnected with breaches of the ITTA. If, in this case, it was sought to establish that these blank certificates were stolen, it would be unlawful for an auditor to access the documents in reliance on a s263 warrant, even if it was suspected that the taxpayer defrauded the Tax Office by unlawfully taking sick leave in reliance on the suspect certificates.



23. It is a fair statement to note that the Applicant moved heaven and earth to have his suspension set aside. Looking at the exhibits, it would appear that this odyssey began with an FOI application to view his personnel file, which, on inspection, was found to contain confidential tax information, concerning not only his affairs, but those of his wife and daughter. It is the transfer of this information from the audit department to the personnel section which is said to constitute a breach of s16 of the ITTA as well as the Crimes Act.



24. Pausing here, and noting that a final conclusion on this is not strictly necessary for my determination, a persuasive argument can be advanced that the personnel section of the Tax Office should be denied access to the tax files of members of the Tax Office. If employers other than the Commissioner of Taxation are denied access to the confidential income tax records of their employees, it is, to say the least, surprising that employees of the Tax Office have that statutory right eroded because the Commissioner, unique amongst employers, is allowed to wear two hats (he administers the ITTA as well as being an “authorized officer” with respect to employees of the Tax Office and, in that capacity, is authorized to suspend any employee charged with having committed any criminal offence against the laws of Australia or of a State punishable either on indictment or on summary conviction; see s62(1) Public Service Act (Cth)). If a doctor, employed by a public hospital, is being treated by one its consultants for a communicable disease, is the personnel section of the hospital entitled to the patient’s clinical records because he happens to be employed by the hospital? Unless there is some overriding issue of public policy involved, I suspect the answer is “No”.



25. It was the discovery that the personnel section of the ATO had received confidential information from the audit section which precipitated the Applicant’s attempts in (i) seeking that the “guilty” person or persons be prosecuted, (ii) his complaints to the Ombudsman, (iii) his FOI requests, (iv) attempting to “freeze” the investigation into his affairs until after the criminal proceedings had been concluded, and (v) his application for a “Dietrich” order to stay the criminal prosecutions.



26. It is the deductibility of the various expenditures incurred in these exercises which is the issue I have to decide. As itemised above, these are made up of:



(a) expenses incurred in preparing documentation and information relevant to the audit and objection processes;

(b) lodging a grievance with the Merit Protection Review Authority (MPRA);

(c) seeking legal aid and advice on a charge of misusing a medical certificate;

(d) seeking legal advice in relation to the criminal charges;

(e) the taking of AD(JR) action against the ATO in the Federal Court of Australia; and

(f) preparing submissions to the ATO in order to claim and maintain hardship pay.



27. Turning to the law - leaving aside whether the Commissioner was wrong in law in using his powers to collect information under the ITTA for purposes of disciplinary proceedings under the Public Service Act and assuming for present purposes that the information thus obtained resulted in the Applicant’s suspension - it is now well established that expenses incurred by an employee in seeking to prevent his employer from terminating his contract of employment where the performance of his duties is in issue are deductible under s51; see per Burchett and Drummond JJ in the Full Federal Court in FCT v Rowe 95 ATC 4691 (on appeal 97 ATC 4317).



28. The issue before the court in Rowe, however, was not so much the deductibility of the expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in fighting a “show cause” notice why he should not be dismissed, but whether, having claimed - and been allowed - said expenditure in one tax year, an ex gratia reimbursement of those expenses in a subsequent tax year constituted income, either under ordinary concepts, or by some form of “symmetry”. Given the peculiar fact situation in that case, whilst strictly speaking their Honour’s observations on the deductibility of the expenses incurred in fighting the appellant’s dismissal may be said to constitute obiter dicta, it was nevertheless a finding which was critical in characterising the receipt of the reimbursement of those expenses and hence must be given the greatest weight.



29. Having said that, the facts in Rowe are a far cry from those before me. This taxpayer’s suspension - and possible dismissal - is not related to performance of his duties as a tax officer, but based on charges of fraudulent conduct unrelated to any activities within the Tax Office. In other words, the expenditure was not incurred in attempting to save his job; his continued employment with the Tax Office will depend on the outcome of his trial, if and when it proceeds.



30. Turning to the expenses claimed in seeking to defend the criminal charges, the first thing to observe is that these charges arise out of activities unconnected with the Applicant’s work in the Tax Office, and hence the expenditure cannot easily be seen as incurred in the course of gaining or producing the Applicant’s assessable income (he derived no income from his wife’s art business). The fact that certain advantageous consequences may flow from successfully defending these charges is not sufficient to make these expenses incidental or relevant to the Applicant gaining or producing assessable income. Indeed, I can see little distinction between the costs incurred in defending the criminal charges and any expenditure incurred in seeking to establish that the Tax Office acted in contravention of s16 of the ITTA.



31. At its highest, the so-called “section 16” expenses may be said to have been incurred in trawling for evidence which, arguably, may be excluded by the judge in the exercise of his judicial discretion if and when the Applicant’s case finally comes to trial. It is then said, presumably, that the exclusion of this material may lead to the Applicant’s acquittal or a nolle prosequi, which, in turn, would automatically result in his reinstatement to his former position. That, says the Applicant, is sufficient to attract deductibility of the expenditure because a successful defence would restore him to the position where he was before the charges were brought. For this proposition, he relies heavily on the decision in Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v FCT 80 ATC 4542. However, in that case, the Court found that the impugned acts of the directors were carried out in their capacities as directors and agents of the taxpayer company and, however misconceived, were undertaken for the benefit of the company, and hence “incidental and relevant” to the derivation of the company’s assessable income. This is a far cry from the facts of this case, where I am unable to perceive that the occasion of the outgoing was the production of assessable income; in the terms of tort law, the “perceived connection” between the expenditure and the derivation of assessable income is “too remote”. In any event, the Applicant was adamant that he was anxious to protect his good name and reputation and, to that extent, the expenditure must be seen as being of a private nature.



32. In the result, I am satisfied that neither the expenses incurred in defending the criminal prosecution nor those expended in seeking to establish that the ATO acted contrary to the provisions of s16 of the ITTA are allowable deductions.



33. That leaves the “Dietrich” order expenditure. It is said that if the Applicant were successful in obtaining a permanent stay of the criminal proceedings:



“This could have placed me in a limbo position, where I would remain on suspension hardship wages indefinitely and not be tried. From that position I could have negotiated a retirement package. That is employment and income related.”



34. It is an ingenious argument, but it must fail, and for a number of reasons. In the first place, it seems to me that if the expenses incurred in defending the criminal proceedings are not allowable, expenditure incurred in aborting them are “tarred with the same brush”. Secondly, I have some difficulty in seeing such expenditure as being “incurred in gaining or producing” the taxpayer’s assessable income. If anything, the expenditure would have been incurred in getting, not in doing, work as an employee, that is, it would come at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income; see FCT v Maddalena 71 ATC 4161 per Menzies J at 4162. Thirdly, to the extent that is sought to be argued that a stay would result in restoring the Applicant to his former position from which he could thus derive income, or else be placed in a position to negotiate a retirement package, the outgoings are of a capital nature.



35. I determine that the “Dietrich” order component of the claimed deductions are likewise not allowable.



36. That leaves the expenditure on the Miller’s Antique Guide. This was claimed in a letter from the Applicant to the ATO, dated 10 March 1997, requesting that consideration be given to allowing a further deduction of $100 in the 1995 income year, being the cost of the Miller’s Guide. The claim was disallowed on the basis that bankcard records revealed that the expenditure was incurred by the Applicant’s wife (Exhibit 2). The Applicant persisted with his claim in further correspondence (Exhibit 3), advancing a number of reasons why the expenditure should be allowed, one being that:



“with my taxation background I am managing the audit affairs of my wife and incurring the expense in that regard s69(2). The deduction is allowable to me. Thirdly, though the bankcard was in the name of the wife, the expense was mine as I use her card to make interstate purchase. I do not have any credit cards in my name. I also have the only family income stream that pays for the credit card purchase.”



37. It seems to me that real issue is not who paid for the book, but how - assuming it was paid for by the Applicant - it relates to his assessable income. It was conceded during the hearing that (for tax purposes) the art business was conducted solely by the Applicant’s wife and that the taxpayer played no part in that business or derived income from it. In those circumstances I fail to see how the expenditure on Miller’s Antique Guide can be an allowable deduction in his hands any more than if he paid for his wife’s bloomers.



38. It follows from the foregoing reasons that the decisions under review are affirmed.
