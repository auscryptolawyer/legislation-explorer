Anthony Nurun Thai v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1994] FCA 1287; (1994) 94 Atc 4489 (1994) 123 ALR 570 (1994) 53 FCR 252 (26 August 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

ANTHONY NURUN THAI v DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. NG396 of 1994

FED No. 624/94

Number of pages - 26

Bankruptcy - Taxation - Administrative Law

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

LOCKHART, BEAUMONT AND WHITLAM JJ

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - sequestration order - whether debtor is indebted for income tax - whether act of bankruptcy committed by debtor by remaining outside Australia within six months of the presentation of the petition with intent to defeat or delay his creditors within the meaning of s. 44(1)(c) Bankruptcy Act - construction of ss. 204 and 205 Income Tax Assessment Act - whether judgment concerning such construction was interlocutory or final - whether leave to appeal from judgment should be granted

Taxation - construction of sections 204 and 205 Income Tax Assessment Act

Administrative Law - whether appellant denied procedural fairness when sequestration order made

Bankruptcy Act 1966: ss. 30, 40(1)(c)(i), 44(1), 47(1), 52, 306(1)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936: ss. 177, 204, 205, 208, 218

DECISION

INTRODUCTION

LOCKHART, BEAUMONT AND WHITLAM JJ This is an appeal from a sequestration order made by a Judge of the Court (Einfeld J) against the estate of the appellant, Anthony Nurun Thai, on a creditors' petition presented by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation. A motion for leave to appeal from a decision of Davies J on preliminary questions arising in the matter is also before the Court. In order to understand the issues arising on the appeal and on the motion for leave to appeal, it will first be necessary to describe the rather complicated history of the proceedings.

THE HISTORY OF THE PROCEEDINGS

(a) The original petition

2. The Deputy Commissioner's original petition dated 29 October 1993, was presented on 26 November 1993 and was returnable on 21 February 1994. The Deputy Commissioner sought a sequestration order against the estate of Mr Thai, who was then said to reside in Double Bay, N.S.W. and to be an accountant. In the petition, the Deputy Commissioner claimed, relevantly: (a) that, at the date of commission of the act of bankruptcy relied on, Mr Thai was ordinarily resident in Australia (para. 1); (b) that Mr Thai was indebted to the Deputy Commissioner in the sum of $636,787.08 for unpaid income tax and additional tax (para. 2); (c) that within six months before the presentation of the petition, Mr Thai committed the following act of bankruptcy viz., on 17 September 1992 Mr Thai "departed from, and remains outside Australia with the intention of defeating or delaying a creditor" (para. 4).

3. On 16 December 1993, Registrar Lane made an order for substituted service of the petition, directing service upon Mr Munro, solicitor, of Gadens Ridgeway, solicitors. On 14 February 1994, Mr Munro filed an affidavit sworn by him on that date stating, inter alia, that he had received instructions to act in the matter from Mr Thai, who had resided in Hong Kong since his departure from Australia on 17 September 1992. Mr Munro sought a direction that certain issues be separately determined as preliminary questions.

THE REASONING OF DAVIES J ON THE PRELIMINARY QUESTIONS

16. For present purposes, the essential reasoning of Davies J for rejecting Mr Thai's grounds of opposition 1 - 4 may be summarised as follows:

(1) The notices of assessment to income tax claimed by the Deputy Commissioner to constitute the "debt" were issued on 2 September 1992. They specified 3 September 1992 as the due date for payment. The Deputy Commissioner relied upon s.204(1), s.205(1) and s.208(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act") which provide as follows:

"204(1) Subject to the provisions of this

Part, any income tax assessed shall

be due and payable by the person

liable to pay the tax on the date

specified in the notice as the date

upon which tax is due and payable,

not being less than 30 days after

the service of the notice, or, if no

date is so specified, on the

thirtieth day after the service of

the notice.

205(1) Where the Commissioner has reason to

believe that a person liable to tax

may leave Australia before the date

on which the tax is due and payable

the tax shall be due and payable on

such date as the Commissioner

notifies to that person.

...

208(1) Income tax when it becomes due and

payable shall be a debt due to the

Commonwealth, and payable to the

Commissioner in the manner and at

the place prescribed."

17. Davies J mentioned, by way of analogy, the views of Dixon J in Resch v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1942] HCA 2; (1942) 66 CLR 198 (at 228) with respect to the Commissioner's power to extend time for payment. His Honour concluded that s. 205(1) likewise authorised the Commissioner to specify an early date for payment in the notice of assessment which is served; that is to say, the words "is due and payable" in s. 205(1) may be read in the sense of "will be due and payable".

(2) Neither party called evidence on the question whether the Deputy Commissioner had reason to believe that Mr Thai may leave Australia. In all the circumstances the inference should be drawn that he acted under s. 205 in specifying 3 September 1992 as the date.

(3) If, contrary to his Honour's opinion, the specification of the date were invalid, s. 204 would operate as if no date had been specified in the notice of assessment, so that the tax would, in any event, have been due and payable on the thirtieth day after service of the notices of assessment; that is, the debt was payable, on any view, before the petition was presented.

THE REASONING OF EINFELD J FOR REFUSING LEAVE TO FILE AND SERVE OUT OF TIME A NOTICE OF APPEAL FROM THE ORDERS MADE BY DAVIES J, FOR REFUSING TO ADJOURN THE HEARING OF THE PETITION, FOR DISMISSING THE NOTICE OF OPPOSITION TO THE PETITION AND FOR MAKING THE SEQUESTRATION ORDER 18. The essential steps in the process of reasoning of Einfeld J were these:

(1) In refusing Mr Thai's application for leave to file and serve out of time a notice of appeal from the judgment of Davies J, Einfeld J was of the opinion that Mr Thai had no chance of succeeding in the appeal. In particular, Mr Thai had failed to demonstrate any basis for a contention that Davies J's construction of s.205 was wrong. Moreover, the delay in applying for leave had not been explained.

(2) In declining to adjourn the hearing of the petition, Einfeld J referred to the affidavit evidence relied on by Mr Thai and said that it -

"...provides no evidence at all to support the

debtor's assertion that he left Australia and

has remained outside Australia, both of which

facts are admitted, without an intention to

defeat or delay the Deputy Commissioner as one

creditor and perhaps other creditors as well."

19. His Honour added:

"Hence, there is no point in granting an

adjournment for the purposes of allowing the

Deputy Commissioner to meet this evidence

because the evidence does not prove anything

relevant that requires any meeting. I should

add in addition, because it is important on

issues of credit and discretion, that the debtor

argues by counsel today, and his solicitor gave

some brief evidence in support of the assertion,

that it was the intention of the debtor to

return to Australia to prosecute this matter and

deal with it but that on Tuesday of this week he

told his solicitor that he had not made any

special arrangements to come and could not get

on a flight."

20. His Honour said that the absence of information as to Mr Thai's intentions and the "excuse" offered in this connection-

"represents an exercise in humbug to which the

Court will certainly not lend its imprimatur."

21. Einfeld J went on to say this:

"...there simply has not been placed before the

Court any material, even by way of hearsay, to

suggest that there exists any evidence anywhere

to support the debtor's contention. In those

circumstances it is simply trivialising with the

Court and wasting its time, not to mention

others', to contemplate that this matter should

be allowed to go any longer at all. For my

part, I do not propose to be party to it.

(3) In the result, his Honour ordered that the notice of opposition to the petition be dismissed.

(4) Counsel for the Deputy Commissioner then applied for the making of a sequestration order and Einfeld J so ordered.

CONCLUSIONS ON THE APPEAL AND ON THE MOTION FOR LEAVE TO APPEAL

22. As can be seen, the matter has had a complicated history. Since many issues arise for determination in the appeal and on the motion for leave to appeal, it will be convenient to consider them in turn and to begin with the question whether the orders made by Davies J were final (as the Deputy Commissioner contends) or interlocutory.

(a) Were the orders made by Davies J final or interlocutory?

23. In the course of argument, we indicated that we had formed the view that the orders made by Davies J were not final orders, but were interlocutory only. Our reasons for this conclusion are as follows.

24. As has been noted, Davies J ordered, by consent, that certain of the grounds of opposition to the petition be determined as preliminary questions. His Honour clearly had power so to order, either by virtue of s. 30 of the Act or the implied jurisdiction of the Court or perhaps by invoking the provisions of Rule 113 of the Bankruptcy Rules.

25. In considering whether the orders made by Davies J, dismissing some of the grounds of opposition and standing over the remaining ground to the hearing of the petition by Einfeld J, were interlocutory or final, it is necessary to refer to the relevant legislative scheme. By s. 47(1) of the Act, a creditor's petition shall be in accordance with the prescribed form and shall be verified and served as there provided (see, generally, Daly v Watson, Full Federal Court, Davies, Beaumont and Gummow JJ, 7 June 1994, unreported). Rule 20 of the Bankruptcy Rules, read with Form 8, contemplates that grounds of opposition to the petition will be specified and that any statement in the petition which is denied or disputed will be identified by the debtor. By s. 52 of the Act, it is relevantly provided as follows:

"52.(1) At the hearing of a creditor's

petition, the Court shall require proof of:

(a) the matters stated in the petition (for

which purpose the Court may accept the

affidavit verifying the petition as

sufficient);

(b) service of the petition; and

(c) the fact that the debt or debts on which

the petitioning creditor relies is or are

still owing.

and, if it is satisfied with the proof of those

matters, may make a sequestration order against

the estate of the debtor.

(2) If the Court is not satisfied with the

proof of any of those matters, or is satisfied

by the debtor -

(a) that he is able to pay his debts; or

(b) that for other sufficient cause a

sequestration order ought not to be made.

it may dismiss the petition.

...."

26. In our opinion, the orders made by Davies J were interlocutory, both as a matter of form and as a matter of substance.

27. As a matter of form, the orders were made in the determination of preliminary questions in which only some of the grounds of opposition raised by the appellant were canvassed. Clearly, other matters remained to be decided on a separate and further occasion, as Davies J noted when giving his reasons for judgment.

28. As a matter of substance, the position is the same. The proceeding before Davies J was confined to the determination of the issues raised by paras. 1 to 4 of Mr Thai's notice of intention to appear. Para. 5 of that notice raised issues of fact and law which remained to be decided on a later occasion. The determination of all those issues and the proof of the other matters of which proof is required by s. 52(1) of the Act must precede the making of a sequestration order. The final orders contemplated by s. 52 are the making of a sequestration order or an order dismissing the petition. The determination of preliminary questions of fact or law is in aid of the making of such final orders. Clearly his Honour was determining anterior or preliminary questions.

29. In support of a contention that the orders made by Davies J were final, counsel for the Deputy Commissioner sought to rely upon the decision and reasoning of a judge of the Court in Tag Pacific Ltd. v McSweeney [1992] FCA 168; (1992) 34 FCR 438. However, for the reasons given recently by a Full Court in NZI Securities Australia Limited v Poignand, (Full Federal Court, Beaumont, Gummow and Carr JJ, 22 July 1994, unreported) we cannot agree with that approach. In NZI Securities, the Full Court held that orders similar in character to those considered by the High Court in Computer Edge Pty Ltd v Apple Computer Inc [1984] HCA 47; (1984) 54 ALR 767 were interlocutory. The Full Court in NZI Securities, referring to comments made by Gummow J in Caboche v Ramsay [1993] FCA 611; (1993) 119 ALR 215 at 225-26 (with the agreement of Ryan and Lee JJ), pointed out that the observations in Tag Pacific were inconsistent with Apple Computer, and should not be followed. We agree. It must follow that the orders now in question were not final.

(b) Should leave be granted to appeal from the orders of Davies J?

30. As has been noted, two motions were before Einfeld J in this connection: (i) on the assumption that those orders were interlocutory and not final, a motion pursuant to O.52 r.10 for leave to appeal from the orders made by Davies J; (ii) on the assumption that the orders were final, a motion to lodge a notice of appeal (as of right) out of time pursuant to O.52 r.15.

31. The form of order as entered on 20 July 1994 purports to record the orders of Einfeld J made on 16 June. The first order as recorded states that:

"1. Leave to appeal from the judgment of Mr Justice Davies

given on 9 May 1994 be refused."

32. No other orders as recorded are relevant for present purposes.

33. It was submitted by counsel for Mr Thai that, as Einfeld J found that the judgment of Davies J was a final judgment, his Honour did not deal with the motion for leave to appeal therefrom which assumed (correctly) that it was an interlocutory judgment.

34. To determine whether Einfeld J dealt with and determined the motion for leave to appeal from Davies J's judgment out of time (on the assumption that it was an interlocutory judgment) it is necessary to examine, not merely the formal order to which we have just referred, but the transcript of the proceedings before Einfeld J and his reasons for judgment. We have done this. In our opinion the submission made on behalf of Mr Thai is correct. The motion for leave to appeal from Davies J's interlocutory judgment was not dealt with by Einfeld J.

35. In those circumstances, the matter, that is the application for leave, not having been dealt with by Einfeld J, we should now consider it.

36. In our opinion, it is appropriate that leave to appeal from the decision of Davies J be granted. His Honour's decision raises an important question of the construction of s. 205, and related provisions, of the Assessment Act. For that reason alone, there would be much to be said in favour of the grant of leave. The special circumstances of this case reinforce that view. With one possible qualification, to be mentioned shortly, unless leave is granted, Mr Thai will never have the opportunity to test the correctness of the decision of Davies J before a Full Federal Court, notwithstanding the practical significance of that decision from Mr Thai's stand point. Fairness and justice require that leave be granted.

37. For the sake of completeness (and this is the qualification indicated) it should be mentioned that it is at least arguable that, in any event, it may have been open to Mr Thai to challenge the reasoning and decision of Davies J in the exercise of his appeal as of right from the sequestration order made by Einfeld J. It is accepted that the sequestration order was final and that an appeal as of right lies from that order. Further, it is established that an appeal lies from the order made as the "operative judicial act", rather than from the reasons for making the order (see Driclad Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 91; (1968) 121 CLR 45 at 64). It may follow that, in any event, it would have been open to Mr Thai to contend before us that one reason why the sequestration order should not have been made was that his grounds of oppositions (1) to (4) had been made out. But, since we have decided to grant leave in any event, we need not now pursue the point.

(c) Is Mr Thai indebted in the sum of $636,787.08 for unpaid income tax together with additional tax for late payment?

38. The question is whether Mr Thai owed $636,787.08 for tax at the date of the sequestration order.

39. It was argued before Davies J by counsel for the Deputy Commissioner that s. 177(1) of the Assessment Act precluded the Court from considering the challenges which Mr Thai made to the five notices of assessment issued by the Deputy Commissioner in relation to Mr Thai's taxable income for the years ended 30 June 1986 to 1990 inclusive, in each case issued on 2 September 1992. Section 177(1) provides as follows:

"177(1) The production of a notice of

assessment, or of a document under the hand of

the Commissioner, a Second Commissioner, or a

Deputy Commissioner, purporting to be a copy of

a notice of assessment, shall be conclusive

evidence of the due making of the assessment

and, except in proceedings under Part IVC of the

Taxation Administration Act 1953 on a review or

appeal relating to the assessment, that the

amount and all the particulars of the assessment

are correct."

40. Davies J held that in his opinion the due date for payment expressed in a notice of assessment is not a "particular of the assessment" which s. 177(1) makes conclusive. He said:

"In my opinion, the due date for payment

expressed in a notice of assessment is not a

particular of the assessment which s. 177(1)

makes conclusive. Sections 166, 167, 168 and

169 deal with the making of an assessment. In

each case, the assessment is an assessment of

the taxable income of the person assessed and of

the tax payable. Those provisions appear in

Part IV of the Assessment Act. The Assessment

Act deals with the question of the date when the

assessed tax is due and payable in Division 1 of

Part VI."

41. No notice of contention with respect to this finding of Davies J adverse to the Deputy Commissioner was filed by the him (see O.52 r.22(3)). Nor did the Deputy Commissioner otherwise seek to challenge this finding before us on appeal (see Chittick v Ackland [1984] FCA 29; (1984) 1 FCR 254 at 256 per Lockhart and Morling JJ).

42. Central to the issues agitated on appeal are ss. 204 and 205 of the Assessment Act the terms of which were set out earlier.

43. The Deputy Commissioner served the notices issued pursuant to s. 205 on 2 September 1992 which purported to make the tax due and payable on 3 September 1992. Mr Thai contended that these notices were invalid because s. 204 was not complied with and that the fixing of a date for payment of tax by notices issued under s. 204 is a necessary prerequisite to the operation of s. 205.

44. The meaning of the word "due" and of the expression "due and payable" where appearing in the Assessment Act has been considered by the High Court from time to time and in different contexts. The cases are conveniently collected in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1 per Gibbs CJ at 8 and 9 and Mason J (with whose reasons for judgment Aickin and Wilson JJ agreed) at 15 - 17. See also Commissioner of State Taxation (WA) v Pollock (1993) 12 ACSR 217 per Ipp J at 230, with whose reasons for judgment Wallwork J agreed at 235; and Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kinny (1988) 15 NSWLR 701 per Smart J at 704. It is unnecessary for present purposes to consider the meaning of the word "due" except where it is used in conjunction with the word "payable" in the composite expression "due and payable" where appearing in ss. 204 and 205.

45. It was established in Clyne that income tax is due when it is assessed and notice is served of that assessment; and that tax does not become payable before the date fixed by whichever limb of s. 204 applies: per Mason J at 16.

46. The principal question in the appeal from the judgment of Davies J is whether the Commissioner's power to issue a notice under s. 205 of the Assessment Act arises only after a notice has been previously issued pursuant to s. 204(1) either specifying the date upon which tax is due and payable (the first limb) or not specifying such date so that the tax becomes due and payable on the thirtieth day after the service of the notice (the second limb).

47. Expressed another way, the question is whether the s. 205 power arises only if a date upon which tax is due and payable has been previously fixed by a notice issued under s. 204. Is s. 205 a provision dependent upon and auxiliary to s. 204, empowering the Commissioner to abridge the time for payment already fixed either by the Commissioner's choice under the first limb of s. 204 or the self operating provisions of the second limb? Must s. 204 operate to fix the time on which the tax becomes due and payable before s. 205 can be invoked by the Commissioner?

48. The function of s. 205 is plain. It is designed to protect the revenue against taxpayers liable to pay tax who seek to escape payment of it by quitting the jurisdiction.

49. The history of ss. 204 and 205 in federal income tax law commences with the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915 (Cth), ss. 36 and 41. Then came the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 (Cth), ss. 40 and 54. When the Assessment Act was enacted in 1936 it included ss. 204 and 205 in a form not materially different from the form which they presently take so far as relevant to the questions which arise in this case. They were introduced as part of a regime whereby taxpayers who wished to leave Australia could not do so without first obtaining a certificate from the Commissioner that the taxpayer was not liable to pay income tax or that arrangements had been made to the Commissioner's satisfaction for the payment of income tax (ss. 211, 212 and 213).

50. Alterations were made to ss. 204 and 205 by the Income Tax Assessment Act (No 2) 1940 (ss. 12 and 13), the Income Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1954 (s. 11), the Income Tax Laws Assessment Act 1981 (s. 24 and Schedule 1), and the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1984 (ss. 109 and 110). Although the regime which previously required a taxpayer to obtain a certificate from the Commissioner before he or she was able to leave Australia has now changed, the Commissioner has a number of means available to him to recover tax from taxpayers, debtors, trustees, liquidators and receivers (see ss. 215 - 218 and 255 - 259), and to prevent the disposal of assets to protect the revenue: Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Rosenthal (1984) 16 ATR 159. Those means include the Commissioner's power to issue a departure prohibition order under the Taxation Administration Act 1953: ss. 14Q to 14ZA. An order of this kind may be made where a person is "subject to a tax liability" and the Commissioner believes on reasonable grounds, that it is desirable to make such an order for the purpose of ensuring that the person does not depart from Australia for a foreign country without wholly discharging the tax liability or making arrangements satisfactory to the Commissioner for the tax liability to be wholly discharged (s. 14S(1)). The expression "tax liability" is defined by s. 2 (the interpretation section) as meaning a liability to the Commonwealth arising under, or by virtue of, a taxation law.

51. Once the Commissioner has issued a notice under s. 205, so that tax becomes due and payable on the date notified by the Commissioner in that notice, a liability to pay that tax to the Commonwealth arises under the Assessment Act not later than the date so notified to the taxpayer. The Commissioner is then in a position to issue a departure prohibition order under s. 14S of the Taxation Administration Act.

52. It is to be remembered that international reciprocity in enforcing the laws of other countries does not extend to enforcement of their revenue laws by permitting actions for recovery of revenue or admitting claims based thereon. Moreover, no relaxation is afforded in favour of countries which are members of the Commonwealth of Nations: Government of India v Taylor (1955) AC 491 (H.L.).

53. Knowledge of the intention of a taxpayer to leave Australia may sometimes come to the attention of the Commissioner in sufficient time to allow him merely to accelerate the time for payment of tax that has already been fixed by a s. 204 notice. But there are other cases where quick action is required. Many examples come to mind and it is unnecessary to state them. Section 205 exists so that, once the Commissioner has assessed a taxpayer to tax (the process of assessment under ss. 166 to 169 precedes the collection and recovery of tax which has been assessed), he may tailor the time for payment to the cut of the taxpayer's travel plans. Sometimes this may be very short indeed. See Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Naidoo (1981) 55 FLR 245 (Everett J) and Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Keeling (1993) 93 ATC 5208 (Beach J) where each s. 205 notice stated the tax to be due and payable on the same date as that notice was delivered.

54. We see no reason to construe s. 205 so as to confine its operation to the case where a notice of assessment has previously issued and the Commissioner wishes to accelerate the date for payment of tax, though such a case is obviously included within the scope of the section.

55. We reject the argument of counsel for Mr Thai that, before the Commissioner can call in aid s. 205, he must first serve a notice under s. 204 and purport to make tax due and payable either on a date specified in the notice being at least 30 days after service of the notice (the first limb) or, in the absence of the specification of any such date, on the 30th day after service (the second limb).

56. The words in s. 205 "before the date on which the tax is due and payable" (our emphasis) do not assist Mr Thai's argument that the Commissioner cannot exercise any rights under s. 205 until such time as he has first issued notices of assessment which make tax due and payable under s. 204.

57. As we observed earlier, Davies J dealt with this argument and called in aid the observations of Dixon J in Resch v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1942] HCA 2; (1942) 66 CLR 198 on a different but analogous point. As we read his Honour's reasons for judgment it is likely that he would have reached the same conclusion without the benefit of those remarks of Dixon J.

58. Section 205 may be invoked where the Commissioner has issued a notice under s. 204 and, before the time for payment of the tax has arrived, he forms the belief referred to in s. 205. The use of the word "is" is also apposite to include the situation where the Commissioner has not issued a notice under s. 204, but has assessed the taxpayer to tax and has reason to believe that the taxpayer may leave Australia before the date on which tax would be payable if a s. 204 notice were to issue (whichever limb of s. 204 may apply). In either case, the use of the word "is" in s. 205 is apt if read in the sense of "will be due and payable" or "would otherwise be due and payable". This construction give effect to the obvious function which s. 205 is designed to serve. The exercise of power under s. 205 only arises in a case where the Commissioner wishes to invoke its provisions before the date when the tax is due and payable. The date on which tax "is due and payable" is thus always prospective when s. 205 is called in aid by the Commissioner. It does no violence to the language of s. 205 to read the word "is" in the sense in which in our view it should be read.

59. Also, s. 204(1) is introduced by the words "Subject to the provisions of this Part, ..." Section 205 is not governed by s. 204; it operates independently of it. It is a separate source of the Commissioner's power to issue a notice to a taxpayer stating that tax shall be due and payable on a date earlier than a date that would otherwise arise under either limb of s. 204.

60. The construction and operation of s. 205 has been considered by courts in earlier cases.

61. We have referred already to Keeling and Naidoo. In Naidoo, as mentioned above, the s. 205 notice fixed the time at which the tax became due and payable as the date on which the notices of assessment were served. Everett J rejected the argument that the Deputy Commissioner was not justified in taking that course.

62. In Keeling, another case where the s. 205 notice nominated as the date for payment of the tax the very same day on which the notice was issued, Beach J said at 5209-5210:

"Under that section (205(1)) it is open to the

Commissioner to specify any date he chooses for

payment of the tax, including the date of issue

of the Notice of Assessment itself, if he has

reason to believe that a person liable to pay

tax may leave Australia before the date on which

the tax is due and payable."

We agree with this passage from his Honour's reasons subject to the qualification that there may be a case where the issue of a notice in those circumstances is an unreasonable exercise of the power under s. 205. There is no suggestion that the present case is such a case.

63. In Seymour v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 92 ATC 4,774 the Commissioner issued an amended assessment on 3 July 1992 making tax due and payable on 6 July 1992. The Commissioner relied on s. 205 to support the notice. Northrop J said at 4,780:

"In order to do that the Commissioner had to

have 'reason to believe that (the taxpayer) may

leave Australia before the date on which the tax

is due and payable' presumably under the

provisions of s. 204."

64. The Deputy Commissioner had previously issued an assessment for tax for the relevant year of income. The case was concerned with the question whether the Deputy Commissioner had reason to believe that the taxpayer would leave Australia and not with the question whether the issue of a s. 204 notice was a prerequisite to the issue of a s. 205 notice. When his Honour's observations are read in the context of the facts of the case, we see no support for the proposition that he regarded the issue of a notice under s. 204 as a necessary prerequisite to the issue of a notice under s. 205.

65. In Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Thai (1993) 93 ATC 4530 Master Malpass heard a motion to set aside a default judgment obtained by the Deputy Commissioner against Mr Thai for an amount of income tax. Following a taxation audit, five notices of assessment had been issued to Mr Thai on 2 September 1992. (They appear to be the same notices that are before us). Master Malpass ordered that the default judgment be set aside because the originating process on which the judgment was founded did not allege the material facts on which the Deputy Commissioner relied. It failed to comply with the rules of the Supreme Court of New South Wales and disclosed no cause of action. Accordingly, he held that the judgment was irregularly obtained and should be set aside. Master Malpass said (with reference to ss. 204 and 205) at 4,536 that it was arguable that the Deputy Commissioner could not invoke s. 205 unless a notice of assessment to tax had already been served on the taxpayer making the person liable to pay tax and specifying the date on which the tax was due and payable. These observations of Master Malpass are obiter dicta and were in any event carefully prefaced by him as being simply arguable questions, as indeed they are. We do not understand the Master to have reached any final view on the questions.

66. It was not in dispute before Davies J (or before Einfeld J or on appeal) that the Deputy Commissioner believed that Mr Thai might leave Australia before the date on which tax would be due and payable. Nor was it in dispute that the Deputy Commissioner had reason to believe that Mr Thai might do so.

67. In our opinion the notices issued by the Deputy Commissioner in reliance upon s. 205 are valid notwithstanding that they were not preceded by the issue of notices under s. 204. The s. 205 notices operated as notices of assessments to tax in their own right and fixed the date on which the tax was due and payable as the following day, 3 September 1992. The amount claimed by the Deputy Commissioner to be due and payable as unpaid income tax, together with additional tax for late payment, constitutes a debt due by Mr Thai to the Commonwealth.

68. Before leaving this question we should mention that Davies J expressed the opinion, by way of obiter dicta, that the second limb of s. 204 should be construed so that even if a notice under s. 205 had been issued which specified a date on which the tax would become due and payable, and the notice was invalid, (his Honour was considering the hypothesis, not argued in this case, as to whether the Deputy Commissioner had or had not reason to believe that Mr Thai may leave Australia):

"Section 204 would operate as if no date had

been specified in the notice of assessment and,

accordingly, the tax would have been due and

payable on the 30th day after the service of the

notices of amended assessment."

We respectfully disagree. Section 204 has two limbs, each independent of the other. In each case there must be a written document, a notice telling the taxpayer the amount of tax to which he has been assessed. The notice may specify the date when the tax is due and payable. If there is no reference to the date for payment (obviously this would be a rare occurrence), then the section operates of its own force to determine that date. The second limb is in the nature of a deeming provision. Each limb of the section depends for its operation upon the terms of the written document, the notice served upon the taxpayer. The notice is the means whereby a taxpayer knows the amount of tax to which he has been assessed and when the tax is due and payable.

69. A notice purporting to state a date for payment of tax which is invalid cannot be a notice on which no date for payment is specified. It has been specified in fact. The date appears on the face of the notice. To satisfy the terms of the second limb of s. 204 there must be a notice which does not specify the date on which tax is due and payable.

(d) Was there no evidence to make the sequestration order and was procedural fairness observed?

70. In this appeal Mr Thai complains that he was denied procedural fairness in the hearing before Einfeld J and that there was no evidence to support the making of a sequestration order. Specifically, it is said that the Deputy Commissioner adduced no evidence at the hearing; alternatively, that if the affidavits filed on behalf of the Deputy Commissioner were admitted into evidence, then his Honour failed to rule on objections to those affidavits and further the deponents were not produced for cross-examination; and, finally, that counsel for Mr Thai was not given an opportunity to address.

71. It is important to bear in mind that there was only one question to be tried before Einfeld J. That was whether, within the period alleged in the amended paragraph 4 of the petition, Mr Thai remained outside Australia with the intent to defeat or delay his creditors. Counsel for Mr Thai now submits that, by virtue of s. 52(1) of the Act, the Deputy Commissioner had to prove at the hearing on 16 June 1994 each and every allegation of fact in the creditor's petition. However, this submission cannot be accepted. It is quite contrary to the way in which the case was managed by the Court and conducted by the parties. It also overlooks the fact that the judgment of Davies J on certain of those matters, although interlocutory, bound the parties, subject to his Honour's findings being reversed on appeal.

72. As has been noted, the grounds of opposition to the petition were specified in the notice filed on 15 February 1994. At the hearing before Davies J on 19 April 1994, counsel for Mr Thai identified the matters in issue between the parties. He also handed up an outline of submissions which said relevantly:

"This is an application to have paragraphs 1-4

of the Debtor's Notice of Intention to Appear

("Notice of Intention") filed 15 February 1994

determined at a preliminary hearing before the

substantive ground of opposition to the making

of a sequestration order - para 5 - is heard on

its merits at the hearing specially fixed on

16-17 June next.

...

If the debtor does not succeed on any of the

grounds in paragraphs 1-4 of the Notice of

Intention then the debtor would seek directions

as to the filing of affidavits on his behalf

going to the issues raised in paragraph 5 of the

Notice of Intention."

73. As to the act of bankruptcy alleged in paragraph 4 of the petition, counsel for Mr Thai said to Davies J:

"... there is no issue that the debtor departed

Australia on 17 September 1992 ... There is also

no issue that the debtor remained outside

Australia since that date up to an including

today ... There is an issue, your Honour, as to

the intention, but for the purposes of today

that will not become relevant. That would

simply become relevant if we have to go on to

the 16 and 17 June hearing."

74. Davies J did, of course, allow paragraph 4 of the petition to be amended. His Honour made no order as to the amendment of Mr Thai's grounds of opposition. Paragraph 5 of the notice filed on 9 May 1994 raised a new issue as to Mr Thai's ordinary residence at the time of the act of bankruptcy alleged against him. However, on the second day of the hearing of this appeal, it was conceded by counsel for Mr Thai that this amended notice was not served on the Deputy Commissioner. It cannot be said therefore, on any view, that Mr Thai's amended notice could have been effective to raise an issue between the parties.

75. There cannot be the slightest doubt that the parties came to the hearing on 16 June 1994 to try the one remaining issue, Mr Thai's intent. This was not simply because the parties had so defined the one remaining issue between them in accordance with the normal ways of the adversary system. The Court is astute to act of its own motion, when necessary, in bankruptcy proceedings. However, as Davies J said:

"The issue of intent to defeat or delay

creditors has been sent apart for separate

determination in a hearing listed for 16 and 17

June 1994."

His Honour made it clear that the fate of the petition would abide the determination of that separate question.

76. That position seems to have been accepted too by counsel for Mr Thai at the hearing before Einfeld J. He commenced his application for leave to appeal out of time this way:

"HIS HONOUR: What do you want to say in favour

of leave to appeal?

MR BEVAN: If the appeal was successful,

your Honour, and if my client were unsuccessful

on paragraph 4 of the amended petition, the

success of an appeal against Davies J's decision

in regards the first three paragraphs of the

notice of intention to appear would enable my

client to succeed and avoid a sequestration

order. It would be my submission that, even if

the Commissioner was entitled to succeed on the

intention to remain out of the country basis, if

it ultimately transpired that Davies J fell into

error and there was in fact no debt, there would

be (no) need to sequestrate Mr Thai's estate."

77. It is convenient now to deal with the hearing before Einfeld J on the question of Mr Thai's intent. Notwithstanding the agreement between the parties that Mr Thai should file any affidavits upon which he proposed to rely by 30 May 1994, he had filed no affidavits.

78. All the affidavits filed by the Deputy Commissioner had been served on Mr Munro long ago, together with the creditor's petition, in accordance with the order for substituted service made on 16 December 1993. (Before Davies J it had been proved that the notices of assessment in respect of the indebtedness to the Deputy Commissioner had been served on Mr Thai on 2 September 1992 at the post office box notified as his address on his last income tax return.)

79. The principal affidavit was that of Mr Ray Love, a recovery officer in the Deputy Commissioner's office at Parramatta. He said that on 7 September 1992 he had personally served Mr Thai with a statement of claim in respect of the amounts claimed in the assessment notices. He annexed a copy of the outgoing passenger card collected from Mr Thai at Sydney International Airport on 17 September 1992. The card showed that Mr Thai left for Hong Kong that day, having indicated that he was a resident departing temporarily for a period of 5 days abroad on business. Mr Love deposed to a telephone conversation with Mr Munro on 21 October 1992. In this conversation Mr Munro allegedly said that Mr Thai had failed to meet him "as scheduled" at the offices of Price Waterhouse on the day that he departed Australia. Mr Love also deposed to the contents of a telephone conversation with Mr Thai on 24 February 1993, in the course of which Mr Love had asked Mr Thai whether he intended to return to Australia.

80. Another affidavit filed by the Deputy Commissioner was that of Mr Robert Crowe, the officer in his Parramatta office handling Mr Thai's objections against the assessments the subject of the notices. He set out the terms of a conversation with Mr Munro on 9 February 1993 about Mr Thai's intention of returning to Australia. He also deposed to the contents of a telephone conversation with Mr Thai on 24 February 1993, in which Mr Thai allegedly said that he would not be returning to Australia.

81. Ms Carolyn Morris, a solicitor with the Australian Government Solicitor, also swore an affidavit filed by the Deputy Commissioner. She annexed a copy of a letter she wrote to Mr Munro on 9 September 1992 confirming his instructions conveyed to her in a telephone conversation after 7 September 1992 (when the statement of claim was served) that Mr Thai was "not now intending to leave Australia permanently or travel overseas at this time."

82. Confronted with these affidavits and having filed no affidavits, Mr Thai appeared to be in a difficult situation when his counsel applied at the commencement of the hearing before Einfeld J to file and read two affidavits sworn that morning. The affidavits had been sworn by Mr Munro and by Mr Thai's wife. Counsel for Mr Thai indicated that these affidavits were prepared "at the last minute" because it "only became apparent to my instructing solicitor yesterday that Mr Thai was unable to return" and give evidence. Counsel for Mr Thai then said:

"Without those affidavits, your Honour, there is

simply no evidence to meet the amended petition

and the success of the amended petition will be

a foregone conclusion. ... Essentially, without

the evidence going on, your Honour, there is

just no evidence to meet the amended petition."

83. Einfeld J was critical of the fact that there was no affidavit in support of either the motion to appeal out of time from the decision of Davies J or the oral application to use this late evidence. His Honour pointed out correctly that it was most unsatisfactory in the circumstances that counsel should seek to explain the delay in filing the motion for leave to appeal and the failure to comply with the agreed timetable as to the filing of affidavits by statements from the bar table. Accordingly, counsel for Mr Thai called his instructing solicitor, Mr Munro, to give evidence on these matters.

84. Mr Munro's evidence as to why the affidavits were filed late was quite unsatisfactory. In cross-examination, Mr Munro was asked about the effect of the agreement between the parties that any affidavits sworn by Mr Thai should have been filed by 30 May 1994. Mr Munro said that he had prepared a draft affidavit and sent it to Mr Thai, but agreed that Mr Thai did not send it back. Yet in his evidence-in-chief Mr Munro said that it was only on 10 June 1994 that he told Mr Thai that he "would like him to be present at the hearing." He also said that it was on the morning of 14 June 1994 that Mr Thai told him that he had been unable to arrange a flight "because flights were booked out, it was a four-day holiday weekend in Hong Kong apparently." It is not surprising that his Honour should have described this excuse as "humbug".

85. After Mr Munro had given his evidence, counsel for Mr Thai completed his submissions on the application for leave to file the affidavits. Einfeld J then gave the judgment which is the subject of this appeal.

86. His Honour admitted the two affidavits into evidence. Much of Mr Munro's affidavit was irrelevant since it dealt with the issue of Mr Thai's indebtedness which was not in dispute before his Honour. Mr Munro referred in his affidavit to the affidavits sworn by Mr Love, Mr Crowe and Ms Morris and, by reference to specific paragraphs in each of those affidavits, sought variously to contradict or supplement their statements.

87. Necessarily anything relevant which Mr Munro said or wrote on any occasion was done in accordance with the instructions of his client, Mr Thai. He had written to Ms Morris on 7 September 1992 saying that his client "intends to go overseas on Saturday next." The letter made no reference to the statement of claim. It may possibly have been written before Mr Munro knew of the statement of claim which was only issued that day. Mr Munro did not say. (The letter appears to have been prompted by the service upon Mr Munro of a notice under s. 218 of the Assessment Act.) But he did deny making the statement attributed to him by Ms Morris in their subsequent telephone conversation.

88. Interestingly, Mr Munro expressly confirmed as a fact what Mr Love said that he had been told by Mr Munro about the way in which the solicitor learned upon arriving at Price Waterhouse that a scheduled meeting with his client's accountant had been cancelled because Mr Thai had left Australia. Mr Munro also made the bald statement that Mr Thai "remained overseas on business." In his oral evidence, Mr Munro told his Honour that he had been told this by his client. Mr Munro did not, however, say that he believed the statement, nor did he set out any possible grounds for such a belief. That statement could not even have been admitted under Rule 141A of the Bankruptcy Rules.

89. Mr Munro also annexed a copy of the statement of the joint assets and liabilities of Mr and Mrs Thai which he handed to Mr Crowe at their meeting on 9 February 1993. This showed a deficiency of $1,443,070.

90. The affidavit of Mrs Thai also contained entirely irrelevant material and statements that are obviously hearsay, as to which sources of information and grounds of belief are not set out. She described herself as the "estranged wife of the debtor" and said that after a number of major arguments during "August/September 1992" Mr Thai announced that he was "leaving" the day before he departed Australia.

91. In his judgment his Honour dealt expressly with the evidence in the affidavits of Mr Munro and Mrs Thai. He did not refer to the affidavits filed on behalf of the Deputy Commissioner. When the matter had first been called on, his Honour had said that he had "not been through the affidavits at all yet." However, it is most likely that, by the time judgment was given, the affidavits referred to in Mr Munro's affidavit had been read by his Honour in order to make that affidavit comprehensible. It is true that counsel for the Deputy Commissioner did not expressly indicate that they relied upon those affidavits. Perhaps they did not need to do so, having regard to the way in which the remaining issue on the petition had been prepared for trial and what was said by counsel for Mr Thai at the outset about the late evidence being required to meet the allegations against his client. Nonetheless, it is worth emphasizing that the practice in bankruptcy proceedings accords with that described in Practice Note No. 5 re-issued by the Chief Justice on 8 April 1994. Even where an affidavit is not formally read to the Court, counsel should request that it be treated as read.

92. The submission by counsel for Mr Thai to the effect that the Deputy Commissioner did not "go into evidence" cannot be accepted. The Deputy Commissioner was in every sense the beginning party. He bore the burden of proof on the issue of Mr Thai's intent to defeat or delay his creditors. When his Honour described the evidence adduced on behalf of Mr Thai as not supporting any assertion that he remained outside Australia "without an intention to defeat or delay" his creditors, the onus of proof was not being reversed. His Honour's language was merely picking up the sense of the allegation made by Mr Thai in paragraph 5 of his notice of opposition that he "did not have an intention to defeat or delay his creditors."

93. The evidentiary burden may, of course, shift. In Re Andrews; Ex parte Manning (1966) 8 FLR 53 Clyne J said (at 58):

"Intent to defeat or delay is a question of fact

and must be alleged by a petitioning creditor in

his petition and proved. It is seldom that the

intent of a debtor can be proved by a debtor's

own statements, and it is usually shown by

inference from the circumstances ... The

evidence necessary to prove the intent must

differ according to the circumstances of each

particular case. The conduct of a debtor in

various forms may give rise to a presumption of

intent obliging the debtor to rebut the

presumption, but in the end this intent must be

established."

And in Barton v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 43; (1974) 131 CLR 370 Stephen J referred (at 376) to facts concerning a debtor remaining overseas requiring explanation by exculpatory circumstances.

94. At the commencement of the hearing before Einfeld J counsel for Mr Thai sought to hand up a document entitled "Debtor's Schedule of Objections to Petitioning Creditor's Affidavits." His Honour did not receive the document and did not rule upon these objections. The objections (which have been added to the appeal papers) are rightly described by counsel for the Deputy Commissioner in their submissions on the appeal as without merit. The most common objection was to evidence of any event occurring prior to 26 May 1993 on the basis that it was "of no probative value as the creditor only relies on events and conduct occurring between 26 May 1993 and 26 November 1993 in the Amended Creditor's Petition." The relevance of such evidence was explained in Re Vassis; Ex parte Leung [1986] FCA 21; (1986) 9 FCR 518, where a similar issue arose, by Burchett J who said (at 521):

"The first question of substance is whether I

should be satisfied that Mr Vassis did remain

out of Australia, during the period alleged in

the petition, with intent to defeat or delay his

creditors. His original departure on 8 January

1983 is outside the period of six months covered

by the petition, but if I should find that he

left Australia with the relevant intent this

would, in my opinion, provide a strong basis for

an inference that he remained away with the same

intent. Of course. the drawing of such an

inference in fact would be by no means

automatic."

95. Contrary to the submission of counsel for Mr Thai, the omission of the trial Judge to receive and rule on these objections could have made no difference to the admission of such evidence. The basis for the objections was misconceived. They were bound to fail.

96. On the first day of hearing this appeal counsel for Mr Thai also complained that his client had not had the opportunity of testing any of the Deputy Commissioner's evidence. He was apparently unable on that occasion to indicate whether any of those deponents had been required for cross-examination. On the second day of the appeal counsel handed up a copy of a facsimile message from Mr Munro to the Australian Government Solicitor. That message was transmitted at 5.24 pm on 15 June 1994, that is after normal business hours on the day before the trial, purporting to "require each of the creditor's witnesses for cross-examination tomorrow." Rule 135(1) of the Bankruptcy Rules provides that a notice for cross-examination is to be served "a reasonable time before the dated fixed for the hearing." Counsel for Mr Thai submitted of that message "that the fact it was made at 5.30 the day before the trial is of no consequence." This submission must be rejected. In the circumstances, the Deputy Commissioner was under no obligation to produce the deponents for cross-examination.

97. The next matter is the opportunity to address. There can be no doubt that, having heard evidence and submissions solely on the two procedural applications, Einfeld J went on to deal in his judgment with the remaining substantive issue of Mr Thai's intent. Counsel for Mr Thai now complains that he was given no opportunity to address on the merits of the evidence adduced by his client. Although counsel for Mr Thai is loathe to concede that his Honour also read the affidavits referred to in Mr Munro's affidavit, it may be assumed that that evidence was also admitted. In these circumstances, neither counsel was invited to address on the merits of the substantive issue.

98. The consequences of this departure from normal trial procedure must have become obvious to counsel for Mr Thai when, after his Honour delivered judgment, counsel for the Deputy Commissioner applied for the making of a sequestration order. However, counsel for Mr Thai made no submission at all.

99. It cannot be doubted that the closing address is an important ingredient of a fair trial. This was emphasized in Stead v. State Government Insurance Commission [1986] HCA 54; (1986) 161 CLR 141, where the trial judge had stopped counsel addressing on evidence touching a critical question which was ultimately determined adversely to that counsel's client. It is true that in that case the issue was whether the evidence of a particular witness should be accepted, but the entitlement of a party to make submissions on an issue of fact was stressed. The High Court made it clear (at 147) that, where there has been a denial of such an opportunity, a new trial must be ordered unless a properly conducted trial could not possibly have produced a different result.

100. The question is then whether there was a denial of an opportunity to address in the present case. Even a truculent and intemperate barrister, who told a trial judge at the close of evidence "your Honour can do what you like", was held by a majority of the Court of Appeal in New South Wales not to have waived his right to address: Escobar v. Spindaleri (1986) 7 NSWLR 51. In his dissent, Samuels JA referred (at 62) to the waiver of a right to address the court and formulated a useful test:

"Counsel can always, I would have thought,

waive, in the sense of not wishing to exercise,

some procedural or other forensic right. If he

does so voluntarily, and with knowledge of the

options open to him, it follows that he has not

been deprived of any right or privilege and

hence has not been denied justice.

In the case before us the question to my mind is

whether counsel for the worker was deprived by

the judge of his right to address, or whether he

simply chose not to pursue it. The worker, of

course, contends for the first of these

possibilities, and submits that the deprivation

is made out because, as is clear, the learned

judge did not explicitly offer counsel the

opportunity to address. I do not consider that

Mayes is persuasive authority for the general

proposition that a denial of natural justice is

established because Manser J did not in terms

formulate the offer to hear counsel in address.

Each case of this kind must turn on its own

facts. ... the test framed in 1 Halsbury, 4th

ed, par 76 at 94, is whether the party has been

"precluded" from addressing. There may be

occasions when preclusion may be inferred from

failure to articulate the right, but the present

case is not, in my opinion, one of them.

Hence the question is whether counsel here was

precluded in a more positive way by being denied

the chance to address; or, again, whether,

knowing he had the right and the opportunity to

pursue it, he chose not to do so."

101. It is common ground that Einfeld J was in the process of leaving the bench when counsel for the Deputy Commissioner applied for the sequestration order. Moreover it is accepted by both parties that these events occurred quickly at a time when it appeared that his Honour was adjourning. If a conscious decision be made not to bring a procedural irregularity to the attention of the Court during the trial, but to reserve such matter to be raised on appeal, it may follow that there has been a waiver. However, having regard to the way in which events here unfolded quickly before the trial Judge, it has not been shown, in our view, that counsel for Mr Thai consciously chose not to pursue his right to address.

102. The case against Mr Thai appeared to be very strong. He departed Australia within days of being served with the Deputy Commissioner's assessment notices and statement of claim. He did not tell his solicitor, who only found out about his departure upon attending an aborted meeting with his accountant. The departure card indicated an intention to be away for five days. Yet almost 21 months later he had still not returned. There was a substantial deficiency when his assets were compared to liabilities. He and his solicitor had made statements about his intention of returning to Australia. This was a case that required an explanation and evidence of exculpatory circumstances from Mr Thai himself. None was forthcoming. The evidence adduced by Mr Thai's counsel lacked substance. Yet, perhaps on account of the very paucity and weakness of that evidence, it is more difficult to be comfortable in a conclusion that the exercise of an entitlement to make submissions on the whole of the evidence could have made no difference. For example, there was the indication in Mr Munro's letter of 7 September 1992 to the Australian Government Solicitor that Mr Thai intended to go overseas, and certain of the statements by Mr Thai or his solicitor were at least arguably equivocal about his intentions in not returning to Australia. In the circumstances it cannot be said that on the evidence it was inevitable that a sequestration order would have been made in any event.

(e) Orders proposed

103. It follows, in our view, that leave to appeal from the orders made by Davies J should be granted, but the appeal dismissed. However, the appeal from Einfeld J should be allowed and the orders made by Einfeld J set aside.

104. Counsel for Mr Thai submits that, if the appeal is allowed, it would be futile to order a retrial of the petition, as amended. This is said to be because, when the amended petition was filed on 28 April 1994, it was not accompanied by an affidavit verifying as required by s. 47(1)(b) of the Act. However, that requirement relates only to the creditor's petition when it is presented, which only occurs once. Rules 12, 13, 15 and 16 of the Bankruptcy Rules proceed on this correct construction of the Act. An amendment allowed under s. 33 of the Act is not subject to verification, which is the procedure laid down for the purposes of instituting a proceeding on a creditor's petition.

Conclusion

105. In the result, the appeal against the judgment of Einfeld J should be allowed and the sequestration order and consequent costs order set aside. The petition, as amended, should be heard again; but not with respect to the issues determined by Davies J on 9 May 1994.

106. As to costs, since the appeal against the decision of Davies J has failed, the orders for costs made on 9 May 1994 should not be disturbed. Each party has been partly successful before us. The Deputy Commissioner has succeeded on the appeal from the judgment of Davies J, and Mr Thai has succeeded on certain of the issues raised in the appeal from Einfeld J. In the result, there should be no order as to the costs of the two appeals. Nor should there be any order as to the costs of the proceeding before Einfeld J.
