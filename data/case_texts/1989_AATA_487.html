REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 14 July 1989

Place: Brisbane

The question at issue in the proceedings is very simply stated as being whether certain arrangements entered into by the applicant resulted in the applicant being liable under the Sales Tax Assessment Acts (Nos. 2,3,6 and 7) ("the Assessment Acts") for payment of Sales Tax levied by the appropriate Sales Tax Acts, The task confronting the Tribunal has been lightened by the parties agreeing to 38 sample transactions as being representative of the thousands of transactions in dispute before the Tribunal.

2. Apparently the applicant is a company registered for sales tax purposes as a wholesale merchant although these vital facts were not established before the Tribunal. If it were not so registered then it clearly was required to be and I infer from Documents T2 to T52 inclusive that the applicant was both incorporated and that it was registered as a wholesale merchant within the terms of section 11 of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) ("Assessment Act No 1").

  1. The applicant company was represented before the Tribunal by its public officer while the respondent appeared through Mr D M Bennett QC with Mr Logan and Mr Hack of counsel.
  2. At the outset of proceedings Mr Bennett sought to have an officer of the Director of Public Prosecutions present in the hearing room. The applicant's representative did not consent to the request and the Tribunal ruled that as the applicant had not consented to the public in general (or the officer in particular) being in the hearing room section 14ZF of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 operated to close the hearing and therefore excluded the officer from the hearing room.
  3. Section 3 of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 2) ("Assessment Act No 2") provides that sales tax imposed by Sales Tax Act (No 2) shall be levied and paid upon the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia and sold by a taxpayer who purchased the goods from the manufacturer. Section 5 of the same Act makes it clear that the sales tax shall be paid by the vendor, Sub-section 3(1) of Act No 1 provides that "Taxpayer" means a person chargeable with sales tax under the Act. The definition applies mutatis mutandis to Assessment Act No 2 (s.s.12(1) of Act No 2).

6. For the purposes of Act No 2 the sale value of goods which are sold shall be the amount for which those goods are sold by a registered person who purchased the goods from the manufacturer to a purchaser who is an unregistered person or a registered person who has not quoted that person's certificate of registration (ss4(1).

  1. "Goods" is defined by subsection 3(1) of Assessment Act No 1 to include commodities but does not include goods which have gone into use or consumption in Australia nor does it include goods sold as second-hand goods and coming within paragraph (b) of the definition. Goods covered by these exclusions are defined to be "excluded goods" by sub-section 4A(9) of Act.No 2.
  2. Comparable provisions are to be found in the Sales Tax Assessment Acts (Nos. 3, 6, and 7) ("Assessment Act No 3", "Assessment Act No 6" and "Assessment Act No 7").
  3. The tax payable in respect of the liability arising under the various assessment acts is imposed by the relevant rates act

10. Every person who during any month makes any of the sales specified in subsections 4(1), 4(2), 4(3) or 4A(1) shall within 21 days after the close of the month furnish a return to the Commissioner and every person liable under section 5 for the payment of tax upon the sale value of any goods to be sold shall within the same period of 21 days pay sales tax upon that value (sections 7 and 9 of Assessment Act 2 - similar provisions appear in the other Assessment Acts).

  1. Where the Commissioner finds in any case that tax or further tax is payable by any person the Commissioner may assess the sale value upon which tax should be or should have been paid and calculate the tax or further tax which is payable (ss 10(1) of Act 2 - similar provisions appear in the other Acts).
  2. Default provisions also apply. Subsection 10(2A) in each of the Acts provides that where:
    • (a) ...
      • (b) the Commissioner is not satisfied with the return made by any person? or
      • (c) the Commissioner has reason to believe, or suspect that any person is liable to pay sales tax

the Commissioner may cause an assessment to be made of the amount upon which, in his judgment, sales tax ought to be levied, and the person shall be liable to sales tax thereon, excepting so far as he establishes an objection that the assessment is excessive. Any person who becomes liable to pay sales tax by virtue of an assessment made under subsection (2A) shall also be liable to pay, by way of additional tax, double the amount of that sales tax. The Commissioner may, for reasons which he thinks sufficient, remit the additional tax, or any part thereof (ss10(2B)).

  1. The Commissioner is required to give notice in writing of an assessment and of the tax or further tax to the person liable to pay the tax or further tax. However, the failure to give such notice shall not invalidate the assessment and calculation made by the Commissioner (ss100) and 10(5)).
  2. A taxpayer who considers that any amount upon which he is required to pay sales tax in respect of any goods is not the sale value of those goods as declared by the Act or who is dissatisfied with any assessment or decision made by the Commissioner by which the sale value of goods is ascertained may lodge an objection in writing with the Commissioner against the amount or value (ss41(1) of Assessment Act No 1 and s.12 of the relevant Assessment Acts).
  3. A taxpayer who is dissatisfied with the decision of the Commissioner may within 30 days after the service by post of notice of that decision request the Commissioner to refer his decision to a Board of Review for review (ss41(4) and s12 of the relevant Assessment Acts). Section 223 of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jursidiction) Act 1986 provides that where the respondent Commissioner had referred a decision or request to a Board of Review before 1 July 1986 (in these applications the reference was made on 2 May 1986) but no decision had been given by the Board before 1 July 1986 the reference shall, with effect from that day, be treated as if the decision of the Commissioner to which the reference relates had been referred to this Tribunal under the relevant tax law as in force after that day and the proceedings continue before the Tribunal. While that provision was of little consequence in the hearing of income tax appeals significant changes occurred in relation to appeals under the sales tax law.
    1. By section 42E of Assessment Act No 1 the taxpayer is, unless the Tribunal otherwise orders, limited to the grounds stated in the objection and the burden of proving that an assessment is excessive or a reviewable decision is incorrect lies upon the taxpayer.
    2. However, although section 39 of Assessment Act No 1 was repealed by the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act (s.137) the operation of the section was continued by section 227 of that Act so as to apply in respect of notices of assessment served before 1 July 1986, as is the present case.
    3. Sub-section 39(1) of Assessment Act No 1 provides, inter alia, that the production of a notice under the hand of the Commissioner specifying liability of a taxpayer under the Act shall be conclusive evidence of any act required to be done or performed by the Commissioner for the purpose of ascertaining the liability so specified and shall be prima facie evidence of the correctness of any calculations upon which that liability is ascertained in proceedings on appeal. The provision applies in respect of Assessment Acts Nos. 2, 3, 6 and 7 (s.12 of those Acts). The section makes the notice of assessment conclusive as to formalities but not as to liability including sale value (D.F.C of T v. Hankin [1959] HCA 2; (1959) 100 CLR 566 @ 577).
  4. The exception contained in the section means that the notices of assessment can only be prima facie evidence that the assessments have been correctly calculated.
  5. The somewhat strange result of sections 39 and 42E when considered together is that in these proceedings the notices of assessment are prima facie evidence only of the correctness of any calculations upon which the liability is ascertained and the taxpayer has the burden of proving that the assessments are excessive. These provisions taken together must mean that the taxpayer discharges the onus if it is able to show on the balance of probabilities that the tax assessed has not been correctly calculated, that the sale values adopted in the assessment are excessive or that the rates of tax applied to the sale values are excessive. The applicant will also succeed if it establishes that goods treated in the assessments as having a sale value are found not to have a sale value because the applicant, as vendor, holds a valid quotation of certificate made in accordance with Sales Tax Regulation 12.

21. No issue arose before the Tribunal as to additional tax assessed (at 100% of the tax assessed) because the applicant had no right to object against assessment of additional tax at the relevant time.

  1. Schedules attached to the notices of assessment set out in considerable detail the description and sale value of the goods alleged to have been sold and to be chargeable to tax. Those schedules reveal that the goods were in the main, boats, trailers, motors and accessories for boats.
  2. The applicant company lodged valid objections against each of the assessments. In essence the objections claim that the goods in question had "nil" sale value, the purchaser from the applicant having quoted its certificate of registration when purchasing the goods. The grounds of objection in each of the notices were given in detail.
  3. By notice dated 28 February 1986 the respondent advised the applicant that the objections had been disallowed and by letter dated 17 March 1986 the applicant by its agent requested that the decisions be referred to a Board of Review and enclosed $2. Neither party seemed to recognise that there were seven objection decisions to be reviewed. The request for review was referred to a Board of Review on 2 May 1986. No issue arose at the hearing before the Tribunal as to whether one application was sufficient to refer the seven objection decisions. No doubt the one application was thought to be sufficient response to the single notice from the Commissioner notifying disallowance of the objections. On the face of it six of the seven applications are not validly before the Tribunal. Paragraph 223(1)(a) of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act has the effect of deeming any reference to a Board of Review by the Commissioner as a reference to the Tribunal under the relevant tax law. Whether the apparent lack of jurisdiction was cured by the transitional legislation is doubtful. However, as the parties do not dispute the Tribunal's jurisdiction to deal with the decision on each objection I propose to deal with each objection decision on the basis scintilla juris.

The Evidence Before the Tribunal

28. The applicant called a public accountant, W, to give evidence but then declined to examine in chief. Cross-examination of W revealed that he had been the accountant for the applicant for a number of years, that he knew persons by the name of Costello and Hopkins, that he was aware of arrangements made between the applicant and a company called Babaki Pty Ltd ("Babaki"), that the applicant was the wholesale arm of a retail business conducted by a partnership of which the public officer was one of the partners and that the partnership, Babaki and the applicant had entered into a series of agreements.

  1. Exhibit 1 ("Acquisition Agreement") is a copy of an agreement (undated except for 1978) between the applicant company and Babaki. The deed evidences that an equitable charge was to be granted by the applicant over goods to secure certain advances to be made pursuant to another agreement described as the "Loan Agreement". It was acknowledged that goods sold by the applicant to Babaki would be subject to an equitable charge created in accordance with the terms of the Loan Agreement and Babaki was to accept title to the goods subject to the equitable charge. The charge was to be reflected in the purchase price of the goods. Babaki was to have no right of indemnity or contribution in respect of the equitable charge or the loan secured by the charge and no right of recourse against the applicant by reason of any exercise of any power of sale or other remedy arising under the charge. Babaki was not to have any obligation to repayment of amounts secured by the charge nor was it liable to indemnify the applicant in respect of the obligation to repay the amounts secured.
  2. Exhibit 3 ("Loan Agreement") is a copy of a Loan Agreement (undated except for 1978) between the applicant and the partnership. The recital indicates that the applicant had sought certain advances from the partnership to enable the applicant to purchase goods, such advances to be secured by an equitable charge over each of the goods acquired. The terms of the agreement provided for the applicant to apply for each advance by delivering to the partnership a list specifying the goods purchased or to be purchased and the purchase price for those goods (the wholesale price). It was specifically agreed in clause 2 that delivery of an invoice addressed to the applicant from the supplier of the goods to the applicant was to be deemed to be delivery of a list.
  3. The partnership retained the right to reject or accept any application for an advance but where an application for an advance was accepted the partnership was to advance forthwith an amount calculated by multiplying the total wholesale price by the prescribed percentage specified of 95%. The agreement charged the goods in respect of which an advance had been made at the prescribed percentage of the wholesale price and the charge could be released upon payment of that amount to the partnership. By Clause 7 of the deed the applicant remained liable for repayment of the advance even though charged goods were sold or otherwise disposed of. Repayment of the advances was to be made within 60 days together with interest at 12% p.a. The essence of the arrangement is found in clause 11 which provided that in the event that the partnerhsip acquired ownership of charged goods the charge created by the deed was to be extinguished and the advance deemed to have been repaid to the extent of the sum secured on those goods.
  4. Exhibit 2 ("Sales Agreement") is a copy of an undated but executed agreement between the partnership and Babaki whereby the parties agreed that Babaki would sell goods to the partnership subject to an equitable charge, the partnership would accept the goods subject to the charge and the sale price of the goods would be calculated having regard to the existence of the equitable charge. The partnership was to have no right of indemnity or contribution against Babaki in respect of the charge nor was there a right of recourse against Babaki.
  5. Further, the partnership was not to have any obligation to make any repayment of amounts secured by the equitable charge and Babaki had no right of indemnity from the partnership in respect of its obligation to repay amounts secured by the charge. Because of the provisions of Exhibit 3 referred to above whereby the charge and the debt were extinguished when the charged goods were sold to the partnership it is unlikely that the terms of Exhibit 2 would ever be enforced.
  6. By deed dated 24 November 1980 (Exhibit 4) and also described as "Acquisition Agreement" the applicant by its power of attorney, John Joseph Costello, entered into a new agreement with Ronald James Hopkins the terms of which were the same as the previous agreement made with Babaki. (Exhibit 1).
  7. Also, by a further deed dated 24 November 1980 (Exhibit 5) the partnership entered into an agreement described as "Purchase Agreement" with Hopkins. In effect this agreement was the same as the "Sales Agreement" (Exhibit 2). Exhibits 4 and 5 merely substituted Hopkins for Babaki in the arrangements. Neither Exhibit 2 nor Exhibit 5 is an agreement for the sale of goods.
  8. In the course of its business as a wholesaler of boats, outboard motors and other marine equipment it apparently became necessary for the applicant to enter into certain agreements with suppliers of boats motors and finance. Exhibit 6 is a copy of an agreement entitled "Outboard Motors Dealer Agreement". That agreement was executed by the applicant and a company which supplies marine outboard motors. In terms of the agreement the applicant agreed to maintain retail premises for the display sale and servicing of the products that the purchase price was to be paid for products on delivery by acceptance of a sight draft presented through the respective bankers. In the case of spare parts the payment was due on 30 days terms. Other terms of the agreement are not relevant to these proceedings.
  9. A somewhat similar agreement was entered into with a supplier of boats, trailers and accessories on 1 November 1978 (Exhibit 7). In the terms of the agreement title to products was deemed to be vested in the applicant at time of delivery by the supplier.
  10. Agreements of a different nature were entered into by the applicant with Borg Warner Acceptance Corporation (Australia) Ltd (on an unreadable date in 1978 and again on 12 March 1980) (Exhibit 8) for the purpose of financing goods held by the applicant on bailment from Borg Warner for the purpose of selling the goods by retail.

(Wholesale) Ltd and Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd entitled "Used Goods Wholesale Plan" (agreement dated an unreadable date in 1976) and "AGC Bailment Plan" (agreement dated 19 May 197B). 48. Exhibit 11(8) is a number of copy documents relating to transactions in respect of an outboard motor purchased by the applicant from the supplier for $388 under quotation of the applicant's sales tax certificate. 86. The mechanism which ensures that the tax is only applied to the last wholesale sale is the scheme established by the legislation requiring registered persons to quote their sales tax certificate when purchasing goods which are to be onsold by wholesale. The operation of this scheme was considered by the High Court in Davies Coop and Company Ltd v F.C.T. [1948] HCA 50; (1948) 77 CLR 299. The context in which the question before the High Court arose was quite different to the present case but the dicta of the Court is equally applicable to the facts of this application. At page 309-310 Latham C.J. described the scheme of quotation of certificates as follows:

"Tax is imposed in relation to the sales value of goods. Sales value in, for example, Act No. 2 (see s.4) is the amount specified in the section where goods are sold by certain persons 'to an unregistered person or to a registered person who has not quoted his certificate in respect of that purchase.' The other relevant Acts contain a corresponding provision. If goods are sold to a person who is in fact unregistered, it would, in my opinion, be immaterial for the purpose of applying this provision that the person ought to have been registered. So if goods are sold to a person who is registered in fact and who has not quoted his certificate (though perhaps he ought to have quoted it), the conditions specified in the section are equally satisfied and tax is payable. Registration or non-registration is a matter of fact, and quotation or non-quotation is, in my opnion, equally a matter of fact, whether or not a registration in one case or a quotation in the other has rightly been made.

In order to determine whether such a provision as that last cited refers to actual quotations by persons actually registered, or only to quotations lawfully made under the Acts and regulations, it is relevant to consider the provisions of the appropriate Act as a whole. The words 'who has quoted his certificate' in themselves are sufficiently wide to cover any actual quotation of a certificate, whether the quotation be right or wrong. There is no reason to be found in any of the Acts taken as a whole for limiting them to cases of lawful quotation. If such a limitation were introduced it would be very difficult to apply the Acts and to carry on business under the Acts. If quotation of certificates is accepted as meaning quotation in fact, then a person who disposes of goods to a registered person who quotes his certificate knows that no tax is payable by him and he need not provide for it in his price. If, however, a vendor is unable to act upon the fact of quotation and in order to avoid tax must be in a position to establish, not only the fact of quotation, but also that the quotation was rightly made, then, as the purchaser from him would be under no obligation to answer any inquiries, the vendor could never be certain whether or not he would be liable to tax. Further, if the quotation of certificates referred to in the Act means only lawful quotation of certificates, then in a case where it was held that there had been a quotation which was not lawful and, accordingly, a seller of the goods was sued for sales tax, he would have to pay the tax, and another tax might be payable at a later stage in the disposition of the goods. The result would be a double tax. The act in no case is intended to bring about this result: see Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (S.A.) v Ellis & Clark Ltd. (1934) 52 CLR, at p.92. Thus I am of opinion that actual quotation and not necessarily lawful quotation is the quotation referred to in s.4 of the Act last mentioned and in the corresponding provisions of the other Sales Tax Assessment Acts. I am therefore of opinion that in the case of all the goods sold by the defendant to registered persons who in fact quoted their certificates, no sales value of the goods was prescribed by any of the Acts, and that therefore no sales tax is payable in respect of them."

Dixon J came to the same conclusion in these words at page 315:

"When s.4(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Acts (No. 2) and (No. 6) speaks of a registered person who has not quoted his certificate in respect of the purchase of the goods, the reference is to quotation in fact. The purpose and policy of the legislation is inconsistent with any application of the theory that an unlawful or improper quotation must be treated as no quotation of the certificate."

Those comments apply equally to Assessment Acts (3) and (7). However, Williams J was not prepared to go quite as far as his brother judges when he said at page 320 "that the vendor, if bona fide, may rely upon the actual quotation of a certificate by the purchaser as a proper quotation whether the quotation is one authorised by the legislation or not". The court therefore rejected the decision of Starke J at first instance to the effect that a quotation of certificate must be lawfully made to be effective. In that case the bona fides of the vendor were not questioned so in a sense it was not necessary for the Court to address the question of a quotation of certificate made bona fides. Nor is it necessary for this Tribunal to address that question because there is no evidence to suggest that the applicant acted other than bona fide in accepting the quotations of certificate by Babaki and Ron Hopkins Wholesale. It should be noted that sub-section 3(3) of Assessment Act (No 1) was enacted after the relevant time. It follows therefore that the applicant must succeed if the purchase orders on which the quotations of certificate were made relate to sales of the goods by the applicant.

  1. Although the basis of assessment adopted by the respondent is not in evidence before the Tribunal the Tribunal is entitled to find on the evidence that the respondent relies on the basis that the applicant sold the goods in each case to the partnership at a price equal to the wholesale price paid by the partnership to the arms length supplier on behalf of the applicant. That is the sale value adopted by the respondent's assessments.
  2. To succeed the applicant must establish that the quotations of certificate held by it were in respect of goods sold to Babaki and Ron Hopkins Wholesale. However, the findings of fact show that in most cases the applicant no longer had possession of the goods when the documentation purporting to evidence of such sales was prepared. Further, and more importantly, the applicant did not have property in the goods in all cases where the goods were on an arms length bailment plan and in most other cases.
  3. If the alleged sales rely entirely for their existence on the documentation and that documentation could have no relevant effect in respect of a sale of the goods the purchase orders would not relate to any sale of goods for the purposes of the sales tax legislation. If the purchase orders and invoices were not intended to change the commercial transactions which had previously taken place they merely evidenced a sham. They would he a paper facade erected for the purpose of seeking to change the commercial transactions which had taken place insofar as those transactions were relevant to the sales tax legislation. No doubt the transactions evidenced by the documents would be effective if the business of the applicant had been conducted according to the documents.
  4. However, the transactions alleged to be evidenced by the documents must be ineffective because they were not brought to the attention of third parties who actually owned the goods in question at the relevant time. Those third parties fall into two groups. The first is the arms length finance companies responsible for financing the bailment plans entered into by the applicant and the partnership. There is no evidence to suggest that these companies were made aware of the sale of goods which they owned until the sale to the retail customer was effected and the goods taken off the bailment plan.
  5. The second category is the retail customers who had entered into contracts for sale of specific goods prior to the transactions with Babaki and Ron Hopkins Wholesale being entered into in relation to the same goods. Those retail customers, it may be inferred, were not told that they would not obtain title to the goods until some time into the future. The partnership must have represented title to the goods when it sold goods to its retail customers. It was not suggested otherwise before the Tribunal. Certainly, in most transactions evidenced before the Tribunal the retail customer had ownership and sole possession of the goods when the transactions with Babaki and Ron Hopkins Wholesale were entered into by the applicant.
  6. Were the alleged sales by the applicant to Babaki and Ron Hopkins sham transactions? The oft cited definition of "sham" is that of Diplock L.J. in Snook v. London and West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786 at 802 where his Lordship stated:

"As regards the contention of the plaintiff that the transactions between himself, Auto Finance and the defendants were a ‘sham', it is, I think, necessary to consider what, if any, legal concept is involved in the use of this popular and pejorative word. I apprehend that, if it has any meaning in law, it means acts done or documents executed by the parties to the 'sham' which are intended by them to give to third parties or to the court the appearance of creating between the parties legal rights and obligations different from the actual legal rights and obligations (if any) which the parties intend to create. But one thing, I think, is clear in legal principle, morality and the authorites (see Yorkshire Railway Macron Co. v. Mac lure (1882) 21 Ch.D. 309, C.A. and Stoneleicrh Finance Ltd. v. Phillips (1965) 2 QB 537), that for acts or documents to be a 'sham' , with whatever legal consequences follow from this, all the parties thereto must have a common intention that the acts or documents are not to create the legal rights and obligations which. they give the appearance of creating. No unexpressed intentions of a 'shammer' affect the rights of a party whom he deceived. There is an express finding in this case that the defendants were not parties to the alleged 'sham'."

  1. The evidence in this case ignores the intentions of the parties when entering into the transactions in question hut there is nothing to suggest that the applicant, Babaki and Ron Hopkins Wholesale had any intention other than that the documents in question would be effective to sell the goods in question in accordance with the documents. That they also intended the transactions to be effective to reduce the amount of sales tax payable in respect of the goods does not thereby suggest that the documents were shams within the definition cited. In particular there is no basis for finding that Babaki and Ron Hopkins Wholesale had any intention of creating legal rights other than the legal rights and obligations which the documents appeared to create. The evidence does not support a finding of sham. The respondent's submission that the documents in question were shams is rejected.
  2. As already intimated the transactions in question were not effective to reduce the amount of sales tax payable because the documents, although intended to be effective, failed insofar as they purported to effect relevant sales of goods within the terms of the sales tax law. I have reached that conclusion on two separate bases.

95. The first is that the documents did not evidence actual sales of goods. Liability for sales tax is imposed by section 3 of the respective Assessment Acts on the sale value of goods sold. In general terms the sale value is the amount for which goods are sold (it is not necessary to explore the other methods of arriving at sale value for the purposes of these reasons). Therefore, the essential element is that there must be a relevant sale of goods - an agreement to sell is not sufficient to make the vendor liable for sales tax because there is no transfer of the property in the goods to the purchaser until sometime in the future and therefore no sale of goods until that time.

  1. The second is that on the facts as found the relevant sales took place between the applicant and the partnership when the property in the goods passed to the partnership immediately prior to the partnership effecting the retail sale to an arms length customer. . That the applicant held or was subsequently sent a quotation of certificate for the purchase of the goods by another entity is irrelevant. This is because such a quotation of certificate did not apply to and was not relevant to an actual sale of goods effected by the applicant.
  2. Because the applicant had disposed of its proprietory rights in the goods to the partnership it was unable to effect a sale of goods to Babaki or Ron Hopkins Wholesale as purchaser because it did not and could not transfer the property in the goods to that purchaser. To say that these paper transactions were effective for sales tax purposes, given that they did not effect a sale of goods, would be to say that the sales tax is a tax on worthless pieces of paper. That would be to say that the sales tax was in the nature of a stamp duty rather than a tax on transactions concerning goods.
  3. Counsel for the respondent also submitted that the Tribunal should apply the so-called "doctrine of fiscal nullity" to annul the chain of transactions between the applicant, Babaki, Ron Hopkins Wholesale and the partnership. It was submitted that although the applicability of the doctrine income tax cases has been rejected in Australia (Oakey Abbatoir Pty Ltd v. F.C.T. 84 ATC 4718, F.C.T. v. John (1989) 1 ATR 1) it is still open to this Tribunal to hold that the transactions in question, having been entered into for the purpose of the applicant avoiding payment of sales tax which would otherwise have been payable, are void for the purposes of the sales tax legislation. However, the High Court has been reluctant to accept the operation of the doctrine in other tax legislation (Comptroller of Stamps (Vic) v. Ashwick (Vic) No. 4 Pty Ltd, 87 ATC 5064).
  4. A summary of the doctrine is to be found in the judgment of Mason CJ, Wilson, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ in John at pages 10-11 as follows:

"The principle which has come to be known as 'the principle of fiscal nullity' derives from the decisions of the House of Lords in W T Ramsay Ltd v. Inland Revenue Comrs [1982] AC300; Inland Revenue Comrs v. Burman Oil Co Ltd (1981) 54 TC 200; [1982] STC 30 and Furniss v. Dawson [1983] UKHL 4; [1984] AC 474. The principle was stated in Furniss by Lord Brightman at 527: ' First, there must be a pre-ordained series of transactions; or, if one likes, one single composite transaction. This composite transaction may or may not include the achievement of a legitimate commercial (ie business) end ... Secondly, there must be steps inserted which have no commercial (business) purpose apart from the avoidance of a liability to tax - not "no business effect". If those two ingredients exist, the inserted steps are to be disregarded for fiscal purposes. The court must then look at the end result. Precisely how the end result will be taxed will depend on the terms of the taxing statute sought to be applied.'"

100. In Brayson Motors Pty Ltd v. F.C.T. 85 ATC 4125 the High Court dealt with the operation of the sales tax legislation in the context of arrangements not unlike those in dispute in this case. The issue before the Court was, however, quite different because the Commissioner was seeking to assess the taxpayer in respect of its sales of goods by retail. In the course of its judgment the Court expressed the view that the sales tax legislation was such as to evince an intention by the Parliament that sales tax was payable on the wholesale value of the goods at the last wholesale sale. The decision is of little assistance in the present case except to reaffirm that the applicant is liable as assessed, if it did in fact make the last wholesale sale to the partnership.

101. Although invited to do so I am unable to conclude on the authorities that the doctrine of fiscal nullity as enunciated by Lord Brightman would have any operation in the circumstances of this case. This is because the facts established before the Tribunal show that the respondent's assessments are correctly based on the sales to the partnership - such sales being in the ordinary course of the business of the applicant and there is no need to void any transaction to reach this conclusion. The paper transactions do not evidence sale of goods and are therefore irrelevant for present purposes. There were no steps inserted in "the sales from the applicant to the partnership which lacked commercial purpose. The fact that the arrangements between the applicant and the partnership had the effect of deferring the time for payment of sales tax to a time after the retail sale had been made is not sufficient, in my view, to give the doctrine any operation. The respondent did not make any submission on the point and his basis of assessment appears to have accepted the arrangements between the applicant and the partnership. I am therefore not required to decide whether the doctrine of fiscal nullity has any operation in the context of the sales tax legislation; that legislation lacking a provision similar in effect to section 260 (or Part IVA) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

102. The objection decisions under review will be affirmed.
