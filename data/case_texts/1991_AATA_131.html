Re Taxation Appeals [1991] AATA 131; 22 ATR 3218 (22 May 1991)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT90/273

AAT No. 6954

Income Tax

[1991] AATA 131; 22 ATR 3218

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Allowable deduction - Taxpayer engaged in tanning wool hides - "facilities charge" incurred in installing equipment for supply of gas used for fuel - Whether allowable deduction - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1981 sec 51(1).

Case M35 80 ATC 260

Case M53 80 ATC 357

Case N84 81 ATC 451

Atherton v British Insulated and Helsby Cables (1925) 10 TC 155

Sun Newspapers Ltd v F.C. of T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337

John Fairfax and Sons Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30

Boys v Chaplin (1969) 2 All ER 1085

Hallstroms Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634

F.C. of T. v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd 79 ATC 4279

W Nevill and Co Ltd v F.C. of T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290

Metals Exploration Ltd v F.C. of T. 86 ATC 4505

HEARING

MELBOURNE

22:5:1991

ORDER

The Tribunal varies the objection decision by reducing the applicant's taxable income by $2,693.00.

DECISION

In brief outline, the applicant was subjected to an audit, after which an amended assessment was issued in respect of the year of income ended 1 October 1985. The adjustment sheet accompanying the amended assessment stated that the applicant's taxable income for the subject year of income was "adjusted to disallow claim for gas facility charge as a result of audit". The amount disallowed was $8,162.00.

2. The substantive issue in dispute is whether a "gas facility charge" is, in the circumstances of this case, an affair of capital, and thus excluded from deductibility under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1985 ("the Act") as claimed in the return.

3. The applicant is a joint venture company established in 1981. Its activities include the tanning of wool skins, for which large quantities of natural gas are required to fuel its boilers and dryers. To enable gas to be supplied for this purpose, the Gas and Fuel Corporation of Victoria ("the Corporation") installed a main, meter and regulators to the applicant's premises in 1981. The applicant commenced operation in 1982 under a particular commercial and industrial tariff from the Corporation identified as "Natural Gas Tariff 14/22".

4. A letter from the Corporation, dated 5 April 1983, sent to its gas consumers - including the applicant - makes it clear that, as at 1 October 1984, the components of Natural Gas Tariff 14/22 (per meter per month) were as follows:

Supply Charge: $ 42.50

Commodity Charge:

First 35 000 MJ @ 0.646c/MJ

Next 265 000 MJ @ 0.493c/MJ

Over 300 000 MJ @ 0.274c/MJ

Minimum Bill: $42.50

It is also clear both from the evidence of Mr Telkis, the principal tariffs analyst of the Corporation, called by the respondent, as well as from a letter dated 1 March 1991 from the Corporation in answer to the respondent's requisitions contained in a letter dated 18 February 1991, that:

(a) the monthly "supply charge" contained in Tariff 14/22

was designed to recoup to the Corporation the average cost of

equipment and installation necessary to supply gas under that

tariff to consumers over and above the capital cost to the

Corporation of having the necessary infrastructure in place to

be able to offer supply under that tariff to consumers;

(b) that equipment included the mains, meter and regulators; and

(c) the "supply charge" was not payable if a special tariff

(referred to as "Tariff 95") was entered into.

5. As a result of discussions between representatives of the Corporation and the applicant, it became apparent from projections that an overall cheaper cost of gas might be achieved by the applicant by going onto "Tariff 95". To adopt that tariff, it was necessary for the applicant to enter into a written agreement ("the agreement") with the Corporation, which it did on 1 October 1984. The only material addition required in the way of extra equipment was the installation of a "maximum demand indicator" on the applicant's premises. However, no increased supply capacity was provided as a result of the agreement.

7. Despite the fact that cl 4(b) of the agreement stated that the "facilities charge" of $10,418.00 was payable on a terms basis by 36 consecutive monthly instalments of $382.00 (amounting to a total of $13,752.00), payable concurrently with the charge for gas, the applicant chose to pay the $10,418.00 "up front". (Another company shared the applicant's premises and gas supply and contributed a proportion of that sum based on its share of gas usage, hence the lesser amount $8,162.00 claimed by the applicant in respect of the "facilities charge").

8. The following breakdown of the amount of $10,418.00 was contained in the Corporation's response to one of the respondent's requisitions of 18 February 1991 ("the requisition answers"):

"Main $1,090 (Depreciated Value)

Meter $1,425 (Depreciated Value)

Regulators $3,309 (Depreciated Value)

M.D.I. $1,156

$6,980

Tax $3,438*

Total $10,418

*The Tax referred to was levied by the State of Victoria

on the revenue of the Corporation pursuant to the Public

Authorities (Contributions) Act 1966."

9. Although cl 4(a) of the agreement couches the "facilities charge" in terms of it being payment for the Corporation's performance of all works necessary to enable gas to be supplied through the Corporation's distribution network (excluding certain specific items), the answer to Question 1 of the respondent's requisitions, as well as the oral evidence of Mr Telkis, clearly establish that, in fact, the $10,418.00 represented the full recovery of the cost of equipment which could be identified as being specifically required to deliver gas to Bowron - which included equipment installed at the time of entering the Gas Supply Agreement and equipment installed within the previous ten years with the later being included at its depreciated value - together with recovery of "public authorities contribution" - a tax levied by the State of Victoria on the revenue of the Corporation pursuant to the Public Authorities (Contributions) Act 1966 (Vic).

10. As evidenced by a letter dated 1 June 1985 from the Corporation to the Secretary of the applicant, the Corporation gave notice of their intention to terminate the agreement on 1 September 1985 in accordance with cl 6 of the second schedule of the agreement.

11. On 11 October 1985, the applicant entered in a new agreement ("the second agreement") with the Corporation (effective from 1 September 1985) on terms virtually similar in effect to those in the first agreement. For the purposes of this application, the only relevant differences between the two agreements (other than increases in various charges relating to commodity usage and demand) was the change of the minimum term of the agreement from one year to three years, and the deletion of a facilities charge in the second agreement.

12. At the expiry of the second agreement on 1 September 1988, a third agreement between the applicant and the Corporation took effect, in substantially similar terms to that of the second agreement (again apart from increases in various charges relating to commodity usage and demand). Again the contract was for three years (thus still on foot), and again did not include a facilities charge. The reason for the lack of a facilities charge in the second and third agreements was explained by the requisition answers by reason of the fact that "the costs of identified equipment required to deliver gas to (the applicant) were fully recovered in Agreement No. 1088 (the first agreement)". The Corporation further advised in those requisition answers that although "facilities charges were abandoned in 1989 in respect of all new contracts entered into .... no facilities or similar charge is included in the consumption (commodity) charge for such consumers."

13. Section 51(1) of the Act provides:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they

are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable

income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or producing such

income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent

to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a

capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in

relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

14. Research by both counsel revealed that there was not a single authority which has dealt with the deductibility of the payment of the kind of fee in issue in this case. However, on reflection it seems to me that the "facilities charge" involved in installing the instant equipment is not unlike the mandatory costs imposed by the authorities when installing a telephone, although that analogy is not without its own hazards since telephone installation costs were, in identical circumstances, viewed by Board of Review No 1 in Case M35 80 ATC 260 as an item of revenue, whereas Board of Review No 3 in Case M53 80 ATC 357 and Board of Review No 2 in Case N84 81 ATC 451 characterised the expenditure as of a capital nature.

15. Not surprisingly, counsel for the respondent placed heavy reliance on Case M53 (supra) in which I reviewed the main authorities said to provide the litmus test for determining what is "capital" and what is "income". In reviewing these authorities, I stated:

"In a thoroughly researched submission, we were taken through

the usual collection of cases, starting with Atherton v British

Insulated and Helsby Cables (1925) 10 TC 155, The Sun Newspaper

case [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 and John Fairfax and Sons Pty Ltd v F.C. of T.

[1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 to name but a few. At best, these cases

merely illustrate the acute difficulties courts experience when

compelled by the Tax Act to characterise an expenditure as

either 'capital', 'capital nature', or 'private or domestic'.

It appears to me that there is no universal solvent, and such

'explanatory' phrases as 'procuring a right of a permanent

character', 'creating a lasting advantage', 'once and for all'

and such variants as 'enduring benefit' establish two things:

(i) that judicial glosses, as a means of separating 'capital'

from 'revenue' are considerably less scientific than chicken

sexing; (ii) there is only one safe conclusion - one must have

regard to the facts of each case, despite Lord Wilberforce's

warning that 'if one lesson emerges from the United States

decisions, it is that case-to-case decisions do not add up to a

system of justice' (Boys v Chaplin (1969) 2 All ER 1085, 1104).

That this area of the law is a jungle of single instances

is aptly demonstrated by the decision of the Privy Council

in the BP Australia case [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386, where at

p 397, their Lordships state:

'The solution to the problem is not to be found by any

rigid test or description. It has to be derived from many

aspects of the whole set of circumstances some of which

may point in one direction, some in the other. One

consideration may point so clearly that it dominates other

and vaguer indications in the contrary direction. It is a

commonsense appreciation of all the guiding features which

must provide the ultimate answer. Although the categories

of capital and income expenditure are distinct and easily

ascertainable in obvious cases that lie far from the

boundary, the line of distinction is often hard to draw in

borderline cases; and conflicting considerations may

produce a situation where the answer turns on questions of

emphasis and degree. The answer 'depends on what the

expenditure is calculated to effect from a practical and

business point of view, rather than upon the juristic

classification of the legal rights, if any, secured,

employed or exhausted in the process' (per Dixon J. in

Hallstroms' Case [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at p 648). As each new

case comes to be argued, felicitous phrases from earlier

judgments are used in argument by one side and the other.

But those phrases are not the deciding factor, nor are

they of unlimited application. They merely crystallize

particular factors which may incline the scale in a

particular case after a balance of all the considerations

has been taken.

One may approach the problem by considering the first of

the matters mentioned by Dixon J above, namely the

character of the advantage sought, and in this both its

lasting qualities and the fact of recurrence may play

their parts. Under this head one might also take account

of the nature of the need or occasion which calls for the

expenditure (Dixon J in Hallstroms' Case).'

Looking at 'the whole set of circumstances' in this

reference, I ask myself: 'what is the nature of the right

acquired by means of the impugned expenditure?' Put this

way, it appears to me that on payment of the connection

fee, this taxpayer brought into existence an advantage for

the enduring benefit of his newly established medical

practice. Seen from this perspective, it fits in neatly

with the various judicial barnacles which have become

encrusted around sec. 51 - 'it creates a lasting

advantage', it is 'once and for all' and 'procures a

right of a permanent nature'. It follows that it is

'like' an expenditure of a capital nature. If the matter

is to be opened up all over again, I consider it to be

more appropriate that this should be done by the courts

rather than a Board of Review."

16. On reflection, it appears to me that the constituent elements that, collectively, separate "capital" from "income" are as elusive now as they were over a decade ago, when I attempted to formulate them. Applying the law as I understand it to this case, I have concluded that all but one of the components of the "facilities charge" are of a capital nature.

17. Looking at the circumstances which followed the termination of the one year agreement under which the fee was payable (a legitimate aid to navigation; cf F.C. of T. v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd 79 ATC 4279), that is, the immediate entering into of two back-to-back contracts with the Corporation totalling a further six years - the last of which is still on foot -in terms of very similar effect (with the notable lack of a "facilities charge" in either of the subsequent agreements), I am satisfied that the components of the "facilities charge" relating to the recoupment of the cost of the mains, meter, regulators and maximum demand indicator ("the recoupment components") are capital in nature. To use the terminology of the various characterisation "tests", the recoupment components of the payment, viewed from a practical and not a juristic analysis, "procured a right of a permanent character", "created a lasting advantage", had an "enduring benefit" and were "once and for all".

18. It was submitted on behalf of the applicant - properly so in my view - that cases such as W. Nevill and Co Ltd v F.C. of T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290, and Metals Exploration Ltd v F.C. of T. 86 ATC 4505 are authority for the proposition that the mere fact that a payment is made in a lump sum does not of itself alter the character of the series of payments which the lump sum seeks to commute. However that principle can afford the applicant little comfort with respect to the recoupment components of the "facilities charge". Had it paid the "facilities charge" by 36 monthly instalments (which had an in-built interest component), the character of the recoupment components would still have been of a capital nature.

19. It was sought to use the same argument on the basis that the recoupment components of the "facilities charge" were a commutation of the monthly "supply charge" payable under the general industrial tariff, and therefore of an income or revenue nature. Although it is true that the monthly "supply charge" was not payable once the applicant had entered into the Tariff 95 agreements, I do not consider that the evidence establishes that the "facilities charge" was in commutation of that monthly charge. This is borne out by the fact that, were either the applicant or Corporation to terminate the current "Tariff 95" agreement, and the applicant to resume gas consumption under the general industrial tariff, it would again have to pay a monthly "supply charge". This leads me to conclude that it is unnecessary to characterise that "supply charge". However, I note that the Board of Review No 1 in 9 CTBR (NS) Case 103 considered that a flat rate service charge payable by all consumers of a particular electricity tariff at the same time as charges for the quantity of electricity consumed was clearly an outgoing on revenue account.

20. For what small comfort it may be to the applicant, I am, however, satisfied that that component of the "facilities charge" which was, in effect, the passing on of the Corporation's liability to Victorian tax on its revenue, is itself of a revenue nature. That component, unlike the other components of the "facilities charge", did not create an enduring benefit or lasting advantage to the applicant's business; it was an amount which the applicant was forced to pay as part of the capital components of the charge. It is clearly severable, thus entitling the applicant to a deduction of that part of $3,438.00 which accords with the proportion of the "facilities charge" it paid, which I calculate amounts to $2,693.00.

21. Since the issue "penalty" was neither raised in the objection nor was sought to be added as a ground of objection by way of amendment, I content myself with merely noting that at the hearing, counsel for the respondent promptly abandoned that part of the penalty said to have been struck on the basis of "culpability" when I voiced some surprise that a failure correctly to characterise an outgoing as "capital" constitutes a culpa. That leaves a penalty of 20% p.a. simple interest on the amount of tax outstanding with which I do not propose to interfere.

22. For the above reasons, the objection decision is varied by reducing the applicant's taxable income by $2,693.00.
