Re Taxation Appeals [1987] AATA 161 (30 April 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT85/3

AAT No. 3416

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

J.E. Stewart (Senior Member)

D.J. Trowse (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - payment made upon surrender of lease - question as to true legal character of the payment - whether it comprised damages occasioned by breach of the lessee's obligation to repair or whether it was paid as consideration to quit premises that had become uneconomic to operate - character of the payment in the hands of the recipient - whether assessable income or of a capital nature

HEARING

CANBERRA

30:4:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and instead it is decided that the amount of $400,000 to which it related was wholly capital and, therefore, formed no part of the applicant's assessable income.

DECISION

The application for review in these proceedings is in respect of the respondent's decision to treat as assessable income of the applicant company in the income tax year ended 30 June 1980 an amount received by it in its capacity as lessor, upon the lessee deciding to terminate the leasing arrangement before the due date.

2. In the applicant's taxation return for the year of income in issue which was prepared by its tax agent, the receipt was described as "Capital Receipt on Surrender of Lease" (Schedule 9). That schedule which accompanied the return described the receipt in the following terms:-

" A net lump sum of $399,450 was received by the

taxpayer from the lessee of its Abattoirs. This

represents an unliquidated amount as damages

occasioned by Breach of the lessee's obligation to

repair and maintain the premises in accordance with

the covenants contained in the lease.

The net lump sum is made up as follows;

Lump sum receipt $400,000

Less Legal fees 550

$399,450"

3. Because Schedule 9, which forms one of the many schedules required to accompany a company return, deals with deduction items as opposed to receipts, it seems appropriate to mention that the applicant included the amount of $399,450 in its profit, and so perforce had later to extract it in arriving at its taxable income to avoid paying tax on it. That latter action of course was consistent with its view that it was an item of capital.

4. On the basis of the above notation contained in Schedule 9, the conduct of the Commissioner in treating the gross receipt of $400,000 as assessable income would seem eminently reasonable. Accordingly the applicant's Counsel, early in his opening address, took pains to point out that the notation was in error; the receipt did not in fact comply with the description given to it in the return. The evidence of the accountant who prepared the return, and who was called as a witness for the applicant, was that the description which he made of the receipt was extracted from the deed of release prepared by the lessee's solicitors. It was contended by Counsel for the applicant that the documentation showed that the lessee was a party who was very much alive to the tax consequences of the transaction, and that the wording of the documents was designed to give the impression that was in fact taken by the Commissioner. At all events, it was the evidence of a senior officer of the lessee company who took part in the discussions which culminated in the surrender of the lease, and whom we shall call A, that that company successfully claimed a deduction for the amount of $400,000 in its taxation return for the year of income in issue.

5. In processing the applicant's return, the Commissioner treated the net amount of $399,450 as assessable income on the basis that it was received for non-compliance with the lessee's covenant to repair, and so came within the provisions of section 26(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. At the hearing the Commissioner, through his Counsel, argued also that the amount was assessable either under section 25(1) or under section 26(j). The issue before the Tribunal is to determine the "true legal character" of the receipt based upon the documents prepared to formalize the surrender of the lease, and upon the other evidence both written and oral, adduced at the hearing (see Cliffs International Inc. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4059 at p4066).

6. The applicant company, which we shall call W, was a private company and had operated as wholesale and retail butchers for many years, catering for the domestic market. On 20 December 1974, it leased an abattoir premises to N Meat Export a division of Y, a large public company. Prior to entering into the lease, W had conducted its own killing operation there and, upon the leasing arrangement being completed, Y took over the business as a going concern. The main features of the leasing arrangement for present purposes were as follows:-

(1) The period of the lease ran from 10 July 1974 to

9 July 1980.

(2) The rental (subject to some minor adjustments)

was $5,000 per week with a royalty charged of

$5.00 per unit for killings in excess of 1,000

units per week. A unit was defined as "an animal

killed on the beef floor of the demised premises

or any group of ten other animals or part thereof

killed elsewhere on the demised premises".

(3) Use of the demised premises was restricted to an

abattoir meat works, food processing, and allied

uses.

(4) As one of a number of obligations affecting the

maintenance of the demised premises, and

structural improvements effected thereon, Y

covenanted to repair and maintain the premises

and all additions made thereto by W, and to yield

them up to W at the end of the period of the

lease or upon its earlier determination. Also in

regard to certain designated items of furniture

and other chattels, Y was to make good all damage

caused to them and replace them with similar

articles of at least equal value in the event of

them being destroyed.

With the exception of those items, Y covenanted

to maintain the demised premises in such

condition as to meet the requirements of certain

stipulated federal government departments such as

the Department of Primary Industry, and certain

overseas governmental bodies such as the European

Common Market, save and except that Y was not

obliged to carry out any structural work or any

replacement or addition of a capital nature.

Y also covenanted to comply at its own expense

with the provisions of the Labour and Industry

Act 1958 (Vic.) and the regulations issued

thereunder, provided that it should not be

required to carry out structural work unless such

work was made necessary by reason of the nature

of the business carried on in the demised

premises. In the event that Y had cause to

install a building, machinery or fixtures, then

it was to bear the whole of the cost. Also so

long as the purpose of those assets was

consistent with the uses detailed in (3) above,

they remained the property of Y, and were to be

removed by it during the currency of the lease,

but not afterwards.

7. It seems that some few years after Y entered into possession, the meat industry encountered adverse economic conditions and the demised premises ceased to be economically viable. Accordingly, A was given authority by his employer company to terminate the lease and negotiate the best arrangement with W that he could.

8. It seems that initial discussions took place in approximately mid-1978 between A and a director of W who at that time was involved in the day-to-day operations of the Company. We shall call that gentleman B. B gave evidence for the applicant company and, as we have already stated, A gave evidence for his company, Y. Both impressed us as honest and truthful witnesses but the evidence of both, more particularly that of A, lacked precision as to the content of the discussions due doubtless to the effluxion of time, and to the fact that over the intervening years A had been involved in the disposition of a large number of other abattoir premises.

9. However, the following salient points emerged from their respective testimonies:-

(1) The matter of terminating the lease was initiated

by Y.

(2) Surrender of the lease took place on 7 September

1979.

(3) The amount of the consideration viz $400,000, for

terminating the lease was determined by W, and

agreed to by Y.

(4) No discussion took place between the parties as

to how the amount of $400,000 was made up.

(5) The directors of W were concerned that the

company might get back a property that would be

difficult to re-lease because of its

deterioration and because the lessor had made

structural alterations that were in keeping with

its business as a meat exporter but would have no

value for abattoirs catering for the domestic

market. Also they were concerned that the

business of the abattoirs had been effectively

lost because the plant had been idle for some

time and all employees had been dismissed.

Particular concerns were the loss of rental

(presumably covering the period from 7 September

1979 to 9 July 1980) and costs associated with

starting up the plant.

It was B's evidence that estimates of cost

associated with the early termination of the

lease were prepared, and it was his figure of

$400,000 that was agreed by his fellow directors

and "readily accepted" (to use his phrase) by Y.

His summation was:-

"If they (the directors of Y) wished to be

released from the conditions under the

lease that we would be requiring a

payment of $400,000."

(That summation seems consistent with a Minute of

a meeting of W's directors held on 21 September

1979 which noted the transaction in the following

terms:-

"On surrender of Lease from 7th September,

1979, (N) paid $400,000 on a walk in walk

out basis.")

(6) For Y's part, the company was aware of the fact

that it had changed the lay-out of the premises

and had obligations under the lease to put those

premises back in their original condition. Also

it was aware that a considerable amount of

repairs and maintenance work was required to be

done to comply with the terms of the lease. It

was A's evidence that Y sought to terminate the

lease because the operation was no longer

profitable resulting from an insufficient margin

between the prices paid for cattle and the prices

received from overseas buyers for its exports of

meat.

It seems that before Y found it necessary to

close down the operation conducted on the demised

premises it had made moves to engage in the meat

industry in other parts of Australia, and it was

felt that the funds tied up in the demised

premises could be employed more profitably in

other locations.

(7) The documents formalising the termination of the

lease were drawn up by Y's solicitors upon

instructions from A.

(8) It was A's evidence that before giving those

instructions he had discussions with his

company's taxation manager who advised him "to

make the payment under the covenant". (That

covenant presumably refers to clause 1(b) of the

lease when one takes cognizance of the terms of

the Deed of Release detailed later in these

reasons at paragraphs 10 and 11.)

(9) The content of those discussions was not advised

to W.

(10) W had its solicitors approve the formal documents

before it executed them.

(11) Upon execution of the documents by both parties,

parts were exchanged, and the cheque for $400,000

was handed over to W.

10. We now turn to the documents themselves. They comprised two separate documents, a Deed of Release and a Deed of Surrender both dated 7 September 1979. Both were executed at the same time (using that term in the broad sense), and it did not come out in evidence as to which document was executed first. (It might be added that it was never made clear why it was considered necessary to prepare two documents instead of one). The preamble to the Deed of Release was in the following terms:-

"WHEREAS:

A. The Lessor and the Lessee are the parties

mentioned in a certain Memorandum of Lease dated

20th December, 1974 ('the Lease'), being the

pieces of land therein described, together with

all improvements erected thereon and together

with all plant therein described (therein called

'the Demised Premises') for a term commencing on

10th July, 1974 and expiring on 9th July, 1980.

B. The Lessor has made claim against the Lessee for

an unliquidated amount as damages and

compensation for the loss allegedly occasioned to

the Lessor by reason of the Lessee's breach of

the Lessee's obligations to repair and maintain

the Demised Premises in accordance with the

covenants by the Lessee contained in the Lease.

C. Without admission of liability, the Lessee has

agreed to pay the Lessor the sum of Four Hundred

Thousand Dollars ($400,000.00) and the Lessor has

agreed to accept the said amount in full

satisfaction and discharge in respect of its

claim against the Lessee and the Lessor has

agreed to grant to the Lessee release and

discharge on the terms and conditions herein set

out."

11. The purpose of the payment is contained in the following clause:-

"In consideration of the Lessee's agreement to pay the

said sum of Four Hundred Thousand Dollars

($400,000.00), the Lessor hereby releases and forever

discharges the Lessee from all actions, suits, costs,

claims and demands whatsoever which it now has or at

any time hereafter may have or, but for the execution

hereof, might have had against the Lessee by reason

of any breach by the Lessee of the Lessee's

obligation to repair and maintain the Demised

Premises in accordance with the covenants by the

Lessee contained in the Lease or by reason of or in

respect of any matter, act or thing in any wise

relating thereto and howsoever arising thereout of

the subject matter of the said Lease."

12. The Deed of Surrender, after instancing the date of making and the names of the parties, was, to the extent considered relevant, in the following terms:-

"WHEREAS:

A. The Lessor and the Lessee are the parties

mentioned in a certain Memorandum of Lease dated

20th December 1974 ('the Lease') being the pieces

of land therein described, together with all

improvements erected thereon and together with

all plant therein described (therein called 'the

Demised Premises') for a term commencing on 10th

July 1974 and expiring on 9th July 1980.

B. The Lessor has agreed to accept from the

Lessee a surrender and termination of the

unexpired portion of the Lease as and from

the day of 1979. (date left blank)

C. The Lessor and the Lessee have agreed to grant to

each other respectively mutual release and

discharge of their respective obligations arising

under the terms and conditions of the Lease.

NOW THIS DEED WITNESSETH:

1. In consideration of the premises and of the

mutual release herein contained, the Lessee does

hereby convey and surrender to the Lessor all the

property demised by the Lease to the intent that

the term of years granted under the said Lease

shall now merge and be vested in the Lessor.

2. The Lessor and the Lessee hereby forever mutually

release and discharge each other from all

proceedings, actions, claims and demands arising

in respect of any breach of any term, condition

or covenant contained in the said Lease or

otherwise arising in respect of the Lessee's

occupation of the Demised Premises thereunder.

3. The Lessee specifically waives any right of

action or claim it may have had pursuant to

Clause 3(d) of the Lease and acknowledges that it

has no proprietary interest, ownership or claim

in any building, fence, engine, machinery,

fixture, fitting or chattel of whatsoever nature

on the Demised Premises.

4. In the event of any dispute arising as to the

interpretation of this Deed of Surrender and a

Deed of Release of even date made between the

parties then it is agreed that the provisions of

this Deed of Surrender shall prevail."

13. The factual narrative can be completed by adding that upon Y going out of possession, the abattoir premises were leased at a reduced rental to a private company, V, comprising younger members of the family which was the main shareholder in W. It seems that most of W's retail butcher shops had been closed down over the years in which Y was in occupation of the lease, and that the company's activities were reduced to livestock production and the renting out of properties. That situation changed again in approximately 1982 when the company saw fit to resume control of the abattoir operation conducted by V.

14. Accordingly the matter in issue calls for determining whether the amount of $400,000 was paid by Y according to the terms of the Deed of Release or was in fact paid as consideration to put an end to a leasehold interest which was proving unprofitable, and so financially burdensome. Because of the terms of the Deed of Release, Y has been able to have the expenditure allowed as a deduction, presumably under section 53AA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. We have no doubt that that was why its solicitor prepared the two deeds as he did. If the consideration which the applicant gave for the $400,000 which it received was as stated in the Deed of Release, that sum (less $550 which was paid as legal fees in respect of the transaction) must be assessed as income pursuant to section 26(1) of that Act. However, if the money was received as consideration for the applicant agreeing to the surrender of the lease, it was, we are satisfied, not assessable as income.

15. As was indicated in paragraph 10, the agreement that was made between the parties and which gave rise to the payment of $400,000 comprised two separate deeds. As such, the general principle of law is that the parties become conclusively bound by what they have stated in the deeds to be their respective undertakings (see Halsbury's Laws of England, Fourth Edition paragraph 1353). But the terms of their arrangement are not conclusive as against the Commissioner nor can they be, as he was not a party to their arrangement. The position is succinctly put by Megarry J. in Income Revenue Commissioners v. Church Commissioners for England (1974) 3 All ER 529 where at p549 he states:-

". . . I do not think that the parties can point to

the resulting product of pluperfect draftmanship, and

say to the inspector of taxes: 'That is conclusive:

you can look no further in determining the tax

liability of either of us.' For the purposes of

taxation, the question is always what is the true

nature of the payment, paying due respect to the

contractual obligations of the parties, on their true

construction, but not according them conclusive

effect."

16. That the ascertainment of the true nature of the transaction is not a matter of discretion for the revenue authority was made clear by the High Court of Australia in Commissioner of Taxes (Queensland) v. Ford Motor Company of Australia Pty. Ltd. [1942] HCA 16; (1942) 66 CLR 261. There the appellant had assessed the respondent on the basis that it possessed goodwill valued at 400,000 pounds. In making that assessment he had relied on a document under seal by which a company had purported to assign to the respondent certain trade marks "together with the goodwill of the business". The document, which contained an agreement between the respondent and the other company in respect of the trade marks and the goodwill, recited that the other company had acquired in Australia a considerable and valuable goodwill. The respondent objected to the assessment on the ground that it possessed no goodwill and had not acquired any as the result of the purported assignment, because the other company had not carried on business in Australia and had had no goodwill to assign to it. The appellant argued that it was not possible to look behind the agreement. Latham C.J. and Rich J., however, with whom McTernan J. agreed generally, said:-

"The Commissioner was not a party to the agreements

and cannot rely upon the recitals by way of estoppel.

They can be used against the company as admissions,

but they are not conclusive against the company. The

company is at liberty to show, if it can, that in

fact the Canadian company did not own any goodwill in

Australia and that accordingly the agreements were

ineffective to assign any such goodwill. Verbal

evidence for this purpose was admitted and, in our

opinion, rightly admitted."

We have no doubt, therefore, that we are required to ascertain the true nature of the transaction. The recitals in the deeds are evidence to be taken into account, but only in conjunction with all the other evidence which was presented to us.

17. In our view, the evidence of both B, the director of W, and of A, the senior officer of Y, points to the payment having been made as consideration for the applicant agreeing to the surrender of the lease. It will be recalled that it was B who determined the amount of $400,000 to be paid by Y Company, and he saw it as a payment to be made in order that Y, might be released from its obligations under the lease. From Y's point of view, it wanted to terminate the lease because the operation that it was conducting on the demised premises had ceased to be profitable. The evidence of A, who negotiated the surrender of the lease on behalf of Y, is quite clear on that point. Accordingly whilst it would seem that a list of outstanding repair items to be effected to the premises and plant had been drawn up by a senior officer of Y to assist in determining whether the consideration of $400,000 to terminate the lease was not too great a sum to pay, A's evidence was that that information was not advised to W, and indeed there is no good business reason why it should have been. Accordingly there is no evidence to show that Y's assessment of the repair work which it had covenanted to effect was ever made known to W. There was never any discussion between the parties (according to the evidence) as to Y's breach of its obligation to repair and certainly there was never any claim made by Y on W in regard to that particular matter.

18. Accordingly the recital made in the Deed of Release that such a claim was made is not accurate. Consequently, in our view, it cannot be said, as the operative clause of the Deed sought to say, that in consideration for receiving the amount of $400,000, W discharged Y from all claims which it might have had against it by reason of the latter's breach of its obligation to repair. In our view the real substance of the transaction was not that at all. Y did not pay over the amount of $400,000 to be discharged from failure of its obligation to repair; it paid over the amount (a large one by any standard) for a far more compelling reason, viz, to quit an interest in land that had become a burden because the operation conducted there was costing it money.

19. Helpful to us in our analysis of the true nature of the transaction in issue is the recent case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Creer 86 ATC 4318. The headnote summarizes the factual situation as follows:-

"The taxpayer was a solicitor. In 1977 he decided to

invest in residential property by leasing it from the

owner and then subletting it to tenants. He also had

in mind that his family company would purchase the

property subject to the head-lease to him. The

taxpayer sought taxation advice concerning such a

transaction and ultimately leased four home units.

The next day, the taxpayer's family company purchased

the units from the owner.

The leases to the taxpayer provided that the total

rent for the five year term of the lease was payable

in advance by one instalment in each of three

consecutive years. The first instalment under each

lease was paid on 30 June 1977 and comprised 80% of

the total rent for the five-year period.

In his return for the 1977 income year, the taxpayer

claimed a deduction for the prepayment of rent and

for the cost of taxation advice which he had

obtained. The Commissioner disallowed the deductions

on the grounds that the prepayment was a capital

amount (on the basis that the family company had

acquired the units at a lower price on account of the

prepayment), that the purpose of the prepayment was

entirely to gain a tax advantage and that the

transaction should be struck down by sec. 260."

20. In the New South Wales Supreme Court, Lusher J. was of the view that the prepayment of rent was not a capital outgoing and had not been used to reduce the purchase price of the units. The rent paid was truly rent and, contrary to what was asserted, neither section 260 nor the doctrine of fiscal nullity applied. The learned judge, therefore, allowed the deductions claimed. On appeal by the Commissioner, however, the Federal Court took a different view. In a unanimous decision it held that the item termed 'total rent' was a capitalized sum payable by instalments, and was not rent accruing from day to day covering the use of the premises for periods commensurate with the payments. The Court accordingly saw the payments as being of a capital nature and so specifically excluded from deduction by the terms of section 51(1).

21. In delivering the main judgment of the Court, Fisher J. stated, at p4320:-

"I see each of the outgoings as a payment which the

taxpayer was bound by his contract to make to Home

Units for the use of the land for the term of the

lease. The nature of such a payment is primarily to

be ascertained according to the true construction of

the lease agreement. To label the payment as 'rent'

does not conclude the matter and, if anything,

obscures an understanding and assessment of the true

nature of the outgoing.

I therefore would decide the matter by construing the

terms of the contracts between the taxpayer and Home

Units and by ascertaining the manner in which the

parties calculated the amount of the outgoing."

(emphasis added)

22. In seeking to establish whether the sum called "rent" was an outgoing on revenue account or was to be seen more correctly as a capitalized amount and thus an outgoing of a capital nature, Fisher J. adopted the classic statement of principle enunciated by Dixon J. (as he then was) in The Sun Newspapers Ltd. and Associated Newspapers Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at p363 as follows:-

"Again, the cases which distinguish between capital

sums payable by instalments and periodic payments

analogous to rent payable on revenue account

illustrate the fact that rights and advantages of the

same duration and nature may be the subject of

recurrent payments which are referable to capital

expenditure or income expenditure according to the

true character of the consideration given, that is,

whether on the one hand it is a capitalized sum

payable by deferred instalments or on the other hire

or rent accruing de die in diem, or at other

intervals, for the use of the thing . . .

There are, I think, three matters to be considered,

(a) the character of the advantage sought, and in

this its lasting qualities may play a part, (b) the

manner in which it is to be used, relied upon or

enjoyed, and in this and under the former head

recurrence may play its part, and (c) the means

adopted to obtain it; that is, by providing a

periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or

enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payment

or by making a final provision or payment so as to

secure future use or enjoyment."

23. Fisher J. considered that in regard to the set of circumstances before him, it was necessary for him to place primary emphasis on the last of the three matters raised by Dixon J., viz. the nature of the payment in consideration of which the taxpayer obtained the use and occupation of the premises. After making a detailed analysis of the basis of the payment he came to the view that the "rent" payment as comprised by the three instalments did not represent (to use Dixon J.'s phraseology) "a periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payments" but was in fact "a final provision or payment so as to secure its future use or enjoyment". Holding that view, (which he considered was supported by the fact that the only reason for providing for three instalments was that one payment would have appeared commercially unrealistic), he determined that the "rent" was a capitalized sum and so was not deductible.

24. An analysis of the nature of payments made under a lease and called "rent" was also made by the Full High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. South Australian Battery Makers Pty. Ltd. 78 ATC 4412. There, Gibbs A.C.J. as he then was, in delivering the leading majority judgment in favour of the taxpayer, acknowledged that to call something "rent" did not conclude the matter; it was necessary to determine that the item called "rent" did in fact answer that description. Similarly, the fact that the payment was called "rent" and was made periodically would not necessarily prevent it from being at least in part an outgoing of a capital nature. He considered that a two-tiered analysis of the payment in issue was required. First, identify the advantage sought by the taxpayer in making the payment, and secondly, determine whether that identifiable advantage was of a revenue or capital nature by applying the classic statement of principle made by Dixon J. in the Sun Newspapers case to which reference was made earlier in para 20.

25. In the result, he reached the conclusion, in which he was joined by Stephen and Aicken J.J., that the advantage which the taxpayer sought and obtained by each payment of rent was the right to continue for the appropriate period in occupation as lessee of its business premises. That advantage was of a revenue nature and hence the cost of obtaining it was properly deductible.

26. On the view that we take of the analysis employed in Creer and in the South Australian Battery Makers case, we see the advantage sought by Y in the instant case in making the "once and for all" payment of $400,000 as terminating an unprofitable leasehold interest. Accordingly, we see the transaction as being of capital or of a capital nature, primarily because of the nature of the advantage sought but also because of the finality achieved by the payment made. Holding that view as we do, we do not see the payment as being made as a result of the lessee's non-compliance with a covenant to repair, and so deductible to Y under section 53AA, and assessable to the grantor of the lease, W, under the complementary provision contained in section 26(1). On the same basis of reasoning, we do not believe that the evidence supports the contention of the Commissioner's Counsel that in the alternative the amount is assessable in W's hands under the general provision, section 25(1), or as an indemnified amount for loss of income under section 26(j). The intrinsic character of the payment in our view was not of the kind contemplated by those provisions. For completeness we would also add that because of the view we take it is not necessary for us to discuss the type of situation instanced in McLaurin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 9; (1961) 104 CLR 381 where compensation becomes payable as an undissected lump sum but based on specific items of expenditure.

27. We believe that our conclusion as to the capital nature of the payment is supported by a number of authorities, one of the best known being the case of Mallett v. The Staveley Coal and Iron Co. Ltd. (1928) 13 TC 772. There, the taxpayer company had a number of mining leases which gave it the right to work certain beds of coal for a considerable number of years. Under those leases it was under an obligation to pay certain minimum rents and royalties and to work the coal according to approved practice and in one case to restore the surface of the land. In 1923 certain of the seams became unremunerative and the company wanted to get rid of those particular leases. After negotiation, it surrendered the particular leases paying in order to get rid of them, in one case 3,500 pounds and in the other case 3,000 pounds, and those amounts were claimed as admissible deductions in determining profits. In the court of first instance the payments were regarded as capital payments and so non-deductible. On appeal the Court of Appeal rejected an argument advanced for the taxpayer on the basis of dicta enunciated in Atherton v. British Insulated & Helsby Cables 10 TC 155 that payments on account of capital were limited to acquisitions of positive assets and did not cover the situation where a payment was made for the purpose of getting rid of an onerous asset. Sargant L.J. stated the view of the Court in the following passage appearing at p786:-

"In the second case, the case of the item of 3,000 pounds,

the payment was being made for the purpose of putting

an end to the existence of a disadvantage or onerous

asset for the enduring benefit of the trade. To my

mind such a transaction is entirely within the words

of the Lord Chancellor as used with regard to the

acquisition of a positive asset. In the case of the

3,500 pounds it seems to me a possibly clearer case than

the other case. It is a payment made for the purpose

of modifying the conditions of an existing asset so

as to make the resultant term more advantageous or

less disadvantageous for the enduring benefit of the

trade. In that case it seems to me that the words of

the Lord Chancellor, in themselves applicable to the

acquisition of a positive asset or possible

advantage, are equally applicable to the case where

the payment is made for the purpose of getting rid of

a permanent disadvantage or onerous liability arising

with regard to the lease, which was a permanent asset

of the business."

28. The same point as made in the last sentence of that passage was made by the High Court of Australia in Foley Brothers Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 13 ATD 562. That case contains certain parallels with the instant case. The taxpayer company engaged in the manufacture, distribution and export of dairy products and had entered into a long term agreement which contained an undertaking that it would not reduce the extent of its trading operations. It breached that undertaking when financial difficulties forced it to undergo a major rationalisation of its activities. The other party to the agreement commenced legal proceedings after some branch offices had been sold with others pending sale. The proceedings were settled on the basis that the taxpayer pay a sum of 38,750 pounds in return for the agreement being rescinded thereby leaving the taxpayer free to carry out its re-organisation. Upon the taxpayer claiming deduction under section 51(1) for the settlement sum and attendant legal expenses, the Commissioner disallowed those claims on the basis that they were capital expenditures. On appeal by the taxpayer the Commissioner's action was confirmed by a single Justice of the High Court of Australia and subsequently by the Full Court. At p5563, the Full Court, commenting upon tests for distinguishing income from capital as canvassed by the Privy Council in Commissioner of Taxes v. Nchanga Consolidated Copper Mines Ltd. (1964) AC 948, stated:-

"The true contrast is between altering the framework

within which income-producing activities are for the

future to be carried on and taking a step as part of

those activities within the framework. There can be

no justification for saying, as was said in the

argument for the appellant, that the cost of a

freedom to alter the framework by discarding part of

it is to be classified differently from the cost of a

freedom to add to the framework."

29. The decision in Staveley Coal and Iron Co. Ltd. and the dicta of the various judges were expressly approved in the case of West African Drug Co. Ltd. v. Lilley (1947) 28 TC 140, and more recently by the Victorian Supreme Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Marbray Nominees Pty. Ltd. 85 ATC 4750. In the West African Drug Co. Ltd. case, the taxpayer company leased business premises in West Africa for a term of 21 years from 1930. There was a covenant in the lease to keep the premises in repair. In June 1939 the premises were destroyed by an earthquake and there was a dispute as to who should be responsible for the cost of rebuilding. The company paid the lessors an amount of 2,753 pounds for the surrender of the lease and the release from any liability. It was held by the Court that the outlay was made to get rid of a permanent disadvantage or onerous liability arising under the terms of the lease and was expenditure of a capital nature. As in the situation before us, the object of the payment was made to quit a leasehold interest that had become a disability to the taxpayer company.

30. Before concluding these reasons, it is pertinent, we consider, to refer to a submission made by the Commissioner's Counsel in regard to the Staveley Coal and Iron Co. Ltd. and West African Drug Co. Ltd. decisions. His contention was that those decisions could not be accepted as authority for the proposition that the receipt in issue was a capital receipt, because the Court in both those cases was dealing with the question of deductibility of the payment, and not with the matter of assessability of the receipt. He pointed to the fact that a payment might not be deductible in the hands of the payer did not necessarily mean that the same payment might not be assessable in the hands of the recipient. That is perfectly true, and reference in general terms to the matter may be found in the judgment of Barwick C.J. in Cliffs International Inc. (supra) at p4065.

31. However, always worth remembering when considering the sorts of issues before us in the instant case, is the much quoted dictum of Lord Macnaghten in London County Council v. A.G. (1901) AC 26 at p35:-

"Income tax, if I may be pardoned for saying so, is a

tax on income. It is not meant to be a tax on

anything else."

With those words in mind, we consider that the amount received by W upon surrender of the lease by Y, cannot be regarded as assessable for tax because it is simply not income. The word "income" is not defined in the Income Tax Assessment Act and, therefore, whether a receipt is or is not income "must be determined in accordance with the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind" (Scott v. Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) (1935) 35 SR (NSW) 215 at p240).

32. As counsel for the applicant argued (correctly in our view) the leasehold interest which W rented out to Y was carved out of its freehold estate and comprised one of a large number of the company's fixed assets. That leasehold formed part of the structure or framework of the total enterprise. If the company as landlord had obtained a premium for granting that interest, that sum would have been a capital receipt in its hands, and an outgoing of a capital nature to Y. Thus, the sum would have been non-assessable to W, and non-deductible to Y. By parity of reasoning, in the event of the leasehold interest being given up or surrendered, as has happened in the instant case, and a payment made in consequence of that surrender, the amount so paid becomes non-deductible to the payer and non-assessable to the recipient. It is clear from the evidence, we believe, that it was no part of W's business to trade in leaseholds (and that point holds true even though the subject property was subsequently relet) and, therefore, the amount of $400,000 cannot be regarded as "the product of . . . any business, or indeed, any revenue producing activity carried on" by it, which might tend to give it the appearance of income (see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Harris 80 ATC 4238 at p4241).

33. For all of the above reasons, we believe that the character of the receipt in W's hands was wholly capital and, therefore, forms no part of that company's assessable income. The objection must be allowed and the Commissioner's decision on the objection set aside.
