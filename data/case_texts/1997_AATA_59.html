CATCHWORDS

TAXATION - Sales tax - claim for exemption - containers - whether manufactured in Australia “for use in marketing goods manufactured in Australia”

WORDS AND PHRASES - “marketing”

Sales Tax Act (No. 9) 1930 s 3

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 sch 1, items 93, 113C

Sales Tax Regulations reg 4



Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kentucky Fried Chicken Pty Limited (1988) 12 NSWLR 643

Herbert Adams Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 27; (1932) 47 CLR 222

Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Lincoln Industrial Cleaners Pty Limited 75 ATC 4208

Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Stewart & Another 84 ATC 4146     

REASONS FOR DECISION



      26 February 1997 Deputy President BJ McMahon



  1. These are two applications to review decisions given in response to Notices of Objection against refusals to refund sales tax. Matter number NT95/107 concerns an application made by a company to which I will refer as the manufacturer. Matter NT95/108 refers to an application by a company to which I will refer as the hirer. As both applications arose out of similar circumstances and deal with identical issues, they were heard together with the consent of all parties.

THE GOODS

  1. The hirer’s claim relates to a range of four classes of goods namely 25 and 50 litre plastic crates, and two sizes of collapsible boxes known internally as CB1s and CB2s. The claim of the manufacturer relates to different styles of collapsible boxes known as CB4s and CB5s.
  2. The plastic crates and the CB1s and CB2s were specifically developed by the hirer in conjunction with the container manufacturers for the use of the automotive industry. Crates were developed as a replacement for cardboard packing previously utilised by automotive component manufacturers. The design and size of the crates were agreed with the vehicle manufacturers. The CB1s and CB2s were introduced to accommodate larger components and also for use as an outer container for components delivered in plastic crates.
  3. The crates are rectangular plastic bins with handles and an open top. They are so constituted in size and design that they are stackable one upon the other. Their principal use is to accommodate most of the small components that go into car production. Another advantage of the crates is that their uniform sizes allow suppliers to deliver in standard quantities which makes purchasing and stocktaking easier.
  4. Plastic crates are reusable and are recyclable until they have reached the end of their useful economic life. They are unadorned except for the hirer’s business name endorsed on the outside of each crate.
  5. CB1s and CB2s are collapsible boxes of different sizes. Each is constructed from galvanised steel frames and plywood panels along with a galvanised steel, four-way entry pallet for ease of handling by forklift trucks. The front panel can be removed to enable the box to be loaded. All four sides of the box can be removed to allow it to be stacked economically. All the boxes are of a uniform size within their class, so that they can be stacked one upon the other when loaded. They too are unadorned except for a note of the hirer’s business name on the outside of each box.
  6. The CB4s and CB5s were developed as an extension to the range of collapsible boxes. Their principal difference is that CB4s are designed to carry dry goods while the CB5 is designed to carry liquids via a plastic bladder which is inserted into the box. CB4s and CB5s are not designed for use in the automotive industry.

THE TRANSACTIONS

  1. CB4s and CB5s were sold by the manufacturer to the hirer, which in turn hired them out to companies which used them for storage and delivery of goods to other manufacturers. For example, some of the CB5s were used for the delivery of vegetable oil by an oil manufacturer to a second manufacturer which used it in the process of bread-making. The evidence was that 98 per cent of the plastic crates, the CB1s and the CB2s, were hired out by the hirer to companies which made vehicle components. The containers were used both for storage and for delivery of those components to vehicle manufacturers.
  2. All of the equipment formed part of the hirer’s pool. Equipment was drawn from that pool and issued to customers upon their request. Thereupon the customer became responsible for the payment of the agreed daily hire charge for the containers and for their safe return to the hirer. If the customer was unable to return the containers, it would be liable to pay the hirer compensation for the loss of the asset. The customer can generally terminate the hire of the containers, either by returning the goods to the hirer or by transferring them to another customer of the hirer. In the latter event, the initial customer would need to have the receiving customer accept a transfer docket from the hirer at the time the goods were physically delivered to the receiving customer. The first customer would then need to provide the hirer with a copy of that transfer docket. Thereupon the hirer would transfer the quantity actually delivered from the first customer’s account to that of the receiving customer. Where a customer of the hirer physically transferred possession of containers to a company that was not a customer of the hirer, the first customer remained responsible for the safe return of the containers to the hirer. As one would expect from a company carrying on a hiring business its first concern was to ensure continuity of hiring charges, its second concern was to secure the containers through periodic audit checks, and its third concern was to preserve the integrity of the containers by servicing them on every recycle. It was not principally concerned with the way in which the containers were used.
  3. There had been a history of the taking of surveys in negotiations between the hirer and the respondent. After the new Sales Tax legislation was introduced, a fresh survey was taken to obtain information about possible claims for exemption under the new legislation. Although the surveys were not designed to give information in relation to the old legislation, nevertheless an examination of the results led the applicants to believe that they may have been entitled to exemptions under the old legislation and that they had unwittingly paid sales tax without being liable therefor. Accordingly claims for refunds were made after certain calculations were done. The rejection of these claims have led to the present reviews.
  4. The manufacturer’s claims for exemption depend upon the success or otherwise of the claims of its customer, namely, the hirer. Because the goods were hired out sales tax was imposed at the relevant time by s 3 of the Sales Tax Act (No.9) 1930 (Act 9) which deals with goods that may have gone into use or consumption in Australia through leasing. The claim for exemption is based upon certain items contained in Division XIII of Schedule 1 of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 which deals with containers. Item 93 provides an exemption from sales tax for “boxes, cases and crates manufactured in Australia for use in marketing goods manufactured in Australia, being goods covered by any item or sub-item in this Schedule ...” It was agreed that all of the containers were properly described as boxes, cases or crates and were manufactured in Australia and that the excluded items referred to in item 93 had no application in the present circumstances. In considering item 93, therefore, the issue was whether the containers were manufactured “for use in marketing goods manufactured in Australia”, which goods were covered by another item in the schedule.
  5. The items exempting goods to be found in the containers and relied upon by the applicants were alternatively items 113, 113A and 113C. Item 113 exempts tax on “materials sold to or imported by an unregistered manufacturer to be used in, wrought into, or attached to, so as to form part of, goods to be manufactured by him, being goods covered by any item or sub-item in this schedule except goods covered by items [not relevant].” Item 113A refers to “goods, being aids to manufacture, sold or leased to, or imported by, an unregistered manufacturer for use by him exclusively, or primarily or principally, in, or in connection with, the manufacture of goods covered by any item or sub-item in this schedule other than [not relevant].” Item 113C refers to “goods applied by a registered person to his own use as aids to manufacture (as defined by regulations made under the Sales Tax Assessment Acts) or as auxiliaries to aids to manufacture (as so defined).”
  6. Initially the applicants relied on each of these three items in the alternative because of the difficulty in readily identifying whether the customers of the hirers were registered or unregistered. The tests are slightly different in each case. Ultimately though, the applicants relied upon item 113C and submissions were directed exclusively to the terms of that item. In deciding, therefore, whether item 113C is available to support a claim under item 93, it will be necessary to determine firstly whether the contents of the containers were applied by a registered person (generally a vehicle manufacturer) to its own use and secondly whether such an application was an aid to manufacture as defined.
  7. Regulation 4 of the Sales Tax Regulations defines “aids to manufacture” relevantly as follows —

“aids to manufacture” means goods for use by a registered person in the course of carrying on a business (where that use is exclusively, or primarily and principally, for the purposes of that business), being:

(a) machinery, implements and apparatus for use exclusively, or primarily and principally:
  • (i) in the actual processing or treatment of goods to be used in, wrought into or attached to goods to be manufactured;
  • (ii) in any processing or treatment by which the goods to which that processing or treatment is applied are used in, wrought into or attached to goods to be manufactured;
  • (iii) in any processing or treatment for the purpose of bringing goods into, or maintaining goods in, the form or condition in which they are to be marketed or used by the manufacturer of the goods;

............

(b) ....

(c) ....

(d) goods (other than those specified in paragraph (a) or (b) or those excluded from this definition by paragraph (f) or (k)) for use as specified in paragraph (a)...”

  1. The goods relied upon by the applicant as exempt and referred to in item 113C are the contents of the container referred to in item 93. Typically, the goods will consist of automotive components, such as headlights which will have been manufactured by the hirer’s customer and will have been delivered by that customer in the hirer’s container to the automotive manufacturer. The question to be determined in that typical case will be whether the automotive manufacturer applied the headlights to its own use as an aid to manufacture, for example, in the sense outlined in paragraph (a)(iii) of bringing the motor vehicle into the form or condition in which it is to be marketed.

GENERAL ISSUES

  1. The first question to be determined is whether the containers were “for use in marketing goods”. The applicant’s case is that the design of the goods was developed in conjunction with the vehicle manufacturers for convenience of delivery and accounting and that accordingly the process of delivery of goods from the component manufacturer to the vehicle manufacturer is part of the marketing of those goods. It is only if the applicant is successful in relation to this issue that the remaining issues become relevant. If the applicants are unsuccessful in demonstrating that the containers were for use in marketing, then whether the goods said to have been marketed were exempt or not becomes irrelevant. If the applicants cannot show that the containers were for use in marketing then they will be unsuccessful in this application. Assuming the necessary use can be shown, then the following three issues will need to be considered.
  2. The second issue is whether those components were applied by the motor vehicle manufacturer to its own use as an aid to manufacture.
  3. The third issue is the sufficiency of evidence of the facts. By and large the applicants relied on surveys which were not designed to elicit facts relevant to the old legislation but from which the Tribunal is asked to make certain deductions.
  4. The fourth issue is whether the applicants should be denied their claims for refunds because this Tribunal is not satisfied that the tax has not been passed on, contrary to the provisions of sub-section 11(1A) of Act 9. This issue is confined only to the claim of the hirer. It is conceded that because of events which need not be recounted in these reasons, and because of a refund made by the hirer to the manufacturer, the s 11(1A) point does not arise in relation to the claim by the manufacturer.

ITEM 93

  1. All boxes, cases and crates are intended to have contents whether or not exempt. The reason for putting goods or substances in these containers will vary from time to time. However, it is clear from item 93 that not all boxes, cases and crates containing exempt goods are themselves exempt. They must be for use in marketing those exempt goods. What is meant by “marketing”? A meaning should be ascribed which is not detached from the reality of trade and commerce. As Hope JA said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kentucky Fried Chicken Pty Limited (1988) 12 NSW LR 643 at 654, one should adopt a robust approach to construction of words and phrases of this nature. It has been well settled for many years that a commercial understanding of terms in this legislation should be adopted because the law is addressed to a person engaged in trade or business (Herbert Adams Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 27; (1932) 47 CLR 222 at 226)
  2. The applicant relied on a definition in the Macquarie Dictionary of the word “marketing” in these terms —
“1 The total process whereby goods are put on to the market
  1. 2 The act of buying or selling in a market.”

  2. It was submitted that the term “marketing” as defined in accordance with its common or ordinary meaning was broad enough to incorporate the act of delivering goods under a contract of sale in the particular circumstances of this case.
  3. It seems to me more appropriate, however, where a commercial term like “marketing” is used, that a commercial approach to the concept should be adopted. The CCH Macquarie Dictionary of Business defines the term to mean —
“any range of business activities directed towards facilitating and satisfying the demand for goods and services of customers, business and government ... marketing also aims at making the product more attractive to customers than the products offered by competing firms in the private business sector ...”


The Shorter Oxford Dictionary does not define the word “marketing” as such, which would seem to indicate that at least when the Third Edition was published the word was to be found not in common or ordinary English usage but in specialised commercial usage. The word “market” is extensively defined and connotes a meeting together of people for the purchase and sale of goods.

  1. Marketing as a concept is clearly dynamic. It is to have expanded or contracted meanings in different contexts. A good commercial definition can be found in Volume 11 of the new Encyclopaedia Britannica Fifteenth Edition at page 505 in the following terms —
“In Western business organizations the marketing function was once seen as encompassing only sales and advertising and perhaps, also, marketing research. The purpose of the staff engaged in marketing was primarily to maximize sales volume. More recently the marketing manager’s responsibilities have broadened to include all activities relating to the firm’s customers, including product development, customer communications, and customer services; in some firms, the principal marketing executives have even been given authority over credit, physical distribution, public relations and research and development. In a few firms, the integrated marketing concept has been pushed to the point where the head of marketing becomes the chief executive officer.”


  1. Whatever approach is adopted in giving content to the word “marketing” it would seem that core requirements include promotion and sale of products with or without delivery.
  2. The use of the word “for” is purposive. It is intended to denote the purpose for which the governed noun is adapted. In Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Lincoln Industrial Cleaners Pty Limited 75 ATC 4208 at 4210, Sheppard J referred to it as requiring “some quality apparent in the article itself to persons who know its character which shows that it is specially fit for the particular purpose specified rather than for any other.” If this test is applied in understanding the meaning of the word “for” in item 93, one would have to find that the plastic crates and collapsible boxes were designed so that they were especially fit for marketing. Whether they were so depends upon the meaning to be ascribed to the word “marketing”.
  3. The word “use” requires something more than transient or insubstantial use (Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Stewart & Another 84 ATC 4146 at 4149). Whilst the applicants point to survey results which they interpret as indicating a use for marketing exceeding 30 per cent, it has to be said that the survey results were equivocal and that such a conclusion is optimistic. For present purposes, however, I am prepared to accept the percentage relied upon. It will not be necessary to decide whether this amounts to something more than transient or insubstantial use.
  4. In order to succeed under this item, therefore, the applicant must show —
(a) in a significant degree;

(b) that the boxes and crates make the product more attractive for sale; and

(c) that the containers facilitate the saleability of the goods contained in them.


  1. In my view the evidence is not sufficient to establish these contentions as facts.
  2. The applicants rely on two aspects of use of the containers to support their case. Firstly they allege that the containers were used for delivering components to the vehicle manufacturers and that delivering was part of the process of marketing. Certainly delivery, as such, is distinct from marketing as such. The former is not subsumed into the latter as was made clear in Nomad Industries of Australia Pty Limited & Another v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4036. The applicants say, however, that in the circumstances presently under consideration, the act of delivery can be regarded as part of the marketing process because vehicle manufacturers, through their trade association, played a part in the design of the containers and in specifying their use by component manufacturers. It seems to me that this does not flesh out the activities to encompass the act of marketing. Item 93 does not refer to “marketing” in segments. As is noted in the above definitions, marketing consists of a range of activities which would encompass all aspects from obtaining the order to obtaining payment. In between would lie the packing, advertising, delivery and contractual arrangements. There is no evidence at all of the contractual arrangements between the component manufacturers and the vehicle manufacturers. There is very little evidence about delivery. There is evidence that many of the containers were used for a substantial part of their life for storage. If storage is part of the process of marketing, it is only a peripheral part. There was positive evidence that the containers were never used for advertising or for making the product more attractive to customers.
  3. They come from a pool. They are not specific to any component manufacturer. They can be used for any sort of product each time they are used by the component manufacturer. The same containers could well be used for different purposes by the vehicle manufacturer until such time as the containers are returned to the pool.
  4. There are certainly no signs on the containers to identify the contents, nor the origin of the contents. As I have noted, the only visible endorsement is the hirer’s business name, no doubt so as to identify the container for audit purposes.
  5. Apart from these uses, the evidence shows a variety of other possible uses, including the use of the containers for storage of either semi finished goods or raw materials, for the movement and storage of semi finished goods, for the movement of finished goods within the manufacturing premises and for the delivery of finished goods. It would be stretching the meaning of “marketing”, no matter how robust an approach was taken, to assume that these were aspects of that activity. Even if the vehicle manufacturers require the goods to be delivered in boxes, and even if they specify the applicant’s boxes (and the evidence is not clear about this — there is a substantial competitor in the market) the particular type of goods contained within the boxes are of such a wide variety that it can not be said that the boxes are marketing any particular goods for any particular manufacturer.
  6. I agree with the submission of counsel for the respondent that item 93 in its terms is intended to cover boxes for marketing exempt goods where those boxes clearly identify the exempt goods in such a way as to be associated with the goods at the point of sale. In relation to consumer goods, for instance, I adopt his example of a container of Coca Cola, a box of chocolates, or KFC containers. All of these containers have something in common, namely, a purpose for application in the actual sale of the product. In my view, non distinctive boxes hired out from a pool for a variety of uses can not be said to be for use in marketing the goods upon which the applicants rely.
  7. The aspect of specification of the hirer’s products is not available to the manufacturer in the case of CB4s and CB5s. Those containers are not used, by and large, in the automotive industry but in a variety of general uses. The connection between the intended use of these boxes and marketing of the variety of products they contain is even weaker in the case of CB4s and CB5s than it is in the case of all the other containers.

CONCLUSION

  1. Whatever exempt contents category is chosen from items 113, 113A and 113C, I have concluded that the applicants do not pass the first test in that they have not satisfied the onus of showing that the containers are for use in marketing those goods. Because of the change in legislation, no good purpose would be served by examining the terms of the items relating to the contents. It will also not be necessary to deal with the question of passing on. The applicants, in my view, simply do not get past the first test.
  2. Accordingly, the decisions under review are affirmed.
