Re Ba Hong Nguyen Ex Parte: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1995] FCA 1036 (12 January 1995)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

RE: BA HONG NGUYEN

EX PARTE: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. VN2835 of 1994

RE: THI THANH PHAM

EX PARTE: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. VN2837 of 1994

FED No. 12/95

Number of pages - 8

Bankruptcy

[1995] FCA 1036; (1995) 30 ATR 63

[1995] FCA 1036; (1995) 54 FCR 403

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

HEEREY J

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - extension of time for compliance with bankruptcy notice - application to rescind vary or discharge extension ordered by Deputy Registrar - whether application should be made on notice to judgment creditor - whether pending application to Hardship Board under s 265(1) Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) sufficient grounds for extension - whether order granting extension should be rescinded ab initio

Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth) ss 41(6B)

Bankruptcy Rules r 104

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) s 265(1)

Re Briggs (1986) 12 FCR 310

Hadkinson v Hadkinson (1952) 2 All ER 567

Re Sterling [1980] FCA 61; (1980) 30 ALR 77

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 12 January 1995

12:1:1995

Counsel for the judgment debtor: Mr R Cameron

Solicitor for the judgment debtor: Nguyen and Associates

Counsel for the judgment creditor: Mr R Brett

Solicitor for the judgment creditor: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The Court orders that:

1. The orders of Deputy Registrar Efthim made 8 December be varied so

that the time for compliance with the bankruptcy notices is

extended until 4.00 pm 12 January 1995.

2. The judgment debtors pay the judgment creditor's costs of the

application, including reserved costs.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules

DECISION

HEEREY J In VN 2835 of 1994 the judgment creditor has applied to rescind, vary or discharge an order made by a deputy registrar extending the time for compliance with a bankruptcy notice. A similar application has been made in VN 2837 of 1994, in which the judgment debtor is the wife of the judgment debtor in the earlier case. There is no material difference between the two cases. For the sake of convenience I shall confine the factual narrative to the first one.

2. The judgment on which the bankruptcy notice was based was obtained on 23 September 1994 in the County Court at Melbourne for the sum of $141,021.62. The bankruptcy notice was issued on 25 November and required payment within 14 days after service. The notice was served on 28 November. Thus the time for compliance with the notice would have expired on 12 December. On 6 December the debtor's solicitor filed an application in the registry seeking orders that the bankruptcy notice be set aside and that the time for compliance with the requirements of the bankruptcy notice be extended

"... until after the determination of the s 265(1) hardship

application under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and up to

and including the hearing of this application to set aside the

bankruptcy notice."

3. In an affidavit filed in support of the application, the debtor deposed amongst other things that the judgment debt arose out of a business which had ceased trading, that he was currently working as a machinist earning approximately $230 per week gross, that his wife was also working as a machinist earning the same amount and that they had four children, three of whom were dependent. He further deposed:

"I have no assets other than my house at 127 McIntosh Road,

North Altona, which is encumbered with the Westpac Banking

Corporation. In total I have no assets nor the income in

which I could ever foreseeably pay the judgment creditor for

the amount claimed."

4. The hardship application was exhibited to his affidavit and included a statement:

"Even if I sell all of my assets, including our residential

home, furniture, personal belongings etc, I could not pay

one-third of the liability."

5. The hardship application referred to was made under s 265(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), which provides:

" 265(1) In any case where it is shown to the satisfaction

of a Board consisting of the Commissioner, the Secretary to

the Department of Finance and the Comptroller-General of

Customs or of such substitutes for all or any of them as the

Minister appoints from time to time that -

(a) a taxpayer has suffered such a loss or is in such

circumstances; or

(b) owing to the death of a person, who, if he had lived,

would have been liable to pay tax, the dependants of

that person are in such circumstances,

that the exaction of the full amount of tax will entail

serious hardship, the Board may release the taxpayer or the

trustee of the estate of the deceased person (as the case may

be) wholly or in part from his liability, and the Commissioner

may make such entries and alterations in the assessment as are

necessary for that purpose."

6. The application to set aside the bankruptcy notice was not served on the judgment creditor. I was informed by counsel for the debtor that no oral hearing took place. An order was made by a deputy registrar on 8 December and entered on 9 December in these terms:

"The Registrar orders pursuant to s 41(6B) of the Bankruptcy

Act 1966 that:

1. The time for compliance with the bankrupt (sic) notice

herein be extended up to 4.00 pm, 20 February 1995, or

until further order.

2. A copy of this order be served on the creditor forthwith

together with a copy of the application to set aside the

bankruptcy notice.

3. Each party have liberty to apply upon 3 days notice to

the other party."

7. The order was not served on the judgment creditor, who only became aware of it on 22 December when his solicitors attempted to file a creditor's petition.

8. Counsel for the judgment creditor urged two grounds, first that the order should not have been made ex parte and secondly, that on a re-consideration of the matter, this Court should exercise its discretion against granting any extension.

9. As to the first ground, I was told by counsel for the debtor, and I accept, that the practice in the registry has been for extensions of the time for compliance with bankruptcy notices under s 41(6B) to be made ex parte. Often, I am told, the solicitor for the debtor will attend before a registrar in his or her office. In the present case, as I have noted, it appears there was no appearance at all, and the order was made by way of an internal administrative decision.

10. Section 41(6B) provides:

"Where, before the expiration of the time fixed by the

Registrar for compliance with the requirements of a bankruptcy

notice:

(a) proceedings to set aside the judgment or order in

respect of which the bankruptcy notice was issued have

been instituted by the debtor; or

(b) an application to set aside the bankruptcy notice has

been filed with the Registrar;

the Registrar may, subject to subsection (6C), extend the time

for compliance with the bankruptcy notice."

11. In my opinion, time for compliance with a bankruptcy notice should not, as a general rule, be extended without notice to the judgment creditor.

12. Much of the reasoning which leads me to this conclusion is contained in a passage from the judgment of Lockhart J in Re Sterling [1980] FCA 61; (1980) 30 ALR 77 at 82-84 in which his Honour discusses the nature of applications to extend time for compliance and the connection of such applications with the setting aside of the notice itself:

"Notwithstanding that the filing of the application to set

aside the bankruptcy notice within the specified time is a

condition precedent to the exercise of the court's power to

extend time, and is perhaps a ground for granting the

extension, in truth the power to extend time is in aid of the

setting aside of the notice itself. Unless the court is to

hear the application to set aside the notice, there is no

purpose to be served in having power to extend time for

compliance with the requirements of the notice. I leave

aside, of course, the power conferred by s 41(6A)(a).

This court, and the Federal Court of Bankruptcy before it,

have set aside bankruptcy notices over many years on various

grounds. Instances of the exercise of this power are where

the notice is not in accordance with the terms of the judgment

and is calculated to perplex the debtor: the notice is issued

for the whole of a judgment debt, yet the judgment debtor has

paid into court in part satisfaction of the debt; in truth no

debt lies behind the judgment: Wilkinson v Osborne (1915) 21

CLR 89; and the sum of which payment is required by the terms

of a notice has in fact been paid by the debtor: Re

Partridge; Ex parte Maidens-Fuller (1945) 13 ABC 185.

In exercising the power to set aside notices the court has

been confronted with practical difficulties. If the

bankruptcy notice is, as it usually is, a 14 day, 21 or 28 day

notice, it may be inconvenient or impossible for the court to

hear and determine the application to set aside within the

time constraints imposed by the bankruptcy notice itself.

...

The question arises as to the circumstances in which the power

conferred by s 42(6A) will be exercised when application has

been made by a judgment debtor to set aside the bankruptcy

notice within the stipulated time. Plainly the word "may" in

sub-s (6A) (" ... the Court may ... extend the time for

compliance with the bankruptcy notice") is used in a

permissive or facultative sense: Ward v Williams (1955) 92

CLR 496.

I mentioned earlier some instances of the exercise of the

power to set aside bankruptcy notices by this court and the

Federal Court of Bankruptcy. They are not exhaustive of the

occasions on which the power will be exercised. The power of

the court to extend time for compliance with the requirements

of a bankruptcy notice is in aide of the power to set aside

the notice itself. Once the court has heard the application

to set aside the notice it may dismiss it or set the notice

aside. In either case, exercise of the power to extend time

for compliance will then be spent.

It is neither necessary nor desirable for me to state

exhaustively the matters which an applicant for extension of

time must show to obtain an order for extension. This will

depend on the facts of each case. Orders for extension of

time will not be made as of course. Grounds must be

established. It is as well to remember that the power to set

aside the notice and the power to extend time for compliance

are necessarily related. For instance, if the application to

set aside is made on the basis that no debt lies behind the

judgment, the court may conduct a preliminary enquiry as to

whether it should go behind the judgment. This approach is

taken sometimes on the hearing of petitions for sequestration

where the debtor alleges that he is not indebted to the

petitioning creditor notwithstanding the judgment: see Corney

v Brien [1951] HCA 31; (1951) 84 CLR 343; Wren v Mahoney [1972] HCA 5; (1972) 126 CLR 212;

[1972] HCA 5; (1972) ALR 307, and Re Wong; Ex parte Kitson (1979) 27 ALR

405.

There are sound reasons why substantially the same

considerations should apply in dealing with applications to

set aside bankruptcy notices. There may be cases where the

application to set aside should be adjourned pending the

outcome of an application by the debtor to a court of

competent jurisdiction to set aside the judgment on which the

bankruptcy notice is founded.

In other cases, the debtor may seek an adjournment of the

application to set aside pending the outcome of an appeal from

the judgment. I respectfully agree with the observations of

C A Sweeney J in Lipov's case (24 ALR at 620) on this matter.

Although what was said by his Honour was referrable to the Act

before the 1980 amendments, it is equally applicable to the

power conferred by s 41(6A).

As the power to extend time for compliance with the

requirements of a bankruptcy notice is in aid of the power to

set aside the notice itself, generally the two will go hand in

hand. For instance if the application to set aside is made on

the basis that no debt lies behind the judgment and the court

decides to hear the dispute as to the existence of the debt it

may give directions as to interlocutory matters and fix the

hearing of the application to set aside at some future date;

but in the meantime extend time for compliance with the

notice."

13. His Honour was there concerned with sub-s (6A) of s 41, but there is no relevant distinction between that subsection and sub-s (6B) with which the present case is concerned.

14. Extension of time for compliance with a bankruptcy notice may have important adverse consequences for the judgment creditor. For example, if a sequestration order is subsequently made the commencement of the bankruptcy may be later than would otherwise have been the case, which in turn may affect rights of recovery by the trustee in relation to property.

15. Moreover, as Lockhart J notes, the power to extend time is a discretionary one and is not to be exercised as of course. It may be, for example, that it can be pointed out that the application to set aside the bankruptcy notice is on the face of it doomed to failure, and therefore no useful purpose would be served by extending the time for compliance. And even where it is appropriate to extend time, the period of such extension is a question on which the judgment creditor has an interest. All these considerations point to a conclusion that the judgment creditor ought to have the opportunity of being heard in opposition to an application to extend the time for compliance.

16. In the absence of some circumstances of urgency, and none were suggested in the present case, granting liberty to apply is not an adequate substitute for the basic right of litigants to be heard in opposition to the making of an order which may affect their interests.

17. In any case, it seems to me that the Bankruptcy Rules, and in particular r 104, have the effect that an application of this sort ought to be served on a person affected.

18. I conclude therefore that the order extending time should not have been made ex parte. The merits of the application to extend time having been debated before me, I therefore shall now consider whether, looking at the matter afresh, I should grant an extension.

19. In my opinion, an extension should not be granted. The debtor does not raise any of the usual grounds on which a bankruptcy notice may be set aside, namely "the form or content of the notice itself, service of the notice or the existence of the debt upon which the judgment and in turn the notice is founded": Re Briggs (1986) 12 FCR 310 at 312 per Toohey J.

20. The whole tenor of the debtor's application to the Hardship Board makes it clear that the debtor is in fact insolvent and is not able to pay the judgment creditor's debt unless granted an indulgence by the Board. Therefore, it seems to me this is not an appropriate case for interfering with the ordinary mechanism of the bankruptcy notice, which is a mode of proof designed to enable a creditor to prove that a debtor is insolvent.

21. When the creditor's petition comes on for hearing it would be open to the debtor to apply for the hearing of the petition to be adjourned pending a determination by the Hardship Board. In the event that the Hardship Board has already heard the matter and acceded to the debtor's application, the debtor could ask the Court to dismiss the petition on the ground that the creditor has no continuing debt. I should make it clear that I am not of course making any comment as to the merits of such applications or giving any indication as to whether I think they should or should not be acceded to. I mention these eventualities as matters which make it clear that the kind of discretion with which the Hardship Board would be concerned should not affect the operation of the bankruptcy notice according to its terms. For those reasons the judgment creditor's application will be allowed.

22. The question arises as to the appropriate form of order which should now be made. Counsel for the judgment creditor argued that the order of the deputy registrar should be rescinded and that there should be a declaration that the debtor has committed an act of bankruptcy on 12 December 1994. He referred to s 37 of the Act, which confers a power on the Court to "rescind, vary or discharge" the deputy registrar's order. He argued that recision was appropriate because the order should never have been made and the rights and obligations of the parties should be reinstated to what they would have been had the order not been made.

23. The difficulty I have with that argument is that it would operate to change rights and obligations retrospectively . The general rule is that an order of a court regular on its face is to be obeyed unless and until lawfully set aside. In Hadkinson v Hadkinson (1952) 2 All ER 567 at 569 Romer LJ said:

"It is the plain and unqualified obligation of every person

against, or in respect of, whom an order is made by a court of

competent jurisdiction to obey it unless and until that order

is discharged. The uncompromising nature of this obligation

is shown by the fact that it extends even to cases where the

person affected by an order believes it to be irregular or

even void. LORD COTTENHAM, LC, said in Chuck v Cremer (1

Coop temp Cott 342):

'A party, who knows of an order, whether null or valid,

regular or irregular, cannot be permitted to disobey it

... It would be most dangerous to hold that the

suitors, or their solicitors, could themselves judge

whether an order was null or valid - whether it was

regular or irregular. That they should come to the

court and not take upon themselves to determine such a

question. That the course of a party knowing of an

order, which was null or irregular, and who might be

affected by it, was plain. He should apply to the court

that it might be discharged. As long as it existed it

must not be disobeyed.'"

24. Here the order of the deputy registrar was one on its face made by an officer having jurisdiction to make such orders. From the time it was made persons affected had to treat it as valid. From 8 December 1994 until today the position has been that the debtor could avoid committing an act of bankruptcy if by 20 February 1995 he paid or secured payment of the judgment debt or compounded it to the satisfaction of the judgment creditor. It is not to the point that the debtor in fact could not have paid or secured payment of the debt and that it is unlikely the judgment creditor would have agreed to a composition of the debt.

25. In the circumstances, the appropriate course is to vary rather than rescind the order. I will therefore vary the order so that the time for compliance with the bankruptcy notice is extended until four p.m. today, 12 January 1995. There will be an order that the debtor pay the judgment creditor's costs of the application, including reserved costs. The same orders will be made in VN 2837 of 1994
