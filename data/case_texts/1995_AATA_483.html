REASONS FOR DECISION



31 March 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



  1. The applicant is a manufacturer and importer of cosmetics and fragrances and a manufacturer of toiletries. It sells its products almost exclusively by wholesale to a variety of retail outlets. During the assessment period, these goods were sold FOB. Insurance, freight and administration was arranged where requested by a wholly owned subsidiary which I will call Services Pty Limited. Goods were distributed from a central store, owned and operated by another wholly owned subsidiary which I will call Warehouse Pty Limited.
  2. Following investigations, an assessment was raised under the Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) 1930 against the applicant. The amount of the assessment totalled $622,649. Additional tax of $249,060 was also assessed. The basis upon which the assessment was raised and its consequential validity were the subject of some contention before me. I will return to this subject later. It is sufficient to explain for present purposes that the figures in the assessment represented the difference between service fees charged by Services Pty Limited to the retail customers and the amount of freight paid by Services Pty Limited to certain freight contractors. The applicant objected to the assessment on various grounds and this application is brought to review the objection decision which affirmed the assessment.
  3. Prior to 1975, the applicant sold goods to its customers on a free into store basis. The cost of freight, insurance and administration formed part of the purchase price of the goods and title passed upon receipt of the goods into the customers’ stores.
  4. In late 1975, the applicant altered its trading terms to provide that it sold goods on an ex warehouse basis. Services Pty Limited was established to be responsible for offering a range of services to the applicant’s customers at the customers’ option, including delivery of goods from the applicant’s then warehouse in a suburb of Sydney. Included among the services offered was an obligation to undertake certain advertising. The net commercial effect of the arrangement was that the customers continued to pay the same net price as previously. The invoice cost of the goods, however, was lowered to about 87 per cent of the former price and a service fee of approximately 13 per cent was charged. Evidence was tendered from some of these retailers to indicate that they understood and accepted these terms.
  5. In 1980, the applicant commenced to warehouse its products in and distributed them from Canberra. This change was made to avoid ongoing union demarcation disputes at the then existing Sydney warehouse. From that time, the operation of the warehousing facility, including the employment of warehouse staff was and continues to be the responsibility of Warehouse Pty Limited. That company operates from a building owned by the applicant, although costs relating to the building are allocated to and borne by Warehouse Pty Limited. Warehouse Pty Limited contracts with the freight contractors. In doing so, it describes itself as distributor of the applicant’s products. Notwithstanding that the legal contract for freight is between Warehouse Pty Limited and the freight contractors, invoices for the freight element of the purchase consideration are despatched in the name of Services Pty Ltd.
  6. During the assessment period (1 March 1984 to 31 May 1992) the applicant negotiated with various freight forwarders with respect to their proposed rates for delivery of goods to the retail customers and then arranged for Warehouse Pty Limited to contract with these freight forwarders. One principal contractor provided parcel delivery services for cosmetics and fragrances. The other principal contractor arranged bulk haulage for toiletries. After the goods were transported to the Canberra warehouse, they continued to remain the property of the applicant until they were loaded on to the vehicle which was to effect delivery from the warehouse to the customers.
  7. During the assessment period, the following trading terms were printed on the reverse side of the applicant’s invoices.

“1

Property in the goods shall pass to the customer at the time when the goods are placed on the vehicle which is to effect delivery from the store or warehouse of [the applicant] or any other store or warehouse from which the goods are despatched to the customer and shall thereafter be at the customers’ risk.”

  1. The trading terms offered by Services Pty Limited included the following clauses —

“1



in consideration of a service fee [Services Pty Limited] shall ... provide the service to the customer in respect of the goods purchased from [the applicant].

2

“The service” when used in these conditions of service comprise at the customer’s option —

(a) on the customer’s behalf taking possession of the goods sold to the customer by [the applicant]

(b) on the customer’s behalf insurance of the goods from the moment property in the goods passes to the customer until they reach the customer’s premises; and

(c) delivery of the goods to the customer or as the customer directs.

3

If any of the goods are returned to [the applicant] with the authorisation of [the applicant] [Services Pty Limited] agrees to credit the customer with the amount of the service fee charged in respect of those goods.

4

Payment of service fee shall be made within 30 days and cheques made payable to [name of group].

5

Irrespective of any terms and or conditions of service contained in a customer’s order form acceptance (by retaining the goods or any other method) of the goods ordered will be conclusive acceptance of the terms and conditions of service.”

  1. Customers that elected to have delivery of these goods carried out by Services Pty Limited were presented with a 3-part invoice, each part being separated by vertical perforated divisions. The first part of the invoice itemised the goods ordered and the price of those goods inclusive of sales tax. The second part of the document was the invoice from Services Pty Limited to the customer for the delivery of the goods. The third part of the document was the remittance advice which provided the customer with a cumulative total.
  2. Reference to these terms was also made in the standard credit application form used by the group throughout the assessment period. It included these words : “It is acknowledged that I am under no obligation to buy these services from [Services Pty Limited] and may terminate this agreement by giving 7 days written notice”. All new customers of the group executed this form during the assessment period. New stores for existing customers were not required to sign the application form.
  3. In 1982 the former arrangements for providing advertising were terminated and the service fee was correspondingly reduced. Thus throughout the assessment period, Services Pty Limited charged customers a service or delivery fee for the delivery service which was calculated as a percentage of the tax exclusive selling price of the goods sold by the applicant. From 1 March 1984 to 30 June 1985 this fee was set at 2 per cent of the selling price of the goods. In July 1985 it was raised to 2.8 per cent and remained at that level until 31 August 1990 when it reverted to 2 per cent. Evidence was given that the increase was intended to recover hidden overheads in administration, including costs incurred in arranging delivery, payment for insurance of goods in transit, invoicing and collecting the service fee and arranging delivery and crediting of returned goods. The evidence was that it was impossible to calculate these overhead costs accurately. I will deal with the insurance situation later in these reasons. The other costs were not separately recorded.
  4. The evidence also was that in accordance with advice the company received from its sales tax advisors, the service fee was reduced to the original level of 2 per cent from 1 September 1990 where it remained until the end of the assessment period on 31 May 1992. There was no evidence that any extraneous factors caused the service fee to be reduced to its original level. There was no evidence to explain why, if the service fee had been considered to be at an appropriate level prior to 31 May 1992, it was no longer considered to be at an appropriate level after that date. The only evidence put before me as the cause of the change was the advice to which I have referred.
  5. The calculation of the service fee was made in advance of, and in anticipation of, the likely costs to be incurred over the period during which it applied. The percentage was such so as to at least recoup the estimated costs associated with providing the delivery service to customers. The service fee did not necessarily reflect the actual cost of delivery of the goods in any particular instance. It was not calculated by reference to weight or distance to be travelled. It was calculated as a uniform percentage rate, so that delivery costs would be known to the customer and would be easily calculable. It was the evidence of the General Manager, Finance and Administration, of the applicant that bearing in mind the variety of the applicant’s customers in terms of size, location and the number of retail outlets, any other basis for calculation of the fee would be disproportionately complex.
  6. Economies were said to have been achieved in 2 ways. Firstly, the fact that only 2 carriers effectively distributed the goods meant that customers did not have to negotiate with a variety of carriers and were able to take advantage of economies of scale. Secondly, economies were achieved through centralised distribution. At the warehouse in Canberra parcels of cosmetic goods were loaded on to separate pallets for each State. The carrier then collected, arranged and consolidated the freight into south bound line haul, north bound line haul and local freight. The interstate depot of the freight forwarder received and then re-organised the freight from large trucks to smaller delivery trucks which delivered to the customers’ store. Deliveries of toiletries were similarly made from the Canberra warehouse by bulk freight direct to the interstate grocery warehouses of the applicant’s customers.
  7. Because the group was able to offer a high volume of freight to carriers it was said that it could obtain a lower freight rate than that available to individual customers. In addition the decentralised location of the warehouse in Canberra was strategically useful to freight forwarders. In particular, the group was able to take advantage of the excess back loading capacity in Canberra. This capacity resulted from the imbalance of freight going into Canberra compared to the smaller amount of freight coming from Canberra. As a consequence of these factors, the group was able to obtain up to 70 or 80 per cent off scheduled rates offered by freight forwarding companies.
  8. Evidence was given by an expert in the transport industry who was asked to analyse likely arms length freight charges and compare them with the service fees that had been charged by Services Pty Limited. As a mean average during the assessment period, he considered transactions in the April-May 1989 and April-May 1990 periods. He also did separate calculations in relation to parcel freight purchases and bulk clients.
  9. His evidence was that the general freight rates quoted by carriers are nominal and rarely correspond to the actual freight rates charged. The true figure depends upon the extent of the discount one is able to achieve. From calculations which he made, the witness was able to offer an opinion that in the parcel freight area the cost of transporting a carton of 8 kilograms would range between $23-05 and $38-39, depending upon the extent of the discount a particular retail customer was able to negotiate with the carrier. He concluded from an examination of the documents that a typical charge made by Services Pty Limited for the same type of delivery was $21. Both figures referred to a hypothetical Canberra to Brisbane delivery route. On a Sydney route, corresponding figures were between $13 and $18-75 on the one hand and $8 as a typical charge by Services Pty Limited. These 2 routes are major carriage routes for freight forwarders and therefore are more economical operations for the freight forwarding companies, who are therefore able to offer the level of discounts to which he referred. Deliveries to customers in country areas outside of metropolitan destinations will incur extra costs of approximately double that of metropolitan delivery. This is not only because country locations involve additional distance but, as well, multiple handling of the goods is required at the interstate depots of the freight forwarder. More importantly the volume of overall freight business in these routes is significantly less.
  10. The expert agreed in cross-examination that the advantages of back loading from Canberra would be available to any purchaser and that the real advantage of central warehousing was the size of the transactions and the fact that the operations were from a single place. It would not matter if the warehouse was based in Sydney or Melbourne.
  11. Throughout the assessment period it was a term and condition of Services Pty Limited’s trading terms that that company would refund customers the delivery fee charged for goods which were subsequently returned by them with the authorisation of the applicant. In these circumstances it was proposed that Services Pty Limited would bear the costs of the cartage fees incurred. In practice, however, Services Pty Limited made neither a profit nor a loss. It had no staff or premises. No insurance policy was issued in its own name. It did have a bank account and financial transactions between that company and the applicant were by exchange of cheques. It was also the aim to make Warehouse Pty Limited a break even company. When goods were returned, the customer received a credit for freight directly from the applicant, even though the goods had been sold FOB. There appears to have been no continuing relationship between Services Pty Limited and the customer once the tripartite invoice in the name of Services Pty Limited had been sent to the customer. All subsequent arrangements were between the applicant and the customer. This pattern governed not only the provision of freight but also the arrangements for insurance.
  12. During the assessment period, Services Pty Limited offered to arrange insurance of the goods in transit for the customer. It obtained coverage by virtue of a worldwide marine policy effected by its American parent with an American company. The excess on that policy during the assessment was US$1,000. Breakages or damaged stock and goods lost in transit were credited to customers for the full amount involved. These claims for lost or damaged goods would often not exceed the excess under the policy and accordingly such losses were effectively self insured by the applicant. The evidence was that there were many occasions during the assessment period when credit was given for goods said to have been short shipped in circumstances where the applicant was unable to verify this claim and where no insurance was available. Because of the excess, there have been relatively few claims on the insurer. They are set out in exhibit B. There have, however, been many claims made on the applicant. Notwithstanding the fact that Services Pty Limited arranges insurance through the applicant’s umbrella policy, claims under the policy are made directly against the applicant and credits are arranged directly between the applicant and its customers. Returns of goods were taken up by the applicant as a debit against sales as if Services Pty Limited did not exist. There were no discussions with the customer concerning insurance and the customers were not advised of the identity of the insurer. Because the umbrella policy covered many risks it was not possible for Services Pty Limited to obtain from the underwriters a figure covering transit insurance alone. This is the reason why insurance was treated as a hidden overhead in calculation of the service fee. Declarations were sent regularly to the insurance company’s Australian representative which simply showed sales values. Premiums were calculated on the basis of these values. The Australian representative was not told in relation to each shipment who were the consignees or where the goods were to be sent.
  13. The evidence was that if the applicant’s umbrella insurance cover was not provided for customers, the cost of effecting separate insurance in relation to each delivery would be high. In the assessment period, the principal parcel freight forwarder charged 1.25 per cent on the value of goods for normal freight, excluding glass and fragile goods, and 6 or 7 per cent on the value of goods where there was no exclusion for glass or fragile goods.
  14. The General Manager, Finance and Administration of the applicant, who gave evidence on the hearing, was unaware of any customer exercising its option to collect the goods from the group’s warehouse and there is no reason to believe that this option was ever exercised by any customer, even if that customer was located near the Canberra warehouse. There was no question that all sales of goods during the assessment period were made to arm’s length customers. Those customers ranged from large department stores to individual pharmacies. Evidence from some of them was tendered to confirm their understanding of the formal arrangements. Clearly, however, from a commercial point of view all goods were sold in the ordinary course of business to unrelated customers. The evidence was that the provision of delivery and insurance services has at all times been optional and the unit price of stock offered to customers is unaffected by the exercise of that option. The group made a profit from the service charge because (it was said) it was efficient at arranging freight and insurance systematically for all or most of its customers in a coordinated, cheaper and more efficient way than could be achieved under any alternative method. Nevertheless it was agreed that most of the dollars of profit referred to in the assessment under review resulted from the period when the higher service fee of 2.8 per cent was being charged. The acknowledgment by the applicant that a profit was made from the service fees and the reasons submitted for appropriateness of that profit are difficult to reconcile with other evidence, namely, that the service fee was set at a level sufficient to cover the outgoings to which it referred and no more. By and large the service fee, when at the lower level, resulted in a loss on bulk freight and a profit on parcels. The evidence indicates to me however that such a loss or profit occurred by chance. The service fee appears to have been calculated without reference in individual cases to the costs of bulk freight or parcel freight. If profits or losses resulted they were not directly referable to the level of service fee charged.

VALIDITY OF THE ASSESSMENT

  1. An assessment is defined in s 3(1) as follows —

“ “assessment” means —

(a) the ascertainment of the sale value of goods and of the sales tax payable on that sale value; or

(b) the ascertainment of additional tax payable under Part VIII.”
  1. An assessment is not a written document although some documents are often loosely referred to as assessments. An assessment, as can be seen, is a process of ratiocination. In Batagol v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243 Kitto J explained that service of the notice of assessment on the taxpayer fixes the ascertainment of the amount of the taxable income and the amount of the tax payable by the taxpayer and brings to an end the process of assessment. The document bringing to an end the process of assessment in the present case was in the following terms —

[omitting details of the office of origin and the name and address of the applicant]

“SALES TAX ASSESSMENT ACT (NO.1) 1930, AS AMENDED

You are hereby notified that you are liable to pay sales tax as shown hereunder in accordance with an assessment that has been made under the provisions of sub-section 25(2) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) 1930, as amended.



Assessment under sub-section 25(2) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) 1930, as amended.

In respect of the alteration of the sale value of cosmetic and toiletry goods sold during the period 1 March 1984 to 31 May 1992 inclusive.



Rate of Tax 20% 30% 32.5%

of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1)

The amount of $249,060.00 additional tax is also due and payable on 1 October 1992.

NOTE :-



A taxpayer who is dissatisfied with an assessment may, within 60 days after the service on the taxpayer of the assessment lodge with the Commissioner an objection in writing against the assessment. Notwithstanding any objection, Section 43(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) 1930, as amended, requires that the full amount must be paid within the time stated.”

  1. The fact that it refers to itself as an assessment, the fact that it refers to the basis under which it was made as being sub-section 25(2) and the fact that it refers to additional tax as having been assessed under s 46 have given rise to arguments directed to the validity of the assessment. In order to consider the submissions that were made, it is necessary first to decide under what basis the assessment was made and the notice of assessment given.
  2. The relevant parts of s 18A are as follows —

“SECTION 18A SALE VALUE OF GOODS IN SPECIAL CASES

18A(1)

Where —

(a) goods (in this section referred to as the “relevant goods”), not being goods treated by the manufacturer as stock for sale by retail, have been sold by the manufacturer to an unregistered person or to a registered person who has not quoted his certificate in respect of the sale;

(b) under an agreement entered into for the purpose, or for purposes that included the purpose, of securing that the amount of the sale value of the relevant goods would be less than the amount that could reasonably be expected to be the amount of the sale value of the relevant goods if the agreement had not been entered into, valuable consideration (in this section referred to as the “relevant consideration”) has been given directly or indirectly, by the purchaser, or by another person, to the manufacturer or another person for, or in connection with, any of, or any 2 or more of, the following acts:

....................

(vi)

the provision of, or procuring the provision of, services in connection with the relevant goods; and

(c) the relevant goods were sold —
  • (i) in a case where the relevant consideration has been so given, in whole or in part, for, or in connection with, any of, or any 2 or more of, any acts referred to in sub-paragraphs (b)(i) to (v) (inclusive) — after 20 September 1978; or
  • (ii) if sub-paragraph (i) does not apply but the relevant consideration has been given for, or in connection with, any acts referred to in sub-paragraph (b)(vi) — after 16 November 1978,

the sale value of the relevant goods shall, for the purposes of this Act, be determined in accordance with the provisions of this section and not in accordance with the provisions of sub-section 18(1), (1B), (1C), (3A), or (4):

....................

18A(3)

For the purposes of sub-section (1), where any valuable consideration given under an agreement entered into for the purpose, or for purposes that included the purpose, of securing that the amount of the sale value of the relevant goods would be less than the amount that could reasonably be expected to be the amount of the sale value of the relevant goods if the agreement had not been entered into is required to be calculated by reference to the value or quantity of goods (which may include excluded goods) sold to the purchaser of the relevant goods, the valuable consideration shall be deemed to have been given under an agreement for or in connection with the doing of acts by way of the provision of services in connection with the relevant goods.

18A(4)

Subject to subsections (5) and (6), for the purposes of this Act, the sale value of goods the sale value of which is required to be determined in accordance with this section is the amount for which the goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold by the manufacturer by wholesale if no agreement of a kind referred to in paragraph (1)(b) had been entered into in relation to the sale of the goods.

....................

18A(6)

Subject to sub-section (5), where —

(a) the sale value of the relevant goods is required to be determined, for the purposes of this Act, in accordance with the provisions of this section;

(b) the Commissioner is satisfied that, having regard to any connection between the manufacturer and the purchaser of the relevant goods or to any other relevant circumstances (including circumstances arising out of any agreement entered into between the manufacturer and the purchaser, or out of any other agreement, that is related, directly or indirectly, to the sale of the relevant goods), the manufacturer and the purchaser were not dealing with each other at arm’s length in relation to the transaction; and

(c) the Commissioner is also satisfied —
  • (i) that the amount for which the relevant goods were sold is less than the amount (in this section referred to as the “arm’s length price”) for which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, the relevant goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold if the manufacturer and the purchaser had been dealing with each other at arm’s length in relation to the transaction and no agreement of a kind referred to in paragraph (1)(b) had been entered into in relation to the sale of the relevant goods; or
  • (ii) that —

(A)

the purchaser could have purchased identical goods from another manufacturer and obtained delivery of the identical goods at or about the time when the purchaser obtained delivery of the relevant goods; and

(B)

the amount for which the relevant goods were sold is less than the amount (in this section referred to as the “alternative price”) for which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, the identical goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold to the purchaser if no agreement of a kind referred to in paragraph (1)(b) had been entered into in relation to the sale of the identical goods,

the Commissioner shall alter the sale value of the relevant goods to the amount ascertained in accordance with sub-section (7), and the sale value so altered shall be the sale value of the relevant goods for the purposes of this Act.

....................

18A(8)

For the purposes of this section, an agreement shall be taken to have been entered into for a particular purpose, or for purposes that include a particular purpose, if any of the parties to the agreement entered into the agreement for that purpose or for purposes that included that purpose.

....................

18A(10)

In this section, “agreement” means any agreement, arrangement or understanding —

(a) whether formal or informal;

(b) whether express or implied; or

(c) whether or not enforceable; or intended to be enforceable, by legal proceedings.”
  1. I am satisfied that the agreements between the applicant and the retail purchasers were entered into for a purpose that included the purpose of securing that the amount of the sale value of the relevant goods would be less than the amount that could reasonably be expected to be the amount of the sale value of the relevant goods if the agreement had not been entered into. That purpose was the applicant’s purpose. It is not necessary, in view of sub-section (8), to find that the retail customers, or any of them, had a corresponding purpose. I find that the applicant had the purpose on the basis of evidence that was given to me that the structure was adopted after advice from sales tax experts, and that the service fees were charged according to rates recommended by those experts from time to time. Indeed the General Manager, Finance and Administration of the applicant clearly agreed in cross-examination that one of the general purposes of the arrangement was to reduce the overall burden of sales tax.
  2. It was submitted for the applicant that the purpose was no more than to avoid sales tax on the value of services provided and that the applicant was doing no more than taking advantage of the options discussed in Commonwealth Quarries (Footscray) Pty Ltd v The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 13; 59 CLR 111 and applied, for example, in Case Z29, 92 ATC 267 . Whether or not there was such a purpose the fact is, and I so find, that the applicant also had a purpose falling within the terms of s 18A(1)(b). The use of the word “included” in that paragraph indicates that it is not necessary for the purpose to be the dominant purpose in order to enliven the paragraph. Accordingly the sale value of the goods is to be determined in accordance with s 18A rather than in accordance with s 18. The question to be decided is whether sub-section (4) or (6) properly grounds the assessment in the present circumstances. There is no dispute that consideration was given by the purchasers to Services Pty Limited in connection with the provision of or procuring the provision of services in connection with the relevant goods.
  3. In view of my finding as to purpose, the sale value, therefore, is required to be found under sub-section (4) or is required to be altered by the Commissioner under sub-section (6). The latter sub-section requires that there be a non arm’s length relationship between the applicant and the retail purchasers. It is clear from the evidence that this was not the case. It follows that an alteration of sale value under sub-section (6) would not have been appropriate. Had it been so, the Commissioner could not have served a valid s 12B notice (to which reference will later be made) until the sale value had been altered.
  4. The notice of assessment directs one initially to a consideration that the assessment process may have been under sub-section (6). This is because of its reference to s 25(2) and to s 46.

“s25(1)

Section 25 is in the following terms —

Where the Commissioner finds in any case that tax or further tax is payable by a person, the Commissioner may make an assessment in relation to the person.

s 25(2A)

s25(2)

Where, under sub-section 18(3A) or (4) or 18A(5) or (6), the sale value of any goods has been altered, the Commissioner shall make an assessment in relation to those goods.

Where :

s25(2A)

a person makes a default in furnishing a return;

the Commissioner is not satisfied with a return furnished by a person; or

the Commissioner has reason to believe or suspect that a person (although not having furnished a return) is liable to pay sales tax,

the Commissioner may determine an amount to be the amount upon which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, sales tax should be paid and may make an assessment in relation to the person.”

  1. Section 46 is in these terms —

“s46

Where, under sub-section 25(2), the Commissioner has made an assessment in relation to a person in consequence of an alteration of the sale value of goods (other than an alteration made under sub-section 18(3A), the person is liable to pay additional tax by way of penalty, equal to double the amount of the difference between the tax properly payable and the tax that would have been payable if the sale value concerned had not been altered.”

  1. If the assessment were made under sub-section 25(2) it could only have been as a result of an alteration of value under sub-section (6). As I have held that that sub-section is not available to the Commissioner on the evidence before me, then either the assessment was made under sub-section 25(1) or if made under sub-section 25(2) was invalid.
  2. Counsel for the applicant submitted that the Commissioner was fixed with the references in the notice of assessment to the fact that the assessment had been made under sub-section 25(2). He said that was reinforced by the reference to s 46, which relates to penalty tax where certain anti-avoidance provisions apply. If, it was submitted, the assessment was authorised under sub-section (4) then additional tax would have been assessed under s 45. The fact that the Commissioner has made reference to s 46, it was submitted, fixed the Commissioner with responsibility for this source of power for his assessment. The combination of a primary assessment being made under sub-section 25(2) and additional tax being assessed under s 46 would render the assessment invalid (the submission continued) as it would be necessary to such an assessment that the pre-conditions of sub-section 18A(6) be established, and on the evidence before the Tribunal as I have held, this is not the case.
  3. Counsel for the applicant relied upon observations of Kitto J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105 where His Honour said at 116 —

“....where there are two provisions of an assessment Act, each giving the Commissioner a power to make an assessment, and each creating a liability to tax in the event of the power it confers being exercised, an assessment made in exercise only of the power given by one of those sections can not be supported as effective under the other.”

  1. This passage was quoted with approval by Wilcox J in Magna Stic Magnetic Signs Pty Limited and Anor v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 5000 at 5010, an observation that was not disapproved on appeal to a Full Court, which is reported at 91 ATC 4216. The observations of Kitto J however were obiter and had no application to the case before him as His Honour makes clear later on the same page. The passage is a statement of his understanding of other observations made by Latham CJ and Starke J in Danmark Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1944) 7 ATD 333 at 344 and 352. In the light of later authority to which I will refer, it seems to me doubtful that the above passage can be accepted as a statement of universal application.
  2. Counsel for the applicant submitted that there were at least 3 separate powers to raise assessments, namely, sub-sections 25(1), 25(2) and 25(2A). As the Commissioner has purported to give a notice of assessment under sub-section 25(2), it was said that he can now no longer justify his assessment by reference to one of the other enabling sub-sections.
  3. In reply, counsel for the Commissioner produced and tendered in evidence a copy of the notice of assessment certified as a true copy by a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation. The effect of this was to enliven s 67(1) which is in the following terms —

“s67(1)

The mere production of —

(a) a notice of an assessment or of the making of a refund decision; or

(b) a document signed by the Commissioner, a Second Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner and purporting to be a copy of a notice of an assessment or of the making of a refund decision,

is conclusive evidence —

(c) of the due making of the assessment or the refund decision;

(d) in the case of a notice of an assessment — except in proceedings under Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 on a review or an appeal relating to the assessment, that the amounts and all of the particulars of the assessment are correct; and

(e) in the case of a notice of a refund decision — except in proceedings under Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 on a review or appeal relating to the decision, that the decision is correct.”
  1. This section corresponds with s 177(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act which was considered by the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Prestige Motors Pty Limited [1994] HCA 39; 123 ALR 306. The court there was concerned particularly with the question whether s 177(1) required that the notice of assessment state the name of the taxpayer. However, their Honours also appear to accept (at 311) the submission of the Commissioner that as there is no prescribed form of a notice of assessment, the only requirements of a valid notice are that the amount of taxable income, or net income in the case of a trustee, be specified in the notice, that the amount of the tax payable be specified also, and that the notice be served on the person assessed to tax.
  2. As I understand the judgment, those concepts readily translate to notices of assessment of sales tax. The fact that the notice of assessment states material other than the amount of the sales value, and the amount of the sales tax, does not affect the notice of assessment provided that it is served on the person assessed to tax. In particular, the reference to the assumed basis upon which the assessment is raised and the reference to the assumed basis upon which additional tax is assessed are mere surplusage which neither add to, nor detract from, the essential validity of the assessment, even if that additional material is misleading. A notice of assessment, therefore, is a bare bones document containing only the essential requirements to which I have referred. This view is consistent with the views expressed by Purvis J in Case X45 90 ATC 364 at 376 and 377. It is also consistent with the observations of Toohey J in Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Richard Walter Pty Limited [1995] HCA 23; 127 ALR 21 at 65. Dissenting from the earlier views of Hill J in Copperart Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 4779, Toohey J considered that an adjustment sheet which accompanied a notice of assessment was not part of that notice of assessment. The Commissioner is not obliged, as part of his statutory duty, to give anything other than the minimum information to which I have referred. The Commissioner may of course be ordered to give particulars of the basis of his assessment in proceedings where the assessment is challenged (Bailey v Federal Commissioner of Taxation ([1977] HCA 11; 1977) 136 CLR 214). He is not obliged by statute, however, to give those particulars as part of the notice of assessment. Consequently references to sections purporting to indicate the basis upon which the Commissioner arrived at the assessment will not preclude the Commissioner from relying upon any other basis of validity. This accords with the view of a Full Court of the Federal Court in Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 103 ALR 156 at 164. If this is difficult to reconcile with what was said in Wade, it seems to me, as much more recent authorities, that the above views are to be preferred.
  3. Officers of the respondent may have thought that they were relying on certain sections of the Assessment Act to justify their process of assessment and the subsequent notice. The evidence before me was equivocal on this subject. The Manager, Large Case Audit, whose name appears on the relevant notice of assessment gave evidence before me. The audit and calculations were carried out by 2 other officers who were answerable to him. They signed an internal document (T 3-3) which was later replicated in the notice of assessment. The officer who gave evidence and who gave his name and authority to the notice of assessment, said that he took no part in the assessment process. He signed the internal document because he had confidence in his officers and because their delegated authority did not extend to the preparation of an assessment of this magnitude. For what it was worth, he said in evidence that had he turned his mind to the construction of the assessment he would have relied upon the sections stated in the notice of assessment. His evidence was not helpful in understanding the logical processes of the assessors. I consider, in any event, that there is no role for evidence of this nature to play. Assessments and notices of assessment have necessarily a statutory finality which can not be influenced by the thought processes or consultations of officers involved in the development of the assessment process. Because of the provisions of s 67, the notice of assessment speaks for itself, whatever the views of those who were previously involved in its authorship.
  4. I consider, therefore, that the due making of the assessment for the amount stated above has been conclusively proved. The only issue to be determined is whether the assessment is excessive. The reference to “particulars of the assessment” in paragraph 67(1)(d) can not mean, in the light of Prestige Motors, a reference to surplus material beyond the basic necessities of the assessment. It must relate to matters such as the numbers and values of particular goods the subject of the assessment.
  5. It follows in my view that the notice of assessment and the preceding process of assessment were based upon appropriate sub-sections of the assessment Act namely, sub-section 18A(4) which enabled the Commissioner to determine the sale value of the goods and sub-section 25(1) which enabled him to make an assessment in which he concluded that further tax was payable. It also follows that notwithstanding the reference in the notice of assessment, the additional tax was assessed pursuant to authority contained in s 45(2). I will deal with the nature of the false and misleading statements required by that sub-section when I come to the question of additional tax.

THE SECTION 12B ARGUMENT



Section 12B of the Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934 is in the following terms —

“s 12B(1)



Where tax (not being tax paid prior to the commencement of this section) in respect of any transaction, act or operation, effected or done in relation to any goods has not been paid at the expiration of a period of three years from —

(a) where the tax is payable in respect of the importation of those goods — the date of the entry of those goods for home consumption; or

(b) in any other case — the close of the month in which the transaction, act or operation was effected or done,

the Commissioner may remit that tax unless he —

(c) has required payment of the tax prior to the expiration of that period; or

(d) is satisfied that the payment of the tax was avoided by fraud or evasion.”
  1. Although the section uses the word “may”, it was held by Hill J in Copperart at 4792 that Parliament intended s 12B to be mandatory and to operate as a release of a taxpayer once the 3 years had elapsed unless the circumstances were such as to come within the exclusions to the section. Unless those circumstances exist, in other words, the Commissioner is under an obligation to remit tax payable in respect of a period earlier than 3 years prior to the making of the necessary requirement to pay the tax.
  2. It was held by Hill J (and not disapproved by the Full Court in the appeal, reported at 94 ATC 4259) that it was not necessary for a s 12B notice to specify a dollar amount. At pages 4792-4793 His Honour said —

“The word “amount” appearing in s 12B(3) is perhaps ambiguous. It can refer to a money figure or it may be taken to refer merely to the fact that there is sales tax payable in some amount not yet quantified. Of the two alternatives I prefer the latter. To require the Commissioner to stipulate the precise figure of sales tax payable when that information may well be known only to the taxpayer and not disclosed to the Commissioner by the taxpayer, imposes in my view an unreasonable burden upon the Commissioner. It is also inconsistent with the explanatory memorandum to which I have already referred.”

  1. I accept the evidence of the applicant that there was no written communication between the Commissioner and the applicant concerning additional sales tax except for 2 letters sent respectively on 6 March 1987 and 19 December 1990. I also accept that there were many conversations between officers of the applicant and the respondent during the period leading up to the assessment and that in a general sort of way, officers of the applicant may have expected a notice of assessment in due course. Nevertheless oral communications of a vague or approximate nature are not, in my view, sufficient to amount to a requirement for the purposes of s 12B. The 2 letters are the only basis upon which the respondent can rely to show that such a requirement to pay was made.
  2. The 2 letters are in almost identical terms. As the second letter has an additional final paragraph, I will quote that one, omitting formal parts —

“SALES TAX

Information held in this Office discloses an underpayment of sales tax. For this reason, you are hereby required to furnish further or fuller returns and pay tax upon the sale value of cosmetics :-

(a) manufactured by the business; and
  • (i) sold;
  • (ii) treated as stock for sale by retail;
  • (iii) applied to own use.


(b) purchased free of tax by quotation of certificate; and
  • (i) sold;
  • (ii) applied to own use.


(c) Imported free of tax by quotation of certificate; and
  • (i) sold


(ii) applied to own use;

during the period December 1987 to December 1990. This should now be done.

It is pointed out that the Sales Tax Assessment Acts require a taxpayer to furnish a return and pay the tax payable under those acts within twenty-one days of the close of any month in which taxable transactions occur. For the purpose of this adjustment it will be satisfactory, however, if one return covering these transactions is furnished. A blank sales tax return is enclosed.

Section 12B of the Sales Tax Procedure Act authorizes the Commissioner to remit arrears of sales tax which is unpaid after the expiration of three years from the close of the month in which the taxable transactions, act or operation was effected unless —

(a) he has required payment of the tax within the three years specified; or

(b) he is satisfied that the payment of the tax was avoided by fraud or evasion.”
  1. It will be noted that the second letter refers to a period “December 1987 to December 1990”. The earlier letter, in almost identical terms, refers to a period “1 March 1984 to 31 January 1987”. Even if both letters are to be regarded as valid s 12B requirements, it is clear that there is a gap between demands made in respect of the period 31 January 1987 to an unspecified date in December 1987. Prior to the hearing before me this was recognised by the respondent. An indication was given that whatever the result, primary tax would be recalculated to exclude sales tax attributable to the months of February to November 1987 inclusive if the period was held to be relevant. No notice of amended assessment has, of course, issued.
  2. The terms of the letters may be contrasted with the terms of the requirement considered by Hill J in Copperart, the text of which is reproduced at 4792. In my view, neither letter amounts to a requirement complying with the with the terms of s 12B. There is no suggestion that the applicant ceased to file monthly returns showing what it believed to be the appropriate sale values. It was not until the Commissioner determined those sale values in accordance with the provisions of s 18A(4) that a different sale value (and the correct sale value as the Commissioner now contends) became known. It was not possible from the terms of the letter of 19 December 1990, or its predecessor, for the applicant to work out what the Commissioner would assess as the sale value.
  3. This is reinforced by the fact that as late as the time of hearing, it was still being suggested by counsel for the respondent (both orally and in writing) that the true sale value under sub-section (4) should be the FIS price, without any allowance being made for freight or any other outgoing. This would have resulted in a value considerably in excess of that shown on the notice of assessment. When questioned, that submission was not pressed. This is an indication, however, that the applicant could not have known, with even reasonable certainty, what the Commissioner intended to find as the true sale value. Until that finding was made, there was no way in which a statutory demand could be made. That demand did not come until the formal notice of assessment. It is clear from the terms of the judgment of Davies J in the Full Court in Copperart that a requirement would not have been made unless and until the Commissioner gave notice of the sale value which he assessed. Clearly neither of the letters to which I have referred comply with the requirements of s 12B(3). Although it was held at first instance in Copperart that the word “amount” appearing in s 12B(3) referred to some amount not yet quantified, it seems to me that the same considerations referred to by His Honour must compel a process of quantifying with some degree of certainty. It is not sufficient that the taxpayer knows the quantity and invoice price of goods. If there is a doubt as to the way in which sale value will ultimately be calculated, then it would impose an unreasonable burden upon the taxpayer to require it to intuit that value. It follows that the Commissioner is under an obligation to remit sales tax assessed in respect of any period prior to 4 September 1989 if it forms part of the tax referred to in the subject notice of assessment.

WHAT IS THE SALE VALUE?

  1. The sale value having been arrived at pursuant to s 18A(4), it is necessary to determine how much the goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold for if the agreement between the applicant and the 2 subsidiary companies had not been entered into, and had not the agreements between Services Pty Limited and the various retail purchasers existed. It was conceded that the total amounts paid by the purchasers would not, in the circumstances, represent the sale value of the goods. It is conceded that it was appropriate to allow the applicant a deduction from that gross sum an amount which had been paid to the cartage contractors by Services Pty Limited. Indeed that is the mathematical basis upon which the assessment was arrived at. It was submitted by the applicant that further deductions should be allowed for charges representing the cost to Services Pty Limited of insurance premiums, administration and other overhead costs. In my view, the applicant has not discharged its onus of proof of showing that the sales value is excessive in those respects. No evidence was offered concerning the amount of insurance premium paid to cover transit risks. I find it hard to accept that this could not have been separated out by the underwriter. It certainly could have been dealt with separately by Services Pty Limited. Similarly no satisfactory accounting was kept of overheads, perhaps for a simple reason. The evidence showed that although Services Pty Limited had separate functions to perform in the overall distribution of the goods, these services in fact were carried out, by and large, by the applicant. The actions of the parties were not in accordance with their documentation, a situation which has been observed to exist on many occasions (see, for example, Albion Hotel Pty Limited v The Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 4; 115 CLR 78 at 92).
  2. I am not satisfied that the service fees should be totally chargeable to the costs of distribution. The rate of fees appears to have been arrived at after consultation with sales tax advisors and not after an assessment of those distribution expenses. The fact that the service fee is expressed as a percentage of sales is, to my mind, evidence of the fact that it is unrelated to the actual costs of distribution, except coincidentally when dollar sums calculated by reference to the service fee are compared with selected dollar sums chargeable for freight. Certainly during the period when the service fees rose to 2.8 per cent and stayed at that level for some years before returning to 2 per cent, the service fees were calculated with even less reference to actual costs. The fees rose and fell almost entirely because of avoidance of sales tax considerations.
  3. It follows that in my view the Commissioner was justified in fixing the sale value in the way he did namely, by taking the gross amount paid by retail purchasers and reducing that by the amount paid by or on behalf of Services Pty Limited to the freight contractors. No other deduction is appropriate. The total amount, however, will be reduced having regard to my finding concerning the operation of s 12B.

ADDITIONAL TAXATION

  1. This Tribunal has jurisdiction under ss 14ZZ, 14ZQ and 14ZT of the Taxation Administration Act to review certain decisions. Excluded from that power are those decisions described in s 14ZT, relating to ineligible sales tax remission decisions. As I have held, the present additional tax was assessed under sub-section 45(2), the false statement required by the sub-section, being the false sales values attributed to the goods. The additional taxation was levied at the rate of 40 per cent but covered an assessment period of more than 8 years. Effectively, the additional tax was therefore less than 20 per cent per year and accordingly the provisions of paragraph 14ZT(1)(b) precludes this Tribunal from reviewing the appropriateness of that imposition.
  2. For these reasons the objection decision is varied in that the sales tax referable to the period prior to 4 September 1989 is remitted. In all other respects, the objection decision is affirmed.
