REASONS FOR DECISION

3 July 1990 Mr T E Barnett, Senior Member

The applicants in this case are partners in a business which is registered under the name of SSS. The nature of the business is registered as "sand contractors" hut the applicants gave evidence that the major activity of the business is the preparation of building sites and the delivery and compacting of sand as fill on the sites, mostly in the form of "housepads". About ten per cent of the business involves selling sand to customers as a separate commodity. As well as being the proprietors of SSS the applicants (who are husband and wife) are also directors of their family trustee company XY Pty Ltd.

Over the years it has been the practice of the applicants to obtain their sand supplies for SSS by means of an arrangement whereby XY Pty Ltd would purchase blocks of land containing sand deposits and then sell loads of sand to SSS as required during the course of its business. SSS would then successfully claim the expenses of buying sand from XY Pty Ltd as a tax deduction allowable for the purchase of raw materials in the income year during which the expenditure was incurred.

On the 24 March 1984 the applicants were proceeding with this arrangement when XY Pty Ltd signed an Offer and Acceptance to buy land at Banjup from ABC for the sum of $250,000. They paid a deposit of $25,000 in two instalments on the 24 and 27 (or 29) March 1984. A condition of the contract was "the removal of any mining claims". There were in fact two mining claims already registered on that property. The applicants would naturally be concerned about the possibility of the property being subject to a mining claim because, as they both explained in evidence, that could lead to an argument about the payment of compensation from the mining claim holder to the applicants which would in all probability require resolution in the courts. Should that happen the claim holder would be likely to seek an injunction to prevent the applicants using the sand pending the hearing and SSS's sand supplies could then be tied up for a lengthy period.

The vendors signed the Offer and Acceptance form on the 26 March 1984. The purchasers' representative is shown as a firm of lawyers. It is not clear at what stage the applicants discovered the existence of these mining claims and the timing of that discovery was in dispute. However on the 28 March the Mining Wardens Court considered the application to register those claims and, as the claimants did not appear, the Warden decided to recommend to the Minister that the claims should be refused.

The applicants heard of this recommendation soon afterwards (probably some time early in April) and sought advice from their accountant and their said lawyers. At this time a hew Deed of Purchase was drawn so that SSS was shown on a separate deed as purchasing the sand from the vendor ABC for the sum of $125,000 and XY Pty Ltd was shown on another deed as the purchaser of the land from the same vendor for the sum of $125,000, subject to the "irrevocable license to SSS to excavate and remove the sand." These documents were held in readiness in the hope that the Minister would accept the Warden's recommendation and refuse the mining claims on the property at Banjup. On 17 April 1984 the Minister did in fact refuse both claims. On the same day the applicants signed the deed to purchase the sand for SSS. On 18 April 1984 the applicants signed the land purchase deed on behalf of XY Pty Ltd. The settlement of both contracts occurred at the same time on 9 May 1984.

In their tax returns for the year 1983/84 the applicants claimed the $125,000 expended by SSS to purchase the sand as a deduction from their taxable income. They based this claim on the ground that it was an expense incurred in the purchase of raw materials and hence deductible under s51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (the Act) which is in the following terms:

51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.

51(2) Expenditure incurred or deemed to have been incurred in the purchase of stock used by the taxpayer as trading stock shall be deemed not to be an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature.

Mr O'Connor QC., for the applicants, argued that the amount was an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or that it was an amount necessarily incurred in carrying on the business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. He argued that the amount did not fall within the exception set out in sub section (1) as it was not an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature. It was, he said, incurred in the purchase of sand as a raw material.

Mr LeMiere for the respondent, argued that it was in fact money spent for the purchase of a capital asset. The sand was still attached to the land and as such was part of the land. Although SSS purported to buy that sand by the terms of the deed of the 17 April, what it in fact acquired, he argued, was a profit a prendre or a mere right to obtain the sand. Only after the sand was separated from the land would it become the property of the applicant. Being an expense of a capital nature it fell within the exception to s51(1) and was therefore not an allowable deduction. Mr LeMiere continued that if that submission was not accepted by the Tribunal then the respondent would assert, as a fall back position that the sand should be treated as stock in trade and this would result in a net reduction in taxable income only in relation to expenditure on sand actually used during the year of income. Finally he argued that if he was unsuccessful on both those points then, in any event, there was a tax avoidance scheme within the meaning of Part IVA of the Act and that, therefore, the expenditure should be disallowed as a deduction in the exercise of the discretion given to the Tribunal by sl77F.

The leading case on determining whether or not this sort of expenditure is of a capital nature is the United Kingdom case of Stow Bardolph Gravel Co Ltd v Poole (HM Inspector of Taxes)(1954) 3 All ER 637. That case involved the purchase of a sand and gravel deposit by means of a contract which was similar in all relevant respects to the deed signed by the applicants on 17 April 1984. The judge at first instance, Harman J, distinguished between expenditure incurred to acquire the means of obtaining sand from expenditure incurred to obtain the sand itself. He reviewed several authorities which dealt with sand/gravel mining expenditure which turned upon whether or not it had been necessary to mine or win the sand before loading it. In that case he found as a fact that the sand was merely lying on the ground available to be raked and loaded and that no winning or mining had been involved. On appeal however a unanimous Court of Appeal found that there was no evidence to support that finding of fact and, in the absence of evidence that the sand and gravel was already severed from the ground, the Court of Appeal applied the normal principle that sand and gravel in situ forms part of the land itself. Under the law governing the sale of real property the contract purporting to buy the sand and gravel was ineffective for that purpose and all that was actually acquired by the contract was the right to take the sand, which is a profit a prendre. The Court of Appeal held that it was, therefore, an expenditure of a capital nature. By implication and by way of dicta however the Court of Appeal (per Evershed M R at p639) approved the principle stated by Harman J that the sand would have been treated as stock in trade (or raw materials) had it not been necessary to mine or win it from the ground.

In order to show that their case fell within that principle the applicants requested the Tribunal to view the operation in progress on the property at Banjup to judge how much "winning" is involved in acquiring the sand; and this was done. After examining photographs of the property before the operation began and comparing them with more recent photographs, and by obtaining a view of the operation, the Tribunal has been able to get a clear impression of how the property appeared at the date of purchase and how the operation to obtain the sand has proceeded.

At the time of the purchase the major feature of this property was a large and extensive hillside which at that stage was covered with grass, bushy scrub and some larger trees. In order to obtain the sand from this natural hillside it was first necessary for the applicants to construct a road to the site and to obtain council approval to commence excavating. They then knocked down the larger trees and scraped away some of the scrub on a portion of the hillside. They then caused the topsoil on that portion to be removed and stockpiled for the purpose of renovating the site after the conclusion of operations, as was required of them by the terms of the council approval. The next step was to commence cutting into the base of the hillside with a frontend loader and loading the material obtained onto trucks. This material consisted of the sand which made up the hillside together with such grass, bushy scrub, roots and topsoil as fell to the base of the hillside when the blade of the frontend loader cut in and undermined it. As the frontend loader continued to cut into the base of the hillside in this fashion the sand continued to fall to the base of the hill and this created a face into the side of the hill which became higher and steeper as the cutting in or "undermining" process continued. By the time of the Tribunal's inspection of the operation a whole side of the hillside had been cut away and the frontend loader was engaged in cutting into the base of the face which had been created. This face into the hillside was approximately twenty metres high and very steep. As the blade cut into the hillside it was observed to cause a cave-in and more sand, grass and bushy scrub would cascade down the face to land at the base of the hill in front of the frontend loader. Several trucks were lining up to be filled in turn and they drove off taking the sand thus acquired.

The full process of converting the natural hillside into loads of sand, therefore, involved a significant degree of "winning the sand". It was not merely buying a pile of sand. Although the excavating or "undermining" process was comparatively simple the $125,000 spent by SSS purchased only the right to win the sand on that land. At law the deed whereby SSS purported to buy the sand itself did not achieve that result as there was no severed pile of sand lying on the ground which could be bought. The sand was in situ and consequently it was still a natural part of the land. On this analysis of the "sand purchase" deed of 17 April 1984 the expenditure concerned takes on the nature of a capital expenditure rather than expenditure on raw materials.

This conclusion is reinforced when the principles that have been established by the authorities regarding such expenditure are applied to the facts of this case. These principles have been clearly set down in the CCH Federal Tax Reporter Volume 3 paragraphs 23 500 to 23 520 inclusive and it is helpful to consider the various indicia of capital expenditure which have been derived from the authorities and approved by Dixon J (as he then was) in Sun Newspapers Ltd and Associated Newspapers Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337).

The first indicium is the "fixed or circulating capital test." Applying this test it is clear that the applicants had not purchased successive loads of sand which would clearly have been expenditure on circulating capital to be used in the course of their business. What they purchased was a source of sand which was part of the structure of the business rather than circulating capital. The right to obtain the sand continued for 80 years and the applicants estimated that the stock would actually last them 10 to 15 years at the current rate of usage. These facts tend to indicate that the expenditure was on part of the fixed capital of the business. The second test is to consider whether the expenditure is "current expenditure or once and for all expenditure." Here the applicants made a once and for all payment of $125,000 to acquire the right to the sand. This was not a payment for loads of sand by way of instalments nor was it an advance purchase of the sand. There was a once and for all payment at the outset for a right which was expected to provide sand supplies for the next 10 to 15 years. Though this by itself is not determinative it does tend to give it the character of capital expenditure. The next indicium is the "enduring benefit test." In this case the sand was not to be used up quickly in the course of the business but was for the enduring benefit of the business over a period of 10 to 15 years at least. The final indicium drawn from the authorities is the "profit yielding structure test" which was referred to by Sir Owen Dixon in Sun Newspapers Ltd and Associated Newspaper Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (Supra). Applying that test the $125,000 expenditure clearly was not merely a working expenditure as it related to the most important part of the whole structure; it established right of access to the source of the sand on which the" whole business was based.

The inevitable conclusion is that the applicants were not buying sand but they were buying the right to obtain the sand over a period of 80 years. They acquired a profit a prendre entitling them to win an unspecified volume of sand to a depth of 40 metres as, when and if they chose to do so. In the view of the Tribunal that expenditure is an expenditure of a capital nature and is not allowable as a deduction within the meaning of s51(1) of the Act.

It is now necessary to consider the operation of s51(2) which deals with deductions claimed when an expense of a capital nature is incurred in the purchase of trading stock. It is accepted by both sides that the overriding reason that the applicants wished to acquire the Banjup property was to obtain secure access to the sand deposits for the purpose of their business. That business was the business of sand suppliers and it was the practice to sell about ten per cent of the sand as a separate commodity and the rest was supplied and sold to clients as fill which would then be compacted onto "housepads" or otherwise used by SSS in the preparation of building sites.

As the sand was still in situ as a natural part of the land when the expense was incurred it cannot be considered as the purchase of the raw material itself and the Tribunal has determined, rather, that it was an expenditure incurred for the purpose of obtaining the raw material. For the same reason it could not be said that it was expenditure incurred in the purchase of trading stock as the sand was still part of the land itself, and the business of SSS was not to buy and sell land or interests in land.

Although 551(2), envisages that in appropriate circumstances an expense can be "deemed" to have been incurred in the purchase of trading stock this subsection does not of itself authorise the Commission (or this Tribunal) to do so. This possibility was not argued by Counsel and despite a fleeting reference to such a possibility by Professor Parsons (R W Parsons Income Taxation in Australia (1985) para 14.43) there appears to be no authority in the Act or in the cases for deeming such a capital expenditure to relate to trading stock in a sand quarrying (as opposed to a mining) business.

This Tribunal is therefore content to follow the precedent already set in the Taxation Board of Review by Dr Gerber in Case S92 85 ATC 671. The report of that very similar case contains a thorough review of the relevant authorities.

Having reached the conclusion that the expenditure fails to qualify as a deduction allowable under s51 it is not necessary to consider Counsels' very well argued summissions as to whether Part IVA might operate in any event to deny the deduction. Suffice it to say that, had the primary finding not been made then Part IVA may not have presented a barrier to the applicant.

The decision under review is affirmed.
