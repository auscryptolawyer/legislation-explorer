CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - Income Tax Assessment Act - estoppel - whether Commissioner bound by original assessments - whether amended assessments a valid exercise of Commissioner’s powers - decisions affirmed

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss166, 169A, 170

Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986, Explanatory Memorandum

Batagol and The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243

Industrial Equity Limited and Another and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and Others [1990] HCA 46; (1990) 170 CLR 649

AGC (Investments) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4180

REASONS FOR DECISION

10 November 1998 Deputy President B.H. Burns



  1. These were two applications by David Atkinson (“the applicant”) for review of two decisions of the Commissioner of Taxation (“the Commissioner”) dated 24 July 1997 to disallow the objection, dated 15 May 1997, against the amended notices of assessment made on 24 April 1997 in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996.
  2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the "T" documents), together with written submissions provided by both parties and the authorities referred to therein. The matter proceeded without the need to call any oral evidence.
  3. The applicant was represented by Mr E. Higgins, Lecturer in Law at Central Queensland University, who spoke to the Tribunal by telephone and the Commissioner was represented in person by an advocate, Mr A. Goss.
  4. The Tribunal makes the following findings of fact which were agreed between the parties as set out in a Statement of Agreed Facts as follows:

‘1. The applicant electronically lodged, through his tax agent Bowler Kimber & Associates, an income tax return for the financial year ended 30 June 1993 which included as income 4 Eligible Termination Payments which in total comprised the sum of $24,962.00. These Eligible Termination Payments were comprised of post June 1983 taxed components in the sum of $23,857.00 and pre July 1983 components in the sum of $1,105.00 (T4, pages 9-10)
  1. The applicant coded the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in the 1993 income tax return lodged in a manner which indicated that the low rate rebate applied in respect of the same. That coding was in accordance with the applicant’s understanding of the requirements and operation of the Income Tax Assessment Act. (T4, page 10)
  2. The applicant electronically lodged, through his tax agent Bowler Kimber & Associates, an income tax return for the financial year ended 30 June 1994 which included as income 6 Eligible Termination Payments which in total comprised the sum of $25,751.00. These Eligible Termination Payments were comprised of post June 1983 taxed components in the sum of $24,742.00 and pre July 1983 components in the sum of $1,009.00. (T5, pages 11-12)
  3. The applicant coded the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in the 1994 income tax return lodged in a manner which indicated that the low rate rebate applied in respect of the same. That coding was in accordance with the applicant’s understanding of the requirements and operation of the Income Tax Assessment Act. (T5, page 12)
  4. The applicant electronically lodged, through his tax agent Bowler Kimber & Associates, an income tax return for the financial year ended 30 June 1995 which included as income 5 Eligible Termination Payments which in total comprised the sum of $57,914.00. These Eligible Termination Payments were comprised of post June 1983 taxed components in the sum of $55,787.00 and pre July 1983 components in the sum of $2,127.00. (T6, pages 13-19)
  5. The applicant coded the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in the 1995 income tax return lodged in a manner which indicated that the low rate rebate applied in respect of the same. That coding was in accordance with the applicant’s understanding of the requirements and operation of the Income Tax Assessment Act. (ST1, page 1)
  6. The applicant electronically lodged, through his tax agent Bowler Kimber & Associates, an income tax return for the financial year ended 30 June 1996 which included as income 6 Eligible Termination Payments which in total comprised the sum of $26,237.00. These Eligible Termination Payments were comprised of post June 1983 taxed components in the sum of $25,348.00 and pre July 1983 components in the sum of $889.00. (T7, pages 20-27)
  7. The applicant coded the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in the 1996 income tax return lodged in a manner which indicated that the low rate rebate applied in respect of the same. That coding was in accordance with the applicant’s understanding of the requirements and operation of the Income Tax Assessment Act. (ST2, page 2)
  8. The respondent issued assessments for the financial years ended 30 June 1993 and 30 June 1994 in which the applicant was allowed the low rate rebate in respect of the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in those years. The effect of allowing the rebate was to reduce the rate of tax applicable to the post June 1983 components returned in those years to zero.
  9. The respondent issued an assessment for the financial year ended 30 June 1995 on 9 May 1996 in which the applicant was allowed the low rate rebate in respect of the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in that year. The effect of allowing the rebate was to reduce the rate of tax applicable to the post June 1983 components returned in that year to zero. (T8)
  10. The respondent issued an assessment for the financial year ended 30 June 1996 on 6 August 1996 in which the applicant was allowed the low rate rebate in respect of the post June 1983 components of the Eligible Termination Payments that were returned as income in that year. The effect of allowing the rebate was to reduce the rate of tax applicable to the post June 1983 components returned in that year to zero. (T9)
  11. The respondent subsequently reviewed the returns which were electronically lodged by the applicant for the years ended 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996 and determined that the assessments which had issued as a consequence of the same were incorrect in that the applicant had received a greater low rate rebate than was allowable in his circumstances for those years. (T10)
  12. The applicant issued amended assessments for the financial years ended 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996 to the applicant on 24 April 1997 in which the low rate rebate was recalculated to allow the applicant the correct low rate rebate that was applicable to his circumstances in those years. The effect of these amended assessments was to reduce the low rate rebate and increase the tax payable by the applicant in those years. (T11, T12)
  13. The applicant objected to the amended assessments for the years ended 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996 on 15 May 1997. (T14)
  14. The respondent disallowed the applicant’s objections for the years ended 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996 on 24 July 1997.’
  15. The issue in dispute between the parties is a legal one, namely, whether the Commissioner is bound by the original assessments issued to the applicant, as the applicant contends, or whether the amended assessments as issued by the Commissioner were in fact a valid exercise of the relevant powers under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”).
  16. In short, the applicant’s case was a two-fold one. Firstly, it was contended that the original assessments issued by the Commissioner are binding upon the Commissioner as they are a definitive, as distinct from tentative, statement of the taxpayer’s taxable income and the tax payable on that income (Batagol and The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243, per Kitto J). The second contention advanced on behalf of the applicant was an estoppel argument, namely, that as a matter of fairness, “the respondent should be prevented from departing from the assumption, representation, promise and assurance given here (either explicitly or implicitly) to the applicant as to the assessments”. To this end, the applicant contended that he had relied on the Commissioner’s representation as to his tax liability as stated in the original assessments to his detriment and that an estoppel should lie against the Commissioner preventing him from reneging on that representation.
  17. On the other hand, the Commissioner maintains that the “self-assessment” system introduced by the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986 (No. 46 of 1986) has significantly changed the taxation landscape in Australia. It is now the case, in the Commissioner’s submission, that the Commissioner usually issues an original assessment relying wholly on information provided by a taxpayer ie. the taxpayer’s “self-assessment”, whether correct or not, but that the Commissioner then has the power to issue amended assessments within a period of four years where the Commissioner finds that the original assessment was wrong. In this way, the Commissioner maintained that it could not be said that the act of issuing original assessments was in any way final or binding as the entire system is predicated on the Commissioner’s ability to conduct audits after the issuing of assessments and to amend assessments where appropriate. The Commissioner also maintained that an estoppel could not lie against the Commissioner to prevent performance of his statutory duties and functions, namely, the correct application of taxation law in order to ascertain the taxable income of a taxpayer and the proper tax payable on that income.
  18. The relevant provisions are the following sections of the Act:

‘Assessment

166 From the returns, and from any other information in his possession, or from any one or more of these sources, the Commissioner shall make an assessment of the amount of the taxable income of any taxpayer, and of the tax payable thereon.

...

Reliance by Commissioner on returns and statements

169A (1) Where a return of income of a taxpayer of a year of income is furnished to the Commissioner (whether or not by the taxpayer), the Commissioner may, for the purposes of making an assessment in relation to the taxpayer under this Act, accept, either in whole or in part, a statement in the return of the assessable income derived by the taxpayer and of any allowable deductions or rebates to which it is claimed that the taxpayer is entitled and any other statement in the return or otherwise made by or on behalf of the taxpayer.

(2) Despite subsection (1), if, in a document given with a return of income of a taxpayer of a year of income and signed by or on behalf of the taxpayer, a question is raised:

(a) that is relevant to the liability of the taxpayer in respect of the year of income; and

(b) on which the taxpayer is not entitled to apply for a private ruling under Part IVAA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953;

the Commission must give attention to that question.

...

Amendment of assessments

170 (1) The Commissioner may, subject to this section, at any time amend any assessment by making such alternations therein or additions thereto as he thinks necessary, notwithstanding that tax may have been paid in respect of the assessment.

(1A) If:

(a) an assessment has been amended in any particular in a way that affected a reduction in the liability of a taxpayer; and

(b) for the purposes of making the amendment, the Commissioner accepted a statement made by or on behalf of the taxpayer;

the Commissioner may, within 4 years from the service of the notice of the amended assessment, further amend the assessment in, or in respect of, that particular in a way that increases the liability of the taxpayer to the extent that the Commissioner considers necessary.

(2) Subject to this section, where there has been an avoidance of tax, the Commissioner may;

...

(b) in any other case:

(i) where the taxpayer is a relevant entity within the meaning of Division 1B of Part VI and the assessment is deemed by section 166A to have been made ¾ within 4 years from the date upon which the assessment is so deemed to have been made; or

(ii) otherwise ¾ within 4 years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable under the assessment;

amend the assessment by making such alterations in it or additions to it as the Commissioner thinks necessary to correct the assessment.’
  1. The Tribunal now turns to a consideration of the matters in issue between the parties. In so doing, the Tribunal would indicate that it has had regard to the various submissions of both parties. The relevant amendments to the Act which introduced the self-assessment system occurred by virtue of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986. In his second reading speech the Acting Treasurer said at p.2555 of House of Representatives, Parliamentary Debates (Hansard), 17 April 1986:
‘... Under self-assessment the Commissioner’s officers will generally not subject income tax returns to the same degree of technical scrutiny that has existed in the past before a notice of assessment is issued. Self-assessment will mean that, after assessments have been issued, there will be more audits and more checking and verifying of information in returns against data obtained from external sources.

...

The more limited initial checking of returns that will occur under self-assessment could mean that the assessment that is made on the basis of a taxpayer’s return produces a tax bill that is higher or lower that (sic) what the law really authorises. To remedy this, the Commissioner’s power to amend assessments is to be widened. The new power to amend to correct an error of law will enable a taxpayer’s liability to be either increased or decreased, generally within a period of three years after the due date for the payment of tax under the assessment. Where the commissioner detects an error that was against the taxpayer, the taxpayer will be paid interest when the original mistake is corrected.’

In Industrial Equity Limited and Another and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and Others [1990] HCA 46; (1990) 170 CLR 649, the majority of the High Court (Mason CJ, Brennan, Deane, Dawson, Toohey, McHugh JJ) had the following to say about the general scheme of the Act subsequent to the relevant amendments, at p.656:

‘While not using the term “self-assessment”, the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986 (CTH) effected changes to the Act which were designed to encourage honest self-assessment, while at the same time widening the powers of the Commissioner to amend assessments.’

Having quoted the above second reading speech, the Court went on to say at p.657:

‘One change made by the amending legislation was the introduction of s. 169A which applies to assessments (not being amended assessments) made on or after 1 July 1986 in respect of income of the year of income that commenced on 1 July 1985 or of any subsequent year of income, and to the first and any subsequent amendment of such an assessment: Taxation Laws Amendment Act, s. 25(1).

... [Quoting s. 169A]

Consequential changes were made to s. 170 which authorizes the Commissioner to amend assessments. These changes took effect with respect to the same year of income and the same assessment date as s. 169A. Section 170(1), which remains unchanged, empowers the Commissioner, “subject to this section”, to amend an assessment at any time “by making such alterations therein or additions thereto as he thinks necessary” and to do so “notwithstanding that tax may have been paid in respect of the assessment”. Section 170(2) empowered the Commissioner , where “a taxpayer has not made to the Commissioner a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment”, to correct “the assessment”. Hitherto, the power had been to correct “an error in calculation or a mistake of fact or to prevent avoidance of tax as the case may be”. True, s.170 contains various limitations of time in regard to the amendment of assessments but there can be no doubt that the section, as amended in 1986, reflects an extension of the Commissioner’s power in this regard. ...’
  1. The Explanatory Memorandum to the Taxation Laws Amendment Bill 1985 sheds further light on the object of the amending legislation. Regarding the self-assessment system, the relevant parts are as follows:

‘GENERAL OUTLINE

Taxation Laws Amendment Bill 1985

This Bill will amend -
  • the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 -
...

. . to facilitate “self-assessment” of income tax by allowing an assessment to issue based on information contained solely in the income tax return of the taxpayer;

. . to permit, subject to the time limits presently specified in the Act, an assessment to be amended to either increase or decrease the liability of a taxpayer so as to enable a correct assessment to be made;

...

FINANCIAL IMPACT

...

Self-assessment of income tax returns

...

This Bill will facilitate the implementation of the proposal, announced in the 1985-86 Budget, to introduce a system of self-assessment of Income tax returns.

Under the proposed self-assessment system, income tax returns will not generally be subject to technical scrutiny before an assessment is made. There will however be some clerical examination of all returns, mainly to prepare them for computer processing, but also to identify obvious errors and incorrect claims. There will be more post-assessment audits and examination of returns. These audits and examinations will in a number of cases give rise to a need to amend assessments to correct errors thus detected.

Under the existing law there are specified limitations on the power of the Commissioner of Taxation to amend assessments. For example, where a taxpayer has made a full and true disclosure of all material facts necessary for assessment, an amended assessment to increase the liability of the taxpayer may be made within 3 years from when the tax became due under the assessment to correct an error in calculation or mistake of fact, but not to correct an error of law. The amendment of an assessment to decrease a taxpayer’s liability is also subject to a 3 year limitation and, similarly, cannot be made to correct an error in law. The limitation prohibiting the amendment of an assessment to correct an error in law is appropriate under the present assessing system as all income tax returns are subject to examination by assessors and errors of law are generally identified and corrected by assessors in the course of making the original assessment.

In order to give effect to the self-assessment system, this Bill will allow the Commissioner of Taxation to make an assessment based on acceptance of information contained in the income tax return of the taxpayer. As the incorrect application of the law by taxpayers will, under the self-assessment system, ordinarily be identified only at post-assessment audit or examination stage, the Bill will authorise the Commissioner to amend assessments to increase or decrease the liability of taxpayers, within the presently specified time limits, to correct errors of law.

...’

In addition, the tax office has issued further material which, although lacking the force of law, is to the same effect (Taxation Rulings IT 2444, 2616, Tax Pack 1993-5).

  1. In submissions on his behalf, the applicant relied upon the authority of Kitto J in Batagol (supra), where the meaning of “assessment” was defined at p.252 as follows:
‘... the completion of the process by which the provisions of the Act relating to liability to tax are given concrete application in a particular case with the consequence that a specified amount of money will become due and payable as the proper tax in that case.’

The applicant’s submission was that at the time of issuing the original assessments by the Commissioner, Kitto J’s definition was satisfied here and, further, it followed that there had been “concrete application” of the provisions of the Act ie. a definitive and final statement of the applicant's tax liability from which the Commissioner could not depart.

  1. The majority of the High Court in Industrial Equity Limited (supra), commenting on Kitto J’s definition of the process of assessment, confirmed at p.658, that “[a]s a description of the process involved, the statement holds good even today.” It goes without saying however that the definition which was proffered well prior to introduction of the self-assessment system must now be viewed in light of the subsequent amendments to the Act which introduced that system.
  2. When one has regard to the amending provisions, the completion of the process of assessment described by Kitto J above will not necessarily occur upon the issuing of original assessments and will ultimately depend on whether the Commissioner decides to conduct an audit upon an individual taxpayer or class of taxpayer. Moreover, the final and definitive application of the law in the course of ascertaining a sum which is the “proper tax in that case” (emphasis added) can not now be said to have occurred until the end of the time (4 years) in which the Commissioner has under the Act to conduct further investigations and audits and issue amendments. Only then could it be said that the provisions of the Act have been given “concrete application” and that an amount will be due and payable as the “proper tax in that case”. Having regard to the scheme as it now stands, the issuing of initial assessments may well be but a step along the way in the process described above by Kitto J.
  3. As the High Court clearly confirms at p.658 (Industrial Equity Limited (supra)):
‘... It is inherent in the process contemplated by s. 169A that the checking of taxpayers’ returns is more likely to take place after assessments have been raised. If the Full Court meant no more than that the Commissioner is not precluded, by reason of an assessment, from making further inquiries into the affairs of the taxpayer, there can be no quarrel with what was said.’

Having regard to the words of ss169A and 170 of the Act and their context in the Act, and taking into account the object of the relevant amendments to the Act as gleaned from the material outlined above, namely, the second reading speech, explanatory memorandum, observations of the High Court and the like, it becomes patently clear that there is nothing to suggest that an original assessment issued by the Commissioner, reliant as it will be on the information provided by a taxpayer which may or may not lead to the correct assessment, is in any way final or binding. To suggest that it is would not only render nonsensical the power of the Commissioner to conduct further investigations and to issue amended assessments which may or may not be in the taxpayer’s favour, but would undermine the very foundations of the self-assessment system. For this reason, the applicant’s submission to this effect must fail.

  1. The second submission of the applicant, namely, that the Commissioner should, as a matter of fairness, be estopped “from departing from the assumption, representation, promise and assurance given here (either explicitly or impliedly) to the applicant as to the assessments”, can be dealt with briefly in so far as it relies on the first submission and must therefore fall with it for the reasons given above. In short, where the Commissioner has the power to amend assessments as he clearly does under s170 of the Act, and where it can be said that the assessment process is not definitively and finally concluded by the issuing of original assessments, it follows that the Commissioner cannot be estopped from issuing new assessments.
  2. What also can be said about estoppel however is that it will not ordinarily operate against a statutory body to prevent the proper performance of its statutory duties and functions. There is a strong line of authority to this effect culminating in the case of AGC (Investments) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4180, in which Hill J said quite unequivocally at p.4195:
‘... there is no room for the doctrine of estoppel operating to preclude the Commissioner of Taxation from pursuing his statutory duty to assess tax in accordance with law. The Income Tax Assessment Act imposes obligations upon the Commissioner and creates public rights and duties, which the application of the doctrine of estoppel would thwart. In my view the doctrine of estoppel cannot be invoked by a taxpayer so as to prevent the Commissioner assessing pursuant to his duty so to do. The cases certainly support that view.’
  1. In any event, were estoppel to lie against the Commissioner in circumstances such as this, it is difficult to see how the applicant would satisfy the requirements, firstly, that there be some detriment suffered by the applicant arising from the Commissioner’s representations, assurances or promises, and secondly, that it would be unconscionable for the Commissioner, having assessed the applicant's tax upon the wrong basis, to depart from it and assess correctly in accordance with law. The Tribunal’s view is that these factors are a further impediment to the success of the applicant's estoppel argument.
  2. The Tribunal’s view, with respect to estoppel, is clearly supported when taking the inverse of the estoppel argument advanced by the applicant. Hypothetically, where a taxpayer overstates his or her taxable income in the course of his or her self-assessment which the Commissioner then relies upon in assessing the taxpayer’s liability (which would be to the Commissioner’s advantage), and it could also be said that the taxpayer’s failure to object to the erroneous assessment is an implied representation that the taxpayer agreed with that assessment, such representation which the Commissioner then relies upon to his detriment in the course of factoring the relevant amount of tax into consolidated revenue, surely it would not then be said that the taxpayer should be estopped from departing from the original statement on which the Commissioner had relied. To suggest that that is so would not only confound all notions of justice, but would contravene s170 of the Act which clearly enables the very act of amending the assessment to which the estoppel would be directed. In the same way, the Tribunal’s view is that the remedy of estoppel cannot lie against the Commissioner to prevent performance of the Commissioner’s statutory duty to ascertain the taxable income and relevant tax payable by a taxpayer which, in this case, has required the Commissioner, pursuant to s170 of the Act, to amend the original assessments to account for tax which the applicant was required by law to pay but has not in fact paid.
  3. In arriving at this conclusion, the Tribunal is sympathetic to the concerns of the applicant who has no doubt done his best to discharge his duties under the Act. At the end of the day, the Tribunal’s task is to apply the relevant law to the facts as found by it which it has done. The Tribunal would however remind the applicant that were he to be of the view that the situation in which he has found himself is the result of an error on his tax agent’s behalf, it may be there are appropriate remedies which lie outside the jurisdiction of the Tribunal. In raising this issue for the applicant's consideration, the Tribunal makes no definitive statement on the topic nor would it be appropriate to do so.
  4. For these reasons, the decision of the Tribunal is that the decisions under review are affirmed.
