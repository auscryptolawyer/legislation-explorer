Re Taxation Appeals [1987] AATA 254 (3 July 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. QT86/1874

AAT No. 3622

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

K.L. Beddoe (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax: Allowable Deductions - self education expenses - non-commissioned army officer transferred interstate to attend course - whether domestic costs of living off-barracks allowable - whether expenditure on living costs incurred.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s.51(1).

HEARING

BRISBANE

3:7:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

The question at issue before the Tribunal is whether accommodation, electricity, heating, gas and personal living costs are allowable deductions to the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1979. These claims arise in the context of self education expenses.

2. The only provision of the Income Tax Assessment Act raised by the notice of objection and the only provision which could conceivably be raised is sub-section 51(1) of the Act.

3. During the year of income the applicant was employed full time in the regular Army as an engineer fitter. He was employed at an Army base near a provincial city and throughout the year of income held the rank of Sergeant.

4. It appears that prior to the year of income under review the Army nominated the applicant, with others, to attend an air engineering course at the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology. The course has been described as a post graduate course although the exact status of the course was not established before the Tribunal. What was established was that the course contained 30 units and all of these units had to be completed to gain the relevant academic certificate. However, the Army was only interested in the applicant completing 21 of the units - this appears to have been the policy in relation to all Army personnel undertaking the course - and the course of study in respect of the 21 units was compressed into one academic year. The applicant's evidence was that the Army personnel attending the course were required to do more units per semester than the civilian students.

5. The applicant also stated that completion of the course was one of the prerequisites for gaining promotion to a position of Staff Sergeant which was the next available promotion open to the applicant. In a subsequent year of income the applicant did in fact attain promotion to Staff Sergeant.

6. Although the applicant was selected by the Army to attend the course at the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology he was entitled to refuse the place if he wished to. The applicant being conscious of the need to complete the course if he wished to obtain promotion did not refuse and he commenced the course prior to 1 July 1978.

7. At the time the applicant was selected to attend the course he was living in the provincial city with his wife in a home which was being rented. For reasons which were not made clear to the Tribunal his wife decided that she did not wish to move to Melbourne and she continued to live in the provincial city throughout the time that the applicant was undertaking the course.

8. As one would expect with the military forces the applicant's removal to Melbourne to undertake the course was not simply a matter of getting in his car and driving to Melbourne. He was posted to a R.A.E.M.E. unit located at Bandiana in Victoria. Bandiana, according to the applicant, is 260 km from Melbourne. The Army therefore detached him from that unit to a unit located at Victoria Barracks, South Melbourne and he remained detached to that unit except during the Christmas/New Year semester break when he was detached back to a R.A.E.M.E. unit and required to undertake military duties.

9. However the support unit at Victoria Barracks did not have barracks accommodation available and the applicant was provided with barracks accommodation at a signals training unit located in a suburb of Melbourne convenient to Melbourne by the electric train service. The applicant took up residence in those barracks and commenced his course at the Institute of Technology. According to his evidence he found the barracks unsuitable for a person engaged in serious study because of noise factors and interruptions which made it impossible to get sufficient sleep after he had finished studying at night.

10. He therefore elected and got permission to live outside of barracks and he rented a flat located in an inner Melbourne suburb.

11. In his return of income for the year under review the applicant made the following claims in respect of self education:

Accommodation

Rent of premises near college $1,822.00

Personal Living Costs

52 weeks by $20.00 $1,040.00

Travelling to College

12 x $20.00 $ 240.00

Stationery Paper and Study Material

(Outside set text books paid by

Army) $ 150.00

Electricity, Heating and Gas $ 202.00

12. In making his assessment the respondent Commissioner allowed the claims in respect of travel amounting to $240 and stationery etc amounting to $150. In his evidence the applicant stated that certain text books and stationery were provided by the Army and the Army paid the college and union fees payable for the course. The Army also paid for the applicant's travel between his home city and Melbourne. It was also established that the Army would have provided married quarters for the applicant and his wife if the wife had been prepared to move to Melbourne but the Army did not provide any allowance to the applicant once he elected to move out of the barracks accommodation provided for him on a single person basis.

13. The applicant's evidence as to the amounts incurred for personal living costs was unsatisfactory although he did assert that the claim represents an amount over and above his normal personal living costs. The basis for claiming at $20 per week was simply not established, and I suspect could not be established, before the Tribunal.

14. Paragraph 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act provides that in proceedings on a review before the Tribunal the burden of proving the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer. The applicant's evidence as to the personal living costs was quite unclear and inconclusive as to the basis for the claim. While it may be assumed some amount was incurred by the applicant the applicant's evidence did not convince me on the balance of probabilities that he had incurred either $1040 or at least $1040 for personal living costs.

15. During the course of the Hearing, counsel for the Commissioner conceded the quantum in relation to rent and electricity, heating and gas but did not make any concession in relation to personal living costs. The applicant therefore fails on the question of whether the amount of $1040 was incurred.

16. The applicant was represented before the Tribunal by his accountant, who sought to rely on the decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in F.C. of T. v. Highfield 82 ATC 4463. In that case the Court allowed deductions for living away from home expenses and meals to a dentist who travelled overseas to undertake a full time post graduate degree in periodontics. Counsel for the Commissioner made submissions about Highfield's case and I will return to them later.

17. Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that the expenses incurred by the applicant which had not been allowed by the respondent were private or domestic expenses not coming within the positive limbs of s.51. Counsel relied upon a number of authorities including Green v. F.C. of T. [1950] HCA 20; (1950) 81 CLR 313, Case 11, 11 CTBR 42, Case K21 (1959) 10 TBRD 118, Case K29 78 ATC 281, Case U49 87 ATC 337, Case H37 76 ATC 312 and F.C. of T. v. Charlton 84 ATC 4415.

18. Of those decisions the only one which I need to discuss is Charlton's case which explains very clearly the present state of the law in relation to accommodation expenses incurred by taxpayers living away from their home. Dr Charlton incurred expenses in relation to his Bendigo flat because he chose to live in Melbourne when he was employed in Bendigo. I use the word "employed" in the sense of employed on his professional duties. The part of the claim relating to the expenses of the Bendigo flat was not allowed by the Supreme Court whereas the claim for expenses on accommodation while temporarily located at Wangaratta was allowed because the applicant was required to travel from his base of operations at Bendigo to Wangaratta for the purpose of performing his duties.

19. Turning then to Highfield's case. In this case the claim included costs for accommodation and meals incurred by the applicant while overseas undertaking a post graduate course in periodontics. At the Board of Review the Commissioner had submitted as a separate argument that the accommodation and meal expenses were not allowable under s.51 being of a private or domestic nature. That submission was subsequently withdrawn by the Commissioner before the Board and the Board asked not to take it into account in making its decision. This somewhat extraordinary action by the Commissioner resulted in what can only be regarded as adverse comments by Mr Harrowell and Mr Pape, Members of Taxation Board of Review No 1. Mr Harrowell's comments are to be found at paragraph 42 et seq in Case N69 81 ATC 358 and Mr Pape's comments are to be found in his reasons at paragraph 28 et seq in Case N69.

20. In the hearing of the present application counsel for the Commissioner sought to resile from the Commissioner's action of withdrawing the arguments in Case N69. He submitted that the Commissioner's action in withdrawing those arguments and in not running the arguments before Lee J. in the Supreme Court was not in accordance with the law and that this Tribunal should now deal with those arguments. It is not clear from the reasons of the Board of Review as to exactly the nature of the arguments which were withdrawn and I suggested to counsel for the Commissioner that this Tribunal should not be asked to speculate as to what those arguments may have been when it was within the power of the respondent in these proceedings to tender a copy of paragraphs 36 and 42 of the written swubmissions in Highfield's case which had been before the Board of Review. Counsel for the Commissioner undertook to submit a copy of this document in a written submission to the Tribunal and the applicant was granted leave to respond to that submission. Paragraphs 36 to 42 of the submission to the Board of Review in Highfield's Case read as follows:

"36. Alternatively, it is submitted that the essential

character of the expenditure claimed by the taxpayer for

board and quarters and meals was private or domestic and

therefore precluded from deduction.

37. In Lunney at pp500-1 Williams, Kitto and Taylor JJ.,

when consider the claims for expenses incurred travelling

to and from work said:-

'In the course of the argument we were referred to a

number of cases in which, from time to time, much the

same problem had been discussed. It is unnecessary to

review these cases but of them we mention Cook v.

Knott (1887) 2 Tax Cas 246; Friedson v. Glyn-Thomas

(1922) 128 LT 24; Ricketts v. Colquhoun (1925) 1

KB 725; (1926) AC 1; Nolder v. Walters (1930) 15

Tax Cas 380; (1930) 46 TLR 397; Blackwell v.

Mills (1945) 174 LT 217; and Durbidge v. Sanderson

(1955) 3 All ER 154. No doubt the legislative

provisions which required consideration in these cases

were not identical with s.51, but the process of

reasoning by which they were decided consistently

rejects the notion that expenditure incurred by a

taxpayer in order to travel from his home to his place

of business is, in any sense, a business expenditure

or an expenditure incurred in, or, in the course of,

earning assessable income. Indeed they go further and

refuse assent to the proposition that such expenditure

is, in any relevant sense, incurred for the purpose of

earning assessable income and unanimously accept the

view that it is properly characterised as a personal

or living expense. This view agrees with that which

we, ourselves, entertain.'

38. The Commissioner submits that there is no essential

difference for s.51 purposes between the nature of the

expenditure being considered by the Court in Lunney and the

board and quarters and meals expenditure claimed by the

taxpayer. The expenditure was clearly a daily cost of

living and that cannot change even if it was incurred while

living in London.

39. It is noted that expenditure of a similar kind was

included in the amount allowed in Finn (supra). However, a

perusal of the report of the case at [1961] HCA 61; 106 CLR 60 suggests

that the operation of the exclusory provisions was not

argued before the Court. Certainly the judgments do not

contain comment on this aspect of s.51. It is submitted

that the Finn case does not preclude a finding by the Board

that the expenditure claimed by the taxpayer for board and

quarters and meals is precluded from deduction by the

exclusory provisions.

40. The Supreme Court of New South Wales considered a

claim for interest and insurance expenses in respect of a

home office in F.C. of T. v. McCloy 75 ATC 4079.

Helsham J. relied on the Faichney and Thomas decisions in

determining that the expenditure was not deductible under

s.51. At p4081 he said:

'It is important not to lose sight of the fact that

the claim of the taxpayer relates to interest payments

on money borrowed to purchase the house in which the

study or home office is situated, and to insurance

premiums for that house and contents.'

Later at p 4083 he quotes Mason J. in Faichney and

comments as follows:-

'Later on, in the course of giving his reasons as to

why a proportionate part of the cost of lighting and

heating referable to use by the taxpayer of the study

should be ruled allowable deductions under sec. 51,

his honour said (p 4250) -

"It is enough that light and heating was provided

in the study whilst the taxpayer was working on

matters which fell within the scope of his

employment. In that respect the expenditure

differs from costs incurred in providing the study,

for in that case what is provided retains its

essential character as part of the taxpayer's

home."

In my view the same reasoning applies in the present

case, and the expenditure, in interest and insurance,

referable to the provision of the taxpayer's home

office, does not bear the essential character of being

an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the

assessable income of the taxpayer.'

41. It is submitted that Mason J. was saying that the

study retains the essential character as part of the home,

i.e. the expenditure to provide the home has a domestic

nature. Helsham J. relied upon this to establish that the

essential character of the expenditure claimed in McCloy

was domestic. He then decided that because of that

character, the claim did not satisfy the positive limbs of

s.51.

42. The Commissioner submits that the essential character

of the expenditure claimed by the taxpayer for board and

quarters and meals was private or domestic and is therefore

not allowable under s.51.".

21. It is apparent that those submissions are not directly in point with the issues that arise in this case and I am not called on therefore to decide whether the decision in Highfield's case applies or does not apply to the facts of this case. Highfield's case concerned expenses incurred by the applicant while overseas attending a course of study. Such expenses were necessarily incurred for the purpose of pursuing the course of study and were not in the nature of ordinary living expenses. In Case T78 86 ATC 1094 the Tribunal decided that expenses incurred by a barrister while studying a post graduate course of study at Cambridge University were allowable. Those expenses included college accommodation, rent, payment for meals and other miscellaneous charges of what might be called a domestic nature. That decision relied on the decision in Highfield's case.

22. In this application the expenses in dispute were incurred by the applicant because his wife chose to remain living in the provincial city and the applicant chose to live in accommodation outside military barracks. No doubt, and this is stating the matter at its highest, in making his choice to live outside the military barracks the applicant was concerned that he be able to study effectively for his course. That does not alter the essential character of the expenses incurred. When the applicant decided to move out of the barracks accommodation supplied by the Army he was required to get Army approval to live outside barracks. He obtained that approval and moved to the flat. What he did is on all fours with the classic example discussed by Denning L.J. (as he then was) in Newsom v. Robertson (1953) 1 Ch 7 of an employee choosing to live away from his place of employment. The applicant chose to live out of barracks so he could get a good night's sleep.

23. Newsom v. Robertson was considered by the High Court in Lunney v. F.C. of T. (1958) 100 CLR 478 where the Court held (McTiernan J. dissenting) that expenses incurred by an employee in travelling between his home and his place of employment were not allowable deductions. The ratio of the Court's decision is that expenditure incurred by an employee in travelling to his place of employment is not incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income derived from that employment.

24. While this application is concerned with accommodation costs (the Commissioner having allowed a deduction for the travelling costs for travel between the flat and the Institute of Technology (subject to the operation of section 82A) by allowing the objection in part) the decision in Lunney's case applies equally to those costs. The expenses in dispute are clearly of a private or domestic nature. They were not relevant or incidental to the derivation of his income as a non commissioned officer in the regular Army and they therefore fall outside the positive limbs of sub-section 51(1).

25. Counsel for the Commissioner did not seek to have the Commissioner's decision on the objection set aside in relation to the travel expenses allowed by the respondent and I do not propose to do so on my own motion.

26. The respondent's objection decision will be affirmed.
