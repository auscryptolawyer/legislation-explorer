TAXATION : Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - Foreign Exchange Loss - Loss on Swiss Francs Loan - Not Allowable



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : Ss.51(1)

Place: Brisbane



By an application lodged in this Tribunal on 16 February 1994 the applicant sought review of an objection decision dated 21 December 1993. That objection decision disallowed in full the applicant taxpayer's objection to assessment dated 18 June 1993 in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1992.



2. The objection contended that the applicant's taxable income should be reduced by $120,117, the amount of taxable distribution from the applicant's family trust ("ETFT").



3. Simply put, the issue before me is whether a foreign exchange loss incurred by a unit trust, the units in which were held by ETFT during the year of income ended 30 June 1987, is an allowable deduction as a carry forward loss for the year of income ended 30 June 1992 under section 80 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The objection decision under review, by disallowing the claimed deduction, has a flow-on effect in that the net income distributed by that trust has been increased, thus increasing the assessable income of the applicant.



4. Subsection 80(2) of the Act provides an allowable deduction for losses incurred in the seven years immediately preceding the year of income under consideration and not allowed as a deduction in any of those years. Such a deduction is allowed from the assessable income of the taxpayer where there is no exempt income.



5. The deduction is only allowed where the loss relates to a year of income ended prior to 1 July 1989 (ss.80(1AA)).



6. Subsection 51(1) of the Act provides:



" All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."



7. At the hearing of the matter before the Tribunal the applicant taxpayer was represented by Mr Olding of KPMG Peat Marwick and Mr Hack of counsel, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor, appeared for the Commissioner. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, subject to an objection by the respondent Commissioner as to the assertions of fact contained in those documents. The Tribunal heard evidence from the applicant; Adrian Carroll, a partner of KPMG Peat Marwick, the group's accountant; and Stephen Clarke, a Westpac bank officer. A number of additional documents were handed up and marked as exhibits.



8. During the period relevant to the matter before me, the applicant was the principal beneficiary of a family trust ("ETFT"). The ETFT held all of the units in three unit trusts ("ETCCT", "NPCCT" and "CHCCT"). Each of those unit trusts owned business premises operated by three separate companies. A structure diagram prepared by Mr Olding illustrates the relationship of these entities for the period from 1985 to 1988 (Exhibit 1). I am satisfied that the applicant was in fact the controlling mind of the entire group. I should say at the outset that detailed documentary evidence in the matter was not tendered. Reliance was primarily placed upon the oral evidence of the applicant as to his recollection of matters. This can, at the best, be only described as hazy and confused.



9. KPMG Peat Marwick and its predecessors have been the group's accountants since 1977. The applicant accepted that the accounts prepared by the group's accountants correctly reflected the information provided to them relating to the financial affairs of the group.



10. In April 1985 the ETCCT entered into the loan agreement with the Westpac Bank for the Swiss francs equivalent of A$900,000. Prior to entering into this arrangement, the three unit trusts all carried individual loans through AGC. There has been little explanation as to the purpose of these original loans. I infer from the very vague evidence given in this regard, that the funds were mainly used in the purchase of buildings and land. The applicant’s evidence was that he saw, as the controlling mind of the group, that if a larger loan was entered into, as a consolidation of all the group's debts, a more attractive interest rate could be negotiated. Mr Clarke confirmed that the interest rate on a Swiss franc loan was then 6% in comparison to the 15% or 16% payable on an Australian dollar loan. Further commercial advantage was gained from this arrangement through savings in stamp duty and other costs incurred in the discharge of mortgage.

Then, as the applicant put it during his evidence, it was:



". . . decided to take on the - assume the position that AGC had with our organisation and borrow the funds through the Westpac and pay AGC out of - pay AGC loans out - lend the money to the other entities who then paid AGC out their loans, and at that stage we became the money lenders then to the other [trust]". (Transcript p.6)



Further, the applicant explained:



"It was our intention to borrow the money and to lend the money to the other organisations, and had that taken - had that gone through to the way we'd intended and hoped at the time when the decision was made to do that, it was our intention to possibly follow on and do other ventures in the money lending side." (Transcript p.12)



Further commercial advantage was gained from this arrangement through savings in stamp duty and other costs incurred in the discharge of a mortgage.



11. Following the foreign exchange loss, however, the group, according to the evidence of the applicant, revised it's strategy to concentrate on the core of it's business.



12. The applicant told the Tribunal that since the "bulk borrowing" in 1985, no part of the organisation, except for the ETCCT, has made a new external borrowing. That is, any funds required by any of the group's entities have been borrowed through the ETCCT. The next external borrowing was apparently in 1989. It was the evidence of the applicant that on at least one occasion since the original foreign exchange loan - the ETCCT has on-lent to other entities in the group and has charged that entity "normal commercial interest rates". The ETCCT was chosen as the borrower for the group, according to the applicant, as it had the most assets. Further, whenever any further assets were acquired by the group since 1985, they have been acquired by the ETCCT. No documentary evidence to support the applicant’s oral assertions as to these matters were tendered.



13. Stephen William Clarke, called by the respondent to give evidence, is the Manager of Legal Litigation and Recoveries of Westpac Bank. It was the evidence of Mr Clarke that Westpac, at the time the transaction under consideration took place, had a policy that the minimum borrowing through an offshore loan facility was $500,000 AUS. Mr Hack attempted to place some weight on this policy, in that it would have influenced the decision of ETCCT to take on the loan. However, I am unable to draw such a conclusion, and even if I were able to do so, it would have no bearing on the outcome of this matter.



14. No additional security was taken by Westpac in relation to the foreign exchange loan but rather a guarantee was given by AGC which continued to hold security over the property of the group under the previous arrangements.



15. The loan was converted into Australian dollars in October 1986 when the equivalent of the principal under loan had increased to $1,601,070. This loss was due to a fall in the value of the Australian dollar as against the Swiss franc. It was part of the resulting loss of $701,070 which was claimed as a deduction for ETCCT and is in issue before me. It should be noted that the deduction claimed is that for the loss relating to the proportion of on-lent to CHCCT and NPCCT, that is an amount of $361,551.



16. It was agreed between the parties that the loss was incurred in October 1986, the time at which the loan was converted to the Australian dollar equivalent and thus comes within the terms of subsection 80(1AA) of the Act, and I so find. None of the loss incurred because of the foreign exchange loan has been passed on to the other entities in the group, and I so find.



17. The loan was used by the ETCCT in a number of ways. The breakdown of the use of the funds is set out in the applicant's affidavit (Exhibit A) as follows:



. amount lent by ETCCT to CHCCT to enable

CHCCT to pay out its loans from AGC -- $A115,293



. amount lent by ETCCT to NPCCT to enable

NPCCT to pay out its loans from AGC -- $A348,850



. balance used by ETCCT to repay as far as

possible its loans from AGC -- $A435,857



$A900,000



No security was taken by the ETCCT for these loans to the other group members apart from the cross-collaterisation of the assets of the group.



18. For the 1985, 1986 and 1987 years of income the interest burden was apportioned between the three entities in proportion to their part of the overall borrowing. From 1988, interest on the base loans, that is the proportion of the original $900,000 borrowing was calculated at the 30-day bank bill rate on the first day of each month for a constant principal. The off-shore loan was replaced by an AGC loan at 2% less than the bank bill rate.



19. The applicant in his affidavit (Exhibit A) at paragraph 7(k) - (m), expressed his intention at the time the loan agreement was entered into:



"(k) My clear recollection was that it was always intended that ETCCT would charge a commercial rate of interest on amounts borrowed externally (the foreign exchange loan and any other loans) and on-lent to other entities in the group. I always clearly understood that this had to occur to ensure that ETCCT would be entitled to tax deductions for its interest and other borrowing costs in relation to the external borrowings.



(l) However, as the group was wholly owned by ETFT from March 1985, I could not see any point in charging interest on loans between the group entities that were not financed by external borrowings. It seemed to me that this would just mean pointless paperwork.



(m) Therefore the policy adopted throughout the period was that -

. where ETCCT borrowed monies externally, and on-lent those funds to other group entities, interest would be charged to generate a commercial return; but

. interest would not be charged on other loans between the group entities not funded by external borrowings."



20. At the time the loan negotiations were taking place a legal opinion was sought from the applicant's solicitors, Messrs Thynne and Macartney in a letter dated 4 April 1985 (T15/122-123). Touche Ross wrote to Thynne and Macartney setting out the circumstances of the loan and explaining:



"The Westpac Banking Corporation Ltd requires a legal opinion as to whether the provisions of the Trust Deed for the ETCCT enable the Trust to engage in the above loan transaction, and borrow and act as unsecured lender to the associated trusts. Would you also please provide an opinion as to whether the Trust Deeds for the [CHCCT] and the NCCT enable each company (as trustee) to borrow funds on an unsecured basis from ETCC Pty Ltd (as trustee)."



The general thrust of the reply from the solicitors was that ETCCT was so authorised.



21. When put to the applicant in cross-examination, he stated that he had no reason to doubt the accuracy of the statements contained in the particular paragraph.



22. A letter of 19 April 1985 which was sent from Touche Ross and Co to the Manager of Westpac Banking Corporation Ltd (T20) was put to the applicant by Mr Hack. The applicant agreed that he had no reason to doubt the correctness of matters put by Touche Ross on his behalf in that letter.



23. Paragraph 2(a) of that letter is as follows:



"The loan monies will be utilised as follows:



2.(a) [ETCC Pty Ltd] as trustee for [The ETCCT] will on lend sufficient monies to [JA Pty Ltd] as trustee for [The CHCCT] to enable [The CHCCT] to repay, in full three (3) existing capital loans held with Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd.



The loan to [JA Pty Ltd] (as trustee) will be shown as an unsecured loan receivable in the financial accounts of [ETCC Pty Ltd] (as trustee). Interest will be payable by [JA Pty Ltd] (as trustee) on the unsecured loan made by [ETCC Pty Ltd] (as trustee), however, the rate of interest or terms or conditions of the loan will not result in any pecuniary disadvantage to [ETCC Pty Ltd] (as trustee)."



24. A similar explanation appears in the same letter in relation to NPCCT.



25. Paragraph 2(c) of the letter goes on to explain:



"(c) The balance of overseas loan monies available will be fully used to part repay existing capital loans held with Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd by [ETCC Pty Ltd] (as trustee). It is estimated that a liability of approximately $220,000 will remain outstanding by [ETCC Pty Ltd] (as trustee) to Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd after full utilisation of funds borrowed overseas by [ETCC Pty Ltd] (as trustee)."



26. By a letter dated 13 March 1989 from Touche Ross to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, an amended assessment was requested for the year of income ended 30 June 1987 for the applicant (T5/19). Page 4 of that letter refers to the interest on the loan funds:



"Interest of Loan Funds



When the funds borrowed by the ETCCT were on loaned to the NPCCT and the CHCCT, it was agreed that the ETCCT should not suffer any pecuniary disadvantage as a result of the interest rate or other conditions imposed upon the loans. Hence, in compiling the accounts for the financial years ended 30 June 1985, 1986 and 1987 the proportion that the NPCCT and the CHCCT borrowed of the amount of $900,000 was applied to the actual interest payable on the loan. As a result, when the loan was converted to AUD on 14 October 1986 interest became payable on AUD $1,601,070 rather than the SWF equivalent of AUD $900,000 and the interest apportioned to each of those entities increased. From the time the loan was converted back to AUD these trusts were paying interest on their proportion of $1,601,070 eg.



NPCCT 38.76% of interest on 1,601,070

Therefore, the trustees of the ETCCT, the NPCCT and the CHCCT as a lender and borrowers agreed that future interest would be calculated on the original loan balances as expressed in AUD and as actually received by the NPCCT and the CHCCT.



Further, it was decided that an interest adjustment should be made in 1987/88 accounts to allow a credit to the NPCCT and the CHCCT to reflect this position in respect of the previous year."



27. When this letter was put to him the applicant agreed that for the first three years following the overseas loan, ETCCT did not seek to profit from the other two trusts. However, ETCCT charged interest to the other two trusts in order to avoid carrying the entire cost of the loan. More correctly, the position to be implied from the above, and I so find, is that from the time of the loan in 1985 to October 1986 interest payable by the other entities to ETCCT was charged on the base amount of $900,000. From October 1986 to the time the position was reviewed, allegedly in mid-1987, the interest payable was calculated on the entire amount of the loan after conversion into Australian dollars.



28. The applicant said he had no personal knowledge of the matters contained in the letter except insofar, as he put it “... my own way of understanding these things". (Transcript p.33). This was the general import of his evidence in relation to most matters put to him.



29. I find that the intention at 1985 was not to make a profit from the transactions involved. That is, interest was calculated on the basis, to use the words of Mr Hack, the import of which Mr Carroll agreed, "that no trust would be advantaged and no trust disadvantaged". (Transcript p.68). It was later that the interest rate charged by ETCCT to the other entities increased to above the external cost of the borrowing. That is, 1988 was the first income tax year that the ETCCT charged interest that yielded a profit from the transaction. This is not explained by the letter from Touche Ross to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of 13 March 1989. Mr Carroll's evidence supports this finding. From that time, interest on the base loans was calculated at the 30-day bank bill rate on the first day of each month for a constant principal.

35. Considering all of the material before, even taking a most beneficial view of the evidence and the authorities relied upon, there is nothing before me to justify a finding that the ETCCT was carrying on business as a moneylender. The matter does not, however, end there.



36. Mr Olding for the applicant submitted that if I were to make the following findings of fact, they alone are sufficient to allow the entire loss to be deductible; not confined to the loss as it relates to those amounts on-lent:

(1) The foreign exchange loan was taken out by the ETCCT with a view to:



(a) on-lending part to NPCCT and CHCCT;

(b) using the remainder of the loan for its own purposes.



(2) Interest was intended to be charged by the ETCCT to the other entities.



(3) The intention was the rate of interest was to be not less than the rate charged by Westpac on the original loan.



(4) The purpose of the ETCCT in entering into the loan was to reduce the interest costs to the ETCCT and other group entities.



37. It is clear that " . . . whether an exchange loss or gain, incurred on repayment of moneys lent, is on capital or revenue account, is one to be determined exclusively on the facts as proved in evidence". (Case M3 80 ATC 28 at 35). This proposition is so obvious it hardly needs the weight of precedent to support it. In direct contrast, I would think, is a comment in the same decision, made just one paragraph earlier:

". . . in a taxing statute which has created two species of receipts, labelled 'capital' and 'revenue' respectively, mere evidence that a company carries on 'business' is, in my view, insufficient to characterise a loss or gain into one or other class. Unless it can be shown that the impugned loss - in the instant case incurred on the repayment of a loan - can be shown to have an independent revenue character, distinct from the original obligation of a capital nature, the loss is a loss of capital. (Cf. International Nickel Australia Limited v. F.C. of T. 77 ATC 4383; [1977] HCA 49; (1977) 137 C.L.R. 347; C.A.G.A. v F.C. of T. 77 ATC 4375; (1977) 137 C.L.R. 373; AVCO Financial Services v. F.C. of T. 79 ATC 4560."



38. The applicant relies on the Federal Court decision in FCT v. Bivona Pty Ltd 89 ATC 4179. There the taxpayer had been incorporated for the express purpose of borrowing overseas funds for use by the group companies of which the taxpayer company was a member. In respect of the particular loan transaction under consideration there, although most of the $4 million which was borrowed in Swiss francs was on-lent to group entitles, a portion was lent to unrelated companies. Contained in the company's Memorandum of Association, as an express object of incorporation, was to carry on business as a money lender. The principal source of the taxpayer's assessable income was the lending of money.



39. On appeal to the Full Federal Court, (FCT v Bivona Pty Ltd 90 ATC 4168), the Tribunal's conclusion that the facts supported the finding that the taxpayer was in the money lending business was upheld. The headnote of the report conveniently summarises the salient factual features of the matter:



" 2. The taxpayer did borrow a substantial sum of money at interest, the bulk of which it on-lent to its holding company at interest. There was no suggestion that any of the interest rates were not commercial rates. The loans to the holding company yielded a profit to the taxpayer, if the loss realised on foreign exchange transactions was put to one side. This loss did not prevent the Tribunal drawing the natural inference that the operations so conducted by the taxpayer were business operations intended to yield a profit. Even if the role of the taxpayer in its activities of lending money could be described as a 'conduit' for the members of the group, that begs, not answers, the question whether the activities of the taxpayer are correctly characterised as its principal business consisting of the lending of money."



40. The High Court in Fairway Estates Pty Ltd v FCT 70 ATC 4061 considered the question of whether the taxpayer in that matter carried on the business of moneylending. Prior to the particular advance under consideration, the sole business activity of the taxpayer had been real estate development. There, Barwick CJ analysed the then respondent's arguments in this way (at 4067):



"It is of course true as the respondent's counsel has emphasised that there were no loans made between the date of the advance to Jubilee and August 1960, or as he preferred to state the fact, only one loan in the first 21 months of the appellant's existence. The force of this fact would be greater in my mind if the question was whether the appellant intended to carry on the business of moneylending. But given that intention, which as I have said, I regard as fully established by other evidence, I do not regard the fact that there was no immediate repetition of the lending of money or that there was a considerable break before there was any continuity in the making of loans as definitive of the question whether the business of lending money was being carried on at the time of the advance to Jubilee."

42. In AVCO Financial Services Limited v. FCT 82 ATC 4246 at 4251, his Honour, Gibbs CJ, explained the concept of a money lender and the treatment of foreign exchange losses:



"Where a taxpayer carries on the business of borrowing and lending money, the moneys used for that purpose are analogous to trading stock - the taxpayer in effect deals in the money. Exchange gains and losses, regularly and frequently made and incurred, in the course of making repayments of borrowed money which is used by a taxpayer in making loans in the course of its finance business are outgoings made in the day to day conduct of the business and for the purpose of carrying on the business as a going concern. The first matter to be considered, in deciding whether a payment is of a capital or of a revenue nature, is what was the character of the advantage sought by the payment: Sun Newspapers Ltd. and Associated Newspapers Ltd. V. F.C. of T., at p.363. The question has to be considered from a practical and business point of view: see F.C. of T. v. South Australian Battery Makers Pty. Ltd. 78 ATC 4412 at p.4419; [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 C.L.R. 645, at p.659, and cases there cited. From that point of view, the additional moneys paid as a result of the unfavourable exchange variations - the exchange losses - were part of the price by which the appellant obtained the money which it used to make a profit - part of the process by which the appellant obtained regular returns. The payments were recurrent and frequent, although irregular, and they involve the exercise of judgment by the officers of the appellant who put its borrowing policy into effect as part of the conduct of the business. The exchange losses were in my opinion losses on revenue account, and of course the gains have the same character. The view which I expressed in Commercial and General Acceptance Ltd. V. F.C. of T., at ATC p. 4377; C.L.R. p. 377, that an exchange gain or loss on the repayment of moneys lent will always be a capital gain or loss, must, on reconsideration, be rejected. In a case such as the present the gains and losses do not have the same character as the repayments that produced them, and, considered separately, but in the light of all the circumstances, are seen to be revenue in character."

43. The applicant submits that it is unnecessary to show that the ETCCT was a money lender as a matter of business but it is sufficient if it is shown that the loss was incurred as an integral part of the operations of the trust which gave rise to regular returns of income.



44. In FCT v Marshall and Brougham Pty Ltd 87 ATC 4522, the Full Federal Court considered the deductibility of an amount of money placed on deposit by the taxpayer, with a finance company which subsequently collapsed with no prospect of being able to repay the funds. The dispute centred on the question of whether the loss was of capital or of a capital nature pursuant to subsection 51(1). There was no argument that the loss fell within the positive limbs. The question was whether the loss was of capital or of a capital nature. The Court found the loss to be an allowable deduction pursuant to subsection 51(1). The taxpayer carried on business as a building contractor, as well as banker for a group of companies in the industry. Bowen CJ described more fully the taxpayer's position with the group:



". . . the taxpayer, in addition to or as part of its role as the management company, was also the 'banker' to the group. It maintained the only creditors' ledger, paid all accounts, and received all progress payments . . . " (at 4524)



. . .



So far as the taxpayer was concerned its own profit was derived not from the building contract into which it enters, but from management and related charges and interest received from moneys which passed through its hands as 'bankers' for the group." (At 4525)



45. Bowen CJ thought the circumstances could be best described as "more an integral part of the whole business carried on by the taxpayer in its management of the affairs of the group, rather than a separate enterprise or a separate use of capital assets of its own, . . . " (at 4528)



46. In Lombard Australia Ltd v FCT 80 ATC 4151, Powell J relied on the reasoning of Mason J (as he then was) in Commercial & General Acceptance Ltd v. FCT (1977) 137 CLR 373 at 383-4 to come to the conclusion at 4172 that:



". . . , if the principal purpose of the borrowing was part of the process of operation of the then taxpayer, by means of which process it obtained regular returns, then the disputed exchange gain ought to have been brought to charge."



Further at 4174, concluded that:



' . . . the taxpayer's business was that of a dealer in credit, its profit being derived from the margin between, on the one hand, the cost to it of obtaining 'holding', 'selling', 'protecting' and collecting the funds (or credit) which it had or which it borrowed, and, on the other, the interest charged to, and received from, its customers.



If this be so, then, so it seems to me, the application by the taxpayer of the funds from time to time borrowed by it cannot be described as expenditure on the structure within which profits were to be earned; rather, the application of those funds by on-lending them was an integral part of the process by which the taxpayer earned its income. It follows, in my view, that the borrowed funds of the taxpayer, wherever borrowed, so applied by the taxpayer ought to be categorized as circulating capital."



47. The Supreme Court of Victoria in FCT v. Marbray Nominees Pty Ltd 85 ATC 4750, in part considered the deductibility of an early repayment penalty relating to a loan entered into by the corporate trustee taxpayer. The trustee was found to be carrying on the business of primary production. The loan was used to assist in the purchase of the farm property. That is, it provided in effect working capital to operate the farm. The application of a substantial part of the loan moneys to discharge liabilities antecedently incurred to the family, the beneficiary of the trust, did not alter the conclusion.



48. Tadgell J at 4756 considered that:



"The attribution to an outgoing of the nature of capital on the one hand or revenue on the other again involves an exercise in characterization. A price to be paid for the surrender of a capital asset will ordinarily be regarded as attributable to capital account because, in the general course of commerce, the benefit to be derived from the surrender is appropriately to be treated as a charge on capital. If, however, an outgoing is fairly to be seen as a loss or an expense necessarily incidental to the continuing conduct of the business, and not as providing an accretion to fixed capital, it will ordinarily be inappropriate to charge it to capital account."



49. Crawford v. FCT 93 ATC 5234 related to whether deductions claimed for charges incurred in respect of a bank bill facility were allowable. The headnote from the report summarises the facts:



"The taxpayer, a medical practitioner, was the chairman and controlling mind of a corporate trustee of a trust of which he and his family were beneficiaries. The taxpayer negotiated the transfer of a loan of $460,000, which had been obtained by way of a bank bill facility, from the trustee to himself. The moneys were then lent back to the trust for use in an industrial estate development. In consequence of the loan, the trustee resolved to pay interest to the taxpayer to reimburse him for the 'interest' paid to the bank and also to pay him the first $500,000 of profit from the realisation of the industrial estate development. However, no payments were made by the trustee to the taxpayer as it had no funds to finance such payments. The taxpayer claimed deductions for the charges incurred in respect of the bank bill facility."



50. Hill J, with whom Von Doussa J agreed, when looking at the circumstance that no assessable income was ultimately derived noted ( at 5240):



"What is significant, where no income is derived, is the connection between the loss or outgoing claimed and the activities which are directed at the gaining or producing of assessable income: Ronpibon Tin [(1949) [1949] HCA 15; 78 CLR 47]."



51. His Honour dealt with the 'losses and outgoings on a bill acceptance facility as holding the same commercial substance as interest for present purposes". (at 5240)



52. After discussing the reasoning of the High Court in Fletcher & Ors v FCT 91 ATC 4950; [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1, his Honour came to the conclusion at 5241:



"Where moneys are borrowed and onlent at a rate of interest no less than that incurred on the borrowing, it will ordinarily be the case that this objective circumstance without more will serve to characterise the outgoing as incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, even where no assessable income is in fact derived in the year of income. This process of characterisation would necessarily assume that the agreement to pay interest on the funds onlent was a real and not a show agreement."



53. There has been no suggestion by the respondent that the agreements to pay interest in the situation before me was in any way a sham. Mr Hack did submit that the attempt to show that ETCCT carried on business as a moneylender was an expost facto reconstruction. That, however, is another matter.



54. The applicant sought to make an analogy with the circumstances in Metal Exploration Ltd v. FCT 86 ATC 4505. There the taxpayer was a public company engaged in mining. The taxpayer was obliged, if requested, to lend particular merchant banks an amount of money equal to the amount outstanding pursuant to loans made by the banks to a subsidiary of the taxpayer. The banks were to pay to the taxpayer an interest rate 1/8% lower than the rate paid by the subsidiary. Had the taxpayer been required to make the loans it would have had to borrow over $5 million. In order to release it from these obligations the taxpayer made payments to the banks. A deduction was claimed for the payments.



55. Murphy J, in the Supreme Court of Victoria, held that the original agreements were entered into by the taxpayer in the course of its business and for the purpose of gaining or producing income and "for the purpose of ensuring that dividends from the operations of its wholly owned subsidiary would force it to in the normal course". (at 4508). It followed therefore that "the resultant outgoing or loss was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or attempting to do so". (at 4509) (emphasis added).



56. In determining the question of the correct characterisation of the payments his Honour considered the source of the payments ie "out of recirculating funds" (cf borrowed funds). Further the purpose of the payments "to avoid the risk of incurring anticipated increasing high interest payments to which it would otherwise be exposed each year, which interest payments would constitute a high drain on the taxpayer's assessable income from other business sources." (at 4510). It was important that "no asset or advantage for the enduring benefit of the taxpayer's business was created by the transactions" but rather an expensive method of carrying on the business was brought to an end.



57. This last comment describes well the intention of the group in entering into the foreign exchange line. An expensive method of carrying on the businesses was sought to be avoided. That the plan backfired is irrelevant. I am satisfied that the ETCCT entered into the loan arrangements for the purpose of deriving assessable income and for the purpose of reducing the cost of borrowed funds to the group. The losses are attributable to the sum borrowed but were incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income, and I so find. I also find that the exchange losses relate to the principal and not the interest. The ETCCT did not carry on business as a moneylender, and I so find. It merely facilitated the arrangements for the reduction of the group's interest costs by entering into the Swiss francs loan. No profit was intended to be made from the on-lending at the time but clearly assessable income was to be derived. In all the circumstances the loss was in respect of the principal and was therefore of a capital nature because it has not been established to my satisfaction that the ETCCT had any intention other than as a facilitator when it organised the Swiss loan. At the time the loan was arranged the ETCCT was not carrying on business as a moneylender and it has not done so since that time, and I so find.



58. For these reasons, the objection decision under review will be affirmed.
