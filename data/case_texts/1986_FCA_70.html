Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia Ex Parte: Just Jeans Pty Ltd [1986] FCA 70 (17 March 1986)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

Ex Parte: JUST JEANS PTY. LTD.

No. V G37 of 1986

Prerogative Writs

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Northrop J.

CATCHWORDS

Prerogative Writs - mandamus - refusal by Federal Commissioner of Taxation to repay overpaid tax to the taxpayer - whether public duty imposed on Commissioner - discretion to refuse mandamus if other remedies available.

Judiciary Act 1903 s.39B

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss.2,4,16.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.8,170,173,193,195,199,201,202,204,206,209

Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1984 s. 108

Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act 1983 s.9

Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 s.29

Federal Court of Australia Rules 0.52 r.17

Batagol v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 C.L.R. 243

The Queen v. The Commissioners of Inland Revenue; In Re Nathan (1884) 12 Q.B.D. 461

The Queen v. Commissioners for Special Purposes of the Income Tax (1888) 21 Q.B.D. 313

G.H. Mitchell & Sons (Australia) Pty. Ltd. v. Minister of Works (1974) 8 S.A.S.R. 7

The King v. Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration; Ex parte Ozone Theatres (Aust.) Ltd. [1949] HCA 33; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 389

The Queen v. Foster; Ex parte The Commonwealth Steamship Owners' Association [1953] HCA 86; (1953) 88 C.L.R. 549

HEARING

MELBOURNE

17:3:1986

ORDER

The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia refund to the Prosecutor, Just Jeans Pty. Ltd., the amount of $3,413,126.86 being income tax overpaid by the Prosecutor under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for the year of income ended the 30th June 1980, the period of 13 months ended the 31st July 1981 and the years of income ended the 31st July 1982, the 31st July 1983 and the 31st July 1984 and that the said Commissioner pay to the Prosecutor the amount of $805,823.71 being interest upon the said overpaid income tax payable under the provisions of the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act 1983.

The Respondent pay to the Prosecutor the Prosecutor's costs (including reserved costs) of this application.

(Settlement and entry of Orders is dealt with in 0.36 of the

Rules of Court.)

DECISION

Just Jeans Pty. Ltd., "the taxpayer", is seeking a writ of mandamus directing the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia, "the Commissioner", to refund the amount of tax overpaid by the taxpayer in respect of five years of income ended 30 June 1980, 31 July 1981, 31 July 1982, 31 July 1983 and 31 July 1984 respectively, and to pay interest on such overpayments. The matter comes before this Court by reason of s.39B of the Judiciary Act 1903 which confers original jurisdiction on the Federal Court with respect to any matter in which a writ of mandamus or prohibition or an injunction is sought against an officer of the Commonwealth. The Commissioner is an officer of the Commonwealth within the meaning of that section; see s.4 of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, "the Administration Act".

2. The facts upon which the writ of mandamus is sought are not in dispute but the Commissioner strongly opposes the granting of the writ. Pursuant to the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, "the Assessment Act", the Commissioner issued and served five notices of assessment of tax on the taxpayer with respect to each of the five relevant tax years. The taxpayer objected to each of the assessments but the Commissioner disallowed each of the objections. The taxpayer requested the Commissioner to treat each of his objections as an appeal and to forward each objection to the Supreme Court of Victoria. Despite the pending appeals, the taxpayer was required to pay and in fact paid the amounts of tax specified in the five assessments.

3. The five appeals to the Supreme Court were contained in two separate proceedings which were heard together. By order made on 21 February 1986, the Supreme Court, constituted by Ormiston J., allowed each appeal. Similar orders were made with respect to each appeal. With respect to each appeal, the substance of the order was that the assessment "be and is hereby amended by excising from the taxable income of the Appellant (taxpayer) the amount of . . . and by reducing the tax payable by the Appellant (taxpayer) to . . .". The Court also dismissed an application by the Commissioner for a stay of each order pending appeal. As a result of the orders of the Supreme Court, the taxpayer's liability to tax was reduced by $3,413,126.24.

4. Under sub-section 199(1) of the Assessment Act, the Supreme Court hearing an appeal may make "such order as it thinks fit, and may by such order confirm, reduce, increase or vary the assessment". In many cases, a Supreme Court, in allowing an appeal, makes an order remitting the matter to the Commissioner for reassessment in accordance with the judgment of the Court; see the first part of sub-section 199(1). An order of that kind does not vary the assessment. In the present appeals, the Supreme Court made orders "amending" each of the five assessments by "reducing the tax payable" by the taxpayer to a specified amount.

5. Following the making of the orders by the Supreme Court, the taxpayer requested the Commissioner to refund the amount of tax overpaid. The taxpayer relied upon s.202 of the Assessment Act, the relevant parts of which are set out:-

"202. (1) Where, by reason of an alteration of

an assessment on an appeal or reference, a person's

liability to tax is reduced -

(a) the amount by which the tax is so

reduced shall be taken, for the purposes

of section 207, never to have been

payable; and

(b) the Commissioner shall -

(i) refund the amount of any tax

overpaid; or

. . .

(2) Where, by reason of an alteration of an

assessment on an appeal or reference, a person's

liability to tax is increased, the amount of the

increased tax is recoverable from the person.

. . . ".

6. Section 202, in its present form, was inserted into the Assessment Act by s.108 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1984 and came into operation on 14 December 1984. Prior to that date, s.202 was as follows:-

"202. If the assessment is altered on the

appeal or reference a due adjustment shall be made,

for which purpose amounts paid in excess shall be

refunded, and amounts short paid shall be

recoverable as arrears."

7. The Commissioner refused to refund the amount of tax overpaid by the taxpayer on the basis that the Commissioner had a policy of not repaying tax paid until the final determination of any appeals and that in any event, the matter was in the hands of the Court, a writ having been issued in the Supreme Court of Victoria on behalf of the taxpayer seeking payment of the amount of tax overpaid by the taxpayer together with interest thereon. It should be noted that on 27 February 1986, a writ was issued out of the Supreme Court of Victoria in which the taxpayer claimed against the Commissioner the sum of $3,413,126.24 together with interest pursuant to the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act 1983 which, to 27 February 1986, amounted to $722,106.64. The Commissioner has entered an appearance to that writ. It should be noted further that on 6 March 1986, the Commissioner filed notices of appeal in this Court from the orders made by Ormiston J. except the orders refusing the application for a stay pending appeals. The notices of appeal were served on the taxpayer on 11 March 1986. This Court has power to grant a stay of orders pending appeals; see s.29 Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 and 0.52 r.17, but there is nothing to suggest that such a stay has been sought.

8. On 6 March 1986 this Court ordered that the Commissioner show cause on 12 March 1986 why the writ of mandamus should not issue. The hearing of the matter took place on 12 March 1986 and at the conclusion of submissions the Court announced that it would order the issue of the writ of mandamus, that it would publish its reasons on 17 March 1986 and hear submissions as to the form of the order to be made.

9. Before referring to the submissions, reference should be made to s.16 of the Administration Act. In its present form, that section was first inserted into the Administration Act at the same time and by the same Act that inserted s.202, in its current form, into the Assessment Act. The relevant parts of s.16 of the Administration Act are set out:-

"16(1) Where the Commissioner is required or

permitted to pay an amount to a person by or under

a provision of a taxation law other than -

(a) a general administration provision; or

(b) a provision prescribed for the purposes

of this paragraph,

the amount is payable out of the Consolidated

Revenue Fund, which is appropriated accordingly.

. . . ".

The Assessment Act is a taxation law; see the definition of "taxation law" contained in s.2 of the Administration Act and s.8 of the Assessment Act.

10. The submissions on behalf of the taxpayer were simple. The Commissioner is an officer of the Commonwealth. By reason of s.202 of the Assessment Act he is under a public duty to refund to the taxpayer the amount of tax overpaid by the taxpayer. By reason of s. 16 of the Administration Act, the amount to be refunded is payable out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund which is appropriated accordingly. The Commissioner has failed to perform his public duty. The writ of mandamus should issue to compel him to perform that duty.

11. Counsel for the Commissioner did not dispute that in an appropriate case a writ of mandamus could be issued against the Commissioner, but submitted that in this case, the writ should not issue. He contended that s. 202 of the Assessment Act did not impose a duty on the Commissioner to refund the amounts claimed by the taxpayer. He contended that in the opening words of sub-section 202(1) words should be inserted so that the sub-section should read:-

"Where, by reason of an alteration of an assessment

on an appeal or reference from which no appeal has

been brought, a person's liability to tax is

reduced . . .".

The words underlined are the words which counsel contended should be inserted.

12. In my opinion, there is no basis to support that contention. In form, the opening words of that sub-section are not ambiguous. A consideration of relevant provisions of the Act disclose a clear scheme by which tax is to be paid. Under s.204 of the Assessment Act, tax assessed is due and payable on the date specified in the assessment, or if no date is specified, on the thirtieth day after service of the notice. Under s.208, when tax becomes due and payable, it is a debt due to the Commonwealth and is payable to the Commissioner. Under s. 209, unpaid tax may be sued for and recovered by the Commissioner suing in his official name. Under s. 201, the fact that an appeal or reference is pending under Division 2 of Part V of the Assessment Act does not interfere with or affect the assessment the subject of the appeal or reference and tax may be recovered by the Commissioner on the assessment as if no appeal or reference were pending. Nevertheless, under s. 206, the Commissioner may grant an extension of time for payment of tax in which case the tax becomes due and payable according to the terms of the extension granted.

13. The combined provisions of sub-section 202(1) of the Assessment Act and s. 16 of the Administration Act constitute an obverse scheme to apply where, by reason of an alteration of an assessment on an appeal or reference, liability to tax is reduced. This scheme came into operation on 14 December 1984. In sub-section 202(1), the words "by reason of an alteration of an assessment on an appeal or reference" indicate that the sub-section applies to all cases when the assessment is altered, whether by the Court or the Board of Review or by the Commissioner following a remit from the Court. It is interesting to note that a Board of Review has no power to remit; see s. 193 and sub-section 195(1) of the Assessment Act. The detailed provisions of the Assessment Act constituting the scheme for the payment and recovery of tax and in particular the provisions of s. 201 are to be contrasted with the absence of similar provisions constituting the obverse scheme. The Court should not rewrite the scheme provided by the legislation.

14. Under s.202 of the Assessment Act, any reduction or increase in tax must be as a result of an alteration of an assessment on an appeal or reference. There must be an altered assessment in existence before s. 202 has any operation. The altered assessment may be an amended assessment made by the Commissioner under sections 170 and in particular sub-section 170(3), and 173 of the Act. The assessment may be altered directly by the order of a Court or a Board of Review. Counsel for the Commissioner contended that having regard to the provisions of the Assessment Act, no assessment could have any effect until notice of an alteration of an assessment, or rather the amended assessment, had been given. Counsel referred to s. 204 of the Assessment Act and argued that notice of an assessment had to be given before consequences flowed from it. In substance, the submission amounted to a contention that the Commissioner had to give himself notice of the altered assessment before sub-section 202(1) applied. He relied upon opinions expressed by Kitto J. in Batagol v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243 at pp 252-3 when considering the ascertainment of tax due and payable on an assessment. Those opinions have no application to the present case. In my opinion, this submission by counsel for the Commissioner must fail.

15. Next, counsel contended that in reality, the taxpayer was seeking an order that the Commonwealth refund the amount by which the tax was reduced. He submitted that mandamus will not go to compel the Crown to pay money. He relied upon views expressed in The Queen v. The Commissioners of Inland Revenue; In Re Nathan (1884) 12 QBD 461. In that case, the relevant statutory provision was that where executors or administrators had proved to the satisfaction of the Commissioners that they had paid certain moneys which had the effect of reducing the value of the estate of the deceased as a result of which stamp duty paid would be reduced, the Commissioners were required to return the difference. The applicants obtained a writ of mandamus requiring the Commissioners to pay the difference to them but the Court of Appeal allowed an appeal from that order. One of the grounds for allowing the appeal was that the Commissioners owed no duty to the applicants. At p. 471, Brett M.R. expressed the opinion that the money had been paid not to the Commissioners in their own right, either officially or individually, but was paid to the Crown for the use of the Crown, it being paid to them merely as the servants and agents of the Crown and that they had no right to deal with it as against the orders of the Crown and in those circumstances, the Commissioners had no duty towards the applicants to refund the moneys. Any action should be by way of petition of right against the Crown. The Master of the Rolls summarised his opinion, namely that mandamus did not lie because the Commissioners were servants of the Crown. Bowen L.J. was of a similar opinion. At p. 478 he adverted to the fact that the Commissioners, having paid the money over to the Crown, possibly did not and probably would not have funds in their hands to satisfy the demand. He said "In substance the moneys have been paid, not to the commissioners, but to the Crown". As a result, he doubted whether under any circumstances, mandamus would be the right remedy.

16. The difference between a mandamus directing the payment of money by the Crown and a mandamus directing the doing of something as a condition precedent to the recovery of money from the Crown is illustrated by The Queen v. Commissioners for Special Purposes of the Income Tax (1888) 21 QBD 313. In that case, the Court of Appeal allowed an appeal and granted mandamus. At p. 322, Lindley L.J. said:-

"With regard to the question whether mandamus

would lie, I have looked into the authorities and I

come to the conclusion that the case is one in

which it will lie. It is no doubt difficult to

draw the line, and some of the cases are not easy

to reconcile: but I think that the counsel for the

applicants were right in saying that the

application is not to enforce payment of money by

the Crown, but to enforce the making of an order by

the Commissioners which it is the duty of the

Commissioners to make, and without which the

repayment cannot be obtained. I think the case

comes within the principle of the cases of Reg. v.

Lords of the Treasury 16 QB 357, and Reg. v.

Commissioners of Woods and Forests 15 Q.B. 767, and

not within the cases cited for the Crown. For

these reasons I think the appeal should be

allowed."

17. In all cases it is necessary to consider the statute imposing the duty on the officer of the Commonwealth, in the present case, the terms of sub-section 202(1) of the Assessment Act. The provisions of s. 16 of the Administration Act remove the difficulties referred to by Bowen L.J. In Re Nathan. It is true that the tax paid was a debt due to the Commonwealth but payable to the Commissioner. Unpaid tax may be sued for and recovered by the Commissioner suing in his official name. Sub-section 202(1) imposes a duty on the Commissioner which, on its face, is a duty owed to the taxpayer. The appropriation made by s. 16 of the Administration Act supports the view that the duty is to the taxpayer.

18. The true position has been stated by Bray C.J. in G.H. Mitchell & Sons (Australia) Pty. Ltd. v. Minister of Works (1974) 8 SASR 7 at p 14:-

"1. Mandamus will not lie against the Crown

or against a Minister of the Crown when acting

purely as a servant of the Crown, but it may go

against him where he is charged with the

performance of some statutory duty and the

applicant for mandamus is entitled to have an act

done in the discharge of that duty without which he

cannot enforce or enjoy some right which he

possesses. As it is sometimes put, mandamus will

lie against a Minister when he is acting, not

simply under a duty to the Crown as its servant,

but as a persona designata. Many cases, I think,

establish this proposition from R. v. Commissioner

of Woods & Forests [1850] EngR 697; (1850) 15 QB 761 (117 ER

646) to Ex parte Cornford; Re Minister of Education

(1962) SR (NSW) 220. Many of the Australian

cases are referred to in Benjafield and Whitman,

Principles of Australian Administrative Law 4th ed.

(1971) at pp. 214-215."

19. In the present case, it is my opinion that, upon the proper construction of sub-section 202(1) of the Assessment Act, the Commissioner, when performing the duty imposed upon him by that sub-section, is not acting as a servant or agent of the Crown, but is acting as a persona designata. Accordingly, mandamus will lie to compel him to perform that duty.

20. If mandamus would otherwise lie, counsel for the Commissioner submitted that as a matter of discretion, the Court should not grant mandamus on the facts of this case. There is no doubt that as a matter of discretion, a Court may withhold the remedy. For example, the existence of another more convenient and satisfactory remedy may be a discretionary bar to the granting of mandamus; see for example, The King v. Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration; Ex parte Ozone Theatres (Aust.) Ltd. [1949] HCA 33; (1949) 78 CLR 389 per Latham C.J., Rich, Dixon, McTiernan and Webb JJ. at p 400. In the past, this discretion was exercised fairly readily, see for example, In Re Nathan, above. In more recent times, less weight is given to the existence of alternative remedies. Thus, in The Queen v. Foster; Ex parte The Commonwealth Steamship Owners' Association [1953] HCA 86; (1953) 88 CLR 549, the High Court, constituted by Dixon C.J., Webb, Fullagar, Kitto and Taylor JJ. granted mandamus because possible alternative remedies available to the prosecution did not "confer an equally convenient, beneficial and effective remedy as mandamus".

21. Under the existing procedures of this Court, mandamus is a simple, convenient, beneficial, effective and speedy remedy which should be granted despite the possible existence of other remedies.

22. In the present case, counsel for the Commissioner referred to the Supreme Court proceedings commenced by the taxpayer to recover the amount of overpaid tax. He contended that this demonstrated the existence of another remedy and that it also showed that the taxpayer was pursuing that remedy. Counsel for the taxpayer gave an undertaking to the Court that if mandamus is issued, the taxpayer would forthwith file notice of discontinuance in the Supreme Court proceedings. The Court accepts that undertaking. In any event, serious questions arise as to whether those proceedings are properly founded. Any action should be against the Commonwealth, not the Commissioner. The orders of Ormiston J. have determined the amount of tax to be refunded to the taxpayer. The duty to refund is imposed upon the Commissioner. If judgment was obtained against the Commissioner, the taxpayer would be in no better position than it is today. In the exercise of my discretion, I would not refuse mandamus based on the possible existence of alternative remedies.

23. Counsel contended further that by the notices of appeal the Commissioner may wish to challenge the form of the orders made by Ormiston J. Sub-section 202(1) of the Assessment Act refers to an "alteration" of an assessment. The orders "amended" the assessments by "reducing" the tax payable. Under sub-section 199(1) of the Assessment Act, the Supreme Court had power by order to "confirm, reduce, increase or vary the assessment" (underlining added). Counsel contended that in the Assessment Act the word "amended" is a term of art referring to the power of the Commissioner to amend an assessment. Counsel referred to sections 170 and 173 of the Assessment Act. He contended that the Supreme Court had no power to "amend" an assessment but was limited to "altering" an assessment. He contended that if mandamus was granted, the Commissioner would lose the opportunity to challenge, on the appeals, the form of the orders made by the Supreme Court.

24. In my opinion, there is no substance in this submission. In reality, the tax to be paid by the taxpayer has been reduced by reason of an alteration of an assessment on an appeal. It is immaterial whether that reduction is described as an "amendment" or an "alteration". This contention is rejected.

25. There remains for consideration the matter of the interest claimed by the taxpayer. Under s. 9 of the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act 1983 the Commissioner is required to pay interest on amounts of tax overpaid by a taxpayer. The relevant provisions of the section are:-

"9. (1) Subject to sections 11 and 12, where -

(a) an amount of relevant tax is paid by a

person to the Commissioner (in this

sub-section referred to as the 'amount

paid'); and

(b) as a result of a decision to which this

Act applies, the whole or a part of the

amount paid is overpaid by the person and

is refunded to the person . . .

interest calculated in accordance with

sub-sections (2) and (3) and section 10 is payable

to the person in respect of -

. . .

(d) in a case where a part of the amount paid

is so refunded . . . - the part of the

amount paid so refunded . . . ".

26. It is not disputed that where the Commissioner refunds part of an amount of tax pursuant to s. 202(1) of the Assessment Act, he is required to pay interest on the amount of the part so refunded and that s. 16 of the Administration Act applies to the payment of that interest. It was accepted that interest was to be paid at the rate of 14.026%.

27. Counsel for the Commissioner contended that the Commissioner was under no duty or obligation to pay interest under s. 9 of the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act until after he had refunded the amount of tax overpaid. He contended that even if mandamus issued to direct the payment of the amount of overpaid tax, mandamus should not direct the payment of interest since the Commissioner was under no present duty to pay interest. He contended that the payment of interest should be made in the usual course of administration.

28. In my opinion, that contention should be rejected. The purpose of s.9 is to provide a formula by which the amount of interest to be paid can be calculated. The section imposes a continuing duty to pay interest in the circumstances stated, the amount of the interest to be determined by reference to the date the payment of overpaid tax is made. I see no reason why mandamus should not issue to compel the payment of interest.
