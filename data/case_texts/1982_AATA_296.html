Re Brian Michael Stoddart and Commissioner of Superannuation [1982] AATA 296 (21 December 1982)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: BRIAN MICHAEL STODDART

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A82/26

Commonwealth Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr Allan N. Hall (Senior Member)

Dr A. Marsh (Member)

Mr D.S. Craik (Member)

CATCHWORDS

commonwealth superannuation _ former Federal Police Officer _ invalidity retirement benefits _ now resident in the United Kingdom _ whether health had become 'so restored' as to enable performance of clerical or any other duties _ whether Public Service Board should be requested to find applicant suitable employment in Australia or the United Kingdom _ decision reviewed in applicant's absence

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.74(1), 75, 154

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, s.40(1)(b)

Words and phrases _ 'restored'

HEARING

CANBERRA

21:12:1982

ORDER

1. The Tribunal varies the decision under review by:

(i) adding the words 'in the United Kingdom' at the end of that decision; and

(ii)deleting the reference to 'duties of a guard or attendant' as duties suitable to be performed by the applicant, those being the words added to the original decision made on 25 August 1980 after reconsideration by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation.

2. In all other respects the decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

1. Mr Brian Michael Stoddart, a member of the Australian Federal Police Force, was retired on the ground of invalidity on 9 June 1978. His incapacity was certified by a Commonwealth Medical Officer as being due to 'hiatus hernia', 'fracture of the left patella and resultant pain', 'anxiety' and 'mild hypertension'.

2. The evidence is that on 9 January 1978 the applicant requested an interview with a Commonwealth Medical Officer with a view to being certified as fit to work only on the day shift. He complained that his hiatus hernia was always worse if he worked on the afternoon or night shift and that he suffered from arthritis of his left kneecap. He considered that further shift work would be detrimental to his health.

3. The applicant was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer in Adelaide on 16 January 1978, who certified (albeit after some apparent indecision) that the applicant was unfit for employment either in his present duties as a police officer or in any other duties and that he was therefore unfit for further service and should be retired. The Commonwealth Medical Officer added, however, that the applicant should be reassessed for his fitness for return to duty in January 1980. We have no evidence as to the events which transpired between the date of this medical examination and the applicant's retirement on 9 June 1978. We were told that the matter did not come under the administrative responsibility of the Commissioner for Superannuation until after the applicant's retirement.

4. By letter dated 5 July 1978, addressed to Mr Stoddart at his place of residence in Athelstone, South Australia (Exhibit T4, p19), the Commissioner wrote explaining the applicant's invalidity benefit entitlements. Included with the letter was a summary of the provisions of the Superannuation Act 1976 relating to invalid pensions. We were told the summary included information with respect to ss.74 and 75 of the Superannuation Act 1976, the provisions of which, so far as relevant, are as follows:

'74. (1) The Commissioner may, by notice in writing given to a person to whom invalidity pension is payable but who has not attained the age of 65 years, require the person _

(a) to submit himself for medical examination by a medical practitioner at a time and place specified in the notice; or

...

75. (1) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, after receiving the report or reports of a medical practitioner or medical practitioners with respect to the health of a person to whom invalidity pension is payable, that the health of the person has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are, in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant), the Commissioner shall so inform such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner.

(2) If _

(a) the pensioner is offered by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority employment (not being employment on a part-time basis) that involves the performance of duties that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, are suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant); and

(b) the person unreasonably refuses or fails to accept the offer within 14 days after the receipt by him of the offer, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows,

the Commissioner may cancel the person's entitlement to invalidity pension."

5. Whilst the applicant should have been aware that his medical condition might be reviewed periodically (cf. Exhibit T4/44), there is no evidence that he was advised of the specific recommendation that his condition should be reviewed in January 1980. On the contrary, it appears that the applicant, in ignorance of that specific recommendation and the implications which it entailed, decided to return to the United Kingdom from whence he had migrated in 1967, in order to be nearer to the elderly parents of himself and his wife.

6. By letter dated 31 January 1980, the Commissioner for Superannuation gave notice pursuant to s.74(1)(a) of the Act to Mr Stoddart (who by then was living at Ilfracombe, North Devon in the United Kingdom) that a further medical examination was required. A medical examination was conducted by Dr D. Dodds, a Commonwealth Medical Officer in Devon on 29 February 1980, following which a decision was made by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 25 August 1980 pursuant to s.75(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 as follows:

'I, Kevin Lawrence Hassett, the occupant of the position of Clerk Class 8 Medical Sub-section, position number 234 and hereby delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, am satisfied, after receiving the report of Dr D. Dodds, oversighted by the Commonwealth Medical Officer Dr Edgely that the health of Brian Michael Stoddart (to whom an invalidity pension is payable) has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind which are suitable to be performed by him, namely, those of a clerical nature and I direct that the Office of the Public Service Board be informed with a view to finding him suitable re-employment.'

7. By letter dated 27 October 1980 (Exhibit T4, p32) the applicant requested reconsideration of the delegate's decision. Further medical investigations ensued, including the obtaining of reports from the applicant's general practitioner, Dr M.T. Fenner of Ilfracombe, Devon (Exhibit T4, p49); a report of a further examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer (Dr R. Cumming) in London on 9 February 1982 (Exhibit T4, p60) and specialist reports from Dr Peter J. Scott, a consultant orthopaedic surgeon dated 23 December 1981, from Dr J.N. MacCaig, a consultant physician dated 23 December 1981 and Dr Simon A. Brooks, a consultant psychiatrist dated 29 January 1982. Having considered this further evidence, the Commissioner confirmed the earlier decision in substance but varied it in detail by adding that in addition to duties of a clerical nature he was of the opinion that the duties of a guard or attendant were also suitable to be performed by Mr Stoddart.

8. The applicant applied to this Tribunal for review of the Commissioner's decision as varied following reconsideration (see s.154 of the Act). As he now resides in the United Kingdom he requested that the Tribunal should review the decision in his absence (see s.40(1)(b) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975). The Commissioner for Superannuation very properly raised no objection to that course of action. In the circumstances the Tribunal agreed to accede to the applicant's request. The applicant was given a final opportunity to lodge further medical or other evidence if he so wished, but that opportunity was not availed of. In the result the Tribunal reviewed the Commissioner's decision on the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (a copy of which had been made available to the applicant) and having regard to the information contained in correspondence to the Tribunal from the applicant and his wife.

9. The substance of the grounds upon which the applicant relied in his various letters (initially to the Commissioner for Superannuation and later to the Tribunal) was that his basic medical condition had not improved since the time of his retirement from the Police Force and that in the circumstances the Commissioner could not have been satisfied that his health had become 'so restored' as to enable him to perform clerical or any other duties. He claimed that following receipt of advice of the original decision made on 25 August 1980 that he was fit for clerical duties, he had attempted to obtain employment in the United Kingdom, but 'because of health reasons' he was unsuccessful (see Exhibit T1, p5).

10. The applicant's greatest concern, however, appears to have been at the possibility that he might be offered a position within the Commonwealth Government Service in Australia. In that event, he would face what he described as 'catastrophic consequences' for his family (see Exhibit T11 _ letter dated 5 July 1982). He explained his problem in a letter to the Commissioner dated 27 October 1980 (Exhibit T4 p37) as follows:

'If you are able to now place me in a permanent daily job suitable to my medical condition ... I will be forced to leave my wife and children in England. My son is 15 1/2 years and is attending his last year at school, and is be taking his final exams in 1981.

Both my two children and my wife after settling in the UK for the last two and half years with all their commitments do not now wish to return to Australia, which leaves me in the position, if I have to return, that I will have to return and split up from my family.

I understood when I was invalided out of my job that there was no suitable job available for me. Had I realised that such a situation like this could arise to break up my family, I would not have returned to the UK.

We originally came back to England because my wife's father was dying of cancer, and is now deceased, but my wife and I both still have elderly parents living here, which if we are split up as a family would cause them a lot of stress as well as me.'

The letter dated 6 June 1982 which the Tribunal accepted as the application for review in this matter, indicated that Mr Stoddart's son had commenced a four year apprenticeship early this year.

11. Finally, the applicant claimed that he regarded the variation of the original decision so as to include as suitable employment for him the duties of a guard or attendant to be 'an insult to my previous status as a police officer'.

The Applicant's State of Health

12. In our view, the first question which arises for decision pursuant to s.75(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 is whether the Tribunal can be satisfied, having regard to the medical reports in evidence before it, that the health of the applicant has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are, in the opinion of the Tribunal, suitable to be performed by him, having regard to the matters referred to in that sub-section. The word 'restored' in the context of s.75(1) of the Act conveys the sense of 'brought back to a healthy or vigorous state' (see definition of 'restore' _ meaning 4(c) in The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary). It is certainly capable, in its ordinary meaning, of embracing circumstances in which there has been an improvement in a person's state of health from a former conditon of invalidity. It is also capable, in our view, of embracing cases where the underlying disability conditions may not have changed significantly, but, on fuller medical investigation, the nature and extent of those disabling conditions is seen to be less than was earlier thought to be the case. For s.75(1) to apply, it is not necessary that the pensioner's health must be found to have been brought back to a perfect state. What is required is that it must be seen to be sufficiently restored to enable the pensioner to perform the duties said to be suitable to be performed by him.

13. We have already referred to the disabilities which were found to incapacitate the applicant for work at the time of his retirement on the grounds of invalidity. What we propose to do is to trace briefly the subsequent history of those conditions so as to enable a comparison to be made between the applicant;s state of health at the date of his retirement and his health at all material times since 25 August 1980 when the decision now under review was originally made.

(i) Hiatus Hernia

14. In his report dated 16 January 1978 (Exhibit T4, p17), the Commonwealth Medical Officer reported this condition had caused disabling pain on 'several occasions' and that it was worse at night. The subsequent medical examinations conducted in the United Kingdom (see in particular the report of Dr J.N. MacCaig, dated 23 December 1981 _ Exhibit T4, p62) have confirmed that the applicant has 'a small sliding hiatal hernia with marked gastro-oesophageal reflux'. Dr MacCaig described this condition as being such as 'would normally cause mild to moderate dyspeptic symptoms but would not prevent anybody from undertaking normal working activities with the exclusion of manual labour or heavy lifting which would exacerbate the symptoms. There is disparity between his apparent symptoms and his request for medication which would be expected to relieve them. I would not normally expect a lesion of this type to prevent an individual from earning his living in the normal way. Were the symptoms severe enough, I would have expected him to have sought surgical correction of his hiatal hernia to enable him to return to normal life and activity'.

15. Although, as we earlier indicated, the applicant was given the opportunity by the Tribunal to provide updated medical information from his own treating doctor, he did not do so (see his letter to the Tribunal dated 28 September 1982). We find this rather puzzling as the only medical evidence provided from the treating doctor, Dr M.T. Fenner, is a report dated 31 December 1980 (Exhibit T4, p49) in which Dr Fenner states that he has 'no information' with regard to the applicant's hiatus hernia. In our view this tends to confirm the reservations expressed by Dr MacCaig in his report of 23 December 1981.

(ii) Left Knee

16. The Commonwealth Medical Officer recorded in his report of 16 January 1978 that the applicant had sustained a fracture of the left patella and that he suffered pain in the left knee on standing 'if he worked at night'. Although the applicant has regularly described the condition of his left knee as arthritic (see for example his personal statement dated 29 February 1980 prior to his medical examination by Dr Dodds _ Exhibit T4, p22) there is no confirmation by any medical practitioner that the applicant suffers from arthritis. On the contrary, Dr Dodds recorded 'no signs of arthritis' (Exhibit T4, p23). Dr MacCaig found 'no evidence of arthropathy' (joint disease _ Exhibit 4, p63). Dr Brooks, although recording a complaint of 'occasional discomfort from his hiatus hernia and his knee problem' made the comment:

'He says that he can just about live on his pension and he occupies himself successfully with his family and with a fair amount of fishing and walking. He told me that he would be 'quite content' if he were left alone.'

The reference to a 'fair amount of fishing and walking' does not suggest undue discomfort or inconvenience from the left knee.

17. Dr Scott, a consultant orthopaedic surgeon, in his report dated 23 December 1981, concluded that the applicant has 'non-disabling aching pain in the left knee and a little cruciate laxity in this knee with a history of injury in 1965 and I think he has sufficient laxity to inhibit his athletic performance but not to interfere with normal activities. Subsequent meniscal tears and osteo-arthritis are unusual with this type of ligamentous injury and he shows no evidence of either at the moment' (see Exhibit T4, p61). Significantly (as with the hiatus hernia), Dr Fenner recorded in his report dated 31 December 1980 that he had 'no information' with regard to the applicant's patella fracture.

(iii) Hypertension

18. At the medical examination on 16 January 1978, the applicant was found to have blood pressure readings of 170/110 and (after rest) 150/90. The history given to Dr MacCaig suggests that he had not previously been diagnosed as hypertensive (see Exhibit T4, p62). With the exception only of the reading taken by Dr MacCaig on 23 December 1981 when his blood pressure was 145/75 the other blood pressure readings taken since the applicant's retirement have all shown diastolic pressure in excess of 90 which would be generally regarded as unacceptably high. It may be significant that by the time the applicant saw Dr MacCaig, he had evidently lost two stone in weight in the previous year. When examined by Dr Cumming, however, on 9 February 1982, the applicant's blood pressure readings were again unacceptably high (170/110 and 160/110). In his report dated 31 December 1980, Dr Fenner had referred to the applicant as having 'a slight degree of hypertension but this should affect him in no way and does not require treatment at present'.

(iv) Anxiety State

19. The Commonwealth Medical Officer who examined the applicant on 16 January 1978 referred to him as being disabled by 'anxiety'. He described him as an 'anxious 40 year old male' and recorded the applicant's history as follows:

'Anxiety state seems to be related to his wife and family, particularly at night on their own.'

It should be noted that there was no psychiatric examination of the applicant prior to his retirement.

20. Although in later medical examinations he was described as 'very anxious' (see Dr Dodds' report on 29 February 1980); 'possible anxiety/depression' (see Dr Cumming's report on 9 February 1982), Dr Brooks, who was the only specialist psychiatrist to examine the applicant, appears to have dismissed the symptoms of anxiety as within the bounds of normal reaction to anxiety-provoking circumstances. He concluded his report dated 29 January 1982 (Exhibiit T4, p66) as follows:

'At the time that I saw Mr Stoddart, he had absolutely no evidence of any psychiatric symptoms at all. I would not say that he does now suffer, or has in the recent past suffered, from any sort of clinical depression. His sleep disturbance appears to be not very significant and of the sort that is quite common in people who are otherwise mentally and physically well."

21. Nevertheless, that the applicant does suffer from some physical consequences of his anxious nature seems clear enough from the report of Dr Fenner who reported as follows:

'He has consulted me on a half dozen occasions in the past year since he has been registered with me, mainly with a disturbance of bowel habit which after investigation, I have concluded is due to anxiety and tension.'

Further evidence of the applicant's reaction to anxiety is to be found in the complaints he has made in his correspondence to the Commissioner and to the Tribunal as to the considerable amount of mental stress and anxiety which the threatened alteration of his pension status has caused him. It is no criticism of the Commissioner for Superannuation, but it is unfortunate that in the time which has necessarily elapsed in conducting proper medical investigation of the applicant's condition that the applicant has had to live with the present uncertainties for the past two years. The evidence suggests that the uncertainties have had some adverse effects upon his health.

Capacity for Work

22. So far as the applicant's capacity for work is concerned, Dr Dodds had concluded as a result of his examination on 29 February 1980 that whilst the applicant was not fit to resume his former duties as a policeman, he could satisfactorily perform other duties such as clerical work. That opinion was shared by Dr Cumming, the Commonwealth Medical Officer who examined the applicant on 9 February 1982. It is consistent with the results of the specialist examinations conducted by Dr Scott, Dr MacCaig and Dr Brooks. Finally, it is entirely consistent with Dr Fenner's opinion dated 31 December 1980 when he said 'with regard to his fitness for work I certainly have not certified him unfit for work whilst he has been under my care. As far as I am aware, he is actively seeking employment in this country.'

23. Of the various medical conditions from which the applicant suffers, we consider that the condition most likely to produce incapacitating consequences from time to time is the hiatus hernia. So far as the evidence goes that condition has remained fairly stable since the applicant's retirement. However, if the applicant were able to work during normal daytime working hours, we consider that the risk of incapacitating consequences would not be significant. It is important in this regard to recall that the applicant himself did not regard himself as totally unfit for work at the time when he requested a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer in January 1978. On the contrary, he appears to have considered himself fit to continue on daytime shift duties.

24. If the applicant were able to undertake a basically sedentary job, we see no reason to anticipate that he would suffer incapacitating pain in his left knee. We do not doubt that he has intermittent pain in the knee, but we are not satisfied that he has osteo-arthritis in the knee joint. It would not, in our view, be incapacitating for sedentary clerical work. The applicant's hypertension, although showing an elevated high pattern, appears not to have required medication and does not appear to have concerned the applicant's treating doctor to any extent. On the evidence, it should not inhibit the applicant from performing the type of work to which we have referred.

25. Whether the applicant ever suffered from an anxiety state in the psychiatric sense is difficult to say given that we did not hear evidence from the Commonwealth Medical Officer who examined him on 16 January 1978 and that no specialist psychiatric examination appears to have been undertaken at that time. However, the probabilities are against the applicant having then had a psychiatric disorder. If we were wrong in that conclusion, the evidence of Dr Brooks leaves no room for doubt that in January 1982 the applicant had absolutely no evidence of any psychiatric symptoms. To that extent, there has been an improvement in the applicant's mental health. Such anxiety as the applicant presently complains of appears to be more related (and in the circumstances quite reasonably so) to the possibility that he may be faced with returning to Australia and being separated from his family.

26. On this evidence we find as a fact that the applicant's health has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a clerical nature provided those duties would be capable of being performed during normal day-time working hours. In our opinion, those duties would be suitable to be performed by the applicant having regard to the duties which he performed prior to his retirement. We have considered whether the applicant would be suitable to be employed as a security guard or attendant but in our view the probabilities are that such work would need to be performed outside normal daytime working hours and that such work might introduce elements of stress which, having regard to the applicant's previous medical history, would be inappropriate for him to perform. We would propose, therefore, to vary the decision under review by deleting the duties of a guard or attendant as suitable for the applicant. The question remains as to the country in which work should be sought for the applicant.

Return to Australia?

27. In the latter part of s.75(1) of the Act it is provided that where the Commissioner is satisfied that the health of a person has become so restored as to enable them to perform suitable duties, the Commissioner 'shall so inform such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner'. In the present case, the delegate of the Commissioner directed that the Office of the Public Service Board be informed with a view to finding suitable re-employment for the applicant (see paragraph 6 above). Mr Wilde, who appeared at the hearing on behalf of the Commissioner, informed us that it was the Commissioner's intention initially to approach the Public Service Board with a request that suitable employment might be found for the applicant in the United Kingdom. Only if such an approach proved unsuccessful would the Commissioner give consideration to requesting the Public Service Board to find suitable employment for the applicant in Australia.

28. Section 75(2)(b) of the Act (see paragraph 4 above) provides, in effect, that if a pensioner who has been offered suitable employment by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority, unreasonably refuses or fails to accept the offer within the time allowed under that sub-section, the Commissioner may cancel the person's entitlement to invalidity pension. Mr Wilde submitted that as no offer of employment in Australia had yet been made to the applicant, the question did not arise in these proceedings whether the applicant could reasonably refuse or fail to accept such an offer. To the extent that no action has yet been taken pursuant to s.75(2) of the Act, there is no doubt, in our view, that Mr Wilde was correct in that submission. However, as we indicated at the conclusion of the hearing, and as Mr Wilde acknowledged, the second part of the decision under review is a decision to inform 'such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner' (see s.75(1) in paragraph 4 above). It is, in our view, encompassed within that aspect of the s.75(1) decision that consideration should be given to the country in which employment for the pensioner should be sought. That issue is relevant, we think, to the question as to what employment may be regarded as 'suitable' for the purposes of the Act. Having regard to the circumstances which are known to exist, it would be inappropriate to request the Public Service Board to find suitable work for the applicant in Australia if the evidence indicated that the applicant could reasonably refuse or fail to accept an offer of employment in this country.

29. The fact is that the applicant has returned to the United Kingdom and he and his family have been living there since the end of 1978. We appreciate the Commissioner's concern that the proper operation of ss.74 and 75 of the Act should not be restricted merely because a pensioner decides to leave Australia. But in our view, the circumstances of the present case are somewhat unusual.

30. In the first place, the applicant did not ask to be retired on the grounds of invalidity. So far as the evidence discloses, what he applied for was medical certification that he should be employed only on daytime duties. The effect of the medical officer's report of 16 January 1978 was that his request for daytime duties was denied and that, by reason of his then existing medical condition, he was seen as unfit for further employment in any capacity within the Federal Police Force. This outcome may well have surprised Mr Stoddart.

31. Secondly, the applicant and his family had migrated to Australia in 1967. Having lost the only avenue of employment in which he had previous experience, he decided, not unreasonably in our view, to return with his family to the United Kingdom. The evidence does not suggest that he made this move for the purpose of precluding the effective review of his pension entitlement. On the contrary, he appears to have been unaware that his medical condition was to be reviewed in January 1980.

32. Most importantly, however, the applicant has now re-established himself and his family in the United Kingdom. His medical history indicates that he has reacted with disabling physical symptoms to the uncertainty surrounding his future pension entitlement. In our view, if the applicant were to be separated from his family, through the necessity to return to Australia to undertake employment offered to him, we cannot believe, having regard to his past medical history, that his prognosis would be good. The tension and stress so imposed would be likely, in our view, to have a deleterious effect upon his physical health and thus militate strongly against his successful re-assimilation into the workforce.

33. Whether the Public Service Board will be able to find suitable clerical work in the United Kingdom for the applicant is another question. We note that such attempts as the applicant has made to find clerical work since August 1980 have been unsuccessful. But what s.75(1) of the Act contemplates is that the Public Service Board as the employing authority will endeavour to find suitable work within the indicated limitations. We see no reason why such an attempt should not be made. The applicant, after all, is still only 46 years of age and has, in our view, the capacity to perform clerical duties.

34. In the circumstances, it appears to us that the Commissioner's decision should be varied so as to direct that the Office of the Public Service Board be informed with a view to finding the applicant suitable re-employment in the United Kingdom. We do not consider that in the light of the applicant's medical history that it would be reasonable in the circumstances of this case to expect the applicant to return to Australia assuming that a suitable job could be found for him in this country.

35. For all these reasons, although we agree in substance with the Commissioner's decision, we vary the decision under review in two respects _ firstly, by deleting the reference to duties of a guard or attendant as duties suitable to be performed by the applicant which was added on reconsideration by the Commissioner (see paragraph 7), and secondly, by adding at the end of the decision the words 'in the United Kingdom'. The effect of these variations is to restore the original decision of the delegate made on 25 August 1980 referred to in paragraph 6 above subject only to the addition of the words 'in the United Kingdom' at the end of that decision.

36. The problem which has emerged in relation to Mr Stoddart exposes the need (at least in cases where the Commonwealth Medical Officer has recommended early review), for the Commissioner to include in his initial letter to a pensioner clear advice that a review can be anticipated. In such cases, the pensioner should be put on express notice that if on review it is found that his health is sufficiently restored to enable him to perform duties considered suitable, he may be offered employment and may risk the loss of his pension if he unreasonably refuses or fails to accept that offer within the time allowed pursuant to s.75(2)(b) of the Act.
