DECISION

LOCKHART J This appeal from the judgment of a judge of the Court (Northrop J) raises questions of the construction and application of Division 8A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") relating to the deductibility of the outgoings of a friendly society from its assessable income. The relevant years of income are the years ended 31 May 1984 to 1987.

2. Division 8A comprises ss. 116E to 116J. It was introduced into the Act in 1984 by s. 36 of the Income Tax Assessment Amendment Act (No 3) 1984 (Act No 47 of 1984) ("the 1984 Act"). It has been amended since then, so it is the form which the relevant sections took during the four years of income that must be considered in this case.

3. On first reading, the sections are somewhat perplexing. The reason is that it is only after their history is examined, which necessarily takes the reader to other sections of the Act (in particular Division 8 of Part III relating to the assessable income of, and deductions available to, life assurance companies), that the meaning of Division 8A becomes clear.

4. Accordingly, I propose to take the rather unusual course of turning first to the law, including the history of Division 8A, and later to the facts of the case.

5. Since 1915 the Commonwealth has always followed the principle of exempting from taxation, premiums received by a life assurance company in respect of policies of life assurance, while taxing other revenue of such a company less an allowance for direct expenses and a proportion of the expenses of management. The exclusion of premiums from assessable income was endorsed by the Royal Commissions on Taxation of 1920 (Third Report, para. 478) and 1932 (Third Report, para. 858) and by the New South Wales Taxation Investigation Committee of 1933 (para. 67).

6. Division 8 of the Act contains special provisions for the taxation of life assurance companies. Prior to 1961, premiums received by a life assurance company in respect of policies of life assurance, and consideration received in respect of annuities granted by such companies, were excluded from the company's assessable income: s. 111. Conversely, expenditure incurred exclusively in gaining such income was not allowed as a deduction: s. 112. The expenses of general management were only allowed to the extent of the proportion thereof which the assessable income bore to the total income: s. 113. Under s. 115 a special deduction was allowed of an amount calculated with reference to the "calculated liabilities" which were to be ascertained in accordance with s. 114, and depended on the actuarial "valuation of liabilities" under the principles laid down in s. 110. The rationale of s. 115 is to be found in the Third Report of the 1932 Royal Commission, para. 858 which reads as follows:

"In our opinion a life assurance company should

be taxed on the basis of its investment income

which cannot be correctly determined without

providing for the interest assumed to be earned

on the investments set aside to provide for the

payment of the liabilities of the company to its

policyholders."

Division 8 did not constitute an exclusive code, so that, apart from the special provisions of Division 8, the general provisions of the Act applied in regard to assessable income and allowable deductions. A life assurance company was therefore assessable on premiums received in respect of policies other than policies of life assurance (e.g. accident and disability policies) (see National Mutual Life Association Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 6; (1959) 33 ALJR 16) and also on its income from investments including rents, interest and dividends; on underwriting commissions; and on profits arising from the sale or disposal of investments including both securities and real property (see Producers' and Citizens' Co-Operative Assurance Co Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 36; (1956) 95 CLR 26; The Australasian Catholic Assurance Co Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 26; (1959) 100 CLR 502).

7. Prior to 1961 a life assurance company was defined by s. 110 as a company the sole or principal business of which was life assurance; also a mutual life assurance company was defined as a life assurance company, the profits of which were divisible only among the policyholders.

8. Thus, if a company conducted other business as well as life assurance business, and life assurance business was not its principal business, it was not a life assurance company within the meaning of the definition in s. 110, and Division 8 of Part III of the Act had no application. In that event the company was not entitled to the exemption under s. 111 for premiums received by it in respect of policies of life assurance or consideration received in respect of annuities granted, and it was not entitled to any deduction under s. 115.

9. There was agitation for change to the provisions of Division 8 because of what was perceived as being the anomalous situation of an insurance company carrying on both life assurance business and other business, but not so that life assurance business was its principal business, yet it was not entitled to the application of Division 8 to its life assurance business. It was thought that the Act should be amended so that Division 8 should apply to a company's life assurance business irrespective of whether or not that business was its principal business.

10. In years shortly preceding 1961 a number of companies had been carrying on life assurance as a subsidiary activity, particularly in association with general insurance. Income derived from the life assurance business of such companies was assessed to tax under the general provisions of the Act and for a number of reasons this placed such companies at a disadvantage when compared with other companies engaged in life assurance that were assessed under Division 8. It was therefore proposed that the provisions of Division 8 should apply in relation to the life assurance business of all companies registered under the Life Insurance Act.

11. Accordingly, the Act was amended in 1961 by the Income Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1961 (No 17 of 1961) which commenced on 12 June 1961 ("the 1961 Act").

12. The definition of "life assurance company" in s. 110 was altered and certain other provisions were modified to ensure that they would operate in an appropriate manner when extended to companies whose business consisted only partly of life assurance activities. The operation of s. 113 was modified so that after the 1961 Act came into operation s. 113 applied in the case of a company engaged in life assurance business as its sole or principal business, by allowing the continuation to it of a deduction for general management expenses to be ascertained by means of the formula provided in the old s. 113 unless the company elected to change over to a new basis that would apply to companies engaged in life assurance as a subsidiary activity and which were then to be assessed for the first time under Division 8. Under the new s. 113 where a company was engaged in life assurance as a subsidiary activity, or where a company carrying on life assurance as a sole or principal business elected to adopt the new procedure, the expenditure incurred in general management of the company's business was an allowable deduction to the extent that it was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. The apportionment was to be made according to the general principles of income tax law which had previously applied in relation to taxpayers other than life assurance companies assessed under Division 8, where it was necessary to determine the deduction allowable in respect of an outgoing that related in part only to the production of assessable income that was subject to tax.

13. In the course of making an assessment the Commissioner determined the proportion of management expenditure attributable to the gaining of assessable income and that proportion of the total expenditure was an allowable deduction. Section 113(2) provided for the case of a company carrying on life assurance as its sole or principal business which had not made an election for the application of sub-s. (1). In that case a proportion of the company's general management expenditure that was to be an allowable deduction was calculated by means of a formula which in effect re-enacted s. 113(1) of the old law. Sub-section (3) of s. 113 re-enacted the provision which was the old sub-s. (2) of the earlier s. 113 and stated that, for the purposes of s. 113, expenditure incurred exclusively in gaining or producing assessable income of a company or exclusively in gaining or producing non-assessable income was to be deemed not to be expenditure incurred in the general management of the company.

14. Until the Parliament enacted the 1984 Act, the income of a friendly society (an expression defined in s. 6(1) as meaning a society duly registered as a friendly society under any Act or State Act or under any law in force in a Territory being part of the Commonwealth), that was not carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to its individual members, was exempt from tax (s. 23(g)(i)). Thus friendly societies not carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to their individual members were exempt from tax in relation to the investment income which they received from their traditional sickness and funeral activities; and from their life, disability and accident insurance businesses. This was perceived by the Government as placing friendly societies at an advantage over life insurance companies. Hence in the 19 May 1983 Economic Statement, the Government announced that it intended to give effect to a proposal to impose income tax on friendly societies in respect of investment income from their life, disability and accident insurance businesses for the 1983-1984 year of income and later years of income. This was achieved by the introduction of the new Division 8A which made friendly societies subject to tax in the 1983-1984 year of income and later income years on earnings from investment of funds from their life, disability and accident insurance business. The position is made clear by the Second Reading Speech of the responsible Minister when introducing the Income Tax Assessment Amendment Bill (No 3) 1984:

"The Bill will make friendly societies subject

to tax in the 1983-84 year and later income

years on earnings from investment of funds from

their life, disability and accident insurance

business. Earnings from traditional activities

of friendly societies in providing sickness and

funeral benefits will continue to be exempt from

tax. In addition, the assessable income of

friendly societies will not include earnings

related to superannuation business and annuities

of a kind which, as I mentioned earlier, are to

be exempt when issued by a life office.

In determining their taxable income, friendly

societies are to be allowed deduction for

expenditures related wholly to the earnings of

the income that is to be assessable. Other

expenditures incurred in earning income but not

directly related to a specific income source,

such as general overheads, will be pro-rated

between exempt and assessable income, and will

be deductible in part on that basis. Within the

aim of achieving a tax treatment broadly

comparable on average to that applicable to the

non-superannuation business of life offices, the

government has devised a less complex

legislative basis of taxing friendly societies

than applies to life offices."

15. The new Division 8A (containing ss. 116E to 116J) did not remove the exemption from tax on the income of friendly societies (in the main arising from superannuation business or from traditional types of business such as sickness and funeral business); but it did remove the exemption from tax of the income of friendly societies derived from their life, disability and accident business and certain annuity business. The amendments introduced into Division 8A thus included in the assessable income of friendly societies income, profits or other amounts (other than premiums) derived from their life, disability or accident insurance business (including investment income) that would, but for the exemption afforded by s. 23(g)(i), be assessable income of the society. The new Division 8A thus taxed friendly societies on investment income from certain annuity business.

16. Under the amendments introduced in 1984 the taxable income of a friendly society was to be ascertained by deducting from its assessable income, as defined by the 1984 Act, any deductions allowable under the Act which related exclusively to earning that assessable income and a proportion of any other allowable deductions (other than those which related to expenditure incurred exclusively in gaining any exempt income) determined on a proportionate basis, namely, the proportion that the income derived from the life, accident and disability business bore to the total income of the friendly society.

17. The 1984 Act was intended to achieve the result whereby the position for income tax purposes of friendly societies was to be brought broadly into line with the position of life assurance companies. In other words, just as the exemption from the assessable income of a life assurance company of premiums received in respect of policies of life assurance or considerations received in respect of annuities granted, continues to be exempt income; so, in the case of a friendly society its income from its traditional sources (superannuation business or sickness and funeral business) is exempt income. But in the case of both life assurance companies and friendly societies, deductions which related exclusively to earnings of exempt income were not allowable deductions; only deductions allowable under the Act which related exclusively to earning the assessable income were to be allowable together with a proportion of any other allowable deductions determined on the proportionate basis of the kind previously mentioned.

18. I pause at this stage to observe that this is a case where the Explanatory Memoranda and Second Reading Speeches with respect to the relevant Bills in 1961 and 1984 have thrown real light on the proper construction of Division 8A.

19. It is with these considerations in mind that I approach the construction of Division 8A. Section 116F provides as follows:

"116F Notwithstanding any other provision of

this Act (other than the provisions of Part

IVA), the assessable income of a registered

organization shall be ascertained in accordance

with, and only in accordance with, this

Division."

20. Section 116F is therefore designed to achieve the result that the assessable income of a friendly society will be ascertained only in accordance with Division 8A, subject to the application of Part IVA of the Act which relates to schemes to avoid income tax and which is of no relevance in the present case. Section 116F ensures that Division 8A has exclusive operation in ascertaining the assessable income of a friendly society. It must be kept in mind, however, that the income of a friendly society that is not assessable income, remains exempt income pursuant to s. 23(g)(i).

21. Section 116G(1) provides as follows:

"(1) The assessable income of the year of

income of a registered organization being a

friendly society shall include so much of the

total income (other than premiums) of the

society of the year of income as is derived from

eligible insurance business of the society."

22. Section 116G(1) thus defines the assessable income of a friendly society as being so much of the "total income" of the society as is derived from its "eligible insurance business" other than premiums.

23. "Total income" in relation to a friendly society is defined by s. 116E as meaning "income, profits or other amounts that would, but for any exempting provision, be assessable income of the (friendly society)". "Total income" thus includes premiums.

24. Section 116E also defines "eligible insurance business" as meaning the business of, or in relation to, the issuing of, or the undertaking of liability under, eligible insurance policies.

25. "Eligible insurance policy" is defined by s. 116E as meaning an insurance policy that is a life assurance policy or an accident policy or a disability policy; but does not include so much of any one of those policies as is a sickness policy, a funeral policy or an "eligible policy".

26. The expression "eligible policy" is defined by s. 116E as meaning:

(a) a superannuation policy;

(b) a policy in relation to an immediate annuity; or

(c) a policy in relation to a roll-over annuity. "Immediate

annuity" is defined by the same provision in relation to a

friendly society as meaning an annuity that is presently payable

by the friendly society to a natural person. A "roll-over"

annuity is defined as meaning a deferred annuity, the purchase

price of which consists wholly of a rolled-over amount or

rolled-over amounts within the meaning of subdivision AA of

Division 2.

27. The effect of s. 116G is that it includes in the assessable income of a friendly society only so much of its "total income" as is derived from its "eligible insurance business" which necessarily excludes its income exempted from tax by s. 23(g)(i) (principally income received from sickness, funeral or "eligible" policies).

28. The practical effect of these provisions is to limit the assessable income of a friendly society to which Division 8A applies to the amount of investment income which the friendly society derives from its life, disability and accident business.

29. The High Court considered the meaning and operation of s. 116G in Independent Order of Odd Fellows of Victoria v. The Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 55; (1991) 173 CLR 417; but the questions decided in that case were different from the presently relevant questions.

30. Section 116H is the primary statutory provision in this case. It reads as follows:

"Notwithstanding any other provision of this

Act, the deductions allowable to a registered

organization in relation to the year of income

are -

(a) any deductions that, apart from this

section, would be allowable to the

organization in relation to the year of

income, being deductions that relate

exclusively to the assessable income of

the organization; and

(b) so much of any other deductions that,

apart from this section, would be

allowable to the organization in relation

to the year of income (other than

deductions that relate exclusively to

exempt income of the organization) as

bears to the amount of those other

deductions the same proportion as the

assessable income of the organization of

the year of income bears to the total

income of the organization of the year of

income."

31. Section 116H thus allows to a friendly society deductions that relate exclusively to the derivation of its assessable income (see paragraph (a)). It denies as allowable deductions, deductions that relate exclusively to exempt income (see the words in parenthesis in paragraph (b)) and apportions the remainder of the deductions in accordance with the formula prescribed by paragraph (b). The purpose of the apportionment is obviously to avoid disputes as to the precise demarcation between objects of expenditure and to achieve a broadly fair result: see Ronpibon Tin NL v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 58-60.

32. Sections 116H(a) and (b) adopt as the formula for deductibility a mechanism which requires recourse to other provisions of the Act that provide for allowable deductions to taxpayers, including s. 51 which is the primary deductibility section and the section with which this case is concerned.

33. Section 51(1) provides for the deductibility of losses or outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.

34. The apportionment to which s. 51(1) is directed and the problems to which it can give rise were discussed by the High Court in Ronpibon at 59 in these terms:

"The charges for management and the directors'

fees are entire sums which probably cannot be

dissected. But the provision contained in s.

51(1), as has been already said, contemplates

apportionment. The question what expenditure is

incurred in gaining or producing assessable

income is reduced to a question of fact when

once the legal standard or criterion is

ascertained and understood. This is

particularly true when the problem is to

apportion outgoings which have a double aspect,

outgoings that are in part attributable to the

gaining of assessable income and in part to some

other end or activity. It is perhaps desirable

to remark that there are at least two kinds of

items of expenditure that require apportionment.

One kind consists in undivided items of

expenditure in respect of things or services of

which distinct and severable parts are devoted

to gaining or producing assessable income and

distinct and severable parts to some other

cause. In such cases it may be possible to

divide the expenditure in accordance with the

applications which have been made of the things

or services. The other kind of apportionable

items consists in those involving a single

outlay or charge which serves both objects

indifferently. Of this directors' fees may be

an example. With the latter kind there must be

some fair and reasonable assessment of the

extent of the relation of the outlay to

assessable income. It is an indiscriminate sum

apportionable, but hardly capable of

arithmetical or ratable division because it is

common to both objects."

35. A deduction is allowable under s. 51(1) if it is incidental to the gaining or producing of assessable income: Ronpibon at 426; and it is incidental if there is a sufficient proximate relationship between the income and the occasion for the loss or outgoing: Kidston Goldmines Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 30 FCR 77 at 84-85; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Roberts [1992] FCA 363; (1992) 37 FCR 246 at 256-7.

36. Although s. 51(1) speaks of deductions being allowed to the extent to which they are incurred etc. or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business etc., the exercise of apportionment required by that subsection does not have to be engaged in under s. 116H because it contains its own regime of apportionment in both paragraphs (a) and (b). To qualify as deductions under paragraph (a) the deductions must be incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of the friendly society or necessarily incurred in carrying on its business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income and must relate exclusively to the assessable income of the friendly society. Thus, general management expenses, for example, would not be deductible under paragraph (a). Deductions that relate exclusively to the derivation of exempt income are not allowable because of the words in parenthesis in paragraph (b): "(other than deductions that relate exclusively to exempt income of the organization)". The remaining deductions relating generally to the friendly society's business qualify provided they satisfy the apportionment provision of paragraph (b) which has regard to the assessable income and the total income of the society.

37. Expenditure for advertising expenses, for example, will be deductible under paragraph (a) if it was incurred in gaining or producing the friendly society's assessable income or was necessarily incurred in carrying on its business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income and relates exclusively to the derivation of that assessable income. If expenditure, properly characterized, relates exclusively to the derivation of exempt income (premiums) then it is not deductible under s. 116H because exempt income by definition is not assessable income, so there can be no deductions in respect of it. This is the purpose achieved by the words in parenthesis in paragraph (b). If such advertising expenditure was not incurred exclusively in deriving either assessable income or exempt income but was incurred partially in deriving assessable income and partially in deriving exempt income it is deductible under paragraph (b). The extent of the deductibility is determined by the application of the formula provided in paragraph (b), namely, the proportion which the assessable income of the friendly society bears to its total income.

38. The construction which I prefer broadly equates the position for income tax purposes of a friendly society with a life assurance society, which was the intention of the Parliament when it enacted the 1984 Act. The Parliament modelled Division 8A substantially upon Division 8 in relation to life assurance companies so as to achieve the result mentioned earlier. In short, s. 116H entitles a friendly society to claim:

(a) allowable deductions for deductions that relate exclusively to

assessable income (as defined by s. 116G): s. 116H(a);

(b) allowable deductions for deductions other than deductions

mentioned in (a) above apportioned according to the formula

contained in s. 116H(b);

but not deductions that relate exclusively to exempt income:

s. 116H(b).

39. Thus construed, in my view, the haze of cimmerian obscurity which at first glance glazes the eyes of the interpreter of Division 8A is lifted.

40. I turn to the facts. In the relevant statements of expenditure of the respondent for the 1987 year of income, the respondent showed a statement of total expenditure by its management funds of $7,304,612. The respondent excluded, for the purposes of Division 8A, expenditure which it claimed related exclusively to exempt income (i.e. premiums excluded by s. 116H(b)) totalling $1,250,224. Such expenditure included items described as "introductory commissions, actuarial fees". The respondent then claimed as a deduction expenses which it said were directly attributable exclusively to the derivation of assessable income (as defined by s. 116F), namely, tax agent's fees of $4,100. As to the balance of $6,050,288, the respondent claimed that they were deductible by apportionment under s. 116H(b). Those apportionable items of expenditure included advertising and public relations expenses of $878,982 (the item in question in the present case), bank charges, data processing expenses, officers' and directors' fees, fares, postage, printing and stationery. It then applied the formula for which s. 116H(b) provides and produced a figure which it claimed to be the deduction pursuant to s. 116H for the 1987 year of income on account of advertising and public relations expenses of $192,956.

41. Curiously, the Commissioner denied to the respondent only the amounts claimed by it for advertising and public relations expenses and did not deny the other components of the outgoings which the respondent claimed fell to be proportioned pursuant to s. 116H(b). The evidence before the Tribunal established that there was a breakdown of the amount claimed by the respondent for advertising and public relations expenses as follows:

"1. Image/corporate/multiple products 21.40% $188,102

2. Investment products 39.60% $348,077

3. Non-investment products 39.00% $342,803

1987 Advertising claim 100.00% $878,982"

42. This shorthand form of categories 1, 2 and 3, as presented to and adopted by the Tribunal, describes the following kinds of expenditure:

(1) Advertising in relation to the company's image and multiple

products;

(2) Advertising directed to investment products specifically; and

(3) Advertising directed to non-investment products specifically.

43. It was common ground before the Tribunal and the Court (both at first instance and on appeal) that the figures were presented by the respondent and accepted by the Commissioner as what the Tribunal described as a "broad brush" apportionment.

44. The respondent's 1987 return was prepared on the basis that most of its general expenses were to be apportioned under s. 116H(b), and the Commissioner assessed on that basis, save only that advertising and public relations expenses were excluded from apportionment. The Commissioner has not disputed at any stage of the proceedings before the Tribunal or the Court the allocation by the respondent of its advertising and public relations expenses into each of the three categories mentioned above.

45. The Tribunal allowed items in category 1 on an apportionment basis under s. 116H(b); allowed the items of expenditure under category 2 in whole on the basis that they fell within s. 116H(a) and rejected all items under category 3 on the ground that they were items that were exclusively applicable to exempt income, namely, the derivation of premiums and therefore excluded from deductibility.

46. The evidence before the Tribunal (and accepted by the Court at first instance) was, so far as relevant, as follows:-

. the respondent's income consisted of both premiums and return upon

those premiums after investment;

. the respondent sought to attract funds to its various products by

providing benefits and maximum rates of return for its

policyholders;

. the respondent's purpose in advertising was to attract funds to

promote its interests and to encourage existing policyholders to

retain their funds in the respondent, thus discouraging early

redemption;

. the respondent sought to advertise a particular product, such as

the product with which this case was most directly concerned,

namely, a form of "flexible insurance" which involved participants

paying the insurer a single "up front" premium, the advantage

being on the maturity of the policy the insured not only receives

the premium back together with whatever bonuses the premium may

have attracted, but those bonuses were given concessional tax

treatment pursuant to s. 26AH and s. 160AAB of the Act.

. In order properly to characterize the investment as "life

insurance" the policies were so structured that on the death of

the assured before maturity the original sum invested is returned

to the estate (depending on what species of bond are involved)

with an additional 2% or 3%. Another species of policy involves an

ex gratia bonus where a death occurs prior to the bonus

declaration period.

47. The evidence points to the conclusion that the advertising of any particular product of the respondent, not only advertises that product with a view to attracting members of the society to become policyholders and thus produce premiums, but it has what the evidence describes as a "spin-off" effect on others. This is the view of the relevant witnesses who gave evidence; and it is the "conventional wisdom of the advertising industry", (see the evidence of Mr J L Clemenger, a director of a large advertising agency that acts for the respondent).

48. The advertising and public relations expenditure in category (1) plainly would not fall within s. 116H(a), because it did not relate exclusively to the assessable income of the respondent as defined by s. 116G(1). Nor would expenditure of this kind relate solely to the production of exempt income of the respondent, that is, principally premiums. It would I think be plainly apportionable under s. 116H(b).

49. As to the expenditure in category 2 relating specifically to "investment products", it cannot be successfully maintained that it related exclusively to the derivation of assessable income (as defined by s. 116G(1)) of the respondent because part of it must have related to the attempt to encourage premiums. In my opinion, expenditure of this kind falls within the description of expenditure to which the "spin-off" effect applies and is apportionable under s. 116H(b).

50. The expenditure in category 3 relating to "non-investment products" also falls in my opinion within the description of expenditure to which the "spin-off" effect applies and is similarly apportionable under s. 116H(b). Plainly it would not be deductible under s. 116H(a). Nor does it relate exclusively to exempt income of the respondent.

51. In my view all the items of expenditure under the heading of advertising and public relations expenses are apportionable under s. 116H(b).

52. The facts are not in dispute and this is a case where in my view the Court sitting in its appellate jurisdiction is in as good a position as were the Tribunal and the primary judge to reach conclusions in relation to relevant facts: see Roberts at 253-4.

53. I reject the argument of counsel for the Commissioner that the respondent was not entitled to deduct any of the moneys expended on advertising because they were devoted exclusively to the derivation of income by way of premiums.

54. This argument was based primarily upon the decision of Starke J in Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1933] HCA 29; (1933) 49 CLR 171 where his Honour held that the relevant expenditure was not deductible. The taxpayer, a life insurance society which derived income from premiums and from other investments, sought to deduct from its assessable income expenditure on a "welfare service" which consisted in the voluntary provision by the society of a nursing service for assured persons and in the issue of pamphlets upon matters relating to health; also expenditure in connection with consultant medical officers who advised the society upon matters relating to its life insurance business and upon information given in the pamphlets issued by it. His Honour held that the expenditure was exclusively incurred in gaining the premium income within the meaning of s. 20(5)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 (Cth) and therefore could not be deducted from the assessable income of the society. His Honour held that the welfare service expenditure was exclusively incurred in gaining the premium income and was connected wholly with the life insurance side of the company's business, its object being to attract life insurance business to the company and consequent premium income and to retain that income over a longer period of time. He said that the expenditure was undertaken to gain premium income and the mere fact that it enured to some extent for other purposes did not alter its real character. He reached a similar conclusion with respect to the expenditure incurred in connection with the consulting medical officers. In my opinion, that case does not bear upon the resolution of the issues in this case as the facts of the two cases are different and Colonial Mutual was a case of the application of established principles to particular facts.

55. Northrop J was of the opinion that there was insufficient evidence before the Tribunal in relation to each of the classes of deductions claimed by the respondent, to enable it to find in favour of the respondent. His Honour held that in the absence of appropriate findings of fact the matter must be remitted to the Tribunal. His Honour ordered that the appeal be allowed and that the Commissioner pay the respondent's costs of the appeal and that there be no order as to the costs of a motion of the respondent dated 11 February 1993.

56. In my view the material before the Court with respect to the 1987 year is sufficient to enable this Court finally to determine this matter in respect of that year.

57. The paucity of evidence in relation to the 1984, 1985 and 1986 years of income is due to the fact that the references of the objection decisions of the Commissioner in respect of each of the four years of income (1984 to 1987) were separate matters before the Tribunal and separate appeals before the Court, both at first instance and on appeal to this Full Court. At all stages, however, the four matters appear to have been subsumed as one matter and the parties adopted the course of taking the evidence with respect to the 1987 year as being broadly representative of the other years. The appeal was conducted before us on this lastmentioned basis; but the absence of evidence renders it inappropriate for this Court finally to dispose of it with respect to the years of income before 1987 other than by agreement between the parties or by reference back to the Tribunal.

58. The course which in my view should be taken is that the Court makes no orders at this stage other than to stand the matter over for a short period so that discussions can take place between the parties in the light of the Court's reasons with a view to resolving the whole affair. If this is not possible then the Court should make final orders with respect to the 1987 year of income and remit the matter to the Tribunal to determine the appeals with respect to the earlier three years of income. Counsel for both parties informed us that their clients wished all matters to be resolved finally in this appeal.

Introduction

FRENCH J A registered friendly society which has operated in Victoria for over 150 years claimed deductions in its income tax returns for the financial years 1983/84 to 1986/87 inclusive. One of those deductions, being for advertising and public relations expenditure, is in issue in this case.

However, under the provisions of Division 8A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, expenditure relating exclusively to assessable income is deductible in the ordinary way. Expenditure incurred in relation to both exempt and assessable income is to be apportioned according to a statutory formula set out in that Division. The Commissioner has challenged Manchester Unity's claimed deductions relating to advertising and public relations expenses and issued amended assessments for each of the years in question. On appeal by the Commissioner to this Court at first instance, Manchester Unity was successful. The Commissioner now appeals from that decision. The Statutory Framework

12. The relevant provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 are taken from the Act as it stood in the 1987 tax year. Reference must first be made to the general deduction provision, s.51(1):

"51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which

they are incurred in gaining or producing the

assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in

carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or

producing such income, shall be allowable deductions

except to the extent to which they are losses or

outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or

domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the

gaining or production of exempt income."

The words "to the extent" appearing in s.51(1) qualify the deductibility of losses and outgoings not wholly incurred in gaining assessable income for carrying on a business and constitute a statement of a principal of apportionment which was not expressed in earlier legislation. At one time, under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 (s.23(1)(a)), the Commissioner had promulgated an administrative rule that wherever an expenditure was incurred in part otherwise than for the production of assessable income, none of it was deductible - Ratcliffe and McGrath, Law of Income Tax 1938 p.48. In practice the rule was not applied and in Victoria Park Racing and Recreation Grounds Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 65; (1934) 52 CLR 9 at p 24, Dixon J accepted that apportionment was necessary for items of expenditure not by their very nature connected with income. The construction of the words "to the extent to which" in s.51(1) as a rule of apportionment was accepted in Ronpibon Tin NL v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p 55 and John Fairfax Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at p 35. Two kinds of apportionment were contemplated by Ronpibon. The first arises when an undivided item of expenditure relates to things or services only some of which are devoted to producing assessable income. The second is necessary when a single outlay serves both income and non-income producing objects indifferently:

"With the latter kind there must be some fair and

reasonable assessment of the extent of the relation of

the outlay to assessable income."

Section 51(1), of course, excludes losses and outgoings from deductibility to the extent that they are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of "exempt income". "Exempt income" is defined in s.6 of the Act as "income which is exempt from income tax and includes income which is not assessable income". "Assessable income" is defined in s.6 to mean "all the amounts which under the provisions of this Act are included in the assessable income". Various categories of exempt income are defined in s.23 and include, in s.23(g):

"23(g) the income of a society, association or club

which is not carried on for the purposes of profit or

gain to its individual members and is -

(i) a friendly society, not being a friendly

society dispensary."

A "friendly society" is defined in s.6(1) as "a society duly registered as a friendly society under any Act or State Act or under any law in force in a Territory being part of Australia".

13. For comparative purposes, reference is made here to Division 8 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which relates to life assurance companies and in particular s.113, which provides for the apportionment of general management expenses. This division and section pre-dated the enactment of Division 8A and, having regard to some remarks in the Second Reading Speech relating to the latter Division, throw some light on its purpose.

In the Second Reading Speech, it was said that because of their specific exemption from tax, friendly societies were at an advantage over life insurance companies in relation to the investment income they received from their life, disability and accident insurance business. The Second Reading Speech went on:

The Tribunal's Approach

18. The Tribunal held that the first category of advertising and public relations expenditure related to both investment and non-investment products and was therefore directed to the gaining of both assessable and exempt income. For that reason it attracted the application of s.116H(b). The second category of expenditure was aimed at attracting premium income with a view to the investment of the premiums to yield assessable income. It was contended for the Commissioner that the premium income being exempt income, this class of expenditure was not deductible. However, the Tribunal took the view that although directed to gaining premium income, the second category of expenditure was proximately related to the consequential derivation of assessable income from the investment of premiums. The judgment of Hill J in Kidston Goldmines Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 30 FCR 77 was invoked in support of that conclusion. On that basis, the second category of expenditure was allowable in full as a deduction under s.51(1). The apportionment formula under s.116H(b) did not apply to it. The Tribunal held that by parity of reasoning the third category of expenditure, being directed exclusively at non-investment products, was not allowable.

The Trial Judge's Approach

19. The summary that follows identifies the principal conclusions expressed in his Honour's judgment. It does not cover all aspects of the reasons.

20. His Honour observed that before Division 8A came into operation, the income of the Society was exempt income. Division 3 of Part II of the Act relating to deductions had no application to it. The special provisions of Division 8A in relation to deductions do not specify what outgoings are to be treated as deductions. This is a matter to be determined by reference to Division 3. The deduction allowed pursuant to that Division must be treated as applying to the total income of the taxpayer as if none of that income were exempt. When deductions from the total income have been ascertained on that basis, the amount that can actually be deducted from the assessable income is to be calculated in accordance with s.116H. In the result, which his Honour described as startling, the apportionment provisions of sub-s.51(1) have no application. Section 116H, his Honour held, is designed to simplify the apportionment of expenditure of a registered organisation between deductions that relate to both its assessable income and its exempt income. The only deductions not apportioned are those that relate exclusively to assessable income and those that relate exclusively to exempt income.

21. His Honour held that the Tribunal had erred in purporting to use principles developed with respect to apportionments under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. There was no reason for the general application of those principles to registered organisations. This was made clear by s.116F. The reference to deductions relating "exclusively" to assessable income or exempt income respectively creates a new concept of deductibility not contemplated by s.51(1). The test for such exclusivity is different from the principles developed by the High Court in respect of the apportionment of outgoings under s.51(1).

22. The policy of the Act, as his Honour saw it, is directed to avoiding a detailed examination of each item of expenditure and to apply a more general approach. His Honour observed that the Tribunal had not made any finding that any part of the expenditure on advertising and public relations related exclusively to assessable income or exempt income as the case might be. Such findings he regarded as essential first steps in determining what deductions are to be included in the apportionable expenses. On this basis, his Honour remitted the matter to the Tribunal.

24. Counsel for the Commissioner took as his point of departure, the proposition that no deduction is allowable under s.116H of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 unless that deduction is allowable under some other provision of the Act. The only provision under which "advertising and public relations expenses" might be an allowable deduction is s.51(1). But deductibility is permitted by s.51(1) only to the extent that the loss or outgoing is incurred in gaining assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on the business for that purpose. Deductibility is denied to the extent that the outgoing is incurred in relation to gaining exempt income. All of the outgoings in question, if not incurred in relation to eligible insurance business, were incurred in gaining exempt income. Expenses in relation to eligible insurance were for the purpose of attracting premium income which was also exempt by virtue of s.116G(1). The fact that premium income was used to derive assessable income made no difference to that characterisation of the advertising and public relations expenditure. There was no severable portion that could be said not to have been directed to gaining or attracting premiums. All outgoings deductible under s.51(1) were to be deducted under s.116H(a) not s.116H(b). An expense with a double aspect could be apportioned pursuant to s.51(1). On this approach the apportionment formula in s.116H(b) would operate in respect of outgoings allowable under other provisions of the Act not involving apportionment as part of their definition of the conditions of deductibility. Examples given included s.53 relating to expenditure on repairs to plant and premises, s.72 relating to rates and taxes, s.73 which deals with payments of subscriptions to associations and s.78 relating to deductible gifts and donations.

Manchester Unity submitted that the solution in Division 8A to the problem of apportioning expenses between assessable and exempt parts of the business was modelled upon the treatment of life companies in Division 8. The purpose of the apportionment by statutory formula was to save trouble and to reduce the room for dispute involved in apportioning each item of relevant expenditure in accordance with the Ronpibon approach to s.51(1). The Commissioner's submissions, it was said, would defeat that purpose. It was submitted that advertising and public relations expenses is a good example of a deduction that can generate endless debate about its apportionment. Section 116H(b) of the Act provides a formula to determine the question of apportionable deductions where a deduction would have been allowable but does not "relate exclusively" to assessable or exempt income.

The Court was also referred to evidence of the "spin off" effect whereby advertising one product could benefit others being sold by the same company. Expenditure directed to the production of assessable income as a "second order effect" was said to be deductible and reliance was placed on Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Total Holdings (Aust) Pty Ltd (1979) 43 FLR 217, Kidston Goldmines Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. None was excluded from s.116H(b) because none of it related exclusively to exempt income.

In the alternative, it was submitted that the Court should hold the Tribunal to have been correct in determining that advertising expenses in Category 2 were deductible in full under s.51 and that Category 3 expenses were apportionable. The Operation of Division 8A

28. In accordance with its general purpose, Division 8A (as it stood at the relevant time) limits the operation of s.23(g) of the Act so that the income to which it applies, and which is thereby exempt from tax, does not include income (other than premiums) derived from the issuing of life assurance accident and disability policies. The first step in that limiting process is to identify the total income of the organisation which would, but for any exempting provision, be assessable income. The term "assessable income" includes all income which, if para.23(g) did not exist, would be assessable income of the organisation.

29. The second step in the limiting process is to identify that component of total income which is "assessable income". Section 116F requires that the assessable income of a registered organisation is to be ascertained "in accordance with, and only in accordance with, this Division". This limitation suggests that there is to be found in the Division a code for the determination of an organisation's assessable income. But the assessable income of registered organisations is defined in s.116G in inclusive language and not, in terms, exhaustively. There is no other provision of Division 8A which expressly completes the content of the term. To avoid a lacuna in the Division, it is necessary to read s.116G as exhaustively establishing the content of the term "assessable income" in relation to a registered organisation. That construction appears to have underpinned the decision of the High Court in Independent Order of Odd Fellows of Victoria v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 55; (1991) 173 CLR 417 although the drafting problem was not alluded to by the court. The society in that case set aside, out of income derived from its eligible insurance business, a fund to meet its expected tax liability in respect of that income. The interest earned upon the fund so established was held not to be derived from eligible insurance business and therefore not to be assessable but exempt income. At p.423 of the judgment of the Court it was said that:

"The criterion of assessability of the income of a

friendly society is its derivation from eligible

insurance business."

The footnote to that observation referred back to s.116G(1). The reasoning and the language of the judgment indicates an underlying assumption that "the criterion" embodied in s.116G(1) is exhaustive.

30. The assessable income of a registered organisation having been established by applying s.116G, the next step in the process of determining its taxable income is to ascertain the allowable deductions from the assessable income. The allowable deductions are provided for in s.116H. The opening words of the section suggest that it was intended to be exhaustive - "Notwithstanding any other provision of this Act, the deductions allowable to a registered organisation in relation to the year of income are...". Nevertheless para.(a) of the section brings in as allowable deductions what would, apart from s.116H, be allowable as deductions relating exclusively to the assessable income of the organisation. That is to say, deductions relating exclusively to income (other than premiums) derived from eligible insurance business are allowable deductions. An expense relating exclusively to non-premium income derived from eligible insurance business is therefore an allowable deduction for the purposes of s.116H if able to be characterised as an allowable deduction under the general provisions of the Act. The section relevant to that characterisation for present purposes is s.51(1). That section is applicable to an expense relating exclusively to non-premium income derived from eligible insurance business. So much of such expense as was "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" or was "necessarily incurred in carrying on" the business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income is therefore an allowable deduction. It is necessary to apply the apportionment required by s.51(1) to that class of expense in order to identify the portion of it (if less than the whole) which answers the description of "deduction" as that term is used in s.116H(a).

31. The process just described permits the determination of deductions in relation to losses and outgoings relating exclusively to the derivation of non-premium income from eligible insurance business. The next step is to determine which losses and outgoings, not in that category, are allowable deductions. It is clear from the words of s.116H(b) and the exhaustive nature of the opening words of s.116H that losses and outgoings relating exclusively to exempt income of the Society are not allowable deductions for the purposes of Division 8A. This leaves the category of losses and outgoings which relate to the derivation of both assessable and exempt income. It is from that class that the "other deductions" referred to in s.116H(b) are to be ascertained, deductions relating exclusively to exempt income being parenthetically excluded by that paragraph. Absent the apportionment provisions of s.116H(b), the proportion of such losses and outgoings allowable as a deduction is determined by application of the apportionment process contemplated by s.51(1) which excepts from the class of allowable deductions losses or outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income. But that apportionment process requires a "fair and reasonable assessment of the extent of the relation of the outlay to assessable income" - Ronpibon Tin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) at 55. It involves the expression of a qualitative assessment in the quantitative terms of proportion.

32. Section 116H(b) substitutes for the qualitative assessment required by s.51(1) a quantitative process which, to use the terminology of the Second Reading Speech, "pro-rates" mixed purpose losses or outgoings between assessable and exempt income. The question whether a mixed purpose loss or outgoing is a deduction so far as it relates to the derivation of assessable income is to be determined by the criteria contained in s.51(1). But the application of the apportionment requirement of that section would forbid characterisation of the loss or outgoing as a deduction except to the extent to which it related to the derivation of assessable income. To import s.51(1) apportionment into the process of characterisation of a payment as a deduction for the purposes of s.116H(b) would, in my opinion, render the apportionment formula in that paragraph otiose. This approach does not fit completely comfortably with the wording of the two provisions but offers the most rational linkage between them. On this basis the first step is to determine whether a mixed purpose loss or outgoing is incurred in gaining or producing assessable income (inter alia) or necessarily incurred in carrying on the income producing business. The process of apportionment that follows is in accordance with s.116H(b). This involves the rejection of the submission by counsel for the Commissioner that the question whether a loss or outgoing is an allowable deduction must be resolved (including the question of apportionment) solely by the application of s.51(1).

The Operation of Section 51(1)

33. The rejection of the Commissioner's submission on the application of the apportionment rule in s.51(1) does not dispose of the contention, also advanced on his behalf, that the entirety of the advertising and public relations expenditure, being dedicated to the derivation of premium income, none of it was allowable as a deduction under s.51(1). It was not in dispute that the Society's premium income was invested and that the Society earned interest on investments which was not exempt income. The Commissioner's contention in this respect requires a consideration of the connection between outgoings and assessable income required to establish deductibility under s.51(1).

34. The problem of devising a general deductibility provision was identified in the Third Report of the Royal Commission on Taxation 1934 in the following terms:

"The problem therefore is to draft sections relating

both to the allowance and disallowance of deductions

which will make it clear that the taxpayer is entitled

to claim any expenditure properly incurred by him in

the production of his income, whether derived from a

trade or otherwise, without at the same time opening

the door so wide as to permit allowance of deductions

for which there is no justification,"

Section 51(1) was the solution to the problem so stated. Importantly it involved the rejection of a requirement that losses or outgoings claimed should have been laid out or expended for the purpose of the production of income. Such a requirement had appeared in s.25(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 - Herald and Weekly Times Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113 at 118 (Gavan Duffy CJ and Dixon J), 122 (Starke J) and 123 (Evatt J); Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavays) Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at 309 (Dixon J); W. Nevill and Co. Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290 at 307 (Dixon J). That is not to say that purpose could not be considered as an aid to characterisation and upon the enactment of the 1936 Act:

"...it became necessary to consider, according to the

circumstances of each case, whether and to what extent

reference to purpose is required or appropriate to

determine deductibility under the first limb of

section 51(1) - Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 33 ALR 213 at

218 (Brennan J).

35. It had sufficed for the purposes of s.25(e) of the 1922 Act that the claimed deduction had been expended "not of necessity and with a view to direct and immediate benefit to trade but voluntarily and on grounds of commercial expediency to facilitate the carrying on of business" - British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v. Atherton (1926) AC 205 at 212 (Viscount Cave LC); Maryborough Newspaper Co. Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1929] HCA 16; (1929) 43 CLR 450 at 452 (Rich J); Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gordon [1930] HCA 49; (1930) 43 CLR 456 at 462 (Dixon J) and 470 (Starke J); W. Nevill and Co. Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation, (supra) at 300 (Latham CJ); Sun Newspapers Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 352 (Latham CJ). The wide range of connections between expenditure and income sufficient to satisfy the general deductibility provisions of the 1922 Act was emphasised in Amalgamated Zinc (supra) and summarised by Dixon J in W. Neville and Co. Ltd v. FCT (supra) at 305 where he said of s.23(1) of the 1922 Act:

"The condition the provision expresses is satisfied if

the expenditure was made in the given year or

accounting period and is incidental and relevant to

the operations or activities regularly carried on for

the production of income."

That construction was also applied to the first limb of s.51(1) in Ronpibon Tin NL v. FCT (supra) at 56-7; Charles Moore and Co. (WA) Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 77; (1956) 95 CLR 344 at 349 at 350; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Green [1950] HCA 20; (1950) 81 CLR 313 at 317; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Snowden and Willson Pty Ltd [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at 443. Consistently with this broad approach to the connection between losses and outgoings on the one hand and the gaining of income on the other, expenditure may be a deduction though only related to income generated in years other than that in which it is incurred. See generally Ronpibon Tin NL v. FCT (supra) at 56; FCT v. Snowden and Willson Pty Ltd (supra) at 436 and John Fairfax and Sons Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at 35 and 46; AGC (Advances) Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175 at 185, 192 and 197. So far as the second limb of s.51(1) is concerned, an expenditure is not "necessarily incurred in carrying on a business" if it is not capable on reasonable grounds of being judged necessary. But within those bounds the judgment is that of the taxpayer. In Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) at 235, Deane and Fisher JJ said:

"The controlling factor is that, viewed objectively,

the outgoing must, in the circumstances, be reasonably

capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from

the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends

of the business being carried on for the purpose of

earning assessable income."

36. Central to the debate in this appeal was the question whether the derivation of interest or other income from premium income earned on the sale of eligible insurance policies was relevant to the deductibility of expenditure incurred in earning (exempt) premium income. As a matter of general principle, the range of possible relationships between expenditure and assessable income sufficient to attract deductibility under s.51(1) does not exclude a connection between expenditure and assessable income where the assessable income is derived from exempt income to which the expenditure primarily relates. It can be accepted that there is some point in the continuum of possible relationships between expenditure and assessable income at which the connection is so tenuous that the expenditure is not "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" nor "necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income" although the second limb of s. 51(1) may permit a more generous approach in this respect than the first. In the end, however, the question whether or not an expenditure which is logically relevant to assessable income is to be treated as "incurred in gaining or producing" it or "necessarily incurred in carrying on" the relevant business will depend upon the application of a selection criterion analogous to the concept of remoteness and informed by policy considerations which may include notions of commercial relevance.

37. Counsel for Manchester submitted that a loss or outgoing is incidental and relevant to assessable income if, inter alia, there is a sufficiently proximate relationship between the income (or business) and the occasion for the loss or outgoing. In that regard reference was made to Kidston Goldmines Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 30 FCR 77 at 84-85. That case concerned, inter alia, the deductibility of interest on money borrowed for the purpose of acquiring and developing a goldmining operation in Queensland. Income derived from the sale of gold produced was exempt under s.23(o) of the Income Tax Assessment Act (which section was effectively repealed in 1990). Surplus funds for gold sales were invested in the short term money market to earn interest for the taxpayer. That was not exempt income. Hill J held that the interest outlaid was in a real sense a cost of the short term investment, just as it was a cost of the activity of extracting ore from the ground and selling gold. It was, so his Honour held, relevant and incidental to the gaining of the interest income just as it was relevant and incidental to the taxpayer's business. The business was directed at earning both assessable and exempt income. The term "proximate" was not used in that case to describe a connection sufficient to attract deductibility. It can be said that the metaphor it implies is indicative of the evaluative judgment that may be required in deciding questions of deductibility. The judgment of the Full Court in Commissioner of Taxation v. Roberts [1992] FCA 363; (1992) 37 FCR 246 was also cited but there is nothing in the judgments of the majority in that case to gainsay the general approach which I have indicated.

38. In my opinion, the connection between advertising and public relations expenditure and the derivation of assessable income is not so tenuous as to take it outside the range of relationships between expenditure and assessable income that would attract deductibility. The derivation of interest from premium investments must be taken to be an integral element of the income producing activities of the Society. As to the various sub-categories of the advertising and public relations expenditure referred to earlier, I am content to adopt the view of the evidence in respect of the 1987 year expressed by Lockhart J in his reasons for judgment, namely that all the categories of expenditure concern both premium and assessable income and that all are therefore apportionable under s.116H(b). I agree with the orders proposed by His Honour.
