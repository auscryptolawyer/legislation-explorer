Re Antoine Redondo and Commissioner of Police [1989] AATA 15 (25 January 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ANTOINE REDONDO

And: COMMISSIONER OF POLICE

No. A88/101

AAT No. 4886

Gun Licences

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Gun Licences - application for grant - objection by Police to grant - application for review - considerations relevant to exercise of discretion under Gun Licence Ordinance - relevance of prior convictions - possession of cannabis for supply - use of cannabis

Gun Licence Ordinance 1937, s.6

Re Hollands and Commissioner of Police (Decision No.470, 22 May 1981)

HEARING

CANBERRA

25:1:1989

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

Applications were made by the abovenamed applicant to the Registrar of Gun Licences for certain gun licences. The applications were referred to the respondent who objected under s.6(2) of the Gun Licence Ordinance 1937 ("the Ordinance) to their grant. That objection is now the subject of this application for review.

2. The applications in question had been made by the abovenamed applicant following a visit by Police to his home on 8 May 1988 which had disclosed the applicant's possession of unlicensed guns and a cross-bow. The guns were: A Remington .222 calibre rifle; a Stirling .22 rifle; and a Gecado air rifle. Licences had previously been held by the applicant for each gun, but that for the Stirling had expired in September 1986 and those for the Remington and the Gecado in September 1987. The applicant said that he had had licences for the Stirling and the Gecado for about 15 years and for the Remington for about 7 years. He claimed that it had been the practice for the Police to telephone him in previous years just before a licence expired, as a reminder, but this had not happened in these instances. He thought that the fact of his brother having a licence for the cross-bow entitled him to have it in his own house. He offered some explanations for failure to renew the licences based on the demands of his business and the birth of a son, but the explanations were entirely unconvincing. This part of the history of the matter is relevant only for this, though it is important enough, that it demonstrates on the part of the applicant at best carelessness about, and at worst contempt for, the requirement to keep his guns licensed.

3. The applicant has had other problems with the law, and it is these that were relied upon by the respondent as the basis of the objections. Before mentioning these problems however, I should summarise the applicant's background. He was born in 1958 in Morocco, of Spanish parents. After moving to France in 1964 the family came to Australia in 1969. The applicant did well at school, but when aged 15 he left school to join the Royal Australian Navy. While at HMAS Leeuwin in Fremantle he received general training, including in firearms. He left the RAN after about two years and returned to Canberra. After some apprentice training he went back to school, to a Secondary College, but he left that to commence an architectural drafting course. In January 1977 he rejoined the RAN where he remained for about four years but which he eventually left after suffering chronic sea sickness and severe depression. He obtained a number of Trade certificates while in the Navy. Back in Canberra from January 1981, he obtained employment in the Public Service until 1985 when he left home and purchased a business. He sold this business in 1987. Since then he has worked as a bricklayer and then in a business of concreting, paving and bricklaying with a partner. At the date of the hearing in December 1988 he had suffered a back injury. He commenced living with a woman employed in the Public Service in mid 1985, and she had a son by him in June 1988. The applicant said that they wish to go on the land.

4. The applicant has had, through his father and more particularly while in the RAN, thorough instruction in the care, safety, operation and use of firearms. He is apparently a competent marksman. He told me in evidence: "I love my firearms ... I am good with firearms and I get satisfaction out of owning firearms and using firearms". I accept that he is technically competent and responsible with firearms for the purpose of my approach to this case.

5. I now go to the applicant's previously mentioned problems with the law. On 22 April 1985 he was convicted in the Supreme Court of the ACT, after trial before a Judge and jury, of possessing cannabis for supply. The possession of the cannabis was undoubted. The jury effectively had to consider whether the cannabis was possessed for supply. The following facts appeared from the transcript of evidence at the trial: As at August 1984, when the offence was committed, the applicant, who said that he had been smoking cannabis since he was about 18, was smoking it "about every day". He had no intention of giving it up at that time. The charges related to his having purchased close to 500 grammes of cannabis, about five times the trafficable quantity, for some $820. He had been window-shopping around Woden Plaza "and this fellow appeared that I had not seen for some time and he asked me if I wanted to buy some cheap cannabis". He arranged to meet this man "half way up Mt. Taylor", which he did "around five, six o'clock p.m.". Apparently flushed with success, the applicant came down from the mountain bearing the cannabis in a green plastic garbage bag and "went to the pine forest to take my dog for a run and just kill a bit of time. Wait for people ... Just had a little smoke, just to try out what it was like". After this he went to a friend's place where there were various people he knew. He told them that he had "made a good score for the money I paid ... I pulled out a little bit, so they could have a smoke, yes", and he and the others had a smoke, using a "bong", a kind of pipe. The Police however had had the premises under surveillance and he was intercepted with the cannabis in his car a little while after he had left. The jury having returned a verdict of guilty of being in possession of the cannabis for supply, the applicant was fined $2,000 in default 80 days imprisonment, and was given seven months to pay by instalments. The Judge interpreted the jury's verdict as meaning that while the applicant had a dominant purpose of possession for his own use, it was also part of his purpose that he would share it with his friends and acquaintances. He was satisfied that the applicant was not a dealer as such and that there was no commercial intent in relation to the cannabis, and noted that the Crown did not contend otherwise. The applicant was sentenced on that basis.

6. The applicant told me in evidence that after the trial he smoked cannabis "Not very often ... I think I smoked once and I got caught for it". He certainly got caught. On 6 February 1987 he was convicted before the ACT Magistrates Court on a charge of possessing cannabis resin, and on a charge of using cannabis. On the first charge he was fined $500, and on the second $100. He had pleaded guilty to the charges. I do not accept the applicant's evidence that he only smoked once after the trial. I was left unpersuaded of this by virtue of his demeanour in giving evidence, but the evidence of Senior Constable Brodie confirmed that as at May 1988 the applicant still occasionally used marihuana.

7. In November 1988, that is to say shortly before the hearing, the applicant joined the Sporting Shooters Association of Australia. This permits him to shoot in any gun club in Australia. On 3 November 1988 he applied for membership of the Majura Gun Club, effective from 1 December 1988 being the commencement of the membership year. I was given to understand that it is not necessary for him to provide his own firearms in order to take part in shooting at a gun club.

8. The Tribunal has, on a number of occasions, complained about the state of the Ordinance. Once again I have to set out what was said by Deputy President Hall in Re Hollands and Commissioner of Police (Decision No.470, 22 May 1981)

"10. As will be observed, however, s.6 of the

Ordinance is silent as to the considerations which

should guide the exercise of the discretionary

powers conferred on the Registrar and the

Commissioner of Police respectively. It does not

follow, therefore, that the powers are arbitrary or

completely unlimited: cf Water Conservation and

Irrigation Commission (NSW) v Browning (1947) 74

CLR 492 at 505 per Dixon J. They are powers which

may appropriately be exercised 'for the purpose of

attaining the object and securing the purpose' of

the legislation, 'such object and purpose being

ascertained by an examination of the terms' of the

legislation: per Latham CJ in Shrimpton v

Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 CLR 613 at 620. Within the

limits of the considerations which the statute by

implication makes relevant, the discretionary

powers are nevertheless very wide: cf R v Murphy;

Ex parte Clift (1980) QdR 1 at 8.

11. The Ordinance makes it clear that the use or

possession of a lethal firearm or weapon is to be

subject to proper controls in the public interest.

The minimum age laid down by s.6 of the Ordinance

for the grant of a pistol licence (18 years)

compared with that for a gun licence (16 years)

suggests a need for greater maturity in the

possession of a pistol and no doubt reflects the

principle that handguns, because of their capacity

for concealment on a person, are intrinsically more

dangerous than rifles. The fact that the

Registrar and the Commissioner of Police are each

recognised as having functions to perform in the

licensing of guns and pistols gives some indication

of the importance and of the nature of those functions.

12. Plainly, in my view, the Commissioner of

Police with his overall responsibility for keeping

the peace in this Territory, has a special concern

under the Ordinance to ensure that firearms do not

fall into the hands of criminal elements in the

community, either directly by the granting of

licences to persons, for example, with a criminal

record or with known criminal associations, or

indirectly, by the granting or renewal of licences

to persons who have demonstrated a lack of

responsibility in the safe keeping of weapons in

their possession. In this latter regard it is an

offence under the Ordinance for a person to fail to

take 'all reasonable precautions' to ensure the

safe keeping of a gun or pistol in his possession

(see s.20). I should not be understood as

suggesting that such considerations are outside the

concern of the Registrar as well; but they are

especially relevant to the Police."

9. The question then is whether in all the circumstances the applicant is a person to whom gun licences should be issued. I am perfectly satisfied that he is not and that the respondent's objections were completely justified for the following reasons:

(i) The applicant was convicted only a little

over three years ago of the offence of

possessing cannabis for supply.

(ii) Notwithstanding the warning that this

conviction should have given him, the

applicant continued, as I have found, to

smoke cannabis and was convicted on a charge

of so doing in February 1987. He was also

then convicted of possession of cannabis.

By May 1988 he had apparently still not given

up his habit.

(iii) For the purpose of obtaining the cannabis

needed for his own habit and, on at least one

occasion, for that of his friends, the

applicant had to indulge in shady dealings

such as that described on a hill side on a

winter evening. To obtain it on other

occasions, including instances reaching into

1988, some such other method of illegal

acquisition must inevitably have been

involved. I do not consider that the

possession of firearms by a person who has

contacts in, or at least has until recently

had contacts in, such a milieu is something

that this community can accept.

(iv) If I had been satisfied that the applicant

had abandoned his habit, and his contacts,

that could have been another matter. I was

informed that the Commissioner has been

prepared to accept the grant of a licence

when about five years has passed since

convictions such as these are recorded.

Whether or not such a period is reasonable

would no doubt depend on the circumstances.

All that it is necessary to say for present

purposes is that only two years has passed

since the last conviction, and less than that

since his last admitted user, I am by no

means satisfied that the applicant has

abandoned his former course of conduct.

(v) The applicant's attitude to the renewal of

the licences was itself careless if not

contemptuous.

(vi) The denial of licences will not prevent him

practising his skill with firearms at gun clubs.

10. The case had some rather sad features. The applicant apparently did well at school notwithstanding his having come to Australia without any ability to speak English. It is unfortunate that this was interrupted by joining the RAN aged 15, and equally unfortunate that neither this nor a period of subsequent service worked out. The applicant has had various employments. He appears to have a stable relationship with the mother of his son, for whom he clearly has a deep affection. On the day of the hearing he was affected by a back problem which was giving him obvious pain, but his demeanour and presentation seemed not to be attributable solely to that. I can only express the hope that this third brush with legal proceedings arising out of his unfortunate habit will lead him to think that it may have done him much harm.

11. The decision under review will be affirmed.
