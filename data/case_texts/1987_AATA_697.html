REASONS FOR DECISION

June 1937 Mr P M Roach (Senior Member)

As this case was argued before us, in the principal question is whether a company (Target) derived assessable income upon the sale of real estate in December 1979. If the "profit" so derived constituted assessable income, the assessment of taxable income in issue before me will be substantially, if not wholly, correct. It might need to be reduced by two factors: $4 relating to subscribed capital; and $2,128 relating to "carry forward losses". How the apparent gross profit on the sale ($196,810 - cf. post) was to be related to the amount of taxable income assessed ($147,799) was not explained. The reason for that would seem to lie in the circumstances leading to the issue of the assessment.

2. In September 1983 the Commissioner assessed Target as having derived $147,799 taxable income in the year of income ended 30 June 1980; and made an assessment of Division. 7 tax in the sum of $35,914.50. Using the powers conferred by the Taxation Unpaid Company Tax Assessment Act ("the TUCT Act"), the Commissioner gave notice of the assessments to persons who had been registered as the shareholders in Target prior to January 1980. They were an individual (RJ); his wife (Mrs RJ); and a company they controlled (MCO). Mr and Mrs RJ were noted in some tax returns of Target (Ex. C) as holding their shares from incorporation in trust for a family trust. (The two ordinary $1 shares recorded as held by MCO were issued in 1979 upon the redemption of four (sic) $1 Redeemable Preference shares held by the father of Mrs RJ. How that was achieved was not explained either - cf. Companies Act, 1962, s61.) MCO existed to benefit the family of Mr and Mrs RJ. (Together they will be collectively referred to as "the Vendors".) As will be apparent later, another family (that of JP) may have had an entitlement equal to that of the family of RJ in the affairs of Target.

3. The figure for taxable income appears to have been derived from a contract of sale (Ex. B) entered into between the Vendors, as Vendors, and another company (Purchaser) on 24 January 1980. Pursuant to that agreement the entire share inpisal owners by the Vendors) in Target was to be transferred to Purchaser for a price of s134. 017. One element of the settlement was that the Vendors upon settlement would cause Target to deliver up to Purchaser or its nominee a bank cheque in the sum of $145,671. According to the contract the only asset of Target at settlement was to be "cash at bank" of $145,671; and that amount represented "total shareholder's funds". Following the sale the Vendors ceased to be concerned in, or to have control over, or to be responsible for the affairs of Target. Then the TUCT Act was enacted. As a result, following the issue of the assessments before me, the Vendors became concerned in and, if the present assessments are upheld, they will be responsible for the unsatisfied tax liabilities of Target, either alone or in conjunction with the family of JP.

4. Once the Vendors had been served with notice of the assessments against Target delivered to them with notice that it was the intention of the Commissioner in due course to proceed to the issue of vendor recoupment assessments against them, the Vendors nominated RJ as their representative with power to object on their behalf against the assessments made against Target. Armed with the right to avail himself of all grounds of objection to the assessment which might have been available to Target, RJ exercised those rights of objection and in doing so advanced three basic contentions:-

  1. that the assessment of taxable income was excessive in so far as it had treated the "profit" on the sale of real estate as constituting assessable income; and, if that should prove to be unsound,
  2. that the assessment was excessive in that in equating taxable income with "total shareholders' funds" the Commissioner had erred in not taking into account as non-income items: $4 subscribed capital; and $2,128 "carry forward losses".

Background

5. As two quite distinct families are concerned with the affairs of Target, it will be appropriate to provide a background introduction covering the period leading up to the first association between them in the course of the hearing JP and RJ both gave evidence. In doing so both gave evidence as if they exercised dominance in the control of their family's involvement in all relevant matters. That was not challenged. I accept that in each family the "controlling mind and will" so far as these matters are concerned lay with JP or RJ (as the case might be).

The Interests of JP

6. JP is a mechanical engineer who was born in 1924. In the early 1960's, whilst still employed by a major company, he and an associate commenced an engineering business on a part-time basis. By 1965 they were engaged full-time in the operation of that engineering business. Those operations were carried out from a factory site jointly owned by the two associates. The businesses themselves were carried out through the medium of two companies which they controlled. In addition JP controlled a family company (JPCO). It held a number of family investments including a .91 hectare (two acre one rood) parcel of land which was to generate the "profit" in question when ultimately sold by Target in December 1979. That land was purchased by JPCO in 1969 (Ex. H). At the time it was purchased JP had in mind the possibility of further development of the engineering business into a form of production which would require use of a substantial site. That prospect, which he envisaged at the age of 45 years, did not eventuate. JPCO continued to hold the land. It derived some small amount by way of rent from an old cottage on the site but overall was not putting its asset to use and was suffering the recurrent burden of clearing blackberries and fire hazards. By the mid-1970's JP had applied his mind to other ways of making use of the asset and gave some thought to development on the site of a set of small factories. That prospect was not without difficulties. The block was in a light industrial area which was already developed a number of small road-frontage allotments. It was later described in a valuation resort (Ex. 0). The site itself had a road frontage comprising only two such allotments and for the rest (7/9 of the whole - 707 hectares or 7 rodes) it extended to the rear of other reveloned lots. In addition it was bisected by a high transmission line which greatly restricted development potential (Ex. H). Using his skills, JP developed his own rough plans for the development of the area in a manner which would not infringe the restrictions arising from the existence of the easement. Although at some stage he had deposited on site some metal roof trusses with a view to their possible use in the event of any factory development, he had not taken any detailed steps towards the implementation of his idea. (JP also had long-term interests in other lands. I am satisfied that those holdings if anything only confirm my view that JP was not in any way a speculator in lands.)

7. Then in late 1976 he received an approach from a real estate agent (Realtor) who specialized in the marketing, letting and management of commercial and industrial complexes. At that time a factory complex existed on allotments at the rear boundary of the site owned by JPCO. One tenant on that site (Carrier) had need to bring substantial vehicles to the portion of the complex which he occupied at the rear of that site. He discussed with Realtor the possibility that he might be able to procure access across the unoccupied land to his rear, and the agent set about making enquiries to that end. Those enquiries led Realtor to the door of JP. When JP was asked whether he would be willing to permit Carrier to have access across the land of JPCO he refused, explaining that he was already interested in developing the site himself. Having an interest in promoting such developments the agent arranged for JP to meet RJ.

RJ and his Family

  1. RJ is a carpenter by trade. He was born in 1940; he married in 1964; and of that marriage there are four children. Following completion of his apprenticeship, he was for some years employed in the course of his trade and gained substantial experience on major construction sites. In 1969, in conjunction with an associate, he started out in business for himself using as the business vehicle a building company jointly controlled with his associate. (I shall refer to that company as SCO). Thereon BCO commenced business as a building contractor but it was not to continue for long under the joint control of RJ and his associate. They came to the partner of the ways and, in a period when there was still uncertainty - later resolved in favour of RJ - as to the long-term control of ECO, RJ caused Target to be incorporated. That was on 2 April 1973 (Ex. E). It was established with a paid-up capital of two dollars with RJ holding one share; and Mrs. R J the second share. The name chosen for Target was identical with that of BCO save that the "(No. 1)" was added after the first two words of the title. From incorporation up to 30 June 1977, Target carried out some undefined managerial role in association with the operations of BCO and other companies controlled by RJ. Although its gross revenues were as high as $57,919 (1976), it did not ever return a taxable income. By 30 June 1977 its accumulated losses recorded in its income tax returns amounted to $2,195 (Ex. C).
  2. During this period RJ was not content that BCO should be the only vehicle for his money-making enterprises or that his money-making endeavours should be restricted to the contracting profits available to a building contractor. (In that capacity, RJ sought and sometimes obtained a share of proceeds of sale for performing to time and budget.) He established other companies which he controlled, although in some instances he did not' have a beneficial interest in the companies. One result was that, in addition to BCO carrying out "hard-line contracting" in which the only reward to RJ's family would be a profit on the building project itself, when opportunity permitted RJ arranged for other companies he controlled to acquire properties alone or in conjunction with others so that BCO could develop those properties as a building contractor. In most cases those properties were sold after development had been carried out by BCO. In one instance such a development took place without any structural development by BCO. In two instances in which the establishment of squash courts was concerned the properties, once developed, were held for some years. In relation to one squash court project the other entrepreneur involved was Realtor. One squash court project was developed with a commercial complex overhead which for some years provided office accommodation for RJ.
  3. Such undertakings were not always commercial in character. In about December 1976 Mr and Mrs RJ purchased a waterfront block with an old boat shed and one bedroom shack on it with a view to establishing their family residence there. The block was purchased at a price of $55,000 and, with the aid of funds borrowed from the bank, a family residence was constructed there. Although this property was intended to be, and in fact was for some years, the private residence of the family it was not totally isolated from the business enterprises of RJ. Title was made available as security for the borrowings by different companies within the family group.
  4. However, there was one commercial undertaking of particular relevance. It involved two blocks of light-industrial land, one of which at the rear adjoined the land owned by JPCO. RJ caused the first and smaller lot to be developed with factory units and then proceeded to develop the larger block with sixteen or seventeen factory units. In both cases development was carried out by BCO and the development was carried out promptly after purchase. Also in both cases lettings to tenants proceeded as soon as was possible. Further, the sites were wholly disposed of within probably less than two years of acquisition. Realtor was the real estate agent to both projects.

12. It was during 1976, and probably towards the end of 1976, that agent received the approach from one of the tenants (Carrier) at the rear of the larger lot enquiring as to the possibility of getting access over the adjoining land. It was that enquiry which led the agent to the door of JP and ultimately brought JP and RJ together for the first time.

JP and RJ: A Comparison

  1. RJ and JP both gave evidence before me. At the time of their first meeting JP was a fifty-two year old who had been in business for eleven years in the engineering field. In that time he had accumulated substantial assets, not least of which was a large and valuable parcel of land in an area zoned for light industrial development. His original plans to develop that land had passed and he had been contemplating how to put his valuable asset to better use. As a witness he was most careful and precise in his evidence and in my view was not disposed to confuse reconstructions of things past as recollections of things past. My assessment was that in his evidence, as in his commercial undertakings, he was of cautious disposition and not given to risk-taking. On the other hand he was astute enough to apply some very practical rules of thumb. He thought that having the building contractor involved as an equity participant in a long-term venture would provide a greater assurance of quality in construction than having an architect supervise a builder who had no particular wish to build well. Similarly, he placed confidence in RJ rather than in the documents, although he was to become agitated and concerned when it appeared to him that the solicitor (S) and Realtor were not performing as expected (inter alia Ex. BR). However, there is no suggestion that any such concern was directed to RJ personally or that there was any reason why he should have doubted the reliability of RJ.
  2. In contrast at the time of first meeting RJ was a thirty-six year old with only seven years experience in carrying on business in large scale construction. Unlike JP, he was of entrepreneurial and risk-taking spirit, bold enough to seize most opportunities, if not every opportunity, that presented and rash enough to put most, if not all, of the available resources of his family at risk. Whereas much of the contracting work undertaken by JP was in the way of numerous small contracts, the nature of the building projects undertaken and controlled by RJ was such that it required a substantial work force with a recurrent need to procure new and substantial contracts in order to ensure the ongoing operations of the business. So it was that for RJ one way of ensuring that BCO could continue in business was to be accepted as the successful tenderer in "hard-line contracting". Another was to promote developments. whether as an equity participant with one or other of the companies in the family group or others. In my view RJ was much mors active in looking for new opportunities than JP. He was also quite willing to modify previous plans and to reorganise planned, current activities in order to take advantage of fresh opportunities or to meet the demands of new exigencies, These then were the two men introduced to each other on the initiative of Realtor rellowing the enquiries he was making for carrier.

Formation of the Project

  1. When JP and RJ first met late in 1976, the situation was that JP was not under any pressure to set about the course of construction he had contemplated for the site; RJ was, as ever, interested in procuring building work for BCO; and Realtor, in his own .interest, was keen to bring JP and RJ together in establishing a project. JP made his plans available to RJ so that he could provide an "estimate". Although it would be many months before any substantial development would take place, I am satisfied that this first contact occurred late in 1976 before B (who gave evidence) became Manager of the Bank of New South Wales at the branch at which JP was a well-established and well-regarded customer. I accept the evidence of JP that he brought to one of the early meetings with RJ, the then manager of the Bank of New South Wales - B's predecessor, so that he might have the benefit of his advice. I also accept that that meeting took place on the site of the adjoining development and that that was on the initiative of RJ who was keen to have an opportunity to show the quality of workmanship to be had from BCO.
  2. When later presented with RJ's cost-estimate, the conservatively minded JP thought the project larger than he could or should manage on his own and enquired whether RJ did "joint ventures". Optimistically, and with some measure of exaggeration, RJ indicated that he did. From that point the parties moved forward to establish a "joint venture". By April 1977 architectural plans had been submitted to the local shire for approval (Ex. V) and by 12 May 1977 development approval had been granted for a complex of 21 factories (Ex. V). In view of the timetable later followed it seems likely that in the early part of 1977 it was expected that there would be an earlier start with a project than was later to eventuate. At one stage somebody prepared a "draw-down" schedule for bank drawings which would have accommodated progress payments to be made between the third 14 June 1977 and 14 December 1977 (Ex. X). However, that was not to be. Even so with the broad notion in mind that JP and RJ would "joint't venture" the exercise. building plans were prepared by architects (Ex. V) and costed by BC0 and JP carried out negotiations with the newly appointed Ianager of the Bank of Mew South Wales (B). I am also satisfied that once the concept of a "joint venture" had been mooted, JP consistently and persistently held to the view that the value of the land to be made available to the "joint venture" through him was to be $375,000. With that figure in mind and also the castings by BCO, JP (for JPCO) made approaches to the bank through B. That happened sufficiently early for the bank to give notice on 25 July 1977 (Ex. W) of a favourable decision made on 15 July 1977 (Ex. W). Despite the fact that contracts of sale of the land to Target dated 30 June 1977 were presented (Ex. G); and despite the fact that the balance sheet of Target to 30 June 1977 showed it as having purchased the land for $375,000 and as being indebted to JPCO for that figure (Ex. C), I am satisfied that no such arrangement had been finalized at that date.

17. I am persuaded on the evidence that by the letter of 25 July 1977 (Ex. W) the Bank gave notice to JPCO that funds would be available by a commercial bill line to cover the construction period and, subject to the security then proving to be acceptable as a trustee security (cf. Fouche v Superannuation Fund Board [1952] HCA 1; (1952) 88 CLR 609) that a loan of $560,000 would be available from Bank of New South Wales Nominees (Nominees) for three years from completion of development; and that the bank required that by 5th August 1977 that JPCO should advise whether or not the offer was to be accepted. I also accept the evidence of B that, given satisfactory performance by the borrower, it was quite common for such loans to be renewed at redemption date.

18. By 5th August 1977, the Electricity Commission for the State of New South Wales had advised the architects, as to their response to the proposed development (Ex J) and on 10th August 1977 JPCO gave notice to the Bank, accepting its loan offers (Ex W). Nothing in the evidence before me indicates that to that date the offer was made to, or purported to be accepted by, any other than JPCO, although B did say (and I accept it) that he had met RJ and was in general terms aware of his involvement but unconcerned as to it. In short, I find that the Bank was content that the reelect could proceed without any equity participation if JP'S family in some way entered into a joint venture with the family of RJ.

19. There was produced in evidence a construction contract dated 12 August 1977 for a price of $532,000 (Ex. P). It is a matter of particular significance that one of the signatories for Target as the building owner was JP although at this stage:

(a) he was not by reason of any formal act a Director;
  • (b) he was not by reason of any formal act a shareholder;


(c) Target had not contracted to purchase the land;
  • (d) the arrangements as to the joint venture had not been finalized; and

(e) finance had only been approved for JPCO.

Even so, having regard to the concern at the dilatoriness of solicitors manifested in the records of 24 August 1977 on the part of JP (Ex. BR); and the circumstance that the valuer's report (Ex. 0) states that, prior to September 1977, the initial works of site preparation had commenced, I am satisfied that the construction contract was signed on or about 12 August 1977.

20. But I am also persuaded that by 24 August 1977 JP was concerned that the project had advanced to the stage it had without matters having been finalised in relation to the sale of the land to Target (Ex. BR). The oral evidence of JP and RJ was that under the joint venture it was contemplated that the two families would each have a 49% interest in the venture with the agent having "1%" and a casting vote. That is confirmed, but the figures became made more accurate and precise, when one considers the solicitor's note of 24 August 1977 (Ex. BP). That note recorded instructions that the two families should have 49.5% interest each; and that the agent would have a 1% interest by way of redeemable preference shares, a place as a Director, and an entitlement to a Director's fee. The same solicitor's note also recorded that 10% per annum was to be paid on a sum of $275,000 "from practical completion". By letter of the same date (Ex. 30) the solicitors forwarded for completion four contracts each providing for a sale of a quarter interest in the land for $93,750 (Ex G). (The covering letter advised that State taxation authorities might seek to have the four documents assessed as giving effect to a single transaction!) The contracts were duly executed by both parties. The works of construction proceeded. Then on 9 September 1977, a valuer provided JP with his valuation (Ex. 0) for the land of $365,000; a valuation based on an assessment of the value of the completed project inferring the value of the land by deducting the estimated cost of completing the project. Why that valuation was obtained at that time is not clear to me, but I am not persuaded that anything significant turns on that. On 25 September 1977, (Ex. AD) JPCO forwarded its cheque for holding fees to 31 August and 30 September, the fees having been notified by the bank by letter of 5 September 1977 (Ex. AD).

21. The next material development was on 4 October 1977 when JPCO advised the Bank (Ex. AE) that it had sold the land to Target; and that Target would be taking up the offer of finance made by the bank. That was followed on 25 October 1977 when JPCO 4 executed four transfers to Target (Ex. M) and Target on the same day executed mortgage security in favour of the Bank of New South Wales in support of the line of credit by way of commerical bills (Ex. K). The transfer and mortgage were duly registered (24 November 1977) (Ex. H). At that point, on the face of it, JPCO had parted with title to land worth $375,000 (or thereabouts) and in doing so had transferred it to a company with an $8 paid-up capital (including $4 in Redeemable Preference shares) over which neither JPCO nor any officer of JPCO had any control. I am satisfied that that action was not as rash as it might have seemed, although it was certainly conduct expressing great confidence in the integrity of RJ. Both JP and RJ gave evidence (and it is confirmed by the solicitor's note of 24 August 1977 (Ex. SP) and by the terms of an unregistered and undated mortgage by Target in favour of JPCO (Ex. Q)) that the arrangement was that JPCO would introduce to the land to the venture at a price of $375,000; that it would await payment until such time as the completed venture had proceeded to a stage at which it could be refinanced raising sufficient funds to enable the purchase price of the land to be paid: that the purchase price would be secured for payment by an unregistered mortagae: that the purchase price would tear interest from the date of practical completion (according to the solicitor's note (Ex. BP)) or from the date of mortgage to the Bank of New South Wales (according to the draft mortgage (Ex. 0)) until payment; and that the rate of interest would be 7.3% per annum.

22. At first that reference to a rate of interest of 7.3% per annum seemed puzzling. It was an unusual rate, and, relative to the rate ultimately charged by Nominees (13.5% nett) was a low rate. However, it was explained quite simply and consistently by both JP and RJ. JP was at all times adamant that the land had a value of $375,000 and he did not resile from that even when the valuation came to hand at $365,000 in September 1977. RJ contended that the price was excessive but more particularly claimed that the project could not support an interest rate of 10% per annum based on a land cost of $375,000. No doubt being more concerned to maintain a high purchase price than he was to obtain a high rate of interest on a lower purchase price, and recognising that whatever amount might be paid to him by way of interest to the extent of 50% it would be balanced by reducing the profit of the joint venture, JP compromised. It was agreed that although the price would stand at $375,000, interest at 10% per annum would be limited to the first $275,000 of that price. In the draft mortgage that came to be converted to an interest rate of 7.3% per annum on $375,000, that is to say, $21,500 interest per annum expressed as 7.3% per annum of $375,000 or as 10% on $275,000. Meanwhile development continued.

Procuring the Tenants

23. By December 1977 meetings were being held between JP, RJ and Realtor settling plans for advertising the pending availability of premises; for procuring tenants; and as to related matters. It was then expected that the premises would be available for occupation from early February. Display brochures and other advertising material were prepared ready for that time. It was then that the difficulties commenced. There were problems in making walls waterproof; there were problems with water penetrating under the suriacs of the pavad areas; there were delay:;; and there were cost overruns. There was no great rush of tenants (Exs. BE, BG. BH) and, instead of loner-term tenancies, in some instances the • parties resorted to accepting substantial parties for short-term occupancies. Even when tenants were let into possession, all did not run smoothly. Sometimes rental payments were not paid and on some occasions cheques were dishonored. The documents exhibited in evidence showed substantial activity throughout the whole of this period (Exs. Y, AB, AC, AJ, AH, AO, AP, AU, AV, AW, AY, EB, EC, BD and BE). They also demonstrate that slowly but surely there was a falling out between Target and Realtor, particularly manifested in the correspondence written on behalf of Target by JP. (By June 1980 the prospect of suing the agent was being considered, although how this should be was not fully explained. By that date Target had long since passed from the control of either or both of RJ and JP - (Exs. -BJ, BK, BN, BL and BM)). The Applicant points to the endeavours to secure tenants as indications of bona fides on behalf of Target in its desire to hold the property as a long-term investment proposition. The Respondent pointed out that, to have been able to have offered the property for sale with all units occupied by substantial tenants under long-term leases, would only enhance the prospects for sale. For my part I did not find the arguments to be of any great significance either way. It was to be expected that, whether a property comprising 21 factories would be offered for sale or held as a long-term rental investment, in either case tenants would be sought on the best terms possible. In my view what is much more significant is what JP was going, particularly when it is borne in mind that JP, according to the formal records of Target and all the evidence, had no status as either a shareholder, or as representative of a shareholder, or as a Director. It was put that the distribution of responsibility between RJ and JP was that RJ would be principally responsible for the works of construction; and that JP would bear the burden of responsibility in relation to organising finance and arranging and supervising tenancies. What they both did was consistent with that division of responsibility. Further, it is especially significant that JP in carrying out his responsibilities in that regard, particularly in dealing with the agent. spoke as the person within Target resposible for those matters. There was no sugcestion in the taris in which he was writing that he was in any way subject to the authority of RJ (Exs. AP, AU, AW). Furthermore. it was understandable that he should express concern, and be concerned, not only about deficiencies in rental revenue such as would contribute to a deficiency in funding, but also to complain about commitments being made by Realtor binding Target to incur expense in meeting the expectations of tenants. In that regard it is important to bear in mind that the original expectation was that the special contribution of JP would be only to stand out of payment of interest on the purchase price of the land until the project had proceeded long enough and successfully enough to enable the purchase price of the land ($375,000 - and accumulated interest - $27,500 pa) to be paid out from fresh, increased external borrowings. There was no suggestion that he would be called on to contribute to any deficiency that might arise in terms of a cash flow. Indeed, according to my assessment, it is possible that the transaction would not have proceeded at all had the prospect of a short-fall in cash flow been foreseen by JP at the beginning. Therefore it was understandable that, when all of the difficulties were encountered - including not least the need to borrow $615,000 rather than $560,000 - JP should be concerned. So it was that by 30 June 1978 (according to the income tax return of Target prepared some time after that date (Ex. C)), although it is recorded as having developed the land at a total cost to that date of $965,418 its only revenues had come from "Interest" $392 leaving a taxable income of $297. (Why that should be so was not explained despite many references suggesting that rent-paying tenants had been in possession from as early as late February 197S.) However, what is more significant in my view is that, according to the balance sheet at 30 June 1978, Target was not only indebted to the Bank for $560,000 and to JPCO for $375,000; but it was also indebted to the Bank (presumably on overdraft) for 526,136; to BCO for $4,017; and to JPCO for $5,723.

24. The next event of particular importance was established upon the evidence to have occurred when RJ wrote to Realtor on 7 September 1978 in relation to various orients in which BCO was involved (Ex. 1) The letter referred to many projects involving RJ: a decision to sell one completed project; sought an option of another site for which development approval HAD BEEN OBATINED: referred to the architects having been instructed to prepare a factory development plan with a view to interesting third parties "in a package deal"; discussed development application plans awaited for another; advised that building approval plans were nearly ready to be collected on another; called for another project "to be pushed along a little harder"; reported the imminent completion of another; and also spoke of the development on the Target land. As to that property the letter said (inter alia):-

"Would you please advise to B's progress or your own with regards sale of this project. What figure would they feel would be saleable at? Do they require full tenancy? etc. etc."

  1. That letter, and the evidence given by RJ at the hearing in relation to it, is of particular significance and is much relied on by the Commissioner in support of his contention that it was then, as it had been from the time Target acquired the property, the intention of Target that the property should be "on-sold" at a profit after development.
  2. Whatever the significance of that letter may have been, the date for long-term finance with Nominees to be taken up (31 (sic).4.1978) had passed. Since finance was originally approved, JP had made a further approach to B at the Bank requesting a further $55,000 to cover additional costs incurred. The need for the increase was not precisely detailed, but having regard to known variations; expenses incurred in rectifying problems; delays in construction; delays in securing reliable continuing tenants - all as experienced by mid-1978 - were no doubt substantial contributing factors.

The Proposal to Sell

  1. On the basis of the foregoing I am satisfied that by the end of 1978 JP was concerned as to the viability of the project and his position in relation to it. Prior to Christmas there was a conference in which Realtor was involved and during which Realtor made large of the difficulties of design (e.g. communal toilets) and of construction (e.g. water problems) and of the poor reputation of the project. At the first meeting after Christmas 1978 Realtor mentioned an institutional investor as an entity which had monies available for investments in real estate in properties of the value of $1,000,000 or more, and Realtor suggested that an approach be made to them. That proposal was approved. Following that, in about February 1979, Realtor communicated to JP and to RJ an offer from the institutional investor to purchase the property for $1,210,000. From that followed the sale in December 1979. Despite the enquiry made by RJ in the letter of September 1978 (Ex. 1), I am satisfied that the thought of sale at any early date had not been in JP's mind until the discussions of December 1978 - January 1979.
  2. B's evidence was that he had first learned that a sale was contemplated only in mid-1979, after the term loan had been taken up from Nominees. His evidence was, and I accept it, that if he had known during 1978 that an early sale was contemplated, he would have recommended that JP seek an extension of temporary bank finance rather than take up a fixed term loan with Nominees, particularly having regard to the substantial penalties which Nominees expected to be paid in the event that its mortgage did not run its full term. Regardless of just when it was that B was first advised of the possibility of a sale of the property, it is at least clear that on 14 June 1979 the Bank supplied Target with figures to settle (Ex AZ) claiming (inter alia) additional interest of $20,371.81 calculated to 29 August 1979. On 3 July 1979 the solicitors to the bank wrote to S in relation to the proposed discharge of mortgage (Ex BA).
  3. The settlement did not eventuate as was then expected. The evidence establishes that in the months following the relationship between Target and Realtor worsened and difficulties with tenants continued (Exs. 3B, BD, BE, 3G and BH). Whatever may have occasioned the delay, on 20 December 1979 Target contracted for the sale of the entire property to the investment institution for $1,200,100 (Ex. J) and settlement was effected that day (Ex. N). The evidence of JP (and I accept it) was that from very early in the year when for the first time he had considered a sale of the property and the institution had made its offer and he had agreed to sell, he had regarded himself as being committed to the sale to that institution and at that price, and that the improvement in the letting of the development which had taken place in the months leading up to December 1979 did not alter that.
  4. The sale itself was not without complications. The purchasing institution was concerned to have a trouble-free investment. It sought to be assured as to the quality of construction and the performance of tenants. As to the former, it sought to be assured that the structures would be maintained in sound condition for five years without the institution being put to any significant expense. BCO entered into appropriate covenants (Ex. S) and, at least according to a draft Deed exhibited in evidence, JP personally assumed liability to BCO to idemnify it as to 50% of any expenses it might incur in that regard (Ex. S). As to the tenancies, the institution called on Target to guarantee the performance of only one tenant. That tenant happened to be Carrier - the company whose initial enquiries had led to JP and RJ meeting (Ex. S).

The Post-Sale Peried

39. In the years that followed, the relationship establishes between JP and RD continued and, in particular they embarked upon another net dissimilar joint venture or land acquired for the purpose. However, all did not proceed smoothly for the family of RJ. Some major developments went very badly for RJ and his family. By December 1982, BCO no longer carried on business as a builder. During 1983 RJ worked as a project manager for some building owners and in 1984 he commenced his present employment as project manager for a substantial builder. Very heavy losses were suffered in relation to a major project and by 1984 the matrimonial home had to be sold. The proceeds of its sale ($500,000) were largely applied in discharging outstanding debts of the family companies. A much more modestly priced home became the family residence. In addition, RJ was unable to maintain his interest in the new joint venture with JP and JP had to purchase his interest - a circumstance which may have been the only reason which forced JP to sell portions of the new development. RJ also had to realise on other investments which had been held long-term.

Conclusions

40. Resolution of the issues calls for findings of fact. No difficult questions of law arise. In making those findings of fact I take guidance, particularly from the following observations of his Honour Gibbs C.J.:

(a) "Although evidence given by a taxpayer as to the purpose with which he acquired property must, for obvious reasons, 'be tested most closely, and received with the greatest caution' (Pascoe v F. C. of T. ...) it would be wrong for a judge to regard the evidence of a taxpayer as prima facie unacceptable." (McCormack v F. C. of T.79 ATC 41li at p. 4121; [1979] HCA 18; (1979) 143 C.L.R. 284 at pp. 301-302)

(b) "The taxpayer's evidence must of course be considered on its merits, in the light of the circumstances of the case, without any prepossession, favourable or unfavorable." (ibid.. at ATC p. 4121; C.L.R. p. 302)

(c) "If the taxpayer gives evidence that -the property in question was not acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale, and that evidence is accepted, he of course,” (ibid. at ATC p. 4121; C. L. R. p. 392) the case does not fall within sec. 28 ( a), even though he does not give evidence or does give evidence but is disbelieved." (ibid., at ATC p. 4121; C.L.R. p. 302)

(e) "[T]he fact that the taxpayer was disbelieved could, in appropriate circumstances, itself give rise to an inference adverse to the taxpayer's case: Steinberg v F. C. of T. 75 ATC 422) at p. 4231; [1975] HCA 63; (1975) 134 C.L.R. 640 at p. 694." (cited in McCormack ibid., at ATC p. 4121; C.L.R. p. 302)

(f) "[I]f the proper inference to be drawn from the evidence is that the taxpayer bought the property for a purpose other than that of profit-making by sale, the appeal will succeed. An obvious example would be a case in which it clearly appeared that a taxpayer purchased a house and for many years thereafter occupied it as his own home. In those circumstances the natural inference, in the absence of evidence to the contrary, would be that the taxpayer had bought the house for the purpose of dwelling in it, and the fact that the taxpayer was not an honest witness would hardly matter." (ibid., at ATC p. 4121; C.L.R. p. 302)

(g) "[I]f the taxpayer's evidence of the purpose with which he acquired the property is not accepted, and it does not appear from the other evidence on the balance of probabilities that he did not acquire the property for the purpose of profit-making by sale, he will fail to discharge his onus of proof. When I speak of purpose I mean, of course, the main or dominant sucrose actuating the acquisition." (ibid., at ATC p. 4121; C.L.R. p. 302)

(h) "The fact that a witness is disbelieved does not prove the opposite of what he asserted." (Steinberg v F. C". of T. ante at ATC p. 4231; C.L.R. p. 694)

(i) "It has sometimes been said that where the story of a witness is disbelieved, the result is simply that there is no evidence on the subject. but although this is no doubt true in Tany cases it is not correct as a universal proposition. There may be circumstances in which an inference can be drawn from the fact that the witness has told a false story, for example, that the truth would be harmful to him; and it is no doubt for this reason that false statements by an accused person may sometimes be recrarded as corroboration of other evidence given in a criminal case." (Steinberg ante at ATC p. 4231, C.L.R. p. 694

(j) "[I]f the truth must lie between two alternative states of fact, disbelief in evidence that one of the state of facts exists may support the existence of the alternative state of facts." (Steinberg ante at ATC p. 4232; C.L.R. p. 694)

41. I am satisfied that Target did not carry on any business of dealing in properties or conduct itself in such a way as to ' constitute the "profit" on sale as income according to ordinary concepts, assessable pursuant to s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Tax Act"). I am also satisfied that there was no stage during the period in which Target held the site at which Target commenced to carry out any "profit-making undertaking or scheme" in relation to the property, not being property acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale. Accordingly, the second limb of s.26(a) of the Tax Act has no application. The question which remains to be answered is whether the "profit" constituted assessable income of Target on the basis that it was "profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by (it) for the purpose of profit making by sale ...": the first limb of s.26(a) of the Tax Act.

  1. The taxpayer is a company. Its "purpose" must be discovered in the "controlling mind and will" of a person or persons who controlled it. Insofar as that "controlling mind and will" is to be identified with JP, I am satisfied that the land was not so acquired by Target. I am satisfied that at all material times the intention of JP was that the land should be developed and held as an investment into the indefinite future. Insofar as P.J is concerned, the question is more complicated. He was a speculator as other experiences had shown him to be and as he freely acknowledged in relation to other matters. But he had also demonstrated a desire to hold on to investments long-term. He claimed the development by Target to belong to the latter category. RJ was also an optimist and an opportunist: "terms to be understood as explained hereafter. what is perhaps more important is that I find him to have been honest, although not completely open in his dealings with JP. I find that when he agreed to the "joint venture" he was honestly proposing his assent to the concept of a Long-term investment as proposed by JP. His optimism was such that he could convince himself that he had the capacity to maintain such an investment long-term and that he wanted to do so. On the other hand, I accept that by September 1978 he was already enquiring about the possible sale of the development. That is possibly something which was forced on him by mounting pressures such as those which were later to cost him dearly. Those enquiries reflected the opportunist in him. . In my assessment he was then considering all possibilities open to him. Without being unfaithful to his commitment to JP, he made enquiries as to the possibility of sale. I accept that JP knew nothing of those thoughts or enquiries at that time. In making my assessment of RJ, I have also considered drafts of an agreement for sale produced from the file of the solicitor S which was apparently prepared during 1978 (Ex. BO). It appeared in three versions, each more developed than the other. By its terms the third version proposed the sale of the entire share capital in Target to an unnamed purchaser for $50,000; and the appointment of JP as a director in place of Mrs RJ. As those matters were not canvassed in evidence - beyond the mere production of the exhibit - I do no more than acknowledge them.
  2. At the end of the day the question is, whether on the balance of probabilities RJ and JP have satisfied me that the property was not acquired by Target for the dominant purpose of profit-making by sale. They have so satisfied me. That being so in the circumstances it is not necessary to consider the action of the Commissioner in quantifying taxable income so as to include paid up capital in and exclude "carry forward losses" from in the calculation of taxable income.

44. I uphold the objection of the Applicant to the assessment of Target.
