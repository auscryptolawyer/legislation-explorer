Levingston NB Pty Ltd v Michael McGurk & Ors [1998] FCA 1218 (25 September 1998)

Last Updated: 30 September 1998

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA



NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

NG 948 OF 1998

BETWEEN:

LEVINGSTON NB PTY LIMITED

(ACN 074 226 476)

APPLICANT

AND:

MICHAEL MCGURK

FIRST RESPONDENT

97 YORK STREET PTY LIMITED

(ACN 082 297 023)

SECOND RESPONDENT

99 YORK STREET PTY LIMITED

(ACN 082 374 669)

THIRD RESPONDENT

95 YORK STREET PTY LIMITED

(ACN 083 977 023)

FOURTH RESPONDENT

96 YORK STREET PTY LIMITED

(ACN 083 976 970)

FIFTH RESPONDENT

LUCKZEN PTY LIMITED

SIXTH RESPONDENT

JUDGE:

TAMBERLIN J

DATE:

25 SEPTEMBER 1998

PLACE:

SYDNEY

REASONS FOR JUDGMENT

The applicant ("Levingston") seeks a continuation of interlocutory orders made against the first to fourth respondents on 14 September 1998, together with a further order made on 17 September 1998.

The orders in question restrain the first four respondents from taking any further steps to effect or complete transaction with respect to strata title lots in a commercial building situated at 95-99 York Street, Sydney ("the building") which is owned by the Bowlers Club of NSW Limited ("the Bowlers Club").

The application has been discontinued against the fifth and sixth respondents. In respect of the fifth respondent the only live issue is in relation to the costs. I note that the issue of payment of these costs has been reserved.

The transactions in question, which are sought to be restrained, arise out of an Agreement made on 15 April 1998 ("the Agreement") between the applicant and the first three respondents ("McGurk"), ("97 York St"), and ("99 York St").

McGurk is the sole director of 97 York St and 99 York St. The sole director of the fourth and fifth respondents is a Mr McDonald ("McDonald"), who is the brother-in-law of McGurk and with whom he has continuously worked out of the same office for about six years. In evidence, McDonald said that although he was the sole director of 95 York St and 96 York St, he did not know who the shareholders of those companies were.

The applicant's case is that partnership assets, or assets in which it has an equitable interest, have been diverted by 97 York St to other closely related entities contrary to the Agreement, so that any profits from ultimate disposal of the partnership property (which comprises the acquisition of rights to acquire floors in the building) will be directed to companies which are under the control of McGurk or his close associate McDonald.

Levingston says that McGurk and 97 York St owed a duty of good faith in relation to Levingston and that this duty has been breached by failing to hold on its behalf options over strata lots in the building, as required by the Agreement.

Levingston also relies on oral and written representations to that effect. It further says that the call options were procured by 97 York St in favour of its associated company, 99 York St. A consequence of this is that profits, which are said to be those profits otherwise flowing to 97 York St to be shared with Levingston, were directed to 99 York St with the consequence that Levingston was deprived of the benefit of its interest in the property which was the subject of the Agreement. The evidence does not disclose that the substitution of 99 York St or the assignment of the call option of Lot 14 ("the car park") was agreed to by Levingston. It is submitted that there is a serious question to be tried whether the substitution of 99 York St and the subsequent transactions were part of a deliberate course of action on the part of McGurk and 99 York St and indeed McDonald to deprive Levingston of the fruits of the Agreement with 97 York St.

In the case of the car park, Lot 14, the evidence discloses that the call option was transferred to an entity known as 95 York St of which McDonald was the sole director. As mentioned earlier, he is the brother-in-law of McGurk and he works out of the same office. He has regular contact with McGurk. He requested and collected a cheque at one stage from Levingston in respect of advertising costs, made pursuant to the Agreement. The evidence does not establish, and it is not submitted, that the substitution of 99 York St as "the purchaser" of the rights to acquire was made by mistake or as the consequence of a drafting oversight. It was said to be knowing and deliberate.

Levingston further alleges misleading or deceptive conduct under s 52 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 (Cth) ("the Act") and relies on ss 80 and 87 of that Act as a basis for injunctive relief. It is also said that there was an implied term of the Agreement that McGurk or 97 York St would not deprive Levingston of the benefit of the Agreement: see Himbleton Pty Limited v Kumagai (NSW) Pty Limited (1991) 29 NSWLR 44 at 59, and Secured Income Real Estate (Australia) Limited v St Martins Investments Pty Limited [1979] HCA 51; (1979) 144 CLR 596 at 607-609.

The nub of the Agreement was that McGurk and 97 York St would acquire rights to buy and sell floors in the building and would split the profits 45% to Levingston and 55% to McGurk and 97 York St. This obligation was expressed to be in consideration of Levingston lending $100,000 to be used by 97 York St in acquiring its rights in the project. It is evident that the Agreement envisaged that 97 York St would itself acquire the rights and that McGurk and 97 York St had agreed to assign to Levingston, and hold on its behalf, 45% of the rights to be acquired by 97 York St. I am satisfied that the rights to acquire floors or parts of floors in the building are property and can arguably be described as partnership assets: see Canny Gabriel Castle Jackson Advertising Pty Limited v Volume Sales (Finance) Pty Limited [1974] HCA 22; (1974) 131 CLR 321 at 326-327. In these circumstances I am not satisfied that damages is an adequate remedy.

A number of matters were raised by the respondents in resisting the claim for interlocutory restraint and I will briefly advert to them.

First, it is said, damages or compensation provide an adequate remedy and that all the Agreement evidences is a right to participate in profits which can be accounted for in due course without the necessity for injunctive relief. In my view, this submission should not be accepted. The transactions in question involve dealing with what is arguably partnership property, or property in which Levingston has an equitable interest, in such a way as to divert the benefits away from Levingston into entities associated with McGurk, without consent or knowledge on the part of Levingston. In the case of the fourth respondent there is evidence, even on the basis of the valuation obtained by the fourth respondent, that the ability to earn a profit in the order of $500,000 has been disposed of to a company with which 99 York St has a close association for a nominal amount of $10. If one accepts the evidence of the valuer tendered by Levingston then the value of that right is in the order of more than $1.7 million. The minimum valuation on the evidence of $1.25 million for the car park indicates a disposition at well below market value and strongly supports the claim for relief.

Second, it is submitted that Levingston is disentitled from relief because of lack of clean hands. I am not satisfied at this stage that the advance by Mr Waterhouse to Mr Levingston and the arrangements made between them was in breach of the Agreement or that any assignment or disposition was made of an interest in the Agreement without consent. In particular, I am not satisfied on the evidence that there is any relevant material lack of clean hands which would disentitle Levingston to relief to which it is otherwise entitled. To constitute a defence of clean hands it is necessary that the conduct in question is related to the relief sought. I am not satisfied that this requirement is met: see Meyers v Casey [1913] HCA 50; (1913) 17 CLR 90 at 124

Third, it is said that there was undue delay because Levingston was aware by 8 April 1998 that 99 York St was to be the purchaser under the call option. However, Levingston gave evidence that, although he was aware of the position of that revision, he did not appreciate its significance until very recently. On the present state of the evidence before me, I am satisfied that this evidence should be accepted.

Fourth, it is said that as the result of the long delay other commitments had been made by the respondents to third parties which would be affected by the orders. I am not satisfied that any such prejudice would necessarily ensue or that it would outweigh the claims of Levingston to interlocutory relief: see in particular cl 7(b) of the assignment by 99 York St to Milstern Pty Limited

Fifth, it is contended that Levingston is a company of no substance and that, therefore, its undertaking as to damages is worthless. Mr Simon Levingston, the sole director of that company has given a personal undertaking to the Court as to damages. The weight of the evidence before me is that he is a person of financial substance. I do not accept the submission that relief ought not be granted because the undertaking as to damages is worthless. Mr Levingston swore an affidavit as to his means. He was cross-examined. Although some doubts were relied on by the respondents as to his credibility, I am not satisfied that a case has been made out on the evidence.

Sixth, the fourth respondent submits there is no sufficient case shown against it on the evidence. In my view, given the close working association between McGurk and McDonald, (who is the sole director of 95 and 96 York St) there is a seriously arguable question as to whether McDonald was aware of the Agreement and the diversion of the call options to entities away from 97 York St. Furthermore, the evidence of the substantial undervaluation for the assignment of the car park from 95 York St to 99 York St even on the most favourable evidence to the fourth respondent calls for examination and lends further support to the case against the fourth respondent.

Seventh, the fourth respondent says that it will be exposed to substantial losses if the injunction is granted. I am not satisfied that this possibility is such as to warrant refusal of relief on a discretionary basis.

Eighth, it is also said for the fourth respondent that the "horse has bolted" and that it is too late for the Court to now intervene. This argument does not carry such attraction in circumstances where it is still possible for the Court to prevent what is alleged to be a deliberate attempt to deprive Levingston of the benefit of its Agreement with 97 York St and to divert those benefits to entities associated with McGurk and his brother-in-law McDonald.

In my opinion, the evidence raises a serious question to be tried in relation to the way in which possible partnership assets have been dealt with by the first three respondents to the knowledge and assistance of the fourth respondent. I consider that the involvement of the fourth respondent in relation to the car park is sufficient to raise a serious contention that relief should be granted against it. It is not necessary for me to express any view on the other claims made and I do not do so at this stage. Nor is it necessary or appropriate to reach any conclusion as to the credibility of the witnesses who have presented evidence before the Court. I am also of the opinion that the balance of convenience is in favour of granting the relief sought.

It was submitted that this is a case in which expedition might to be granted. I am not satisfied that a sufficient case for expedition has been made out but I will hear further submissions from the parties in relation to expedition.

In relation to costs, I am satisfied that the general rule should apply; namely, that the unsuccessful party should pay the costs of the successful party. Accordingly, the first to fourth defendants should pay the applicant's costs of the application for interlocutory relief. I direct the applicant to bring in draft short minutes to give effect to the above reasons.
