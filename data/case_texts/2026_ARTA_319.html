Free and Tax Practitioners Board [2026] ARTA 319 (10 February 2026)

Tribunal Number: 2025/2994

Tribunal: Senior Member R Olding

Place: Brisbane

Decision: 1. The Tribunal sets aside the decisions under review and in substitution decides:

(a) the applicant is given a written caution regarding the contraventions of the Code of Professional Conduct set out in the Tribunal’s reasons for decision; and

(b) the applicant must, within 12 months of the date of these orders, undertake and complete remedial education in the following course, or an equivalent course approved by the respondent in writing, and provide evidence of satisfactory completion of the course to the respondent within seven (7) business days of doing so: TASA 2009 and The Code of Professional Conduct Course offered by The Tax Institute

(a) disclosure of information tending to reveal the identity of the applicant; and

before 10 March 2026, is prohibited.

(c) the Tribunal’s registry is not to cause the decision and the Tribunal’s reasons to be published, other than to the parties, except with the written approval of a member of the Tribunal.

Statement of Reasons

WHAT IS THIS CASE ABOUT?

  1. The Tax Practitioners Board concluded that the applicant failed to act with honesty and integrity in his communications with the Board and failed to comply with his tax obligations in respect of his personal affairs. Those conclusions informed the Board’s view that the applicant is not a fit and proper person to be a registered tax agent.
  2. Consequently, on 13 February 2025, the Board decided to terminate the applicant’s tax agent registration and to prohibit him from re-applying for registration for two years. The applicant has applied for review of those decisions.
  3. The case gives rise to some construction issues. In particular, the meaning of tax compliance obligations in respect of a tax agent’s “personal affairs” and in that regard the meaning of entities over which the applicant has “informal control”. Additionally, there is an issue regarding whether copies of notices of assessment of income tax filed with the Tribunal engage statutory conclusive evidence provisions, such that the Tribunal must treat the assessments as correct in these proceedings, even where they are contested by the applicant.
  4. The applicant also seeks continuation of the Tribunal’s interim confidentiality order, which is opposed by the Board.

STATUTORY FRAMEWORK

  1. The regulatory regime for tax agents is found in the Tax Agents Services Act 2009 (Cth) (TASA).[1]
  2. The object of the TASA, as set out in s 2-5(1), is:
    to support public trust and confidence in the integrity of the tax profession and the tax system by ensuring that *tax agent services are provided to the community in accordance with appropriate standards of professional and ethical conduct.
  3. That object is promoted by a system of registration of tax agents, a Code of Professional Conduct and sanctions for non-compliance with the Code.
  4. The Code is contained in Part 3 of the Act. Section 30-10 relevantly provides:
(1) You must act honestly and with integrity.

(2) You must comply with the *taxation laws in the conduct of your personal affairs.

  1. If a tax agent fails to comply with the Code, the Board may:
    (a) give the tax agent a written caution;

    (b) give the tax agent an order under s 30-20;

    (c) suspend the tax agent’s registration for a period under s 30-25; or

    (d) terminate the tax agent’s registration under s 30-30.

  2. The orders the Board may make under s 30-20 include an order requiring the tax agent to undertake a course of training or education or provide tax agent services only under supervision of a registered tax agent; or limiting the services the tax agent may provide or requiring the tax agent to notify existing clients of the Board’s findings.
  3. Additionally, under s 40-5(1) of the TASA, the Board may terminate the registration of a tax agent who ceases to meet a requirement for registration. One such requirement, specified in s 20-5(1), is that the Board is satisfied a tax agent who is an individual “is a fit and proper person”.
  4. If the Board terminates a tax agent’s registration, under s 40-25 the Board may also determine a period of not more than five years during which the person may not apply for registration.
  5. The Tribunal’s authority to make a non-publication order is derived from s 70 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth). Considerations the Tribunal must have regard to are set out in s 71, as discussed below.

ISSUES TO BE DETERMINED

  1. It is the Board’s decisions of 13 February 2025 that are before the Tribunal for review.
  2. The applicant submitted that the review requires consideration of these issues:
    (a) Issue 1: Did the applicant fail to act honestly and with integrity and thereby breach s 30-10(1) of the TASA?

    (b) Issue 2: Did the applicant fail to comply with taxation laws in the conduct of his “personal affairs” and thereby breach s 30-10(2)?

    (c) Issue 3: Is the applicant a fit and proper person for the purposes of s 20-5(1)(a)?

    (d) Issue 4: Should any sanction be imposed on the applicant, and whether the respondent correctly determined, in accordance with s 40-25(1), that the applicant should be prohibited from applying for registration for a period of two years from the date when the termination takes effect?

    (e) Issue 5: Should a non-publication order be made?

BACKGROUND

  1. The Tribunal stayed the operation of the Board’s decisions pending the Tribunal’s decision on the application for review and made interim confidentiality orders.[2]
  2. The significant persons and entities relevant to these reasons are:
    (a) The applicant.

    (b) The applicant’s wife.

    (c) A Family Trust created by the applicant. At all relevant times, the applicant the Family Trust had a corporate trustee, OPL.

    (d) A self-managed superannuation fund (SMSF). The initial corporate trustee, OPL, was replaced on 15 March 2017 by another company, OKPL, which was in turn replaced, on 3 February 2018, by another company, MPC, which has been the trustee continuously since that date.

    (e) A senior accountant, Mr Smith.[3]

    (f) The applicant’s Former Employer.

  3. The applicant was the director of OPL from 31 January 1990 until he was replaced by the applicant’s wife as the sole director on 14 March 2014.[4] The applicant was reappointed as a director on 30 June 2021.
  4. The applicant’s wife has held all the shares in OPL at least since 10 December 2014. Previously, the shares in OPL were held by the applicant.[5]
  5. The SMSF was established for the benefit of the applicant and the applicant’s wife. However, the applicant ceased to be a member of the fund in 2017 and did not resume being a member until 2021.[6]
  6. The applicant’s wife became the sole director of MPC on 3 February 2018, the date on which MPC became the trustee of the SMSF. The applicant’s wife has held all the shares in MPC at least since 28 February 2018. Previously, the applicant held all of the shares in MPC.[7]

ISSUE 1: DID THE APPLICANT FAIL TO ACT HONESTLY AND WITH INTEGRITY

  1. The Board based its submissions on this issue on communications relating to:
    (a) the applicant’s control over the SMSF; and

    (b) termination of the applicant’s former employment.

  2. The facts set out below are largely uncontroversial. The factual controversy relates to the applicant’s state of mind when making the relevant statements and in giving his evidence before the Tribunal. The Board asserts that the applicant’s evidence in that regard should not be accepted and that he lied to the Board and the Tribunal. Those are serious allegations warranting close examination.

Statements regarding the applicant’s “control” over the SMSF

  1. In his application for registration as a tax agent submitted on 24 September 2020, the applicant answered “No” to the question:
    Do you or any associated entities that you have direct, or indirect control over, have overdue tax obligations?
  2. The applicant accepts that the SMSF had outstanding tax lodgement obligations at that time and that he was aware that those obligations were outstanding when he submitted the application for registration.[8] The applicant’s response to a query by the Board regarding why he answered “No” to the question about overdue tax obligations of associated entities included this statement:
    Regarding the non-individual entities mentioned, it is crucial to clarify that at the time of my declaration, I was not a director, nor did I have formal or informal control over either entity.
  3. The Board accepts that the applicant did not have formal control over the SMSF as the applicant’s wife, not the applicant, had been the sole director of the corporate trustee of the SMSF following the applicant’s bankruptcy in 2014. However, the Board submits that the applicant had informal control over his wife’s actions in that regard. The Board does not go so far as to say the applicant’s wife was a “shadow director”.[9]
  4. Further, the Board submits that the applicant was dishonest in asserting before the Tribunal that he held a belief that the Board would not “take it as a negative that a self-managed superannuation fund was behind on its tax obligations”. The Board says that is so impossible to believe that the Tribunal should not believe it.
  5. It is important to consider the applicant’s response against the context in which it was given. The question to which the applicant answered “No” was whether he exercised “control” over the fund, albeit “formal or informal” control. It was not, for example, whether the trustee was accustomed to acting in accordance with the applicant’s wishes or some other lesser test than control. The applicant was neither a director nor a shareholder of the trustee company at that time and had not been for well over two years. At the time he made the statement by submitting the form, he was also not a member of the SMSF.
  6. In my view, it is not inherently improbable that a person who is a qualified and experienced accountant might genuinely take the view that he did not “control”, either formally or informally, a trustee company of which he was neither a director nor shareholder and which acted as trustee of a superannuation fund of which he was not a member. I consider that an accountant could at the same time exercise influence over a company of which his wife is the only director and shareholder but hold a genuine belief that he did not in any sense control the company, formally or informally. I accept that the applicant held such a belief.
  7. The Board asserts that the applicant having informal control of the company is consistent with the evidence of the applicant and his wife in the Fair Work Commission (FWC) proceedings concerning the applicant’s dismissal from employment by his Former Employer – discussed further below - and in correspondence from Mr Smith. A transcript of the FWC proceeding is in evidence along with a copy of Mr Smith’s correspondence.
  8. The Board submits, in respect of the applicant’s statement to the effect that he did not exercise formal or informal control over the SMSF, that:
    This statement is untrue because the Applicant had exercised some degree of indirect or informal control over the [SMSF].
  9. That submission, even if accepted, does not answer the question before the Tribunal. The question that the applicant answered in the negative was whether he had direct or indirect control over relevant entities. It was not whether he exercised some degree of control. Still less would the submission, if accepted, support the serious allegation that the applicant deliberately lied in the application and before the Tribunal.
  10. The Board also says that in the FWC proceedings the applicant “conceded that he acted as his wife’s ‘proxy’ in regard to the corporate trustee of the [SMSF]”.[10] However, reference to the parts of the transcript of the FWC proceedings referenced in the Board’s submissions does not, in my view, support that broad and unequivocal statement.
  11. It is true that the applicant responded “Yes” to the cross-examiner’s suggestion that, after he ceased to be a director and his wife assumed that position, the applicant “continued as her proxy”. However, the questioning at that point was largely about the Unit Trust not the SMSF. Additionally, the applicant went on to qualify his response, saying:
    I would convey to [the applicant’s wife] what was going on and get her actual instructions. I wasn’t a proxy without any recourse.

    And later, when it was put to the applicant that he effectively made decisions concerning OPL, the applicant similarly qualified his response:

    With the reservation I said before, if it was a weighty type of thing I would talk with [the applicant’s wife] before I would cast a vote.[11]

  12. Similarly, examination of the nominated pages of the transcript of the FWC proceedings recording the evidence of the applicant’s wife does not, in my view, support an unqualified submission that her evidence would corroborate a finding that the applicant acted as her proxy. The applicant’s wife did agree that the applicant was her proxy for OPL but that was in response to a question relating to the affairs of the Former Employer. It was not about OPL generally and was not in the context of OPL’s role as trustee of the SMSF. OPL was not acting in that capacity at that time and had not since 2017.
  13. The applicant’s wife agreed that she did not manage the accounts of OPL and that she signed tax returns and other documents without reading them, leaving such matters to her husband and the accounting firm, and stating that she did not “have a business head”. Nevertheless, the answers the applicant’s wife gave to cross-examination questions revealed some awareness of significant correspondence and the like.[12] Further, there was no suggestion that the applicant exercised any form of coercive control over his wife.
  14. The Board also referred to correspondence from Mr Smith stating the applicant’s family trust and superannuation fund were “under his governance”. However, those comments followed an explanation that the applicant had been a director of the practice entity until his bankruptcy and remained a senior accountant until his dismissal. The reference to the trust and superannuation fund being under his governance was qualified “save that a staff member may have done some of the number crunching for his superannuation fund when required”.[13]
  15. Viewed in that context, the statement appears to relate to the conduct of the accounting work rather than control of the SMSF. In any case, Mr Smith and the applicant had parted professional company acrimoniously. In those circumstances, it would not be appropriate to give this correspondence significant weight when its contents have not been tested.
  16. Overall, I am left with the impression that the applicant’s wife largely left accounting and related matters to her husband and the accounting firm but not that the applicant exercised any form of control over her. It could readily be concluded that the applicant’s wife generally acted in accordance with her husband’s advice and left accounting and taxation matters largely in his hands. That is scarcely surprising; the applicant is, after all, a chartered accountant and tax agent charged with responsibility for the SMSF’s accounting and taxation affairs.
  17. However, as already mentioned, that is not the test. I am not persuaded that the applicant informally controlled the trustee company. The applicant’s wife had formal control over trustee company as its sole director. She was also the sole shareholder. She was not altogether ignorant of its affairs. She was not, so far as the evidence reveals, suppressed or otherwise controlled by the applicant.
  18. In any case, the relevant question for current purposes is whether the applicant should be taken to have lied to the Board when he answered “No” to the question about not having informal control. An enduring impression left with me after hearing the applicant’s evidence was of a person who carefully parsed and analysed questions put to him to ensure that his questions were literally correct. Aside from the considerations already mentioned, with the benefit of observing the applicant giving evidence, it does not strike me as surprising that the applicant would take the view that he did not control the SMSF, informally or otherwise, when he was not at the relevant time a director or shareholder of the fund’s trustee company nor a member of the fund.
  19. What the applicant considered might amount to informal control was explored in cross examination. The applicant responded:
    An example would be if she was incapacitated, incapable of making a decision, and I make a decision. I think that would be informal control. But that never happened.[14]
  20. That may not, on one view, be an example of informal control. It is certainly not the only circumstance that might be regarded as informal control. But it is not an irrational or implausible explanation.
  21. Having regard to the circumstances prevailing at the time the applicant answered the relevant question on the application form and my observation of his approach to answering questions before the Tribunal, I am satisfied the applicant was not deliberately dishonest in answering the informal control question in the negative or in his evidence in that regard before the Tribunal. His requests for clarification of questions were tedious at times, but his answers in respect of the informal control question were not, in my view, dishonest.
  22. Accordingly, considered against the context in which they were given, I would not regard the applicant’s responses to the question of informal control, either in the 24 February 2020 application form or before the Tribunal, as weighing against his honesty and integrity.

Statements regarding termination of the applicant’s employment

  1. The second basis on which the Board submits that the applicant failed to act with honesty and integrity relates to the applicant’s application for renewal of his tax agent registration which the applicant lodged with the Board on 20 January 2024.
  2. The application form required a response to the question:
    Do you have any matters that may affect your good fame, integrity and character . . . to disclose?
  3. The applicant did not disclose that he had been dismissed from his previous employment for (as the Former Employer asserted) serious misconduct, including dishonesty.
  4. The applicant had been employed by his Former Employer, which operated an accounting practice, or its predecessor firm, for some 33 years. The business came to be conducted by successive unit trusts. The units in the relevant Unit Trust were initially held by or on behalf of Mr Smith as to 60% and the applicant, through the trustee of the Family Trust, as to 40%. The unit holdings were adjusted to 50% each when the applicant qualified as a chartered accountant. Later, a third unit holder obtained 20% of the units, with Mr Smith’s and the Family Trust’s unit holdings reducing to 40% each.
  5. In September 2020, the applicant was summarily dismissed from his employment by the Former Employer, for “an alleged failure to follow reasonable management directions on several occasions and engaging in dishonest conduct”. The next day, the applicant commenced operating an accounting practice under his own trading name. The Board approved his registration as a tax agent in February 2021.
  6. The alleged failure to follow reasonable management directions related to the applicant:
    (a) Failing to respond to an ATO request as directed by Mr Smith, apparently because the applicant considered some other form of engagement with the ATO to be more appropriate in the circumstances.

    (b) Failing to provide a response to emails from Mr Smith requesting an explanation for the representation in the SMSF’s 2017 tax return that its accounts had been audited by Mr Smith, when they had not. The applicant asserted, and Mr Smith denied, that this controversy related to a process of pressuring and intimidation concerning the applicant’s attitude to the terms of a proposed sale of the accounting practice.

    (c) Attending the office of the Former Employer despite a written direction that he not have contact with any work-related parties.

    (d) Failing to hand over office keys and his work laptop.

  7. The Former Employer’s allegations of dishonesty included that the applicant:
    (a) falsely stated that he did not lodge or direct anyone to lodge the SMSF’s 2017 tax return; and

    (b) falsely represented that Mr Smith had audited the SMSF’s 2017 accounts when he had not.

  8. The applicant does not accept the Former Employer’s characterisation of the events preceding his dismissal. He commenced proceedings in the FWC for unfair dismissal.
  9. The FWC proceeding was listed for hearing in January 2021. A few days before the scheduled hearing, the parties’ representatives advised that an in-principle settlement had been reached and the hearing was vacated. However, the settlement discussions broke down and on 24 February 2021 the respondent to those proceedings advised the FWC that a binding agreement could not be reached.
  10. The matter proceeded to a Member-Assisted Conference which also failed to resolve the matter. Ultimately, a hearing was held in mid-May 2021, with the Commissioner directing the parties to file written closing submissions by 11 June 2021. On 3 June 2021, the applicant withdrew his application.
  11. The FWC made an indemnity costs award against the applicant in April 2022, accompanied by detailed reasons. The Commissioner’s reasons and a transcript of the FWC proceedings were before this Tribunal. The reasons, although concerned only with the costs issue, include adverse findings against the applicant in respect of the Former Employer’s allegations.
  12. The applicant’s communications with the Board concerning the FWC proceedings form part of the Board’s case for terminating the applicant’s tax agent registration, as discussed below.
  13. The allegation is not based on the 20 January 2024 response in isolation. Rather, the Board emphasises that it was made in the circumstances where the applicant:
    (a) had withdrawn his unfair dismissal application; and

    (b) had previously advised the Board by email dated 19 January 2021 (extracted below) that the Former Employer had conceded that the dismissal was unfair.

  14. However, although those contextual matters are raised, it is the 20 January 2024 statement that the Board relies upon, not the 19 January 2021 communication (other than as context for the 20 January 2024 statement). In any case, for the reasons indicated below, the 19 January 2021 statement is not, in its factual assertion rather than its interpretation of events, false.
  15. It is apparent that the Board was aware of the applicant’s dismissal from his employment. The matter is referenced in the minutes of the Board’s meeting of 18 February 2021. Then, on 19 January 2021 the applicant sent an email to an officer of the Board stating:
    I’m contacting you so that I am assured that you understand unreservedly that, in regards to the settlement of my matter before the Fair Work Commission, the settlement was sought by my former business partner and employer.

    This unequivocally means that they have accepted my employment was unfairly terminated. I trust that you will make the appropriate recommendations in my matter before the Board.

  16. That email was acknowledged by the officer the next day, with an assurance that “[t]he information will be included to [sic] the Board.”
  17. There are a number of other contextual features which, in my view, are important to a proper consideration of this matter:
    (a) Although the applicant withdrew his unfair dismissal claim, he does not accept the Former Employer’s allegations. While it is correct to say that the applicant was dismissed for alleged serious misconduct including dishonesty, it does not follow that he did, in fact, engage in serious misconduct or dishonesty.

    (b) In any case, the Board does not base its allegation of failing to act with honesty and integrity on the matters that were controversial in the FWC proceedings – that is, on the conduct alleged by the Mr Smith on behalf of the Former Employer. Those matters were not agitated in the current proceedings. Rather, the particulars of the Board’s allegations relate to the failure to disclose this matter in the renewal application lodged in January 2024.[15]

    (c) The FWC proceedings were brought to an end, by the applicant’s withdrawal of his claim, on 8 June 2021. The FWC’s decision awarding costs against the applicant was handed down in April 2022. The impugned response to the question about whether the applicant had matters affecting his good fame, integrity and character was made almost two years later on 20 January 2024.

  18. It is difficult to understand how an accountant of some 30 years standing could genuinely believe that a party to litigation putting forward a settlement offer “unequivocally means” that that party has accepted the other party’s allegations. It is well known that litigants settle litigation to avoid the financial and other costs of litigation without accepting the allegations made by the other party.
  19. However, it is important to examine precisely what the applicant said in the 19 January 2021 email. The only factual assertion is that the Former Employer sought to settle the matter. That is not challenged by the Board. The statement that this – the Former Employer seeking the settlement - unequivocally means the Former Employer accepted that the applicant’s employment was unfairly terminated, is a matter of opinion; that is, the opinion that the fact that the Former Employer sought to settle the litigation means that the Former Employer accepted that the applicant’s employment was unfairly terminated. It was open to the Board’s officers to challenge that opinion or form the view that settlement of the proceedings did not mean that the Former Employer accepted that the termination was unfair.
  20. The applicant gave evidence that “managing the escalating demands [of the FWC litigation] became unfeasible, both financially and professionally, so I reluctantly opted to withdraw my unfair dismissal application”. Clearly, though, he does not accept the Former Employer’s allegations.
  21. The applicant gave evidence that by the time he completed the renewal application in 2024 he believed these events were not relevant. By the time the applicant made the 20 January 2024 statement, some three years had passed since the applicant’s email on 21 January 2021. Well over two years had passed since the applicant withdrew his application to the FWC which brought the proceedings to an end.
  22. Without more, I would accept that it was not unreasonable for the applicant to take the view that issues arising in proceedings that were terminated some two years previously, without any formal ruling, were not relevant to the matters that may affect his good fame, integrity and character. The allegations arose, after all, in the context of an acrimonious parting of the ways of former business partners and in his mind were never accepted by the applicant.
  23. However, as mentioned, the FWC’s findings in the costs decision handed down in April 2022, included adverse findings against the applicant. Again, the context is important. It is not a case where the allegations had not been aired when the proceeding was terminated. It was not until after the FWC had heard the evidence in the case and made directions for filing of closing submissions that the applicant withdrew his application.
  24. The FWC’s findings in the costs decision may be summarised as follows; the Commission:
    (a) was not satisfied that the applicant made the application vexatiously or without reasonable cause;

    (b) was not satisfied that it should have been reasonably apparent to the applicant that, at the time he filed it, the application had no reasonable prospects of success, the Commission determining that the applicant “considered that he would be able to demonstrate that the dismissal was harsh, unjust or unreasonable, and therefore unfair;

    (c) accepted “without any hesitation” that the applicant had completed the 2017 SMSF accounts in late June 2020, asserting that Mr Smith had audited the accounts “when he knew this to be untrue”.

    (d) characterised the applicant’s failure to comply with the direction to respond to ATO queries as “belligerent”;

    (e) concluded that the applicant’s “concerns and paranoia around the potential sale of the business . . . makes no sense at all”, since dismissing the applicant took the potential sale off the table and left the owners with a business that would prove difficult to sell.

  25. The FWC’s finding summarised at (c) above is damning. If taken into account as a fact, it would certainly justify a finding that the applicant failed to act with integrity.
  26. However, the point of reciting these findings made in April 2022 is not for the Tribunal to adopt them. I do not. I make no findings in relation to the matters listed above other than that the findings were made by the FWC. As already noted, it is the applicant’s failure to disclose the issue, rather than facts as found by the FWC, that is the basis of the Board’s allegations.
  27. Rather, the findings are recited as context for the applicant’s response on 20 January 2024 in the negative to the question about whether he had matters that may affect his good fame, integrity and character to disclose.
  28. I accept that the applicant continued to genuinely believe the Former Employer was at fault and that he, the applicant, was a man of integrity and good character. He believed that when he submitted the application for renewal and continues to believe it.
  29. However, the question on the form also addresses the applicant’s good fame. The existence of a finding by the FWC that the applicant asserted in a return that the SMSF’s accounts had been audited, when he knew they had not been, is undoubtedly relevant to the applicant’s good fame. The FWC’s reasons, including this finding, are published in full on AUSTLII. The applicant may contest the finding, but the very existence of the finding is capable of affecting the applicant’s good fame.
  30. The applicant continued to approach the issue on the basis that he did not accept the allegations and that the substantive FWC case was finalised without the issues being resolved.
  31. However, it must have been clear to the applicant that the Former Employer did not accept that the termination was unfair. Even if the applicant genuinely believed that to be the case when it was thought a settlement had been reached, that cannot have been so after the settlement fell through. In any case, by the time the FWC reasons for the costs decision were released, it must have been clear beyond doubt that the Former Employer maintained its allegations and did not accept that the termination was unfair.
  32. In his oral evidence to the Tribunal, the applicant asserted that even after the costs decision he did not know the FWC had made a finding that he had engaged in dishonest conduct.[16] After everything that had occurred in the FWC proceedings and the FWC’s unequivocal findings set out in its reasons, without further explanation it would defy commonsense to accept that evidence as truthful.
  33. The applicant offered no explanation for these assertions in either his cross-examination or in re-examination. The FWC reasons were provided in the context of a substantial costs award against the applicant in an amount far in excess of the amount he was apparently prepared to accept in the failed settlement negotiations. It was a serious matter for the applicant. The applicant read the FWC’s reasons[17] and must have seen the serious finding of dishonesty in relation to the SMSF audit. He did not assert otherwise. He did not say that he misunderstood the findings. In those circumstances, I cannot accept the applicant’s evidence that he did not know the FWC had made a finding of dishonesty against him.
  34. The applicant denied that he did not disclose the finding that he had been terminated for reasons that included dishonesty because it would count against him in the Board’s deliberations. The finding of dishonest conduct was in relation to an issue directly relevant to the applicant’s professional responsibilities as a tax agent; that is, the accuracy of the SMSF’s income tax return. The applicant could have disclosed the FWC findings to the Board and put forward submissions as to why, as he asserts, they should not count against him. He did not say that the matter did not occur to him when he completed the form. I conclude that he made a deliberate choice not to disclose the findings.
  35. The Code’s requirement is that tax agents act both honestly and with integrity. I accept the applicant’s submission that a finding of dishonesty would require a finding of intent. However, in circumstances where an official adjudicatory body has made serious findings of dishonest conduct directly related to the applicant’s obligations as a tax agent, integrity would demand that, when prompted to disclose matters that may affect the tax agent’s good fame, integrity and character, the tax agent would disclose the circumstances to the Board for consideration. The applicant made a deliberate choice not to do so. I conclude that the applicant failed to act with integrity in relation to the statement that there were no matters relevant to his good fame to be disclosed.

Tax compliance obligations as an individual – income tax

  1. The assessed income tax shortfall relating to the applicant’s 2021 return arises primarily from disallowed deductions in the total amount of $57,250. The most significant disallowed amount relates to a deduction of $52,800 for superannuation contributions. The ATO disallowed the deduction on several bases including that the SMSF was at the relevant time not a complying superannuation fund.
  2. The applicant maintains that he was entitled to these deductions but agreed to forego the deductions as part of a settlement in which the ATO remitted penalties previously assessed at 50%. The applicant accepts that the SMSF was at times a non-complying fund but denies that it was non-compliant at the relevant time.
  3. The applicant has not pointed to any evidence supporting that assertion. Nor, so far as the evidence indicates, has the applicant sought to obtain relevant documentation or information from ATO; for example, regarding whether notices as to the SMSF’s compliance status had issued at the relevant times. Nor did the applicant explain – other than because it formed part of a settlement in which penalties were remitted - why he accepted the ATO position without exercising objection rights if, as his submissions to the Board indicated, he was entitled to the deductions.
  4. Referencing the relevant explanatory memorandum,[18] the applicant says that he should not be treated as failing to comply with taxation laws if he adopted a reasonably arguable position in respect of the claiming of the deductions. The difficulty with that submission is that, for the reasons indicated above, I am unable to reach a state of satisfaction that the applicant was entitled to, or had a reasonably arguable position in support of, entitlement to the deductions.
  5. Thus, the Tribunal is left with the position that the applicant has been assessed for the shortfall and has not objected against the assessment. It is not the Tribunal’s role in a proceeding of this kind to undertake a merits review of the taxation decision, still less to do so when the applicant has not objected against the relevant assessment. In any case, I could not treat the assessment as other than accurate on the basis of the evidence before the Tribunal.
  6. Accordingly, I must proceed on the basis that the Board’s allegation in respect of the applicant overclaiming deductions, at least to the extent of in excess of $50,000, is made out. However, I accept the applicant’s unchallenged evidence that the context for the applicant not challenging the disallowance of the deduction was that this formed part of a broader settlement in which penalties were withdrawn.

Tax compliance obligations as an individual – GST

  1. The applicant accepts that he failed to lodge annual GST tax returns by their due dates.[19]
  2. Both returns were lodged on 29 May 2023. The 2021 return was due on 16 May 2022. The 2022 return was due on 15 May 2023. Thus, the 2021 return was 378 days late and the 2022 return was 14 days late. As the applicant became registered as such on 18 February 2021, he was in breach of the ongoing requirement to lodge these returns while a registered tax agent.
  3. The applicant provided some context for the failure to lodge on time relating to the business being dormant as a result of the COVID pandemic and difficulties he experienced in “locating” the returns, presumably on ATO electronic systems. Nevertheless, it is the applicant’s responsibility to ensure his returns are lodged by the due date. These were, after all, not returns from some overlooked, dormant entity. The applicant conducted his accounting practice in his own name. These were, therefore, returns for his practice “entity”.
  4. The applicant accepts that, by failing to lodge these annual GST returns by their due dates, he was in breach of s 30-10(2) of the Code. At the date of the applicant’s second witness statement – 11 September 2025 – his GST returns are up to date. On the basis that the next return is not due until 15 May 2026,[20] I find that his GST lodgements remain up to date at this time.

Tax compliance obligations – the Family Trust

Interpretation of “personal affairs”

  1. The applicant argues that a tax agent does not breach s 30-10(2) by failing to cause associated entities to comply with taxation obligations. That is, in the applicant’s submission, it is only taxation obligations of the tax agent as an individual that are relevant for this purpose.
  2. The expression “personal affairs” is not defined in the TASA or explained in the relevant explanatory memorandum which contains no reference to associated entities. The applicant correctly notes that there is no judicial authority regarding the meaning of “personal affairs” in the context of s 30-10(2).
  3. Further, the applicant submitted that in Tribunal cases in which a tax agent has been found to have breached s 30-10(2) in respect of associated entities, those entities were entities that conducted the tax agent’s practice as a tax agent. I cannot identify and the applicant did not suggest any basis in the text of the legislation or policy for confining the expression to practice entities. Additionally, the applicant noted that in GJ Brown & Co Pty Ltd and Tax Practitioners Board[21] the relevant finding related to an associated entity was in respect of whether the tax agent was a fit and proper person, not whether s 30-10(2) had been breached. In other cases, the parties accepted that personal affairs extended to associated entities and, unlike in this case, the issue was not the subject of detailed submissions.[22]
  4. The applicant also pointed to Birdseye and Tax Practitioners Board[23] as a case where “the Tribunal specifically found that the concept of ‘personal affairs’ did not extend to a non-tax agent company”. That statement is literally correct. However, the Tribunal’s decision was not founded on construction of the legislative expression. Rather, it was based on the Tribunal’s finding that the tax agent had ceased to be a director of the company years before the relevant debts accrued.
  5. However, the Tribunal in Hill and Tax Practitioners Board[24] expressed the view that a restrictive interpretation that confined the obligations to a tax agent’s tax affairs as an individual would be unlikely to be correct, as it would be inconsistent with the objects of the TASA and duplicative of existing taxation law obligations. The Tribunal favoured the view that the obligation extended to the tax agent’s conduct in relation to a company over which the tax agent exercised practical control. For the reasons that follow, I respectfully agree.
  6. The starting point for consideration of the meaning of the expression is the text of the provision in the broader context of the legislation and its stated statutory object. The text of the provision imposes the obligation on “You”, which in context can only refer to a registered tax agent (or BAS agent), in relation to “your personal affairs” – that is, the personal affairs of the tax agent. The question is therefore how the composite expression “your personal affairs” is to be construed in context.
  7. Subsection 30-10(2) appears in Part 3 of the TASA and forms part of the Code of Professional Conduct which “regulates your personal and professional conduct as a registered tax agent or BAS agent”.[25] The Code is part of the statutory architecture by which the TASA object of “[supporting] public trust and confidence in the integrity of the tax profession and the tax system” is promoted by ensuring tax agent services are provided in accordance with appropriate standards of professional and ethical conduct.
  8. Thus, it is not only professional conduct as a tax agent that is regulated. Personal and ethical conduct is also within the focus of the regulatory regime. That favours a broader rather than a narrower construction of s 30-10(2) subject, of course, to the construction being reasonably open on the text of the provision.
  9. It is not necessary for me to determine the outer limits of application of the expression “your personal affairs”. For present purposes, it is sufficient to determine whether the applicant’s obligations as a director of OPL are properly characterised as part of his personal affairs. There is no question that the obligations of a company are the responsibility of its directors. On that basis, I consider that a construction that requires a tax agent to comply with taxation obligations that fall upon the tax agent as a director of a company, at least where the tax agent exercises practical control over the company, is reasonably open. Construing the provision as extending to an obligation to comply with taxation laws in relation to the tax agent’s responsibilities as a director does not, in my view, require impermissible violence to the statutory text.
  10. This construction is also consistent with the heading to s 30-10(2): “Honesty and integrity”, which forms part of the TASA.[26] Failing to comply with the taxation obligations of a company in the control of a tax agent is clearly relevant to the tax agent’s integrity.
  11. The alternative construction that would confine the section’s operation to the taxation affairs of the tax agent in their individual capacity would give rise to perverse outcomes. For instance, a tax agent who fails to comply with taxation requirements imposed upon the tax agent in their individual capacity would be in breach of the Code, but a tax agent who is the sole shareholder and director of company, and allows the company wholly within the tax agent’s control to be non-compliant, would not. That would be a surprising intention to attribute to the Parliament. It would not be consistent with the protective objects of the TASA set out above. It would not support public trust and confidence in the integrity of the tax profession and the tax system if tax agents could be non-compliant with obligations relating to entities they own and control without any consequence for their tax agent registration.
  12. Accordingly, I proceed on the basis that the applicant will have breached the Code if, as a director of OPL, he failed to cause OPL to comply with its taxation obligations including in its capacity as trustee of the Family Trust.

Allegations relating to the Family Trust – failing to lodge returns by their due dates

  1. The applicant failed to cause OPL as trustee of the Family Trust to lodge returns for the 2017 to 2021 income years.
  2. The ATO issued a Have you forgotten to lodge? letter in November 2021 and a request to lodge letter the next month. Various communications ensued but the returns were not lodged.
  3. The ATO ultimately issued default assessments for the 2017 to 2021 income years in July 2022 along with penalty notices for failure to provide the returns. The assessments included in the net income of the Family Trust amounts said to be distributions from the Unit Trust, totalling to $301,029 across the five income years. The ATO assessed the trustee for tax on this income on the basis that there was no beneficiary of the Family Trust presently entitled to the income.
  4. The applicant ceased to be a director of OPL in 2014. However, he was re-appointed as a director on 30 June 2021. Thus, OPL’s failure to lodge returns continued at a time when the applicant was both a registered tax agent and a director of OPL.
  5. Accordingly, on the view I have adopted regarding the meaning of “your personal affairs” in s 30-10(2), the applicant breached s 30-10(2) by failing to cause OPL to lodge the Family Trusts returns.

Allegations relating to the Family Trust – outstanding liabilities

  1. The applicant does not deny that OPL had outstanding liabilities totalling $332,367.19 as at 30 September 2024. The failure to meet these obligations continued after the applicant resumed his role as a director of OPL.
  2. Accordingly, on the view that I have taken regarding the meaning of “personal affairs”, this allegation is established. However, there is relevant context to this breach discussed below.

Tax compliance obligations – the SMSF

  1. The Board’s allegation is that the applicant failed to comply with taxation laws relating to his personal affairs because he failed to cause MPC to lodge tax returns for the SMSF for the 2021, 2022 and 2023 income years. This is based on the applicant’s alleged informal control of MPC.
  2. For the reasons indicated above, I do not accept that the applicant exercised informal control over MPC. In any case, I am not persuaded that the taxation obligations of MPC as trustee of the SMSF can be regarded as the applicant’s affairs in circumstances where he was neither a director or shareholder of MPC.
  3. Accordingly, I conclude that this allegation has not been made out.

ISSUE 3: IS THE APPLICANT A FIT AND PROPER PERSON

  1. The Board submits that, taking into account a range of factors, the Tribunal should conclude that the applicant is not a fit and proper person. Those factors, the applicant’s responses and some observations, are summarised below:
    (a) The applicant breached s 30-10(1) and s 30-10(2) of the Code.
  2. The applicant denies that he breached s 30-10(1) of the Code. For the reasons set out above, I have concluded that the applicant failed to act with integrity by not disclosing the FWC’s adverse rulings relevant to his good fame.
  3. The applicant denies that he breached s 30-10(2) other than in respect of the two annual GST returns that were lodged late. For the reasons already given, I concluded that the applicant also failed to comply with taxation obligations relating to his personal affairs by failing to cause OPL to comply with lodgement and payment obligations in respect of the Family Trust.
    (b) The applicant understated his taxable income in his personal income tax returns for the 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2020 income years, resulting in the ATO issuing amended assessments and penalties for recklessness.
  4. The applicant denies that he understated his taxable income for these years and says the Tribunal should take into account that he has contested the assessments and also that he has in place and is complying with a payment plan.
  5. The ATO’s amended assessments for the 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2020 years increased the applicant’s taxable income by $21,946; $25,129; $18,735; and $27,879 respectively. Penalties at 50% of the tax shortfalls for recklessness were also assessed. The differences in taxable income appear to relate to a contested amount of $18,000 included in the applicant’s assessable income for each income year, along with expenses disallowed in various amounts.
  6. The applicant objected against the assessments. In the course of interactions with the ATO, the applicant agreed to withdraw the objections so far as they related to the $18,000 payments on the basis that the applicant would pursue the private binding ruling process in respect of that element of each assessment. It is not clear why this process was suggested to or adopted by the applicant.
  7. The ATO allowed the (amended) objection in full. That leaves only the adjustments relating to the $18,000 payments each year. The applicant says those amounts were invoiced by the Family Trust to the Unit Trust and on that basis represent income of the Family Trust, and not his personal income. He lodged a private ruling request to that effect on 30 September 2025. A ruling had not issued at the time of the hearing.
  8. The applicant gave evidence that the primary tax in dispute relating to these issues is in the order of $25,000. A favourable ruling (followed by a favourable objection decision) would result in a refund in the order of $50,000 to $60,000 after allowing for withdrawal of penalties of approximately $12,000 and interest of approximately $21,000. The Board did not dispute these assertions which I accept.
  9. It is not part of my role to adjudicate on the subject matter of the private ruling request. However, I can say that it is not a case where it is clear on the face of the matter that it is doomed to fail. The Board did not suggest otherwise.
    (c) As a director, the applicant failed to cause OPL to lodge income tax returns for the Family Trust for the 2017 to 2021 income years, resulting in default assessments and penalty notices.
  10. The applicant submits that he was not complicit in OPL’s failure to comply with taxation obligations to lodge these returns when they fell due because he was not a director of OPL at the relevant times. I have accepted that the applicant did not control OPL.
  11. However, I also determined that the applicant’s wife largely left these matters to the applicant to attend to. In those circumstances, it is, in my view, unrealistic for the applicant to assert that he was not complicit in these returns not being lodged. There is no suggestion in the evidence that the applicant’s wife would have resisted the applicant if he sought to prepare the returns for her approval and lodgement. While he did not have legal responsibility, as a practical matter of family dynamics, attending to tax obligations fell fairly in the applicant’s lap.
    (d) The applicant has been found by the ATO on multiple occasions to have failed to comply with taxation laws; and

    (e) has demonstrated a comprehensive and persistent failure to comply with his taxation obligations across several years, including the accrual of significant debts, both in respect of his individual tax affairs and those of the Family Trust, exceeding $370,477.02, necessitating the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation to commence proceedings in the District Court of Queensland to recover taxation liabilities.

  12. It is true that the Deputy Commissioner commenced proceedings in the District Court seeking to recover outstanding tax liabilities. However, the position is not as clear cut as this submission would suggest.
  13. The District Court proceedings were commenced in May 2023. The applicant filed a notice of defence. However, for reasons not explained, neither party has taken steps to advance the proceedings since June 2024.
  14. The ATO assessed the trustee of the Family Trust for substantial amounts of primary tax and penalties for each of the 2017 to 2021 income years. The apparent basis of those assessments was that amounts were distributed by the Unit Trust to the Family Trust. It was on the basis that no beneficiary of the Family Trust was said to be presently entitled to the income that the ATO assessed the trustee of the Family Trust. The notice of assessment is issued to “The Trustee” of the Family Trust; it does not name the trustee.
  15. Those amounts assessed against the trustee of the Family Trust were included, along with substantial interest alleged to have accrued, in the amounts sought to be recovered in the District Court proceedings against the applicant personally. The statement of claim asserts that the applicant was the trustee of the Family Trust.
  16. All of this is rather curious since the applicant maintains, and I find, that OPL was the trustee of the Family Trust, not the applicant. There is a copy of the trust deed for the Family Trust, which is dated 17 January 1991, in evidence. OPL is a party to the deed as the trustee of the trust. There are minutes of a directors’ meeting for OPL in which it is named as trustee of the Family Trust in evidence. Indeed, the Board’s Statement of Facts Issues and Contentions names OPL as the trustee of the Family Trust.
  17. Not surprisingly in those circumstances, the applicant’s Conditional Notice of Intention to Defend the recovery proceedings includes in its reasons for asserting the applicant is not liable for the claimed amounts that he is not the trustee of the Family Trust. Of the total of $370,441.02 claimed, $172,142.57 is stated to be primary tax due as trustee of the Family Trust and $114,520.36 is said to be penalties relating to that primary tax liability.[27] There are also substantial interest charges claimed, a significant proportion of which must be referable to the primary tax and penalties relating to the trust.
  18. OPL as trustee of the Family Trust has objected against the assessments on the basis that there was a beneficiary presently entitled to the net income of the Family Trust in the relevant years of income; namely, the default beneficiary which is said to be MPC, the trustee of the SMSF.
  19. This is not a case where it is obvious the applicant’s private ruling request is doomed to fail. The same may be said of the trustee’s objection. So, what is the Tribunal to do when faced with allegations that the applicant or his entities have failed to pay the correct amount of tax, but those liabilities are subject to ongoing objection or other internal ATO processes?
  20. The Board points to item 2 in the table to s 350-10(1) in Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA). Under that provision, production of a notice of assessment under a taxation law is conclusive evidence that:
    (a) the assessment was properly made; and

    (b) except in review or appeal proceedings under Part IVC of that Act – the amounts and particulars of the assessment are correct.

  21. On this foundation, the Board submits that “the Tribunal should proceed on the basis that these decisions are correct”.
  22. Subsequent to the hearing, in further submissions provided at my invitation, the Board clarified that it made that submission in respect of the assessment against the applicant for the 2021 income year and the assessments against the trustee of the Family Trust for the 2021, 2022 and 2023 income years.[28] Nevertheless, in that same context in its further submission the Board referenced tables in the Deputy Commissioner’s Statement of Claim listing assessments said to support the claim that the defendant (that is, the applicant, not OPL) as trustee of the Family Trust was assessed to these liabilities.
  23. Having regard to the background I have outlined, while it was accurate for the Board to state that the Deputy Commissioner sued the applicant for $370,477.02, that presents a misleading picture. Even if the TAA provisions mentioned above require the notices of assessment to be treated as conclusive evidence of the liabilities – which I discuss further below – those provisions do not have that effect in respect of the Deputy Commissioner’s Statement of Claim.
  24. At most, they could require the assessments to be treated as conclusive evidence of the due making of the assessments and the amounts and particulars assessed. However, the notices of assessment relating to the Family Trust do not mention the applicant, only “The Trustee” of the trust. So, at most the copies of notices of assessment in evidence relating to the Family Trust could be conclusive evidence of the amounts to which the unnamed trustee has been assessed. They cannot be conclusive evidence that those amounts are owed by the applicant. To be fair, the Board in its clarified submissions does not assert otherwise.
  25. However, the Board does present the fact that the applicant has been sued for some $370,000 as a matter to count against him. On the face of the evidence, it is difficult to see how that claim can succeed so far as the liability relates to the Family Trust. Since the applicant is not and has never been the trustee of the Family Trust, the stated basis of the claim appears to be erroneous.
  26. The implications of the Board’s submission that the Tribunal “should proceed on the basis that these decisions of the Commissioner [relating to the Family Trust] are correct” are startling. The Board’s submission is that I must treat those assessments as counting against the applicant and supporting the Board’s claim that he is not a fit and proper person, notwithstanding that the applicant has objected against the assessments.
  27. This is not a mere hypothetical or academic consideration. ATO officers are not infallible; they may have erred in making the assessments. Or additional information may be provided at objection that leads to a favourable decision. The applicant’s amended objections in respect of the 2017 to 2020 income years were, after all, allowed by the ATO.
  28. I accept the Board’s submission that it is not the Tribunal’s role in these proceedings to conduct a merits review of the assessments. But I do not accept that, in assessing whether he is a fit and proper person, I must hold the existence of the assessments against the applicant in a vacuum, so to speak, without regard to any context.
  29. The applicant’s objections against the Family Trust assessments were made on the basis that OPL as trustee is not liable because there was in fact a beneficiary presently entitled to the income of the Family Trust, namely MPC as trustee for the SMSF. There is in evidence a copy of a minute under which the directors of OPL resolved to appoint MPC as default beneficiary.[29] The Board did not submit that the minute is fabricated, not effective or otherwise should not be accepted. The Board did not engage at all with the applicant’s submissions regarding the accuracy or otherwise of the assessments. It is not immediately obvious why the applicant’s assertion that MPC is not presently entitled to the income would not be accepted.
  30. As for the Board’s reliance upon the TAA, Sch 1, s 350-10(1), item 2, it is not clear to me that this provision is engaged based on the evidence before the Tribunal. For item 2 to apply, the relevant notice of assessment must be produced before the Tribunal.
  31. What has been produced are copies of the notices of assessment. Original notices have not been produced. Section 350-10(4) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a copy or extract of a document made or given for the purposes of a taxation law, and signed by the Commissioner, a Second Commissioner, a Deputy Commissioner or a delegate of the Commissioner, is evidence of the matters in the document to the same extent as the original document would have been. No such signed copy or extract was produced in evidence.
  32. Accordingly, it seems to me that the conclusive evidence provisions are not engaged. However, even if they are, I would not accept that that requires me to approach the assessments without regard to the context that the applicant has objected against the assessments. As I have foreshadowed and for the reasons indicated above, this is not a case where the objections are clearly doomed to fail. While I do not rule on what should be the fate of the objections, I do observe and take into account that they contain a rational basis on which the objections could, subject to the ATO’s deliberations and any further information that emerges, be allowed.
  33. In that regard, the Board submitted:
    With respect to the objections, the Respondent accepts that generally, if they are substantially upheld, they may be relevant for the Tribunal to consider. However the timing of the objections and this hearing makes the matter hypothetical.

    (The Board’s emphasis.)

  34. I do not understand why the objections, if upheld, “may” be relevant. In those circumstances, I cannot conceive how the outcome of the objections would not be relevant.
  35. Regardless, it is unsatisfactory for the Tribunal to be called upon to determine the weight to be given to what are grave allegations of non-compliance with taxation laws on the basis of extant assessments when an objection, that is not obviously frivolous or without merit, remains to be determined. It is unfortunate that the parties did not work together with the ATO to ensure the timely determination of the objections such that their fate would have been known before the Tribunal was called upon to take them into account in determining whether the applicant is a fit and proper person.
  36. In that regard, the Board submits that the applicant “only lodged these objections during litigation when the status of his registration is under threat . . .” The assessments were issued in July 2022. It was not until 28 April 2025 that the applicant lodged the objection against the assessments. The ATO advised that it regarded the objection as invalid as it lacked sufficient detail. The applicant lodged a further or revised notice of objection on 1 October 2025.
  37. There is indeed a long delay between the issue of the assessments and the lodging of the objection. The applicant gave unchallenged evidence, which I accept, that a range of health and personal issues confronting the applicant and his family contributed to the delay.
  38. Aside from these issues, the Board did not particularise in the Contentions in its Statement of Facts Issues or Contentions or written submissions the occasions on which the applicant “had been found by the ATO on multiple occasions to have failed to comply with taxation laws”.
  39. I accept that the applicant has failed to comply with taxation laws in multiple respects as outlined above. Without underplaying the significance of such non-compliance, I note the following contextual factors:
    (a) The applicant acknowledges that his tax compliance record is unsatisfactory and set out in unchallenged evidence, which I accept, steps that he had taken to ensure proper compliance in the future.[30]

    (b) Generally, the non-compliance occurred or had its genesis in the period before the applicant became a registered tax agent. That does not make the non-compliance irrelevant to whether the applicant is a fit and proper person, but it does, combined with the steps taken to ensure future compliance, mitigate the factor to a not insignificant degree.

    (c) Similarly, as already noted, I have accepted the applicant’s evidence that he and his family were confronted by a range of health and other personal issues that, although not excusing the compliance failures, do provide relevant context. Additionally, there is unchallenged evidence, which I accept, of difficulties the applicant experienced in accessing materials and information required for the Family Trust’s returns arising out of the breakdown of the relationship with the Former Employer.[31]

    (d) The applicant has taken concrete steps to bring his affairs into order. His lodgements, and those for his associated entities, are up-to-date. He has a payment plan in place, to which he has been adhering, for remaining personal liabilities which at 3 October 2025 stood at $7,662.32. This liability may be eliminated if the private ruling application is successful, but in any case is now a modest debt that has been reducing by the applicant’s adherence to the payment plan.[32] The Family Trust’s returns are up-to-date[33] and, as noted, the assessments are subject to an objection that, on the face of the matter, is not frivolous or obviously without merit. The SMSF has no outstanding returns or liabilities.[34]

    (e) The degree of non-compliance is not necessarily as stark as the Board’s raw presentation of the proceedings for recovery of the $370,477.02 might suggest, for the reasons set out above.

    (f) The applicant gave unchallenged evidence, which I accept, that in difficult circumstances, he gave priority to his client work over his own compliance responsibilities. Again, that does not excuse the conduct, but it is relevant context which I take into account.

Integrity issues

  1. In submissions, the Board emphasised the lack of integrity in respect of disclosure of the issues relating to termination of the applicant’s employment, submitting that that in itself is sufficient to conclude that the applicant is not a fit and proper person to be a tax agent.
  2. I have found this to be a difficult issue. On the one hand, for the reasons I have indicated, I accept that the applicant failed to act with integrity in not disclosing the FWC’s adverse findings. Additionally, the non-disclosure was ongoing; the applicant had many months to reflect upon the findings and accept that they should be disclosed to the Board, particularly in light of his email of 19 January 2021 asserting that the Former Employer had accepted that his termination was unfair.
  3. Further, the applicant continues to deny there was any lack of integrity. That suggests a lack of insight into the need for frank and open communications between tax agents and their regulatory body which is a cornerstone of the effective operation of any regulatory regime.
  4. On the other hand, it is clear the applicant maintains that he is the innocent party and remains aggrieved by his perception of the actions of the Former Employer. His assessment of the whole matter is inevitably filtered through the lens of the emotion attached to his sense of grievance. It was, after all, the applicant who initiated the proceedings against the Former Employer.
  5. Those feelings of grievance may or may not be well-founded. For the reasons indicated earlier, it is not part of my role to adjudicate upon the matters that were the subject of the FWC proceedings. However, I have no doubt the feelings of grievance are real, and that the applicant’s perception of the matter is impacted by those emotions.
  6. My consideration of the matter comes down to this. Having regard to the human dimensions which I believe have heavily influenced if not clouded the applicant’s response, I do not see the applicant’s failure to disclose the FWC’s findings as quite as reprehensible as the Board suggests.
  7. Nevertheless, there is, for the reasons I have indicated, a lack of integrity in the applicant failing to disclosure the adverse findings. Those findings, whether accepted by the applicant or not, were directly relevant to the applicant’s good fame as a person to be entrusted with the preparation of income tax returns. They are clearly matters that should have been disclosed to the Board, with whatever explanation the applicant might have seen fit to provide. Simply remaining silent, while perhaps an understandable course given the time that had passed, is not indicative of an open and honest approach to dealing with the Board.
  8. Additionally, there is a concerning lack of insight in the applicant still maintaining that he did nothing wrong. There is also a concern regarding his honesty in maintaining, in evidence before the Tribunal, that he was not aware of the FWC findings.
  9. In summary, I take the approach that the integrity issue is not of the same gravity as the Board asserts. However, I take it into account as a significant matter in determining whether the applicant is a fit and proper person to be a registered tax agent.
  10. The position may have been different if the FWC findings were in respect of matters unrelated to a tax agent’s duties. However, the findings regarding the statement that the SMSF accounts had been audited relates directly to the applicant’s fitness to be a tax agent. Whether the applicant simply hoped the findings would not come to attention is not clear. What is clear is that a tax agent acting with integrity would have disclosed these serious findings relating to his duties as a tax agent to the Board. The applicant did not do so.

Fit and proper person - summary of considerations

  1. Considering the above matters holistically, I observe that there has been substantial non-compliance by the applicant with taxation laws. I have also concluded that he failed to act with integrity in not disclosing the adverse FWC findings to the Board. These are serious matters.
  2. However, they must be considered in the context I have outlined above. That includes the emotional response of the applicant to circumstances in which, rightly or wrongly, he considers he has suffered a grievous wrong and also the health and other personal factors to which I have alluded. Again, these do not excuse the conduct but in my view they provide a significant degree of mitigation.
  3. It is also relevant that the applicant accepts that his non-compliance with taxation laws was not satisfactory and has taken steps both to rectify the issues arising out of the past and put in place procedures to prevent recurrences. Further, there is no evidence brought to my attention of unsatisfactory conduct in relation to his clients or of any additional relevant circumstances other than those discussed in these reasons.
  4. Overall, considering matters as they stand today, I am persuaded that the applicant is a fit and proper person to be a tax agent. I do emphasise, though, that this conclusion is based on the evidence as it stands before me today. The applicant would be well-advised to ensure full transparency in his future dealings with the Board and not rely on contestable constructions of documents or events.

ISSUE 4: SANCTION?

  1. I have found that the applicant breached the requirements in relation to his personal tax compliance obligations and failed to act with integrity in relation to disclosure of the FWC’s adverse findings. Nevertheless, I have concluded that the applicant is a fit and proper person to be a tax agent.
  2. Having regard to the circumstances that led me to conclude that the applicant is a fit and proper person, and that it is no part of the Board’s case that he failed to act appropriately in relation to the tax affairs of his arm’s length clients, I am satisfied that suspension or termination of the applicant’s registration is not appropriate.
  3. Rather, the protective objects of the TASA regime will be achieved in the circumstances of this case by a decision along the lines sought by the applicant; namely a written caution and an order requiring the applicant to undergo and furnish evidence of completion of training in relation to requirements of the Code of Professional Conduct. The training addresses my discomfort regarding the applicant’s insight into the need for transparency in his dealings with the Board.

ISSUE 5: SHOULD A NON-PUBLICATION ORDER BE MADE?

  1. Under s 70 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth), the Tribunal may make an order prohibiting disclosure of information tending to reveal the identity of the applicant or a witness in the proceeding or any person related to or otherwise associated with the applicant or a witness in the proceeding. The applicant seeks such an order.
  2. In considering whether to make a s 70 order, s 71(1) provides that the Tribunal must have regard to the following:
    (a) the principle that it is desirable that hearings of proceedings in the Tribunal are held in public;

    (b) the principle that it is desirable that evidence given before the Tribunal is made available to the public;

    (c) the principle that it is desirable that evidence given before the Tribunal and the contents of documents given to the Tribunal are made available to all the parties to the proceeding;

    (d) any reasons in favour of making an order, including the following:

    (i) in any case—the circumstances of the parties to the proceeding and other persons connected to the proceeding;

    (ii) in any case—the harm (if any) that is likely to occur to a person if the order is not made;

    (iii) in relation to an order under section 70—the confidential nature (if any) of the information;

    (e) any other matters that the Tribunal considers relevant.

  3. The applicant lives and practises his profession in a country town. In that context, he argues that without the orders he seeks he will suffer irreparable harm to his reputation and professional standing within the small geographic community in which he practises because:
    (a) his business is driven by client relationships and word of mouth referrals;

    (b) public confidence in him will be eroded; and

    (c) he estimates the majority of his clients would cease to engage his services.

  4. I accept that the applicant may suffer some harm to his reputation and public confidence in him if the circumstances of this case become public. That is a risk every person conducting a regulated business supported by a disciplinary regime runs if they breach regulatory requirements. If public confidence in the applicant is eroded, that will be a consequence of the very conduct the TASA regime was established to regulate. I do not accept that this factor weighs significantly in favour of the confidentiality orders sought.
  5. Such loss of reputation or public confidence may translate into some loss of clients and thus financial detriment to the applicant and his wife. However, the assertion that “the majority” of his clients would cease to engage his services is mere opinion and conjecture. I do not take that conjecture into account. The same may be said of the assertion that such loss of clients would result in cessation of the applicant’s accounting and tax practice with consequent detriment to clients and staff.
  6. In regard to the impact on the applicant’s reputation and any consequent financial impact, there are two balancing factors. First, the serious findings of the FWC are already in the public arena – although I accept that publication of these reasons may direct local media attention to them. Secondly, it is, as these reasons make clear, no part of the Board’s case that the applicant neglected his clients. To the contrary, I accepted the applicant’s evidence that he prioritised attention to his clients’ affairs over his own personal obligations, to his detriment.
  7. There is no particular aspect of the evidence before the Tribunal that the applicant identifies as confidential. Details of the dispute with the Former Employer and the FWC’s findings are set out in greater detail in the published reasons of the FWC than I have included in these reasons.
  8. I have not identified, and the applicant has not drawn attention to, any other circumstances relating to the applicant or other persons associated with the proceedings, or any other factors, relevant to the consideration of this issue.
  9. Having regard to these circumstances and the first two principles stated above regarding the desirability of transparency in Tribunal proceedings, I am not satisfied that a confidentiality order should be made.

DISPOSITION OF THE REVIEW

  1. In relation to the substantive matter, I will make the decision sought by the applicant, setting aside the decision under review and substituting a decision requiring a formal caution and the completion of relevant training.
  2. In respect of confidentiality, in case the applicant wishes to contest my decision, I will, in effect, extend the confidentiality order made on 19 June 2025 for a period of 28 days and order that these reasons are not to be published other than to the parties, their representatives – and the ATO to the extent necessary for the Board to consider whether to appeal the decision - before the expiry of that time.
