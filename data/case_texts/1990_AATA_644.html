VT88/172 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 644; (1990) 21 ATR 3685; 90 ATC 589 (4 September 1990)

Last Updated: 2 June 2009

INCOME TAX - expenses of application to Australian Broadcasting Tribunal - whether expenses "of capital, or of a capital nature



INCOME TAX - legal expenses - whether each invoice to be considered as a separate "amount allowable as a deduction"

REASONS FOR DECISION



4 September 1990 Mrs R.A. Balmford, Senior Member



1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent disallowing an objection by the applicant company to the inclusion in the respondent's assessment of its taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1986 of an amount of $339,934 being the expenses of an application to the Australian Broadcasting Tribunal ("ABT").



2. The applicant requested on 6 June 1988 that the decision on the objection be referred to this Tribunal, and that request was complied with by the respondent. By virtue of sub-section 189(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") that referral is deemed to constitute the making of an application to this Tribunal for review of the decision on the objection.



3. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as modified by section 14ZG of the Administration Act 1953 and numbered folios 1 to 12, together with other materials lodged at the hearing. Three witnesses gave evidence at the hearing, namely the managing director of the applicant, his son, who is the secretary and a director of the

applicant, and a communications and media consultant with extensive experience in the radio and television industries. The applicant was represented by Mr T. Murphy of counsel and the respondent by Mr G. Nettle of counsel.



4. Having considered the evidence, I find the facts set out in and the following six paragraphs. Since 1933, the applicant has operated a commercial radio station in a provincial town. Sub-section 83(1) of the Broadcasting Act 1942 ("the Broadcasting Act") requires an applicant for a commercial radio licence to undertake, if successful, to "provide an adequate and comprehensive service pursuant to the licence" to the service area in respect of which the licence is granted. Adequacy is seen as related to the quality of programmes and comprehensiveness to the range of programmes. Commercial broadcasters are expected to provide programmes and services that satisfy the needs of the communities they are licensed to service.



5. In 1982, following concern as to the limited services available in country areas, the Broadcasting Act was amended to provide for the grant of supplementary FM radio licences to persons who already held commercial AM radio licences. The intent was to introduce choice in country areas more economically than by the grant of new, independent licences. The scheme was outlined in the following terms, so far as is relevant for present purposes, in the Second Reading Speech (Senate Hansard, 9 December 1982, pages 3375-3376).



"The Bill allows the licensee of a commercial AM broadcasting service or a commercial television service to lodge with the Minister an application for a supplementary licence to operate one additional commercial service within his service area. In the case of broadcasting this additional service must be an FM service.



...

Once an application has been received the Minister may either accept the application and forward it to the Australian Broadcasting Tribunal, together with a specification of the area to be served by the licence and other technical specifications, or reject the application on planning or technical grounds, giving his reasons to the applicant.



...



Once the Tribunal receives an application from the Minister it may conduct a public inquiry into the grant of the supplementary licence, and must determine whether an independent commercial licence would be viable in that area, and if so, whether it would be in the public interest, on specified grounds, to invite applications for such a licence.



As a result of its consideration, the Tribunal will either recommend to the Minister that applications for an independent licence be invited, or grant the supplementary licence for up to five years.



After eight years of operation, or earlier if the licencee so elects, special renewal provisions provided for in section 86A will come into operation. At such a renewal the Tribunal must re-examine the question of whether an independent licence would be

viable, and if so, whether it would be in the public interest to invite applications for such a licence.



If the Tribunal determines that the area still cannot support an additional independent licence or it is not in the public interest for such a licence to be granted, the supplementary licence will be renewed for up to three years. The viability of an independent service and the public interest consideration must be re-examined at each subsequent three yearly renewal."



6. In 1984, the then Minister indicated that areas where no application for a supplementary licence had been lodged would be the first to be considered for the grant of independent licences.



7. In 1974, before the amendment to the legislation, a second commercial radio licence was granted in Canberra. Previously 2CA, the only commercial station, had had a "horizontal" range of programmes, intended to provide something for everybody, including material of specifically local interest. The new station, 2CC, perceived that 18-45 year olds were the largest spenders in the community, and directed their programming exclusively at that market: a "vertical" range. 2CA floundered, and changed its format to one similar to that of 2CC, dropping the purely local services. The community suffered by the loss of the range of programmes, and by 1982 the two stations together had a smaller share of local advertising than 2CA alone had had in 1974. This account exemplifies a general experience that where two stations operate in the same area, one with vertical and one with horizontal programmes, the latter is disadvantaged, and the purely local services disappear.



8. When applying for the renewal of its AM licence in 1983, the applicant gave the undertaking described in paragraph 4 above, on the basis that it was providing a service to a horizontal market and would continue to do so.



9. The expectation of the applicant, as expressed at the hearing of this matter, was that if an independent commercial FM station was established in the area, and the applicant attempted to continue to provide its existing services, i.e. to provide an adequate and comprehensive service as required by the Broadcasting Act, the applicant would be forced into bankruptcy. It would be compelled, in order to survive, to abandon talk programmes, and such services as football coverage. It would probably have extensive music programmes on relay, with "windows" for local advertisements, rather than its present coverage of different local interests. Those controlling the applicant did not wish to be forced into operating in this way: they wished to continue to provide a general local service. Even if the applicant were successful in obtaining a supplementary FM licence itself, it was not certain that it would break even. There would certainly be losses in the initial years.



10. The applicant lodged an application for a supplementary licence, pursuant to the 1982 legislation. The ABT conducted a hearing into the matter during the 1986 financial year, and it is the applicant's expenses of that hearing which are the subject of the present application for review. The amount of the expenses is not in dispute, and invoices evidencing it were in the material before this Tribunal. It was made up of legal expenses, consultancy fees and travelling expenses. The application was unsuccessful. An appeal to the Federal Court was successful, and the matter was remitted to the ABT for a rehearing, which had not taken place at the time of the hearing of this matter.



11. The principal submission of the applicant was that all of the expenses were in terms of sub-section 51(1) of the Act, "losses and outgoings ... incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income", and were not "losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature". Accordingly, they were allowable deductions by virtue of that sub-section.



12. The expenditure was incurred, Mr Murphy submitted, for the purpose of enabling the applicant to continue to meet its obligations under the Broadcasting Act. It had undertaken to provide a horizontal service to the region in which it operated. If an FM licence was granted to another operator, it would not be able to continue to provide that horizontal service. Both stations would provide similar vertical programming, in order to survive financially. Thus, it was necessary for the applicant to apply for the supplementary licence in order to continue to carry out the undertaking, prescribed by the legislation, which it had given in 1983. Further, it was the personal wish of those operating the station to continue to provide the local service which it had been providing since 1933.

13. Mr Murphy referred to Sun Newspapers Ltd v FCT [1938] HCA 72; (1939) 61 CLR 337, Broken Hill Theatres Pty Ltd v FCT [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 85 CLR 423, FCT v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 and Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v FCT [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 33 ALR 213. He submitted that the facts in the present case were analogous to those in Snowden & Willson, as to which Dixon J said (at page 437):



"An examination of the facts does not support the view that the proceedings in Parliament and before the Royal Commission imperilled the existence of the business or the capital assets of the company. The proceedings were not necessarily directed at a winding-up of the company or a stoppage of the business. Precise definition or distinctions are difficult in such an affair. But what the company had most to fear was the embarrassments in the present and future conduct of its business and, no doubt, a decline in its custom.



There is no satisfactory ground for saying that the expenditure was an affair of capital."



He also relied on the Magna Alloys case in which Brennan J said (at page 229):



"... it was the business purpose of vindicating the methods by which [the business] was conducted that brings the expenditure within Section 51(1)."



and Deane and Fisher JJ (at page 239):



"The criminal proceedings in respect of which the outgoings were incurred arose out of the day to day business activities of the taxpayer. The outgoings did not involve the acquisition of any enduring or tangible asset. They represented expenditure incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business which should properly be seen as being of a revenue character."



14. Similarly, in the present case, Mr Murphy submitted, the taxpayer did not see the expenditure as directed to the acquisition of a capital asset. The evidence was that if it were successful in obtaining the supplementary licence, the applicant would, in fact, make a loss on the operation of that licence.



15. Mr Nettle, in reply, submitted that the expenditure was incurred principally in order to acquire an FM licence, which would be a new asset of enduring benefit to the applicant, conferring access to an increased listening audience, and consequently to increased advertising revenue. The other purpose of the acquisition was to keep out a prospective competitor so as to preserve an existing monopoly. Thus, to adopt the words of Dixon J in Sun Newspapers, at page 364:



"... in principle the transaction must be regarded as strengthening and preserving the business organisation or entity, the profit-yielding subject, and affecting the capital structure."



The present case was stronger than Sun Newspapers and other similar decisions such as Hallstroms Pty Ltd v FCT [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 because it involved, as well as the preservation of the profit-yielding structure, the acquisition of a capital asset. The expenditure was accordingly an outgoing "of capital, or of a capital ... nature" in terms of sub-section 51(1).



16. I accept the submission of Mr Nettle. The essential nature of the activity in question is not, in my view, affected by the expectation of the consequences of the applicant's success or failure in its application to the ABT. As Jenkinson J said in FCT v Ballarat & Western Victoria TV Ltd (1978) 78 ATC 4630 at page 4637:



"[R]egard may ... be had to practical business considerations in determining whether what has been obtained [or, in the present matter, what is sought to be obtained] is in truth and substance a capital asset."



Mr Murphy's principal submission having failed, I now turn to consider his two subsidiary arguments.



17. The material before the Tribunal showed legal expenses amounting to $228,363. Of this amount $99,759 was accounted for by fees paid to the applicant's solicitors, a large Melbourne-based firm. The first relevant invoice, dated 16 July 1985, showed an amount of $7467 under the heading "Interim Account Re Supplementary Broadcasting Licence Application ..." with the narration "To our professional costs for acting for you in all matters relating to application for a supplementary licence ... up to and including the preliminary hearing at ... on 4 July 1985. The last relevant invoice, dated 25 June 1986, showed an amount of $4292 under the heading "re licence renewal ..." and with the narration "To our professional costs for advising you and acting for you in matters relating to your supplementary licence application and in certain other matters during May and June, with all due skill, care and attention". In between were 11 invoices issued monthly from July 1985 to May 1986 inclusive, each for an amount of $8000 under the heading "Retainer Amount" with the narration "General Retainer for [month]".



18. Mr Murphy submitted that the total of $88,000 charged over the 11 months for a general retainer was expenditure incurred for the provision of services of a general nature, although in the year of income the supplementary licence application was the major component of services provided. It was an ongoing recurrent payment which reserved the services of the firm to the applicant, and was accordingly deductible under sub-section 51(1) in accordance with its ordinary meaning.



19. On the evidence of the managing director of the applicant, I find that legal fees paid by the applicant to the firm in the two previous income years were very significantly less than in 1986; the increase in 1986 was almost wholly referrable to the ABT proceedings; in 1987, fees paid to the firm amounted to $14,000, (which presumably included the costs of the appeal to the Federal Court); the retainer was principally related to the perceived expertise, with regard to the ABT, of one partner in the firm; the general retainer was continued in subsequent years (although the amount charged for it was not in evidence); and for a number of other matters the applicant employed local solicitors in the town where it operated the radio station.



20. No evidence was called from the firm of solicitors (which also represented the applicant in the present proceedings) as to the text of the general retainer; as to the extent of any work done in the period of 11 months which was unrelated to the ABT application; or as to why the firm's only charges for a period of considerable activity (as evidenced by the invoices in respect of counsel's fees and travelling expenses) were expressed to be related only to the general retainer, rather than to specified professional services. I note that the whole amount was claimed in the applicant's tax return for 1986 as being related to the ABT application (as was the whole of the amount of $4292 despite its being described, in the invoice of 25 June 1986, as relating also to "certain other matters").



21. Having considered the evidence, I accept the submission of Mr Nettle, that despite the description of the charges of $88,000 as being paid for a monthly general retainer, the applicant has not discharged the onus imposed on it by section 190(b) of the Act of establishing that, in respect of that amount, the assessment is excessive in that that amount was not directly related to the ABT application.



22. Mr Murphy made a further submission in relation to section 64A of the Act. That provision reads, so far as relevant:



" 64A.(1) In this section, "legal expenses" means expenditure incurred by the taxpayer -



(a) for the services of a barrister or solicitor;



(b) ...; or



(c) in connexion with proceedings before a court, board, commission or similar tribunal,



...



(2) Legal expenses incurred in the year of income in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income shall, except to the extent to which they are expenses of a private or domestic nature, be allowable deductions.



(3) The deduction allowable under this section shall not be greater than the amount (if any) by which $50 exceeds any amount allowable as a deduction under sub-section 51(1) in respect of legal expenses.



23. It is not in issue, and I find, that the whole of the amount the subject of these proceedings falls within the definition of "legal expenses" in sub-section 64A(1), and did not include any expenses of a private or domestic nature. Accordingly, a deduction is allowable under the section.



24. Mr Murphy submitted that the language of the section required that each invoice be considered separately, and that there should be a deduction of $50 in respect of each invoice.



25. However, I accept the submission of Mr Nettle, in reply, that, on its proper construction, the expression in sub-section 64A(3) "any amount allowable as a deduction under sub-section 51(1) in respect of legal expenses" refers to the totality of the deductions allowable to the taxpayer in respect of that heading

in the relevant years. On that basis, as Mr Nettle conceded, an amount of $50 is properly allowable to the applicant as a deduction under section 64A in respect of the year ended 30 June 1986. The objection decision under review will be varied accordingly.
