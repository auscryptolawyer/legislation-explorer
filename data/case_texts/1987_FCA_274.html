Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Margaret Ruth John [1987] FCA 274 (21 August 1987)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: MARGARET RUTH JOHN

No. G510 of 1986

Taxation

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Bowen C.J.(1), Fox(2) and Beaumont(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - whether losses on sale of shares an allowable deduction - Curran scheme - whether transactions creating those losses in the course of a share trading business.

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 (Cth), s.44(2), s.51.

HEARING

SYDNEY

21:8:1987

Counsel and Solicitors for Appellant: D.E. Grieve Q.C. with N.R. Burns instructed by Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel and Solicitors for Respondent: D.G. Hill Q.C. with B.R. Pape instructed by R.I. Rosenblum & Partners.

ORDER

The appeal be allowed.

Set aside the orders made by the Supreme Court; in lieu thereof order that the appeal to the Supreme Court be dismissed.

The respondent pay the costs of the appeal and of the proceedings below.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

This is an appeal by the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") from a decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in its Administrative Law Division. It was held by Yeldham J. that Margaret Ruth John ("the taxpayer") was entitled to a deduction claimed in her return subject to a minor adjustment. The deduction was claimed by the taxpayer as a member of a partnership, The Malindi Trading Co. The partnership was formed on the 14th April, 1977 and the taxpayer was a member, together with nineteen other persons, her interest being a one-twentieth interest. The partnership in its return for the income year ended 30th June, 1977 claimed a loss arising upon the sale of certain shares which it had acquired in six private companies, Compinge (Holdings) Pty. Limited, Compinge (N.S.W.) Pty. Limited, Compinge (Sydney) Pty. Limited, Compinge (North) Pty. Limited, Compinge (Tamworth) Pty. Limited and Compinge Pty. Limited. The shares were purchased by the partnership on 27th April 1977, the partnership then received a tax-free bonus share issue on 28th April 1977 and, on the following day, 29th April 1977, the partnership sold the shares. Treating the bonus shares as having been paid for in full, in accordance with the decision in Curran v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 46; (1974) 131 CLR 409 and treating the distributable funds applied by the Compinge companies in paying for the bonus shares as not being assessable income by reason of s.44(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ("the Act") as it then stood, the partnership claimed that upon the sale it had for tax purposes made a loss. The taxpayer, who had a one-twentieth interest, claimed one-twentieth of this loss, $128,864.00, as a deduction in her return of income.

2. If one has regard to the amount outlaid by the partnership in acquiring the shares in the Compinge companies and the total amount which it received, the partnership made a small commercial profit. However, if the partnership was entitled to treat the bonus shares as having been fully paid up and the amount capitalised in payment of the shares as a dividend which was non-taxable by virtue of s.44(2), then the partnership had for taxation purposes made the loss in question and Mrs John was entitled to claim her deduction.

3. On the reference to the Supreme Court of New South Wales Yeldham J. found that a small portion of the funds which were capitalised and applied in payment of the bonus shares consisted of the proceeds of a life insurance policy and therefore s.44(2) was not attracted in relation to this portion of the capitalised sum. However, he held that the rest of the capitalised sum came from profits from the sale of assets not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit and did attract s.44(2) of the Act. He held, therefore, that the taxpayer succeeded and should have her objection upheld except to the limited extent to which the dividend declared did not satisfy s.44(2). He ordered that the appeal be allowed and that the matter be remitted to the Commissioner for amendment of the assessment so as to allow as a deduction an amount equal to the taxpayer's share of the loss derived by the partnership during the year of income. He further ordered that the Commissioner pay the taxpayer's costs.

4. The Commissioner appealed on six grounds as follows:-

1. That his Honour erred in holding that the partnership

known as the Malindi Trading Co. ("the partnership")

had been engaged during the year of income in the

business of share trading.

2. Alternatively, if his Honour correctly held that the

partnership had been engaged in the business of share

trading, his Honour erred in holding that the

acquisition and disposal by the partnership of shares

in Compinge Pty. Limited, Compinge (Holdings) Pty.

Limited, Compinge (North) Pty. Limited, Compinge

(Tamworth) Pty. Limited, Compinge (N.S.W.) Pty. Limited

and Compinge (Sydney) Pty. Limited formed part of that

business.

3. His Honour was in error in not holding that the

acquisition and disposal by the partnership of shares

in the companies referred to in paragraph 2 above was

part of an "arrangement" to which the provisions of

section 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

applied.

4. Alternatively, his Honour erred in not holding that

the doctrine of fiscal nullity applied in the

circumstances of this case.

5. That his Honour erred in not holding that the

acquisition by the partnership of shares in each of

the companies referred to in paragraph 2 above was

illegal and void on the ground that it was in breach

of section 67 of the Companies Act 1961 (New South

Wales).

6. That his Honour erred in not holding that the

acquisition by the partnership of shares in each of

the companies referred to in paragraph 2 above was

illegal and void on the ground that it was in breach

of sections 32 and 34 of the Securities Industry Act

1975 (New South Wales).

5. On the hearing of the appeal before us the Commissioner abandoned grounds 5 and 6. So far as ground 4 is concerned, his Counsel indicated that this was inserted for the purpose of preserving the position of the Commissioner should the matter proceed further. It was formally submitted that the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Oakey Abattoir Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 55 ALR 291 was wrongly decided and that the relevant transactions of the partnership were fiscally null in the sense recognised in W.T. Ramsay Limited v. Inland Revenue Commissioners [1981] UKHL 1; (1982) AC 300.

6. I turn first to grounds 1 and 2 of the notice of appeal. The questions here are whether the partnership of which the taxpayer was a member carried on a business of share trading during the period 14th April to 30th June 1977 and whether the transactions relating to the Compinge companies formed part of that share trading business. It was common ground that if the partnership, and in turn the taxpayer, were to have the advantage of the decision in Curran's Case, it would have to be established that a share trading business had been carried on.

7. The purchase, the bonus issue and the re-sale of the Compinge shares, was arranged before the partnership was formed. It was not a transaction arising in the course of an existing share trading business carried on by the partnership. After the partnership was formed in April the Compinge transactions were carried out promptly before the end of April. The sale, bonus issue and re-sale were completed in three days. True it is there were dealings in shares by the partnership between 14th April and 30th June, that meetings were held, purchases were discussed and records were kept. But the question remains whether the sale and re-sale of the Compinge shares was truly in the course of a business of share trading. On the evidence in this appeal, this Court is, to my mind, in as good a position as the learned trial Judge to draw inferences and reach a conclusion on this matter. In doing so due weight must be given to the view which his Honour formed and the conclusion to which he came.

8. The learned trial Judge made a careful review of the evidence and heard various witnesses. He made a finding that a share trading business had been carried on in the relevant period and that the transactions in question formed part of that business. Counsel for the Commissioner attacked this finding. He asked the Court to draw the inference that no share trading business had in truth been carried on. However, he did not suggest, either before the trial Judge or before us, that the partnership agreement, or the transactions of the partnership which were in evidence, were a "sham". The trial Judge took the view that the evidence supported the inference that the partnership was formed for the purpose of obtaining the benefit of a Curran deduction and it was agreed that in order to achieve this it was necessary to establish a genuine share trading business.

9. Steps were taken on a fairly elaborate basis to ensure that a share trading business was in fact carried on. From mid April 1977 until 1980 the activities of buying and selling shares, trades and options were substantial. The total capital contributed to the partnership was $100,000, the contribution of the taxpayer being $5,000. Books and accounts were kept in relation to the affairs of the partnership, regular meetings of the partnership committee and sub-committee were held and the enterprise was conducted in a regular, systematic and business-like manner. Reference to the minutes of the meetings of the sub-committee indicates the care and attention given to investment in various purchases from time to time.

10. The share trading activities were managed by the sub-committee of the partnership and a Mr Peter McNeill, an experienced share trading manager, was engaged on a retainer and a share of the profits to assist. The sub-committee met, on an average, twice a week during the year of income. Two firms of sharebrokers acted on behalf of the partnership. It is true that one aim of the partnership was to engage in sufficient activity to be classed as a share trading business and to attract the operation of s.44(2) of the Act and the benefit of the Curran Case but it was also an objective, according to the evidence of the witnesses, that it be conducted with a view to commercial profit. The trial Judge accepted the evidence of the witnesses and it was not directly put to any of these witnesses that the apparent share trading was really designed to produce an "illusion" and that there was in fact no share trading business.

11. In the result, as I have said, his Honour concluded there was a share trading business and that the Compinge transactions formed part of that business.

12. I find myself unable to agree with the conclusion of the learned trial Judge. Before stating my own conclusion in more detail it is convenient to mention four significant cases.

13. In F.A. & A.B. Limited v. Lupton (1972) AC 634 the taxpayer company, which was incorporated in 1952, had for many years carried on a business of dealing in stocks and shares. In the years 1959-60, 1960-61 and 1961-62 it entered into numerous transactions in stocks and shares other than the five particular transactions described as "dividend stripping" transactions which were under consideration in the case. It was not disputed that in those three years the taxpayer company was a dealer in stocks and shares. The Commissioners who first heard the appeal held that the five "dividend stripping" transactions were within the scope of the trade of dealing in shares. On appeal the learned primary Judge (Megarry J.) reversed the decision of the Commissioners. He regarded the transactions as artificial devices remote from the trade and entered into to secure other advantages. The taxpayer company appealed in respect of one only of the five transactions. The Court of Appeal by majority dismissed the appeal. An appeal was then taken to the House of Lords in respect of the one transaction. Lord Morris of Borth-y-Gest said at p643 -

"It does not follow that because a person is

carrying on a trade as a dealer in shares every

transaction into which he enters will be a dealing

in shares in the course of his trade."

14. In the end it was held that the particular transaction under appeal was not part of the trade in shares. Lord Morris of Borth-y-Gest somewhat emphatically stated at p650 -

"It would be a complete delusion to regard the

transaction in this case as a share dealing

transaction coming within the area of trade of a

dealer in shares. It was something very different."

15. In Thomson v. Gurneville Securities Ltd. (1972) AC 661 a similar result was arrived at. Again the taxpayer company was carrying on an existing business as a dealer in shares. In the year of income in question it entered into complex transactions with a view to tax avoidance. A question arose as to whether these transactions were to be regarded as trading transactions in the course of its business of trading in shares. The House of Lords held that they were not.

16. Uninstructed by other authority one might have been pardoned for thinking those cases had a bearing on the situation in the present appeal. However, they have been distinguished and are not to be regarded as applicable under the Australian tax legislation. As Gibbs J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Patcorp Investments Limited [1976] HCA 67; (1976) 140 CLR 247 ("Patcorp"), referring to the reasoning in these two decisions in the House of Lords, said at p 292 -

"This reasoning cannot, I think, be reconciled with

that accepted in Investment and Merchant Finance

Corporation Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation,

and if the two sets of statutory provisions were

the same in relevant respects it might be necessary

to reconsider what was said in the latter case in

the light of these decisions of the House of Lords.

However, the scheme of the English legislation is

very different from that of the Australian Act. In

particular the English legislation does not contain

a provision like s.260 of the Act which is aimed

generally at tax avoidance. The presence of s.260

makes it impossible to place upon other provisions

of the Act a qualification which they do not

express, for the purpose of inhibiting tax

avoidance. In other words, it is not permissible to

make an implication which does what s.260 fails to

do in preventing the avoidance of tax. If it is

suggested that a taxpayer has engaged in a device

to secure a fiscal advantage, and the relevant

provisions of the Act do not expressly deal with

the matter, the case depends entirely on s.260.

These considerations are sufficient to distinguish

the two decisions of their Lordships. Moreover, the

Parliament, by enacting s.46A of the Act (which was

not in force at the time material to this case),

has legislated in an attempt to overcome what it

regarded as the undesirable effects of the decision

in Investment and Merchant Finance Corporation Ltd.

v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation, and that is a

further reason why we should not reconsider the

authority of that case."

17. Investment and Merchant Finance Corporation Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 35; (1971) 125 CLR 249, mentioned by Gibbs J., was a case in some respects similar to the case before us on this appeal. A taxpayer company which carried on the business of dealing in shares in the income year in question purchased a controlling interest in a private company which had a large amount of profits available for distribution. In the year of income ended 30th June, 1964 a dividend was declared by the private company out of accumulated profits and paid to the taxpayer company. In the accounts of the taxpayer company this dividend was non-taxable by virtue of the provisions of s.46 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936. The taxpayer company, after receiving the dividend, sold the shares in the private company at a price commensurate with their lessened value and claimed in its income tax return a deduction in respect of the loss on the resale of the shares.

18. Windeyer J. at first instance upheld the claim of the Commissioner of Taxation disallowing this loss. On appeal the High Court (Barwick C.J., Menzies and Walsh JJ., McTiernan J. dissenting) reversed this decision and held the taxpayer company was entitled to the deduction of the loss.

19. In Patcorp (supra) several taxpayer companies were involved. Each company carried on the business of dealing in shares. In the course of that business each company bought shares in Austin Sales (Australia) Pty. Limited ("Austin Sales") and subsequently sold them at a loss having in the meantime performed a "dividend stripping" operation. The Commissioner of Taxation argued that notwithstanding the existing business of share dealing carried on by the companies, these particular transactions were not in the course of the ordinary share trading activities and so the losses were not deductible. The High Court rejected this argument and held that the taxpayer companies were entitled to deduct the losses.

20. It will be noted that in all of these cases the taxpayer company was engaged in the business of dealing in shares on an established basis. In the two last mentioned cases the High Court did not regard the circumstances as taking the particular transactions in question outside the established share trading business.

21. In the present case the position is different. Here there was no established business of trading in shares. Here 20 persons, in the main strangers to each other, were brought into a partnership in April 1977 for the purpose of taking advantage of the decision in Curran's Case. There was a pre-arrangement to purchase shares in the Compinge companies, to declare a bonus dividend and then to resell the depleted shares and claim the loss. The partnership was formed to carry out this pre-arrangement and the parties were advised that in order to obtain the advantage of a deduction similar to that in Curran's Case it would be necessary that they carry on a business of trading in shares. Promptly after the formation of the partnership on the 14th April the purchase of the shares in the Compinge companies on the 27th April, the declaration of the bonus issue on the 28th April and the resale of shares in those companies on the 29th April pursuant to the pre-arrangements were carried out. It is true that the advice to carry on a business of trading in shares was heeded and that in pursuance of this advice a flurry of activity took place between April and 30th June, 1977 by way of buying and selling shares, albeit this activity resulted in a loss for that year.

22. In my opinion, it is necessary to look at the whole of the circumstances of the case when determining whether the Compinge transactions were carried out in the course of a business of trading in shares. For myself, looking at the whole of the circumstances, I am unable to conclude that the Compinge transactions were part of a business of trading in shares. Indeed, to so hold would, to my mind, be contrary to ordinary concepts of business. These transactions were not something occurring in a long-standing business of share dealing. The pre-arranged transaction concerning the Compinge companies was promptly carried out in April 1977 after the entry into the partnership on the 14th April, 1977. It constituted fons et origo of all that occurred. In my opinion, the partnership transactions relating to the Compinge companies were not transactions in the course of a business of dealing in shares.

23. In view of this conclusion it is not necessary to discuss the application of s.260.

24. In the result, I would allow the appeal with costs.

This is an appeal from a decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales, constituted by Yeldham J., in which his Honour allowed in part an appeal by the taxpayer, the present respondent, against an assessment of the Commissioner of Taxation for the year ending 30 June 1977.

2. Because certain matters argued before the learned judge have not been pressed before us, it is not necessary for us to deal with the facts in as much detail. In summary the issues relate to an amount claimed as a loss or outgoing under s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which arises, or is said to arise, from the application of Curran's case (Curran v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 46; (1974) 131 CLR 409) to dividends declared in compliance with s.44(2) of the Act by five companies and satisfied by the issue of "bonus" shares to a partnership, of which the taxpayer was a member.

3. The submissions on behalf of the Commissioner are essentially two: that the partnership was not at the relevant time trading in shares, and did not satisfy the terms of s.51 so as to permit the deduction of the amount applied in or towards payment for the bonus shares, and that, on alternative bases with which it is not necessary to deal, the relevant arrangement was struck down by s.260 of the Act.

4. It is necessary to say something more of the facts. The terms of the partnership in question, known as the "Malindi Trading Co.", were established by deed dated 14 April 1977. The taxpayer had a one-twentieth share therein. Its expressed purpose was to "trade in shares, rights to shares and options". Its original capital was $100,000 subscribed as to $5,000 by each of the 20 partners. Some time prior to that date one or more of its promoters had discussed the possibility of acquiring shares in companies which would be suitable for obtaining the benefit of the decision in Curran's case, and a particular group of proprietary companies was named. A solicitor, Mr Rosenblum, who became a participant, was active in forming the partnership and in its affairs generally and might be regarded as its principal guide and adviser. The partnership formed a management committee, and a sub-committee was created of which Mr Rosenblum was a member. A person said to be expert in dealings on the stock market, and who was later given an investment advisers licence (a Mr. McNeill) was appointed, at a small annual fee, and he became a member of the sub-committee. Shareholders were subsequently appointed. Over the ensuing months there were many share dealings often in well known stock, and the partnership continued until at least June 1980, which is as far as the evidence takes us. There were 18 meetings of the sub-committee before 30 June 1977. There were a number of distinct dealings up to that date, but the net result was a loss, in commercial terms.

5. As a result of steps carried to an advanced stage before 14 April, the partnership on 27 April 1977 acquired half the shareholding in each of six selected companies. The other half was acquired by a similarly constituted partnership, which had the same objects. The financial arrangements to fund the purchase were complex but it is not necessary to deal with them in any detail. They are to be found in the affidavit of Mr. Rosenblum, set out in his Honour's judgment. The cost to the partnership was $2,894,150.96.

6. On 28 April 1977 each of the companies declared dividends out of profits arising on the revaluation or sale of assets not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit (see s.44(2)) and satisfied them with issues of shares. In one case the dividend was not declared wholly and exclusively out of profits arising from the revaluation of assets not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit, so that there were no losses attributable to the issue of the shares and that case does not arise for consideration. Thus it is that I referred at the outset to five companies. On 29 April the shares in the six companies including the "bonus" shares just issued, were sold to a third party. For income tax purposes, the vendor shareholders claimed heavy losses arising wholly or largely from the debts attributable to the issue of the bonus shares.

7. The deductibility to the shareholders of the amounts applied in payment of the shares issued to them depends upon their being traders in shares, so that losses or outgoings can be deducted under s.51, or, on an alternative view, upon the shares held being regarded as trading stock (see definition in s.6) to which the trading stock provisions of the Act can be applied.

8. A schedule of share trading activities of the partnership was tendered, and it shows many share dealings, even before 30 June 1977. The challenged transactions were however among the first.

9. The question raised seems to me to be one of fact: was the partnership of which Mrs John was a member engaged in the business of share dealing at the relevant time, or, was the particular transaction part of a business of dealing in shares. The question can be stated in a number of slightly different ways, but in the end must satisfy the tests in s.51. It is submitted on behalf of the Commissioner that the loss was incurred in a distinct and separate loss-producing operation, which went to reduce taxable income received from a different source, or sources. In Curran, these questions did not arise for decision.

10. It is difficult to see any answer to the Commissioner's submission. The particular arrangement had already been worked out in some detail, and had progressed virtually to the point needing the partnership to carry it on, at the time this was formed. It was put into operation almost immediately, and was not simply part of a business of buying and selling shares. The particular shares were of course bought and sold, but solely for the purpose just discussed. A painless taxation loss, not profit, was the motive. (See Ure v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 50 FLR 219; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ilbery [1981] FCA 188; (1981) 58 FLR 191). The transaction was not repeated, and so far as appears it was a unique activity distinctly outside the mainstream of the alleged business. The companies concerned were private companies, whose collaboration had already been obtained. One does not have to decide that s.260 applied in order to take account of the fact that the purpose of the transaction was to obtain an income tax advantage, and apart from any tax advantage, there was a loss.

11. I have taken note of contrary submissions put on behalf of Mrs John. One is that trading was "the main goal". This may or may not be a fair statement of the overall situation, but it leaves out of account the relationship required between every item of loss or outgoing and the production of income. In some, perhaps many cases a minor item properly takes its character from a larger aspect of which it forms a part, but the position is otherwise in this case, where there is a separate and distinct dealing of major importance. It seems to me that it is probably in this area where I have the misfortune to differ from the learned judge. He was very much influenced by the stated object of the partnership and the many conventional share dealings which took place. The Curran activity was rather subsumed in the whole. I give effect to a difference of opinion on the facts with some reluctance, but his Honour did not have any advantage over this Court in that regard.

12. I am therefore of the view that the appeal should be allowed. There is no necessity in the circumstances to deal with the arguments concerning the application of s.260.

In her return of income for the year ended 30 June 1977, Mrs. John disclosed a substantial income as beneficiary of a trust. She sought to deduct from that income the sum of $128,864.00 as her share of a loss claimed to have been suffered by a partnership, of which she was a member. That partnership (The Malindi Trading Co.) engaged in a number of dealings in listed shares and those dealings resulted in a small loss which is of no fiscal consequence. But the partnership also undertook a number of transactions involving the issue to the partners of a large number of bonus shares by a group of private companies which is said, on behalf of Mrs. John, to have been of considerable fiscal consequence. It is claimed by the partners that, by reason of the notional "cost" of the bonus shares, the partnership suffered a substantial "loss" for taxation purposes. The partners, including Mrs. John, claimed to deduct a share of the partnership loss of $2,577,286.00 (i.e. $128,864.00). Although Mrs. John contends that the decision and reasoning in Curran v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 46; (1974) 131 CLR 409 permits the deduction, the Commissioner of Taxation refused to allow it. Mrs. John appealed to the Supreme Court of New South Wales. Her appeal having been allowed by Yeldham J., the Commissioner now appeals to this Court.

2. Mrs. John's claim to the deduction raises a number of difficult, and important, questions. There is no dispute about the primary facts, but the parties are at issue with respect to the proper inferences to be drawn from the primary evidence.

3. The origins of this litigation were described by Mr. Gleeson Q.C., then appearing for Mrs. John, in his opening remarks to Yeldham J.:

"MR. GLEESON: Your Honour has already had occasion to

look at arrangements entered into by taxpayers to

take advantage of the decision of the High Court in

the case of Curran v. The Commissioner of Taxation

[1974] HCA 46; 131 CLR 409. That is a case in which the taxpayer

exposed an anomaly, or what was regarded by the

legislature as an anomaly, in the Income Tax

Assessment Act. As a result of the anomaly exposed

by the conduct of the taxpayer in Curran's case and

the decision of the High Court (which was given in

November 1974) the legislature moved to remedy the

anomaly by legislation introduced in 1978. As the

facts of the present case will indicate, over that

period of four years or so between the decision in

Curran's case and the amending legislation in 1978

there were a number of taxpayers and their advisors

who had the ability both to read and to count. Their

ability to read led them to read the decision in

Curran's case and their ability to count led them to

put two and two together and come up with four."

(Emphasis added)

4. At one stage in the course of argument before us Mr. Hill Q.C., now appearing for Mrs. John, suggested, albeit faintly, that the transactions now in question were nothing more than a series of ordinary commercial dealings without any fiscal objective. The suggestion must be rejected. As Mr. Gleeson's opening made it clear, Mrs. John's advisors could both read and count. Their plan was for Mrs. John to take advantage of the Curran scheme. The evidence plainly established that the scheme was the raison d'etre of the partnership.

5. One of the principal questions for determination is whether Mrs. John, or the partnership of which she became a member, were, in relevant respects, engaged in the business of share trading. Mr. Curran was a city stockbroker and the Commissioner there accepted that the transactions in question were part of his business dealings. Mrs John, on the other hand, was not a stockbroker. Her husband was a businessman in Tamworth but she had never had any previous dealings in stocks or shares.

6. Although the decision in Curran was subsequently followed in London Australia Investment Company Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 50; (1977) 138 CLR 106 (per Gibbs J. at p 119; and per Jacobs J. at p 132), the reasoning of the majority in Curran was not unanimous. The headnote reads:

"A taxpayer, who carried on a business of trading in

shares, bought 200 shares in a private company for

$186,046.48. The company thereafter allotted to him

191,000 shares which were paid up by the company

through the declaration and application in his favour

of a dividend under such circumstances that that

dividend was exempt under s.44(2)(b)(iii) of the

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1969 (Cth). Later on

the same day he sold the 200 shares originally held

for $197.52 and the other 191,000 shares for

$188,631.60. He claimed that in computing his

taxable income a deduction should be made of an

amount of $191,000 being the amount applied in his

favour in paying up the 191,000 shares, so that the

entire transaction gave rise to a loss of

$188,217.36. The Commissioner of Taxation assessed

him on the basis that the cost to him of the 191,000

shares was nil.

Held, by Barwick C.J., Menzies and Gibbs JJ., Stephen

J. dissenting, that the taxpayer was entitled to a

deduction of the amount of $191,000. By Barwick C.J.

and Menzies J., on the ground that although the

declaration and application in the taxpayer's favour

of a dividend of $191,000 was exempt under

s.44(2)(b)(iii), he was entitled to treat himself as

having paid for the shares the amount so applied; by

Gibbs J., on the ground that the 191,000 shares

became trading stock of the taxpayer and that the

only practicable way of reaching a true result was to

bring them into account at an appropriate value, as

though they had been bought, that is, at their par

value of $191,000."

The argument advanced on behalf of the Commissioner was as follows (at p410):

"The taxpayer had no opportunity to obtain money from

the company; his entitlement was only to the shares.

He did not make any payment for them. Although the

shares were trading stock, their market value could

not be brought in under s.31 since they had been sold

by the end of the year. In looking at the matter

under s.51 it is necessary to take account of the

whole transaction, and the fact is that the taxpayer

did not incur the large loss claimed by him and had

no right to be paid the dividend declared by the

company...Indeed, if a trader in shares were

bequeathed shares by will and decided to bring them

into his trading stock, the full amount received on

their sale would be assessable and he would not be

entitled to a deduction representing their market

value." (Emphasis added)

(It will be noted that the Commissioner conceded the

critical conclusion that the bonus shares in the private company were part of Mr. Curran's trading stock. In the present case the Commissioner disputes that the bonus shares issued by the group of private companies, the notional "cost" of which it is sought to deduct, were trading stock of the partnership or of Mrs. John.)

7. Barwick C.J. said (at p415):

"Thus, where a company having distributable profits

impliedly effects their distribution by the issue

against them of bonus shares fully or partly paid up,

the recipient of the shares, having regard to the

definition of 'dividend' in s.6 of the Act, must

treat himself as having received income to the amount

of the profits of the company applied to pay for the

bonus shares and, in my opinion, will be entitled to

regard those shares as having cost him that amount of

money, even though the resolutions of the company do

not provide for payment to him of that sum of money.

Whether or not the recipient of the bonus shares must

pay income tax in respect of the amount credited to

him by the company in connexion with the issue of the

bonus shares depends on the provisions of the Act.

But, in my opinion, whether or not he pays income tax

on the amount so credited can have no relevance to

the question whether he is entitled to treat himself

as having paid the amount credited to him by the

company as the cost of the bonus shares."

In the present case, s.44(2)(b)(iii) of the Act in

the circumstances of the issue of the bonus shares by

Stewart Bacon (the private company) exempts from

income tax the amount credited to the appellant in

respect of the issue of the shares. But that does

not mean, in my opinion, that the appellant is not to

be regarded as having paid for those shares the

amount of their paid-up value."

Menzies J. agreed, saying (at p417):

"In my opinion...it ought to be concluded that they

(the resolutions of the company) did result in the

declaration of a dividend and its application to pay

up in full the shares to be issued as bonus shares.

By reason of this, and the appellant's acceptance of

the bonus shares, a payment was made for those shares

out of a credit created in favour of the appellant by

the declaration of the dividend. It matters not that

the dividend had to be so applied and was not payable

in cash. The significance of this is simply that the

dividend was one within s.44(2)(b)(iii) of the Act."

8. Gibbs J. (as he then was) approached the matter differently. Barwick C.J. treated the issue of the bonus shares as involving a "cost" to Mr. Curran. Menzies J. held that Mr. Curran had, in effect, made a payment for the shares. Neither the Chief Justice nor Menzies J. referred to s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), but it would seem that they regarded the notional "cost" of the bonus shares as deductible under that provision (see Parsons, Income Taxation in Australia: Principles of Income, Deductibility and Tax Accounting at pp435-6). Certainly Mr. Curran relied on s.51(1) (see at p410; p411). In Gibbs J.'s view, the question was one of ascertaining the proper method of bringing to account the "notional" cost of the bonus shares as part of a stockbroker's trading stock. His Honour thought that the problem was essentially one of the appropriate accounting treatment of the item in the context of the trading stock provisions of the Act (ss.28, 31 and 51(2)) (at pp417-8). He said (at pp419-20):

"The Act does not deal fully with the manner in which

a profit and loss account is to be kept by a trader,

and in particular does not expressly provide as to

the manner in which trading stock acquired during the

year of income is to be brought into the account.

Where the Act is silent recourse must be had to

'common understanding and commercial principles' and

that method must be adopted which will be 'calculated

to give a substantially correct reflex of the

taxpayer's true income'....Speaking generally, common

understanding and ordinary commercial principles will

require purchases to be brought into the account at

their actual cost."

9. His Honour expressed his reasons for finding in favour of the taxpayer as follows (at p421):

"In a sense...it may be said that the shares cost the

appellant $191,000 and that it was appropriate to

treat their acquisition as a purchase for that

amount. However, I do not need to base my decision

on that ground. In my opinion it was not possible to

arrive at the appellant's true income without taking

the bonus shares into account as trading stock

acquired, whether or not those shares could properly

be regarded as having been purchased. The

appellant's trading account would not reveal the real

situation if it brought in at no value shares which

were in fact valuable, because the amount which it

would then show as income would include the value

which the shares possessed when they were first

brought into stock. The case may be compared with

that of a trader who takes into his trading stock

articles which he received by way of gift or under a

bequest. Cases of that kind not falling within s.36

of the Act may be rare, but they can be envisaged.

In such a case an account will not reveal the true

result of the trading unless those articles are

brought in at an appropriate value, e.g., market

selling value....The only practicable way of reaching

a true result in a case of that kind would be to

bring the articles into the account at an appropriate

value as though they had been purchased, and there is

no provision in the Act that would require any

different approach..."

Gibbs J. noted (at p418) that "although it may be surmised that the issue was made with an eye to its consequences under the taxation law", it was not suggested that s.260 of the Act applied.

26. In my opinion, nothing turns on the circumstance that the transactions in question were entered into by Mrs. John as a member of a partnership rather than by Mrs. John as an individual. The Act recognizes the "schizoid nature of the partnership as both a distinct firm or profit centre in accounting and economic theory and as a non-entity in general legal theory" (see Fletcher, The Law of Partnership in Australia and New Zealand, 5th ed. at p307). The statute requires that the accounts of the business be prepared on the basis that the partnership is a distinct commercial operation, but no tax is levied on the earnings of the partnership (s.91). Its income is credited to partners in the appropriate shares (and taxed in their hands (s.92(1)). In the same way, the Act, as it then stood, allowed a partner a deduction in respect of "his individual interest in a partnership loss" (s.92(1)). It follows that "carrying on business in partnership or being a partner confers no special rights or entitlements for taxation purposes" (see Fletcher op cit.)

28. The learned Judge's finding that the actions of the partnership constituted "regular, extensive and systematic" share trading is challenged by the Commissioner. It is said that because the real objective of the partners was to gain a tax advantage, the dealings in the listed shares were a "facade", i.e. something done with a view to creating an illusion of share trading activity. The Commissioner relies on the circumstances that the partnership made a loss on its transactions in the listed stocks and that the management committee spent more than one-half of the partners' capital ($54,000.00 out of $100,000.00) in the five business days following 19 April 1977. It is said that the partnership's manner of operation was haphazard and that this was reflected in Mrs. John's lack of interest in the results of the stock exchange transactions and in the modest fee paid to Mr. McNeill.

29. On behalf of Mrs John, reliance is placed upon the facts that the management committee received and considered written reports from Mr. McNeill offering advice as to the acquisition of a portfolio of listed shares.

30. The authorities establish that, generally speaking, if an activity is carried on in a regular, extensive and systematic manner, it is appropriate to infer that a business is being carried on (see the cases cited in Grant v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 85 ATC 4806 per Yeldham J. at pp 4811-13). Whether the stock exchange transactions, entered into by the partnership can be described as "regular, extensive and systematic" is, of course, a question of degree.

31. In my view, there is considerable force in many of the Commissioner's complaints. The purchases and sales of the listed shares were necessarily on the periphery. What mattered was whether a Curran type of deduction was earned as a result of the exercise. The forward planning made this clear. It reflected the reality of the situation, which was that each partner was to advance the relatively small amount of $5,000.00 in return for the considerable tax deduction anticipated. If the deduction were earned, the results of the transactions in the listed stocks would be of no more than marginal significance - something hoped for but, relatively speaking, unimportant.

35. It is submitted on behalf of Mrs. John that the bonus shares literally, at least, satisfied the statutory definition of "trading stock". By s.6(1) of the Act, such stock is defined to include "anything produced, manufactured, acquired or purchased for purposes of manufacture, sale or exchange". But, as Professor Parsons points out, literal satisfaction of the definition is not enough (op.cit. at pp793-4):

"Clearly, it is important to know when assets are

trading stock of a business. The question is a

composite one. It is of no necessary consequence

that an item is trading stock in the sense that it is

within the definition of trading stock or within the

meaning of the word without the aid of the

definition. The trading stock provisions (Subdiv. B

of Div. 2 of Pt III) operate only when items that are

trading stock, within the definition of that meaning,

are assets of a business or were assets of a

business, presumably of a continuing business. And

they operate only when they are or were revenue

assets of such a business. It would be to allow the

definition of 'trading stock' in s.6 to deprive the

word 'trading' of any significance if it were held

that Subdiv. B applied in relation to

'anything...acquired...for purposes of manufacture',

even though the item will serve its purpose in

manufacture by being plant of a manufacturing

business."

36. On behalf of Mrs. John, it is argued that the circumstances that the original Compinge shares were private company shares, that a bonus issue was made on them so as to obtain a tax deduction and that they were purchased with a view to immediate resale could not preclude the treatment of the Compinge shares as trading stock for tax purposes. Further, the suggestion that the Compinge transactions stood apart from the partnership's dealings in listed securities is resisted. Reliance is placed upon the reasoning in Rowdell Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 61; (1963) 111 CLR 106, Investment & Merchant Finance Corporation Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 35; (1971) 125 CLR 249, Patcorp Investments Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 67; (1976) 140 CLR 247 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Westraders Proprietary Limited [1980] HCA 24; (1979) 144 CLR 55.

37. Rowdell's Case concerned the application of s.260 of the Act in a dividend-stripping operation. It was common ground that the taxpayer carried on the business of dealing in stocks and shares. Dixon C.J. observed that, s.260 apart, when the taxpayer bought the shares in a company from its shareholders, what it paid to shareholders, although calculated at, e.g. assets value less ten per cent, came to the shareholders as capital; but the taxpayer paid for the shares a sum of money which would form an outgoing under s.51 in its business of trafficking in stocks and shares (at p118).

38. In Investment & Merchant Finance, supra, it was held that shares acquired by a dealer in shares were trading stock for the purposes of ss.6(1), 28, 29, 30 and 31 of the Act notwithstanding that they had been acquired for the purposes of dividend-stripping. Barwick C.J. thought that the purchase of the shares was part of the taxpayer's business as a dealer in shares, that the cost of the shares was deductible under s.51, and that the taxpayer's perception of a fiscal advantage was irrelevant (at pp254-5). Relying on statements in the House of Lords in Griffiths (Inspector of Taxes) v. J.P. Harrison (Watford) Ltd. (1963) AC 1 and Bishop (Inspector of Taxes) v. Finsbury Securities Ltd. (1966) 1 WLR 1402, Menzies J. was of the opinion that the acquisition of the shares was a transaction of a trading character. Walsh J. said (at pp270-1):

"But when shares are bought by a dealer in shares and

it is intended that they are to be resold and that

this will probably occur in the not distant future, I

do not think they are to be denied the description of

trading stock, either because the trader expects or

intends that they will be sold at less than their

cost price or because he seeks to obtain a commercial

advantage from the transaction otherwise than from a

profit on the resale, that is, an advantage from an

expected dividend and from an expected taxation

benefit."

39. Patcorp, supra, was another case of acquisition of shares by a dividend-stripper. It was common ground that the stripping company carried on share trading. Mason J. (as he then was), sitting at first instance, noted that the share trader, in the course of its trading business, had regard to the fiscal advantages which were available under the Act (see at pp252-3). His Honour said (at pp273-4):

"When the Full Court decided the I.M.F. Case (1971)

[1971] HCA 35; 125 CLR 249 it had before it two decisions of the

House of Lords, Griffiths (Inspector of Taxes) v.

J.P. Harrison (Watford) Ltd. (1963) AC 1; and

Bishop (Inspector of Taxes) v. Finsbury Securities

Ltd. (1966) 1 WLR 1402 which supported the

conclusion reached by the majority of this Court.

Since then the House of Lords in F.A. & A.B. Ltd. v.

Lupton (Inspector of Taxes) (1972) AC 634 and

Thomson (Inspector of Taxes) v. Gurneville Securities

Ltd. (1972) AC 661, has held that where a taxpayer

enters into a transaction for the purpose of a

dividend-stripping operation with the manifest object

of securing a tax advantage the transaction does not

constitute a dealing in stocks and shares and

therefore forms no part of the trading activities of

a dealer in stocks and shares. The Commissioner

relied on this decision but in my view, having regard

to this Court's decision in the I.M.F. Case, I should

not follow it."

An appeal to the Full Court was dismissed by Gibbs, Stephen and Jacobs JJ., McTiernan J. dissenting. McTiernan J. preferred the views expressed in Gurneville and F.A. and A.B.. Gibbs J. said of these cases (at p292):

"This reasoning cannot, I think, be reconciled with

that accepted in Investment & Merchant Finance

Corporation Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation

[1971] HCA 35; (1971) 125 CLR 249, and if the two sets of

statutory provisions were the same in relevant

respects it might be necessary to reconsider what was

said in the latter case in the light of these

decisions of the House of Lords. However, the scheme

of the English legislation is very different from

that of the Australian Act. In particular the

English legislation does not contain a provision like

s.260 of the Act which is aimed generally at tax

avoidance. The presence of s.260 makes it impossible

to place upon other provisions of the Act a

qualification which they do not express, for the

purpose of inhibiting tax avoidance. In other words,

it is not permissible to make an implication which

does what s.260 fails to do in preventing the

avoidance of tax. If it is suggested that a taxpayer

has engaged in a device to secure a fiscal advantage,

and the relevant provisions of the Act do not

expressly deal with the matter, the case depends

entirely on s.260. These considerations are

sufficient to distinguish the two decisions of their

Lordships. Moreover, the Parliament, by enacting

s.46A of the Act (which was not in force at the time

material to this case), has legislated in an attempt

to overcome what it regarded as the undesirable

effects of the decision in Investment & Merchant

Finance Corporation Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of

Taxation, and that is a further reason why we should

not reconsider the authority of that case."

40. His Honour then dealt with the question whether the shares in question were "trading stock" (at p292):

"The expression 'trading stock' is defined in s.6(1)

of the Act to include anything purchased for the

purposes of sale. The shares in the stripped

companies answered that description. The purchases

were made, not as 'individual forays in particular

stocks' (as in Williams v. Federal Commissioner of

Taxation [1972] HCA 48; (1972) 128 CLR 645, at p 656), but by

companies which carried on the business of share

trading. The only reason advanced for isolating the

transactions in question from the general run of the

business of the appellant companies was that they

were designed for the purpose of tax avoidance, but

for the reasons I have given I consider that that

reason cannot be accepted, apart from s.260. I

therefore hold that the appellant companies were

entitled to a deduction under s.51 in respect of the

expenditure incurred in the purchase of the shares in

the stripped companies, unless s.260 avoided any part

of the transactions."

41. Stephen and Jacobs JJ. were also of the view that the I.M.F. Case was decisive of the question.

42. The I.M.F. Case and the Patcorp Case were referred to by Mason J. (Aickin J. concurring) in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Westraders Proprietary Limited [1980] HCA 24; (1980) 144 CLR 55 at pp 70-1. Murphy J. dissented but accepted that the I.M.F. Case and the Patcorp Case had decided that shares subjected to a dividend-stripping operation may be trading stock (at p78). Westraders was also a case of an admitted share trader.

43. In my opinion, the I.M.F. Case, Curran's Case, the Patcorp Case and Westraders may be distinguished for our purposes. As has already been stated, they were all cases of acknowledged and established share traders. Mrs. John's position was different. It appears that when Mr. Mitchell's proposal was put to her, she was not engaged in any business. She had not previously traded in shares and the dominant reason for her entry into the scheme was the significant tax deduction it appeared to offer. Her partners were not stockbrokers. Nor were they persons otherwise engaged in the business of trading in shares. Mrs. John's case is not unlike that of the taxpayer, a solicitor, in Loxton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1973) 47 ALJR 95, a decision of Gibbs J. The headnote reads:

"During the year of income ended 30th June, 1968, a

taxpayer applied for and was allotted shares in two

mining companies for which he paid application moneys

and, in respect of the shares in one company, an

immediate call. He also acquired, as appurtenant to

the shares he took up, options to take up further

shares, and he acquired more options by purchase.

Before the end of the year of income he (a) sold the

shares first allotted to him, (b) sold some of the

options, and (c) exercised the rest of the options

and sold the shares allotted to him. These

transactions resulted in a net profit to the

taxpayer. The purpose of the acquisition of the

shares and the options was to obtain taxation

deductions under ss.77AA and 78 (1)(b) of the Income

Tax Assessment Act 1936-1968. He hoped for a

commercial profit from the transactions, but that was

not his object. To achieve his purpose it was not

necessary to sell the shares and options at a profit;

it was enough that they should be resold without too

great a loss. (Emphasis added)

Held: (1) This case was an exception from the

generalization that assets which are not acquired for

personal use should be regarded as having been

acquired with a view to deriving income from them or

with a view to selling them sooner or later at a

profit: though it might be true to say that, because

it was an essential part of the purpose with which

the shares were obtained that they should be resold,

the property was acquired for the purpose of sale, it

would be contrary to the evidence to find that they

were acquired for the purpose of profit-making by

sale. The first limb of s.26(a) of the Income Tax

Assessment Act was not applicable.

(2) If it were assumed that the taxpayer carried

out a scheme in the course of which he bought and

sold mining shares and options, the scheme was not

carried out for the purpose of making a profit.

Accordingly the second limb of s.26(a) was not

applicable.

(3) The question whether the taxpayer engaged in

an adventure in the nature of trade was one of fact

and must be answered in the negative. The profit

could not be described as income within the ordinary

understanding of that term and was not taxable under

s.25(1) of the Act."

44. Gibbs J. said (at p98):

"The final question is whether the profit made by the

appellant can be regarded as income within ordinary

usages and concepts. The profit represented a

capital gain resulting from the realization by the

appellant of assets which had appreciated in value.

It is impossible to say that the profit was made in

the course of carrying on a business, and the profit

could only be regarded as income if it could rightly

be said that in entering into the transactions the

appellant engaged in an adventure in the nature of

trade. Shares may of course be the subject of trade,

and an isolated transaction may in some circumstances

amount to an adventure in the nature of trade, but

the finding of fact that I have made that the

appellant did not buy the shares and options in

question for the purpose of making a profit on their

resale appears to me in the circumstances of this

case to be fatal to the contention that the appellant

carried on an adventure in the nature of trade. The

fact that in the first transaction the shares were to

be sold only at a time selected by the brokers, whose

object was to maintain their price, only means that

the proposed realizations were intended to be as

advantageous as possible. The question whether the

appellant engaged in an adventure in the nature of

trade is one of fact and I answer it in the negative.

In my opinion the profit gained by the appellant

cannot be described as income within the ordinary

understanding of that term and is not taxable within

s.25(1) of the Act."

See also William Deane Q.C., The Profit of Having to Pay Less Tax Vol. 1 Australian Tax Review (1971) at p75; Parsons, op. cit. at p147.

45. Gibbs J. was, of course, speaking of income and not of a deduction. But his Honour's reasoning is relevant here because of his conclusion that the taxpayer's purpose of obtaining a tax deduction took the transactions out of the class of adventures in the nature of trade (see also, in respect of claims for deductions, Ure v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4,100 per Deane and Sheppard JJ. at pp 4,108-4,111; Magna Alloys & Research Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; 80 ATC 4,542 per Brennan J. at p 4,549; per Deane and Fisher JJ. at p 4,559; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ilbery 81 ATC 4,661 per Toohey J. at p 4,668; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gwynvill Properties Pty. Limited 86 ATC 4,512). This is not to say that the partnership agreement or the Compinge transactions were shams. The Curran scheme which was adopted was real and took effect but, in my opinion, Mrs. John has not established that the Compinge transactions were embarked upon as an adventure in the nature of trade. It would be more accurate, in my view, to describe the events of April 1977 as an adventure in the nature of tax minimization.

46. It is essential to the success of any Curran scheme that the bonus shares be properly characterised as trading stock. As I understood the argument advanced by Mr. Hill Q.C. on behalf of Mrs. John, it acknowledged that a deduction could be permitted only if the taxpayer could show that the Compinge bonus shares were trading stock. I cannot accept that the shares had that complexion. It must follow that the loss claimed by the partnership was not deductible.

47. The Commissioner presented an alternative argument that s.260 applied here. It is not necessary to deal with this submission.

48. I would allow the appeal and make consequential orders.
