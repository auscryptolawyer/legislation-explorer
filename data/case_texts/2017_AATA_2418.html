Decleah Investments Pty Ltd and Anor as Trustee for the PRS Unit Trust and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2017] AATA 2418 (1 December 2017)

Last Updated: 5 December 2017

Decleah Investments Pty Ltd and Anor as Trustee for the PRS Unit Trust and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2017] AATA 2418 (1 December 2017)

Division: TAXATION & COMMERCIAL DIVISION

File Number(s): 2016/0200

Re: Decleah Investments Pty Ltd and Anor as Trustee for the PRS Unit Trust

APPLICANT

And Commissioner of Taxation

RESPONDENT

DECISION

Tribunal: Egon Fice, Senior Member

Date: 1 December 2017

Place: Melbourne

The Tribunal sets aside the decision made by the Commissioner on 12 November 2015 and in substitution determines that the correct valuation of the property for the purposes of the GST Act was $8,155,000 or, accepting there were 591 lots, $13,798.65 per lot. The Tribunal also sets aside the Commissioner’s decision regarding the application of shortfall penalties at the rate of 25%. In substitution the Tribunal determines that the correct rate of tax shortfall penalty is 50% for recklessness

Egon Fice, Senior Member

REASONS FOR DECISION



Egon Fice, Senior Member



1 December 2017



  1. Decleah Investments Pty Ltd (Decleah) together with Prince Removal and Storage Pty Ltd (Prince) are trustees of the PRS Unit Trust.
  2. By a Transfer of Land which was accepted by the then Office of Titles Victoria on 21 April 1999, Prince became the registered proprietor of the land described in Certificate of Title Volume 7869, Folio 067 of Crown Allotment 51G in the parish of Nar Nar Goon, County of Mornington (the Land). Consideration paid by Prince for the Land was $670,000. It comprised of an area of 54.37 hectares.
  3. The Land was acquired for the purposes of subdivision. On 28 March 2002 a planning permit was issued by the Cardinia Shire Council. An Amended Planning Permit was issued on 2 July 2002. The approval was for subdivision into 600 lots, subsequently reduced to about 550 lots.
  4. Decleah was registered by ASIC on 28 May 2002 and given the Australian Company Number (ACN) 100 717 191. It was allocated the Australian Business Number (ABN) 11 100 717 191 and registered for the Goods and Services Tax (GST) on the same date. GST registration became effective on 1 July 2002.
  5. Decleah became a joint trustee of the PRS Unit Trust on 1 July 2002.
  6. The Cardinia Shire Council endorsed Plan of Subdivision PS 510552Q on 14 October 2003. The Land Titles Office registered that Plan of Subdivision on 5 November 2003.
  7. When this dispute came before the Tribunal, Decleah was part way through a 14 stage development on the land. However, prior to making the application for review, valuation issues had already arisen. Decleah lodged business activity statements (BASs) reporting GST payable from the sale of 284 lots from the subdivision between 1 January 2004 and 31 March 2006. Decleah applied the margin scheme to those sales.
  8. Division 75 of A New Tax System (Goods and Services Tax) Act 1999 (GST Act) deals with sale of Freehold interest in land. Section 75-1 explains that Division 75 allows a taxpayer to use a margin scheme to bring within the GST system taxable supplies of freehold interests in land, strata units and long-term leases. The use of the margin scheme is an alternative to applying ordinary GST rules to the supply of sales of a freehold interest in land.
  9. Therefore, instead of calculating GST as 1/11 of the consideration received on the supply, GST may be calculated as 1/11 of the margin which is the difference between the consideration paid on the acquisition of the land and the consideration received for its sale. If a land developer decides to sell the freehold interest in land and decides to apply the margin scheme, the acquisition of that land does not give rise to a creditable acquisition. Section 75-20 of the GST Act provides:
    • (1) An acquisition of freehold interest in land, a *strata unit or a *long-term lease is not a *creditable acquisition if the supply of the interest, unit or lease was a *taxable supply under the *margin scheme.
    • (2) This section has effect despite section 11-5 (which is about what is a creditable acquisition).
  10. Section 75-10 of the GST Act deals with the amount of GST on taxable supplies. Where the supplier of an interest in land has acquired that interest before 1 July 2000, provided the supplier has obtained an approved valuation, the margin for the supply is the amount by which the consideration for the supply exceeds that valuation of the interest

    (s. 75-10 (3)).
  11. The dispute in this case centres on the obtaining of an approved valuation. It is a dispute with a lengthy history, some of which needs to be stated so as to give context to the present issues.

GENESIS OF THE DISPUTE

  1. Decleah lodged BASs reporting GST payable from the sale of 284 lots from the subdivision of the property in question between 1 January 2004 and 31 March 2006. Decleah applied the margin scheme and calculated the GST payable per lot at $45,000 as at 1 July 2000 for the tax periods between 1 January 2004 and 30 September 2005. For sales settled in the tax periods between 1 October 2005 and 31 March 2006, Decleah used a value of $20,000 per lot to calculate its GST payable under the margin scheme.
  2. In June 2006 the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) conducted an audit. The ATO determined that Decleah had not provided a complying approved valuation and therefore it used the consideration method, that is, the purchase price of the entire land, for the application of the margin scheme.
  3. In the course of the audit, Decleah provided to the Commissioner three valuations. They were as follows:
    • (a) an undated valuation from Mr Tom Gibson of Elders Real Estate (the First Valuation) who assessed current market value of the property as at 10 December 2003 at $12,780,000;
    • (b) a valuation dated 14 July 2006 from Mr Tom Gibson of Alex Scott & Staff who assessed the current market value for the property at 1 July 2002 at $10,750,000; and
    • (c) a third valuation dated 22 September 2006 from Mr Tom Gibson valuing the property as at 1 July 2000 at $20,000,000.
  4. The Commissioner did not accept any of those valuations to be approved valuations for the purposes of s. 75-10(3) of the GST Act. The Commissioner issued assessments of the GST net amount and penalties. In December 2006 Decleah lodged with the ATO an objection to those assessments and on 17 May 2007, a further objection. The Commissioner disallowed Decleah’s objections and on 14 September 2007 Decleah lodged an application with the Tribunal seeking a review of the Commissioner’s objection decision.
  5. On 16 November 2009 Mr Gibson provided a fourth valuation which valued the land, as at 1 July 2000 at $34,000,000. The Commissioner also rejected that valuation on the ground that it did not satisfy the requirements of the GST Act to be regarded as an approved valuation.
  6. In a letter dated 15 December 2009, the ATO acquired the services of Mr Mark Murray, of O’Briens Valuers & Property Consultants, to prepare an independent expert report. Mr Murray was provided with the third and fourth reports prepared by Mr Gibson and his instructions set out a series of questions which the ATO asked Mr Murray to answer. That expert report was prepared specifically for the purposes of the proceeding before the Tribunal.
  7. Mr Murray provided his report valuing the land at $8,155,000 as at 1 July 2000 for GST purposes. His report is undated. Mr Murray also provided a separate report to the ATO giving his opinion about the third and fourth reports provided by Mr Gibson. It is, effectively, a critique of those reports given the significant disparity between the figures arrived at by Mr Murray and Mr Gibson.
  8. The ATO and Decleah settled the Tribunal proceeding prior to hearing on 8 February 2010. The agreed value was $9,378,250 which equated to $17,051 per lot. The terms of settlement included the following:
2.7 The Commissioner acknowledges that:

...

2.7.2 the valuation of $17,051 per lot sold will not be applicable to tax periods on or after the tax period ended 31 March 2006, unless the taxpayer holds a supporting valuation of the land that complies with requirements under Division 75 of the GST Act.

...

3.2 The taxpayer acknowledges that:

...

3.2.2 the Commissioner will not be required to accept any valuation per lot sold for tax periods on [or] after the tax period ended 31 March 2006 unless the taxpayer holds a supporting valuation of the land that complies with the requirements under Division 75 of the GST Act...
  1. In a letter dated 5 March 2013 the ATO informed the Trustee of the PRS Unit Trust (Decleah) that because it failed when requested to provide full details of all real property sales at the review stage (presumably in accordance with the settlement agreement), the review was to be escalated to an audit. The ATO also reminded the Trustee that income tax returns for the 2010, 2011 and 2012 income years were overdue.
  2. The ATO issued an interim audit report by letter dated 19 December 2013. The report invited any further comments or information prior to 13 January 2014 to give the ATO an opportunity to consider the Trustee’s response and to attempt to resolve concerns before the audit was finalised. The finding by the auditor was that for the sales made in the relevant tax periods under audit, the Trustee had not shown that the valuation provided was an approved valuation. The auditor arrived at that conclusion essentially because:
    • (a) Decleah had not produced the instructions provided to the valuer for the purposes of the preparation of his report and therefore the basis on which the report was prepared was unclear;
    • (b) the assumptions made by Mr Gibson in his cash flow analysis did not reflect the position of the land as at 1 July 2000. He referred to current sales of $150,000 per lot which did not reflect the position as at 1 July 2000;
    • (c) the assumptions made by Mr Gibson in his cash flow analysis were inconsistent with other parts of the report and were not supported by lay evidence. Specifically:
      • (i) the report referred to construction costs of $9,700,000 without evidence of that expenditure;
      • (ii) the $9,700,000 for construction costs was inconsistent with an earlier costs advice attached to the valuation dated 25 September 2006 which stated that the average cost in June 2000 would have been in the order of $14.1 million. The September 2006 valuation was not accepted as an approved or complying valuation;
      • (iii) the Estate Master model referred to total sales revenue of 550 lots, however the report referred to 600 allotments; and
      • (iv) there was no evidence as to how many lots were approved for development as at 1 July 2000; and
    • (d) the reported market value of the land was not within a reasonable range of the market value as at 1 July 2000.
  3. The auditor referred to a determination made by the Commissioner in accordance with

    s. 75-35 of the GST Act. It provides:
    • (1) The Commissioner may, by legislative instrument, determine in writing requirements for making valuations for the purposes of this Division.
    • (2) A valuation made in accordance with those requirements is an approved valuation.
  4. The determination titled A New Tax System (Goods and Services Tax) Margin Scheme Valuation Requirements Determination is referred to as MSV 2005/3. It was superseded by MSV 2009/1 which came into effect on 1 March 2010. While there are some differences between the two determinations, they contain three and four methods respectively which may be applied for the purposes of making an approved valuation. MSV 2005/3 states the following about its application:
3. This determination specifies the requirements for making valuations for calculating the margin for taxable supplies of real property made on or after 1 December 2005 for the purposes of Division 75 of the GST Act.
  1. MSV 2009/1 has a similar application statement except that it applies to valuations for taxable supplies of real property made on or after 1 March 2010.
  2. Plainly, for a valuation to be regarded as an approved valuation, it must be made in accordance with the requirements set out in the relevant MSV Determination.
  3. The auditor found that the valuation obtained from Mr Gibson did not apply MSV 2009/1 and accordingly applied s. 75-10 (2) of the GST Act which effectively provides that the margin for the supply is the amount by which the consideration for the supply exceeds the consideration for the acquisition of the interest in question.
  4. After considering the response provided by solicitors for Decleah, the Commissioner provided a Completion of audit report on 26 February 2014. The Commissioner noted that Decleah had again relied on the same valuation provided for the purposes of the audit conducted in July 2013 (the fourth valuation). It did not comply with the requirements for an approved valuation in accordance with the GST Act. As a result, the Commissioner determined that Decleah’s additional activity statement liability amounted to $594,209 and it was also liable to an administrative penalty in the amount of $356,525.40. The administrative penalty was levied at the rate of 50% of the GST shortfall amount with a 20% uplift.
  5. On 28 April 2014 Decleah lodged an objection to the assessments. Following a request for further information from the ATO on 16 July 2014, on 7 August 2014 Decleah’s solicitors provided what was described as a corrected November 2009 valuation from Mr Gibson (the fifth valuation) (also referred to as the corrected November 2009 valuation). In that valuation, Mr Gibson valued the land as at 1 July 2000 at $22,000,000. Mr Gibson purported to use what he described as a discounted cash flow program to generate a residual land value as at 1 July 2000. It also appears that Mr Gibson accepted that following what appeared to be difficult negotiations to arrive at a plan acceptable to the Council, the subdivision was approved with 600 lots.
  6. The Commissioner issued his Notice of Objection Decision on 12 November 2015. In the reasons for coming to a decision, the Commissioner referred to three valuations provided by Mr Gibson. They were the 22 September 2006 valuation for $20,000,000; the

    17 November 2009 valuation for $34,000,000; and the 7 August 2014 valuation for $22,000,000.
  7. The Commissioner concluded that the margin in this case should be calculated on the acquisition cost of the property, that is, $670,000. The acquisition cost, divided by 562 lots resulted in an acquisition cost of $1,192 per lot. The Commissioner concluded that all three valuation reports provided by Mr Gibson were invalid as they did not constitute an approved valuation for the purposes of the GST Act. Where an approved valuation has not been obtained, the amount of GST on taxable supplies is governed by s. 75-10 which provides:
    • (1) If a *taxable supply of *real property is under the *margin scheme, the amount of GST on the supply is 1/11 of the *margin for the supply.
    • (2) Subject to subsection (3) and section 75-11, the margin for the supply is the amount by which the *consideration for the supply exceeds the consideration for your acquisition of the interest, unit or leasing question.
  8. Section 75-10(3) applies where an approved valuation has been obtained.
  9. The Commissioner also referred to the agreement reached in February 2010 for the purposes of settling the dispute which was then before this Tribunal. The terms of that agreement included a determination to increase the value of the land by 15%, that is, from $8,155,000 to $9,378,250. That resulted in a lot valuation for the purposes of the margin scheme of $17,051 as at 1 July 2000. In effect, the objection was allowed in part, with a reduction in the GST shortfall during those tax periods.
  10. On objection, the Commissioner also reduced the administrative penalty from 50% to 25% and the 20% uplift was removed.
  11. The issue which I am required to determine on this application is whether Decleah held an approved valuation during the relevant period. In doing so, I am required to determine whether the valuations provided by Mr Gibson were in accordance with the requirements:
    • (a) for sales before 1 March 2010, MSV 2005/3; and
    • (b) for sales after 1 March 2010, MSV 2009/1.
  12. I am also required to determine whether the shortfall penalty imposed by the Commissioner should be remitted either in part or in full.

APPROVED VALUATION FOR THE PURPOSES OF THE GST ACT

  1. As is stated in s. 75-35 of the GST Act, an approved valuation is one which is made in accordance with the requirements set out in the relevant legislative instrument. Over the tax period in question, the MSV changed as at 1 March 2010. The first periods prior to that date require the application of MSV 2005/3 and the periods following that date require MSV 2009/1.

MSV 2005/3

  1. This Determination commenced on 1 December 2005 and remained in operation until 28 February 2010.
  2. Clause 8 of the Determination provides:
A valuation complies with the requirements determined by the Commissioner under subsection 75-35(1) if it is made in accordance with any of the following methods and is made by the time specified in paragraphs 15 and 16 below.
  1. The Determination then sets out three methods of valuation which may produce an approved valuation for the purposes of the GST Act. The method relevant in this case is as follows:
Method 1:

9. A written valuation by a professional valuer determining the market value of the interest, unit or lease at the valuation date. The valuation must be made in a manner that is not contrary to the professional standards recognised in Australia for the making of real property valuations.

10. The valuation must include a signed certificate which specifies:
(a) a full description of the property being valued;

(b) the applicable valuation date;

(c) the date the valuer provides a valuation to the supplier;

(d) the market value of the property at the valuation date;

(e) the valuation approach and a valuation calculation; and

(f) the qualifications of the valuer.

11....

MSV 2009/1

  1. This Determination commenced on 1 March 2010 and remained current for the remainder of the taxation periods in question. Clause 3 which deals with the application of the Determination relevantly provides:
3. This determination specifies the requirements for making valuations for the purposes of applying the margin scheme Division 75. The requirements applied to valuations for taxable supplies of real property made on or after 1 March 2010.

4....
  1. Clauses 11 and 12 set out the requirements for making valuations for the purposes of Division 75. They provide:
11. A valuation of the interest, unit or lease made in accordance with the requirements set out by the Commissioner in this determination is an approved valuation of that interest, unit or lease.

12. The Commissioner has determined the following requirements for making valuations for the purposes of Division 75.
  1. MSV 2009/1 provides four methods for making an approved valuation. The relevant method in this case is:
Method 1:

13. For a valuation by a valuer to be an approved valuation for the purposes of Division 75 that valuation must be made in accordance with the following requirements:

(1) the valuer must be a *professional valuer;

(2) the valuation must be in writing;

(3) the valuation must determine the market value of the interest, unit or least at the valuation date;

(4) the valuation must be made in a manner that is not contrary to the professional standards recognised in Australia for the making of real property valuations;

(5) the valuation must include a signed certificate which specifies:

(a) a full description of the property being valued;

(b) the applicable valuation date;

(c) the date the valuer provides a valuation to the supplier;

(d) the market value of the property at the valuation date;

(e) the valuation approach and valuation calculation; and

(f) the name and qualifications of the valuer;

(6)...

(7) the valuation must be made by the time specified in clauses 21 to 23 below.

  1. The time limits referred to above are as follows:
21. The valuation under methods 1 to 3 must be made by the due date for lodgement of the Business Activity Statement for the tax period to which the GST payable on the taxable supply of the real property is attributable.

22. However, if the Commissioner has allowed a further period under paragraph

75-5(1A)(b) of the GST Act for the supplier and recipient to agree in writing that the margin scheme is to apply in working out the GST on the supply, the valuation must be made by the later of:

(1) 8 weeks from the end of the further period that the Commissioner has allowed under paragraph 75-5(1A)(b); or

(2) 8 weeks from the date of the Commissioner’s decision to extend the further period under paragraph 75-5(1A)(b).

23. If the valuation is not made within the time period specified in clauses 21 and 22, the Commissioner may for good reason allow an additional period within which a valuation may be made.
  1. Mr C Sievers of counsel, who appeared on behalf of the Commissioner, referred me to the Federal Court of Australia (Middleton J) decision in Brady King Pty Ltd v The Commissioner of Taxation (No 2) [2008] FCA 1918. His Honour said this about Determinations at [31]:
The fact that there may be matters of subjective analysis undertaken is encompassed and envisaged by the Determination which relies upon a professional valuer undertaking the task and coming to a valuation. However, in reaching the final valuation, the professional valuer must not deviate from the method of valuation dictated by the terms of the Determination.
  1. The valuation method adopted by Decleah in this case was that set out as Method 1 in both Determinations. The expression professional valuer is defined in both determinations in the following way:
Professional valuer means:
(1) a person registered or licensed to carry out real property valuations under a Commonwealth, a State or a Territory law; or

(2) a person who carries on business as a valuer in a State or a Territory where that person is not required to be licensed or registered to carry on a business as a valuer; or

(3) a person who is:
  • (a) a member of the Australian Property Institute and accredited as a Certified Practising Valuer; or
  • (b) a member of the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors and accredited as a Chartered Valuation Surveyor; or
  • (c) a member of the Australian Valuers Institute and accredited as a Certified Practising Valuer.
  1. Mr Gibson described himself as a Certified Practising Valuer and an Associate Member of the Australian Property Institute. There was no question that he satisfied the professional valuer definition.
  2. Because the interest in the land acquired by Decleah arose prior to 1 July 2000, the valuation date for the purposes of the margin scheme is 1 July 2000. Furthermore, as is described under Method 1 in both Determinations, the valuation must determine the market value of the interest at the valuation date.
  3. Mr Gibson explained that he used what is described in accounting terms as a discounted cash flow method of valuation of an asset. Before examining that methodology in some detail, I should refer to what Mr Gibson said in his witness statement of 24 February 2017 regarding the instructions he was given for the purposes of making the valuation. He said his instructions were to perform the valuation on an as is basis. His understanding of that expression was that it referred to actual costs and sales post valuation date. He mentioned that if he had been asked to perform a valuation on an as was basis, he would have been restricted to using only data known or predicted at the valuation date. However, because he was asked to perform the valuation on an as is basis, that allowed him to use actual costs and income as at the time of making the valuation, which was in August 2014.
  4. Mr Gibson testified that he did not have regard to the zoning of the land as it was on 1 July 2000 due to the basis upon which his valuation was made. He referred to the lots that were actually subdivided. As for development and construction costs, Mr Gibson said that he relied on actual costs incurred since the commencement of the development. He also said that the valuation was based on the actual subdivided lots created rather than an estimate made based on the permit granted in 1993. That being the case, Mr Gibson explained that the only method which allowed him to provide a valuation on an as is basis was a discounted cash flow method.
  5. With respect to Mr Gibson, as Mr Sievers explained at the hearing, there are serious problems with the methodology he adopted.
  6. The first difficulty arises from Mr Gibson’s understanding of the context in which the expression as is was used. In his second witness statement dated 29 May 2017 Mr Gibson said that he attended a meeting in September 2009 with officers of the ATO. Mr Gibson said that the main purpose of that meeting was to clarify the basis upon which the ATO required valuation of the land to be carried out for GST purposes. He said the earlier valuations provided were discussed and the ATO officers made it clear that the valuation was required on a different basis to those reports. Mr Gibson explained:
The ATO required the valuation to be based on the actual price at which the subdivided parcels of land had been sold after 1 July 2000, and the actual development costs involved in creating those subdivided blocks, and to discount those into a cash flow. The ATO Representatives were very clear that this was the approach which the ATO required. That is what the whole meeting was about.
  1. Mr Gibson then referred to a letter received from the ATO dated 16 October 2009 which referred to discussions between Decleah’s counsel and the Commissioner’s counsel.

    Mr Gibson assumed that the ATO officers who attended the meeting were advising him of the ATO’s policy in relation to the issue and that the subsequent letter was written in light of that policy. He therefore interpreted the reference to as is in the ATO letter as being a reference to the basis of costs and expenses at the time the valuation report was made. That is what caused him to use the methodology he did in providing his valuation. When it was put to Mr Gibson in cross-examination that if he had misinterpreted the instructions given to him by the ATO and that what was requested was in fact an as was at 1 July 2000 valuation, his valuation did not comply, Mr Gibson agreed.
  2. The ATO letter of 16 October 2009 deals in some detail with what the ATO considered to be a complying valuation. It stated:
That valuation should be a valuation of the market value as at 1 July 2000 of the interest described in Certificate of Title volume 7869, Folio 067 of Crown allotment 51G in the Parish of Nar Nar Goon.

...

The valuation should be a market value of the real property interest that was in existence as at 1 July 2000...
  1. Significantly, the expression as is basis appears in the following context:
... the market value for the subject property should have regard to the physical and legal state of the subject property as reflected at the date of valuation, being 1 July 2000. The valuation is to be completed on a ‘as is basis’ after consideration of all known factors affecting the market valuation as at the date of valuation. It is the value of the interest in the land, improvements, buildings and machinery fixed to the land, and any property rights connected to the interest that were in existence, in the condition, and under the approved zoning that applied as at the valuation date, 1 July 2000.
  1. A proper reading of the expression as is as it appears in the above quoted paragraph makes it clear that the expression refers to the land in its condition, subject to its legal state, as at 1 July 2000. There is no logical basis for Mr Gibson’s conclusion that he could take into account the condition of the land (then subdivided) and the legal state of the land, particularly regarding zoning approval, at the time he prepared the valuation in August 2014. In other words, the as is expression is a shorthand method of explaining that what was required was the relevant circumstances relating to the land as it existed at the valuation date. Without words connecting the expression as is with the date on which Mr Gibson performed the valuation in 2014, there was no basis for the conclusion which he reached. It appears Mr Gibson did not seek to establish the contextual connection of the expression with what is required under the legislation. To qualify for the application of the margin scheme, the margin to be applied is calculated as the difference between the consideration received for the subsequent supply of an interest in the land and the value of that interest as at the date of its acquisition or, in this case, 1 July 2000.
  2. Mr Gibson claimed he had achieved the valuation of the land prior to its subdivision and subsequent sale of a number of those lots in the subdivision by valuing the land as at

    1 July 2000 using the discounted cash flow method. With respect to Mr Gibson, this involves a serious misunderstanding of the fundamental basis upon which that methodology is based. Wayne Lonergan, in his text The Valuation of Businesses, Shares and Other Equity, 4th edition, 2003, explains the principle of discounted cash flow, at 22, in the following way:
The fundamental valuation principle is that the value of any asset is the present value of the future cash flow stream flowing from that asset. This principle, in various forms, is validly and profitably used by purchasers in evaluating investment and acquisition opportunities. The risk associated with the cash flow stream is reflected in the discount rate which is used to convert the timing of future cash flows to a present value. The calculation of the present value of future cash flows is generally described as the DCF basis of valuation.
  1. And at 64:
A DCF valuation is based on the generally accepted theory that the value of a business depends on the future net cash flow of the business discounted back to a present value at an appropriate discount rate. Future cash flows comprise two elements:
  • the cash amounts expected to be generated each year after paying all cash costs (including the proprietor’s remuneration in the case of small businesses) and cash outgoings (such as capital expenditure and working capital requirements)
  • the net cash amount expected to be received upon the ultimate sale of the business.
  1. It should be clear that a discounted cash flow methodology is used in valuations of property at a particular date after discounting for risk of uncertain future events. Those events include income and expenditure. Many other factors may also need to be considered in determining the risk; and the level of risk will then determine the discount rate. Most importantly, it is forward looking and not backwards looking. Once the risk factors are known and have been taken into account and income and expenditure is incurred, there is no further risk up to the time those factors are accounted for. Looking backwards by using actual income and actual expenditures does not result in a valuation of the property in question at an earlier date. There is no risk factor involved. As Lonergan states in his text, the methodology provides for the forecasting of future cash flows over a sufficiently long period of time and discounting those cash flows at an appropriate discount rate.
  2. Lonergan’s text also describes the concept of an as is valuation. At 380, he describes it in the following way:
‘As is’ valuation differs from an ‘on completion’ valuation. An ‘as is’ valuation is the value of a property based on its existing use and does not take into account the alternative use of the property.
  1. In the matter before me, Mr Gibson has in fact incorporated into his valuation the on completion value of subdivided lots in the land in order to arrive at the valuation of the land as at 1 July 2000. Respectfully, it makes no sense.
  2. Even where the discounted cash flow methodology is used in its proper sense, there appears to be considerable resistance by the courts to adopt the methodology in respect of land development. The High Court of Australia (Jacobs J) dealt expressly with this valuation method in Albany v Commonwealth of Australia (1976) 12 ALR 201.
  3. Albany involved the acquisition by the Commonwealth of a substantial portion of land close to Darwin and a compensation claim brought by Albany. Albany argued that the valuation of the land should be based on its best use at the date of acquisition which was for mainly residential urban development. It used the discounted cash flow method of valuation. On its face, the use of the discounted cash flow method in the circumstances was likely to be appropriate if the risk factors could be properly identified and an appropriate discount calculated for risk. However, the Court was unhappy about that methodology in the circumstances.
  4. Jacobs J said the following about the use of a discounted cash flow method in the valuation of land, at 206 – 211:
The process of discounted cash flow is one which is known as a method of estimating present value of a capital asset in accounting processes. It is not a process which had previously been used by any of the plaintiff’s valuers in the valuation of land.... First, it should be noted that the rate per cent of discount for the future cash flows reflects the amount of profit and the degree of risk involved in the venture. In addition, the rate of discount must take account of the interest payable upon the amount outlaid in purchase of the lands. The method of valuation based on a hypothetical subdivision which has been used in those cases where such a method of valuation has found to be appropriate, specific provision is made for the interest on the purchase price of the land to be subdivided. Secondly, the estimate of incomings and outgoings in the projected number of years of development takes account of the estimated rise in the will value of land over the period and the estimated increase in development costs over that period.... A profit and risk factor is calculated as a matter of judgement based upon comparable developments....

Both methods involve many assumptions which must necessarily be made. The projected period of development must be determined. The cost of development must be estimated. The fact of profit and risk must be determined in each case. There is, however, in the case of a projection by a discounted cash flow, the further projection of future rises in land prices and development costs.

...

I return now to the valuation by the plaintiff’s valuers upon the basis of a discounted cash flow. Although I propose to analyse the factors and assumptions which have been made in this valuation, I should now say that I am not satisfied that this could be an acceptable method of valuation in the present case. I express no opinion upon the question whether or not, in other circumstances and in other cases, a method of valuation by way of discounting the anticipated cash flow is a proper method of valuation of land.... There is no evidence that the application of this method has either in theory or inexperience produced results consistent with methods of valuation based on comparable sales or on that method of valuation upon the basis of hypothetical subdivision which has, where necessary, in applied in the past.
  1. I have also examined discussion of this topic in Alan Hyam’s text The Law Affecting Valuation of Land in Australia, fifth addition, 2014. The learned author has referred to a number of cases (pages 212 – 219) where valuation by using the discounted cash flow method has been examined and noted a general dislike of this methodology by the courts. Nevertheless, there has not been an outright rejection of the use of this methodology. In fact Hyam referred to the decision of the Queensland Land Court in Thirty-fourth Philgram Pty Ltd v The Crown (1993) 14 QLCR 13 where the presiding member said, at 28:
... It is essentially a method used by property developers to enable them to ascertain the highest price they can pay for a parcel of land in a development project, having regard to the circumstances of that project. Those circumstances, however, are too diverse and subjective to accurately reflect the market value of the land.
  1. The point which needs to be made in this case is that the literature and cases to which I have referred above regarding use of the discounted cash flow methodology in the valuation of an asset make it clear that the methodology involves assessing the value of an asset at a date which precedes any development of the asset, be it by way of subdivision or the building of structures upon that land. In other words, that valuation is based on an as is basis at the valuation date by estimating the value of those factors which would increase or decrease the present value at some future point in time; coming to a conclusion about the risk of those events occurring or not occurring; and applying that risk factor to the valuation at the date of valuation. In this case, Mr Gibson has not applied that process. In fact, he has operated in reverse, thereby removing all allowances for risk, taking known increases in the value of lots of the original land following subdivision as well as the expenses in subdividing those lots. There is no risk factor involved. Some of the risks of such development, which were not taken into account by Mr Gibson because that risk had passed by August 2014, were:
    • zoning of the subject property at 1 July 2000 (part residential, part public park and recreational zone);
    • planning permit required for redevelopment (the first planning permit appears to been granted on 28 March 2002);
    • known and potential contamination issues and their risk to future development and timetable;
    • demolition of improvements if required for redevelopment;
    • GST liability on the supply of the property; and
    • impact of encumbrances on the property.
  2. The flawed nature of Mr Gibson’s valuation methodology must necessarily result in a valuation that does not comply with either MSV 2005/3 or MSV 2009/1. That is because, even if the discounted cash flow method met the standards recognised in Australia for making real property valuations, because he has misapplied the methodology, his valuation is not in accordance with professional standards and is worthless. Given the serious misapplication of the discounted cash flow methodology, no purpose is served in examining whether Mr Gibson’s valuation can be said to comply with International Valuation Standards. It simply cannot.
  3. In his 7 August 2014 valuation Mr Gibson added that he also considered the Direct Comparison approach to valuation. As I understand that method, it involves a comparison of similar properties in the same location or nearby to the one in question as at 1 July 2000, including any restrictions or zoning requirements which might apply to the property being valued. Although Mr Gibson referred to the current selling price of lots which were part of the development in question as at 2014, I could find nothing in his valuation report dealing with the direct comparison method. His focus was clearly on the discounted cash flow method.

COMMISSIONER’S VALUATION

  1. There was, at some point in this proceeding, a suggestion that the Commissioner was bound by the Deed of Settlement entered into between Decleah and the Commissioner of Taxation following lodgement of a proceeding in this Tribunal. A copy of that Deed of Settlement was in evidence. The relevant contents, which are found at clause 2.7, are set out at [19] above. As stated, the agreed valuations may be applied in the future only where an approved valuation was obtained.
  2. Given that I have found that the valuation provided by Mr Gibson in August 2014 was not an approved valuation and therefore did not comply with the requirements of Division 75 of the GST Act, I find the provision in the Deed of Settlement does not require the Commissioner to maintain a valuation of $17,051 per lot after 31 March 2006. Furthermore, given that the GST Act for the purposes of the margin scheme requires an approved valuation to be produced for the purposes of determining the GST payable

    (s. 75-10(3)), I find that neither the Commissioner nor Decleah can now rely on the valuation previously agreed upon for the purposes of resolving the first review application brought to the Tribunal by Decleah.
  3. Despite the Commissioner allowing an extended period of time for Decleah to lodge the fifth valuation report provided in August 2014, not one of the five valuation reports provided by Mr Gibson was found to be an approved valuation. Therefore, in compliance with Method 4 of MSV 2009/1, the Commissioner has relied on a report prepared by Mr Mark Murray, a Certified Practising Valuer employed by O’Briens Valuers and Property Consultants. Mr Murray also provided a critique of Mr Gibson’s fifth valuation report which he considered did not comply with the requirements of Division 75 of the GST Act. Amongst other things, Mr Murray noted that Mr Gibson, although stating that he employed the Direct Comparison valuation method, made no reference to value rates on a direct comparison assessment.
  4. Mr Murray gave oral evidence and he relied on the valuation report he prepared on 21 January 2010. The first point made by Mr Murray was that despite his research and investigations, there appeared to be no available sales evidence regarding comparable properties transacted at the relevant date. He noted that permits were in existence for the property in question in 1994 permitting subdivision of the land and that permits for development issued in 2003. He was of the opinion that prior to 2003, development as envisaged by Decleah remained uncertain and while permits were in existence for the subdivision of the land, its value then was probably subjective. He therefore preferred to use a direct comparison approach as opposed to a cash flow analysis. Although Mr Murray was of the opinion that a discounted cash flow methodology was not an unreasonable approach to the valuation of a development property, the assumptions adopted by Mr Gibson, that is actual sales and costs, did not appear to reflect the position of the land as at the relevant date of 1 July 2000.
  5. Utilising a direct comparison approach, Mr Murray considered a reasonable assessment to be $150,000 per hectare (GST exclusive) which equated to a rounded assessment of $8,155,000 as at 1 July 2000. In my opinion, Mr Murray’s valuation should be accepted as it falls within the parameters of MSV 2009/1. I find it is an approved valuation. It is the only approved valuation before me in this matter.
  6. My finding necessarily results in Decleah having paid significantly less GST in the relevant periods than it was required to pay, resulting in exposure to tax shortfall penalties.

PENALTIES

  1. This proceeding is concerned with the quarterly periods between 1 October 2009 and 30 June 2012. Subdivision 284-B of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (the Administration Act) deals with penalties relating to statements. A taxpayer’s liability to an administrative penalty is determined by s. 284-75 which, for the relevant period, provided:
    • (1) You are liable to an administrative penalty if:
      • (d) you make a statement to the Commissioner or to an entity that is exercising powers or performing functions under a *taxation law (other than the *Excise Acts); and
      • (e) the statement is false or misleading in a material particular, whether because of things in it or omitted from it.
  2. There are exceptions to subsection (1). They are found in subsections (5) and (6) which provide:

(5) You are not liable to an administrative penalty under subsection (1) or (4) for a statement that is false or misleading in a material particular if you, and your *agent (if relevant), took reasonable care in connection with the making of the statement.



(6) You are not liable to an administrative penalty under subsection (1) or (4) if:
(f) you engage a *registered tax agent or BAS agent; and

(g) you give the registered tax agent all BAS agent all relevant taxation information; and

(h) the registered tax agent all BAS agent makes the statement; and

(i) the false or misleading nature of the statement did not result from:
  • (i) intentional disregard by the registered tax agent or BAS agent of a *taxation law (other than the*Excise Acts); or
  • (ii) recklessness by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law (other than the Excise Acts).
  1. Given that the claimed false or misleading statements were made in BASs lodged over the relevant period; and those statements were made as a consequence of valuations obtained by Decleah; the above exceptions would appear not to apply in this case.
  2. In determining whether an administrative penalty should be applied and, if so, at which base level, I need to take into account all of the circumstances which led to Decleah determining the net amount in each of the BASs lodged in the relevant period. Principally, those figures were determined by the incorrect application of the margin scheme, which Decleah chose to apply for the calculation of GST payable.
  3. The first valuation report prepared by Mr Gibson valued the property as at 10 December 2003 in the amount of $12,780,000. By that time, a planning permit had issued in March 2002 and in valuing the property, Mr Gibson noted that the streets had been fully constructed. The land had been zoned residential. The valuation was for a part of the entire land in question, being 284 lots. He valued each lot at $45,000. In his report, Mr Gibson said the valuation was current at the date of valuation only and that the method of valuation did not include any costs incurred up to the date of valuation or to be incurred in the future. It did not include the possibility of costs for clearing any environmental hazard. He also noted that the valuation was made on the basis of information provided by the owner which he assumed to be accurate at the date of valuation.
  4. The most significant problem, but not the only problem, is the date of the valuation. The GST Act requires land acquired prior to the commencement of the GST to be valued as at 1 July 2000. As a Certified Practising Valuer, it would be reasonable to expect Mr Gibson to have known the requirements for valuation in order to apply the margin scheme, particularly given that in his report he mentions valuation in order to arrive at a true value for margin scheme purposes.
  5. The second valuation conducted by Mr Gibson is dated 14 July 2006. He noted that valuation was provided for GST purposes and he valued the current market as at 1 July 2002 at $10,750,000. Again, there is no explanation regarding selection of an incorrect valuation date. Mr Gibson noted that zoning at that time was part residential and part public park and recreational use and that a permit had issued for the subdivision approving 40 lots. Mr Gibson appeared to place significant emphasis on the fact that a subdivision permit had issued at that time. Mr Gibson said he used a direct comparison and cash flow approach to valuation on the basis of costs of development being $20,000 per lot; and discounting potential profit by a risk factor of 18%. Mr Gibson compared three properties, although it cannot be said that they were comparable. The sale dates were between one and two years after the valuation date of the subject property and only one was zoned residential but was significantly smaller than the subject land. He expressed similar cautions to those expressed in his first valuation.
  6. The third valuation performed by Mr Gibson was for the purposes of the proceeding then current in the Tribunal. His report is dated 22 September 2006. That report valued the property at $20,000,000 as at 1 July 2000. That amounted to $33,333 per lot on a 600 lot subdivision. Otherwise, the comments and comparisons made by Mr Gibson are identical to the previous report save for the addition of one further property which, while zoned residential, was significantly smaller than the property in question. Mr Gibson provided no explanation for the significant increase in value of the property in the two months since his previous valuation, taking into account that the valuation was now at the correct date, some two years prior to the previous valuation. With respect to Mr Gibson, this valuation makes little sense when compared with the valuation said to be as at 1 July 2002 and made two months earlier than this valuation.
  7. The Commissioner obtained a report from the Australian Valuation Office (AVO) on 12 November 2009. That report sought to analyse the valuation methodology adopted by Mr Gibson. Mr Luke O’Brien, the author of that report, raised numerous concerns with the methodology adopted by Mr Gibson, including the importance of valuation as at 1 July 2000. At that time, while it could be assumed that the land had potential for subdivision; a planning permit for Stage 1 of the development comprising 41 lots was only granted on 28 March 2002 and amended on 2 July 2002. This was of course post valuation date. Mr O’Brien also referred to International Guidance Note No. 9 – Discounted Cash Flow Analysis for Market Valuations and Investment Analyses. He quoted the following:
Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis is a financial modelling technique based on explicit assumptions regarding the prospective income and expenses of the property or business. Such assumptions pertain to the quantity, quality, variability, timing and duration of inflows and outflows that are discounted to present value. DCF analysis, with appropriate and supportable data and discount rates, is one of the accepted methodologies within the income capitalisation approach to valuation.
  1. The important aspect of the above quoted passage is that the modelling is prospective not retrospective. For unexplained reasons, Mr Gibson has not appreciated that. Given that the report was prepared for the purposes of the proceeding in the Tribunal at that time, it is reasonable to infer that its contents were made known to Decleah and its representatives.
  2. Mr Gibson’s next report was delivered to the Commissioner on 16 November 2009. Mr Gibson valued the land at $34,000,000. He said he had adopted the discounted cash flow approach because it was the only approach which allowed for the use of actual cost and income. Again, Mr Gibson’s misunderstanding of the purpose and use of a discounted cash flow analysis for valuation of an asset is patent. Necessarily being a prospective analysis based on a hypothetical structure, actual income and expenditure is unknown. A purchaser of the property in question in 2000 could not possibly foresee actual income and expenditure nine years into the future. Hence the need to discount for risk, amongst other things. To attempt to do this analysis retrospectively based on actual cost and income ignores the obvious. Had a purchaser in 2000 been aware of the actual costs and income of the project nine years in the future, no doubt the seller would also have been aware of that and those factors would have been factored into the price at that time. Given that the purchase price the land was $670,000, those factors clearly were not known to the parties. It should have come as no surprise to Decleah or its representatives that this valuation was not accepted by the Commissioner.
  3. The fifth valuation provided by Mr Gibson was substantially the same as the previous valuation save for what Mr Gibson referred to as errors which reduced the valuation figure to $22,000,000. No explanation was given regarding the errors claimed to have been made in the previous valuation.
  4. Given this history, it is difficult to understand why Decleah continued to accept Mr Gibson’s valuations. Mr Gibson attempted to explain the basis for his valuations. In his second witness statement dated 29 May 2017 he referred to a meeting with representatives from the ATO where he said he was told that the valuation required was on an as is basis, that being on the basis of costs and expenses incurred at the time of the report. He then decided, for reasons I am unable to discern, that those figures could then be discounted to arrive at a valuation figure of the land in question as at 1 July 2000. He suggested that the terminology used by the ATO officers was incorrect and they should have referred to the land as was.
  5. Mr Gibson also referred to a letter from the ATO dated 16 October 2009 which referred to valuation of the land on an as is basis. However that letter went on to explain that the value required was the interest in the land, improvements, buildings and machinery fixed to the land, and any property rights connected to the interest that were in existence in the condition, and under the approved zoning that applied as at the valuation date, that being 1 July 2000. In my opinion, that explanation could hardly have been made more clearly. The only conclusion I can draw is that Mr Gibson chose to disregard that statement.
  6. Despite the concerns expressed by the ATO from as early as October 2005, when the ATO commenced an audit and the Commissioner told Decleah representatives that the valuations obtained from Mr Gibson were not approved valuations for the purposes of the GST Act, Decleah persisted in claiming highly inflated valuations as at 1 July 2000. The valuation of the property in question for the purposes of the GST Act is a material particular in the calculation of GST payable under the margin scheme. The misstatement of that valuation can properly be described as providing the ATO with a false and misleading material particular. For that reason, I find that the administrative penalty provisions are enlivened in accordance with s. 284-75 of the Administration Act. The issue remaining is the appropriate base rate which should be applied in the circumstances.
  7. The Commissioner, in his objection decision, considered whether Decleah’s conduct was reckless or whether it failed to exercise reasonable care. Apparently the auditor determined that the behaviour demonstrated recklessness as to the operation of a taxation law. In his objection decision the Commissioner determined that the auditor’s finding of recklessness was incorrect and instead determined that the behaviour demonstrated a lack of reasonable care. Therefore, the penalty was reduced from 50% to 25% of the shortfall amount. In addition, although the auditor increased the penalty by 20% on the basis that a previous audit for the tax periods between 1 January 2004 to 31 March 2006 also resulted in the imposition of penalties, in his objection decision the Commissioner determined that a 20% uplift was inappropriate because those administrative penalties were not imposed in relation to the incorrect application of the margin scheme. Hence this was not repetition of the same conduct.
  8. Nevertheless, Mr Sievers submitted that despite the findings of the Commissioner regarding penalties and in particular the base penalty, the appropriate level for the conduct in this case was 50% because Decleah’s conduct was reckless. Mr Sievers agreed with the remission of the 20% uplift.
  9. There can be no doubt that the Tribunal is not restricted to making the same decision made by the Commissioner in relation to penalties; or finding that they should be remitted in part or in full. The Tribunal is required to make an assessment based on the evidence before it on the hearing of this matter; and that evidence may well be different to the evidence which was before the Commissioner when he made the objection decision. This was stated by the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia (Bowen CJ, Smithers and Deane JJ) in Drake v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 24 ALR 577. Bowen CJ and Deane J said, at 589:
The question for the determination of the Tribunal is not whether the decision which the decision-maker made was the correct or preferable one on the material before him. The question for the determination of the Tribunal is whether that decision was the correct or preferable one on the material before the Tribunal.
  1. If further support were needed for that proposition, it may be found in the recent decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia (Reeves, Farrell and Gleeson JJ) in Frugtniet v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2017] FCAFC 162. The Court said, at [116]:
... Section 43(1) of the AAT Act, read in conjunction with s. 25 (4 a) and Drake, prescribes the task of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to be essentially the same as the task imposed on the Administrative Decisions Tribunal by s. 63 (1) of the ADT Act. The principle stated by Bowen CJ and Deane J in Drake is that the question for the determination of the Tribunal is not whether the decision which the decision-maker made was the correct or preferable one on the material before him. The question for determination of the Tribunal is whether that decision was the correct or preferable one on the material before the Tribunal. That principle was applied by the High Court of Australia in relation to the review of a decision by the Tribunal concerning fitness and propriety in Shi v Migration Agents Registration Authority [2008] HCA 31; (2008) 235 CLR 286 (‘Shi’ i). Shi makes it clear that, depending on the decision under review, the tribunal may have regard to material that was not before the original decision-maker: in Shi, that comprised material relevant to the fitness and propriety of Mr Shi at the time of the relevant tribunal’s decision.
  1. Miscellaneous Taxation Ruling MT 2008/1 (the Ruling) deals with penalty relating to statements and the meaning of, amongst other things, reasonable care and recklessness. The original ruling was made on 12 November 2008. The ruling to which I refer below is a consolidated ruling which came into effect on 11 July 2012. In that regard, the Ruling states, at clause 10:
The administrative penalty provisions consolidate and standardise the different penalty regime that previously existed. In addition, the provisions apply in respect of various taxes and collection systems including income tax, FBT, GST, petroleum resource rent tax and pay as you go withholding and instalments.
  1. The meaning of the expression reasonable care is not a defined term and the Ruling refers to The Australian Oxford Dictionary for its ordinary meaning. The Ruling then states, at clause 27 – 29:
27.... Taking ‘reasonable care’ in the context of making a statement to the Commissioner or to an entity within the meaning of subsection 284-75(4) means giving appropriately serious attention to complying with the obligations imposed under a taxation law.



28. The reasonable care test requires an entity to take the same care in fulfilling their tax obligations that could be expected of a reasonable ordinary person in their position. This means that even though the standard of care is measured objectively, it takes into account the circumstances of the taxpayer. This aspect of the test is addressed in the Revised Explanatory Memorandum to the A New Tax System (Tax Administration) Bill (No. 2) 2000 where it states at paragraph 1.69:

Reasonable care requires a taxpayer to make a reasonable attempt to comply with the provisions of ITAA and regulations. The effort required is one commensurate with all the taxpayer’s circumstances, including the taxpayer’s knowledge, education, experience and skill.



29. Judging whether there has been a failure to take reasonable care turns on an evaluation of all the circumstances surrounding the making of the false or misleading statement to determine whether a reasonable person of ordinary prudence in the same circumstances would have exercised greater care.
  1. The Ruling draws a parallel with the law of negligence. It provides, at clause 31:
In proving negligence under the common law, the benchmark standard of care demanded of the person subject to a duty of care depends on what a reasonable person of ordinary prudence would have done or would not have done in response to a foreseeable risk of injury. This involves the application of an objective test generally without regard to the personal characteristics or idiosyncrasies of the person whose conduct is in question. To this extent there is a difference between the test for proving negligence and determining whether reasonable care is shown in complying with tax obligations which does have regard to an entity’s particular circumstances including their knowledge, education, experience and skill.
  1. The Ruling makes it clear that whether or not the person has tried to act with reasonable care is not the test. What is relevant is whether, on an objective analysis, reasonable care has been shown to have been taken.
  2. The Ruling distinguishes recklessness from failure to take reasonable care. It states, at clause 99:
The legislative context apparent from a reading of items 1, 2, 3, 3A, 3B and 3C of the table in subsection 284-90(1) indicates that ‘recklessness’ connotes conduct that is more culpable than a failure to take reasonable care to comply with a taxation law but less culpable than an intentional disregard of a taxation law. The scheme of uniform penalties regime is to impose the higher penalty in response to conduct that goes beyond mere carelessness or inadvertence by displaying a high degree of carelessness.
  1. The Ruling explains that like the test for determining whether reasonable care has been shown, a finding of recklessness depends on the application of essentially an objective test. Present must be conduct that falls short of the standard of a reasonable person in the position of the entity. Dishonesty is not an element when establishing recklessness. The actual intention of the entity is not relevant. Reckless behaviour will occur where it falls significantly short of the standard of care expected of a reasonable person in the same circumstances as the entity.
  2. The Ruling refers to a number of court decisions which have dealt with recklessness. The following description, found at clause 102, is of assistance in this case:
Recklessness assumes that the behaviour in question shows disregard of or indifference to a risk that is foreseeable by reasonable person. The Full Federal Court in Hart v. FC of T (2003) 131 FCR 2003; [2003] FCAFC 105 (Hart) at paragraphs 33 and 43 endorsed the following comments of Cooper J in the BRK (Bris) Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2001] FCA 164; 2001 ATC 4111; [2001] FCA 164; (2001) 46 ATR 347 (BRK) at paragraph 77:

Recklessness in this context means to include in the tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing there is a real, as opposed to a fanciful risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act and regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the content of the tax statement. So understood the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to gross carelessness.
  1. The Ruling also refers to statements made by Megaw J in the case Shawinigan Ltd v. Vokins & Co Ltd [1961] 2 Lloyd’s Rep 153 at 162; [1961] 1 WLR 1206 at 1214; [1961] 3 All ER 396 at 403 where his Honour said:
Recklessness is gross carelessness – the doing of something which in fact involves risk, whether the doer realises it or not; and the risk being such having regard to all the circumstances, that the taking of that risk would be described as ‘reckless’. The likelihood or otherwise the damage will follow is one element to be considered, not whether the doer of the act actually realise the likelihood. The extent of the damage which is likely to follow is another element...

... If the risk is slight and the damage which will follow if things go wrong is small, it may not be reckless, however unjustified the doing of the act may be. If the risk is great, and the probable damage great, recklessness may readily be a fair description, however much the doer may regard the action is justified and reasonable. Each case has to be viewed on its own particular facts and not by reference to any formula.
  1. In this case, Decleah obtained no less than four valuations from Mr Gibson prior to the final fifth valuation which was rejected by the Commissioner. It must be said that those responsible for obtaining the fifth and final valuation from Mr Gibson must have been aware of the serious risk that it too would be rejected by the Commissioner, particularly given the basis upon which both the fourth and the fifth valuations were made.
  2. Although Mr Gibson attempted to maintain that his use of a discounted cash flow method had to be accepted by the Commissioner, despite the Commissioner making it clear that the valuation should be the market value of the property interest that was in existence

    1 July 2000. He even went so far as to explain that the market value for the subject property should have regard to the physical and legal state of that property as at the date of valuation, in other words, on an as is basis at 1 July 2000. The valuation provided by Mr Gibson did not produce that result. I have little doubt that Mr Gibson was aware of that. The fact that the property was purchased for $670,000 on 21 April 1999 and that he was of the view it was valued at in excess of $20,000,000 on 1 July 2000, should have been cause for serious alarm and rethinking by Mr Gibson.
  3. Also, it should have been cause for alarm to those persons responsible for obtaining a valuation on behalf of Decleah. Prudent persons in their position would, in my opinion, have sought a valuation from another valuer well before the fifth valuation was provided by Mr Gibson. There was no evidence of any attempt to obtain an alternative valuation.
  4. Therefore, the degree of risk that the valuations provided by Mr Gibson would not be accepted by the Commissioner or this Tribunal on review were significantly high, particularly given the large disparity between what the Commissioner had previously valued the property at and Mr Gibson’s fifth valuation. It would have been obvious to those responsible that if Mr Gibson’s valuation were not accepted, significant damages would flow not only due to an understatement of GST which was payable on the supply of the subdivided lots, but also penalties for incorrect and misleading statements made to the Commissioner.
  5. In my opinion, the valuation obtained by Decleah from Mr Gibson and the provision of that valuation to the Commissioner for the purposes of the margin scheme and its GST liability was reckless. Decleah had been made aware on at least four occasions prior to the fifth valuation of precisely what was required to comply with the GST Act and to produce an approved valuation. Just how one could believe that applying actual costs incurred and income produced in a subdivision undertaken up to 12 years after the statutory valuation date could provide a market value for the land at valuation date is incomprehensible. The discounted cash flow method of valuation is prospective, that is, forward-looking and based on a hypothetical subdivision in the circumstances present at valuation date. It takes into account the risk that the development will proceed as planned in the future. Plainly, at valuation date, these factors are essentially a guess, albeit by a person with some experience in such developments in a particular area. Comparisons of the market value of similar properties in the same location are probably the best guide at that time. Although Mr Gibson purported to also use a comparative value analysis, his valuations do not reflect that.

CONCLUSION

  1. I have found that Decleah did not provide an approved valuation for the purposes of the GST Act when applying the margin scheme to calculate GST payable on the supply of subdivided lots between 1 October 2009 and 30 June 2012. I have found that the valuation of the land in question as at 1 July 2000 was $8,155,000, as assessed by Mr Murray.
  2. The Commissioner, in his objection decision, applied the valuation agreed between the parties for the purposes of resolving the first dispute brought before the Tribunal in 2007. However, I have found that the deed of settlement terms preclude the Commissioner from applying the valuation agreed between the parties for the purposes of settling that dispute. That is because Decleah has not produced an approved valuation following settlement. As Mr Sievers submitted, the GST payable by Decleah under the margin scheme can only be calculated by reference to an approved valuation. In other words, the valuation of Mr Murray must be applied. I accept that submission.
  3. It necessarily follows that I have found Decleah had a significant tax shortfall as a consequence of lodging the BASs throughout that period based on a valuation by Mr Gibson which was not an approved valuation. Therefore, the penalty provisions in s. 284-75(1) of the Administration Act were enlivened.
  4. Although the Commissioner in his objection decision found that the base penalty rate should be 25% because Decleah’s behaviour demonstrated a lack of reasonable care, I have found that Decleah’s behaviour was reckless. Accordingly, I have found that the correct base penalty rate is 50% of the tax shortfall.
  5. In light of the findings I have made, I conclude that the objection decision made by the Commissioner on 12 November 2015, regarding the correct valuation to be used in determining GST payable under the margin scheme between 1 October 2009 and 30 June 2012, was incorrect. I set aside that decision and in substitution determine that the correct valuation for the purposes of the GST Act was $8,155,000 or, accepting there were 591 lots, $13,798.65 per lot. I also set aside the Commissioner’s decision regarding the application of shortfall penalties at the rate of 25%. Instead, I find that the correct rate of tax shortfall penalty is 50% for recklessness.
