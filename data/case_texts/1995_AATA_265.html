Last Updated: 12 February 2009



SALES TAX - Four wheel drive vehicles - Whether such vehicle is used mainly in carrying out activities in agricultural industry - Whether exempt from sales tax if used by cane inspectors employed by a sugar mill - Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act, Item 3, First Schedule.



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO QT95/72

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



Tribunal: Dr P Gerber, Deputy President

Date: 14 September 1995

Place: Brisbane



DECISION



The objection decision under review is affirmed.



( Sgd. P Gerber)

Deputy President



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO QT95/72

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



Tribunal: Dr P Gerber, Deputy President

Date: 14 September 1995

Place: Brisbane



REASONS FOR DECISION



1. The Applicant (“the taxpayer”) is a company registered for sales tax purposes as a manufacturer of sugar. The issue in dispute and the Commissioner of Taxation (“the respondent”) is whether a Mitsubishi four wheel drive vehicle with a utility body type, acquired by the taxpayer in June 1994, qualifies for sales tax exemption.



2. What precipitated the events which, ultimately, led to a reference to this Tribunal was a ruling by the Townsville branch of the respondent, dated 19 January 1993, stating that “under the legislation as it now stands, the exemption for cane inspectors’ vehicles is not available”. This ruling was in conflict with an earlier ruling, given to Coopers & Lybrand by the respondent on 14 March 1985, to the effect that such vehicles did qualify for sales tax exemption under the item 13(1) in the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act (the precursor to Schedule 1, Item 3 of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992. The earlier 1985 ruling reads as follows:



SALES TAX: CANE INSPECTORS’ VEHICLES



Reference is made to your letter of 6 March 1985. Generally speaking sugar mills fall into the following categories:



(a) Mills that have their own farms and process their own sugar cane and the cane of other farmers; and

(b) Mills that do not have their own farms but collect the sugar cane from the fields of other farmers and transport the cane via locomotives and rolling stock to the mill for processing.



In both of the above instances, cane inspectors’ vehicles, purchased by such mills, will qualify for exemption from sales tax under Item 13(1) in the First Schedule of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act provided these vehicles are of the four wheel drive type which satisfy the requirements of this item.



3. The reason advanced for the reversal of the respondent’s earlier ruling was that:



“... this Office no longer holds the view that a four wheel drive vehicle used by a sugar mill in the course of inspecting cane on growers’ properties is for use in ‘carrying out activities in agricultural industry’. The vehicle is regarded as being for use in the sugar milling industry, that is manufacturing industry.



Where a sugar mill also grows its own cane and uses a four wheel drive vehicle both in tending the crop and in carrying out cane inspection work on other farms, exemption would apply only where it could be demonstrated that the vehicle is to be used mainly in activities on its own farm. This Office is of the view that a sugar mill using a four wheel drive vehicle for both activities mentioned above would have difficulty in meeting the ‘mainly use’ criterion.”



(It should be noted in parenthesis that Item 13(1) in the First Schedule was very broad in its sweep, providing sales tax exemption for “machinery, implements or apparatus ... for use in agricultural industry and ... had to be goods of a kind, [which] in the opinion of the Commissioner of Taxation, [were] used exclusively, or primarily and principally, in agricultural industry”. In other words, before the passing of the 1992 Classification Act, it was merely the opinion of the respondent that of “the four wheel drive type” motor vehicles used by sugar mills qualified for sales tax exemption.



4. On receipt of this ruling, Coopers & Lybrand, on behalf of the Australian Sugar Milling Council Pty Ltd, wrote to the respondent on 4 June 1993, (i.e. prior to the acquisition of the vehicle now in dispute), seeking confirmation “that cane inspectors’ vehicles which are purchased for use by its various members continue to qualify for sales tax exemption”. (This letter, although referred to in various “T” documents, was not made an exhibit; another document, “T”4, although indexed, was likewise not included. Both were forwarded to me after the hearing at my request. As these document were not contentious and in possession of both parties, I did not consider it necessary to recall counsel for further argument)



5. The respondent replied to this letter on 23 July 1993:



Sales Tax: Australian Sugar Milling Council Pty Ltd



We refer to your letter of 4 June 1993.



We cannot agree that vehicles owned by sugar mills and used by cane inspectors who provide services to non-mill owned cane farms are exempt under Item 3 in the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992.



Item 3 requires that goods be used mainly in carrying out activities in agricultural industry. The term "agricultural industry" is not defined. However, "agriculture" is defined (section 3(2) of the Act):



"Agriculture" includes:



(a) viticulture, horticulture, pasturage, apiculture, poultry farming and dairy farming;



(b) other operations connected with the cultivation of the soil, the gathering in of crops and the rearing of livestock;



The purpose of paragraph (b) is not to qualify or extend the meaning of any paragraph (a) operation. Paragraph (b) acts to include, in the definition of "agriculture", an operation that the draftsman could not specify in paragraph (a), for example, cane farming. This operation may not fit within the ordinary meaning of viticulture or horticulture, but, as with those activities, it is an operation connected with the cultivation of the soil or the gathering in of crops. Thus, even though cane farming may not come within paragraph (a), it would still fall within the definition of "agriculture" as an "other operation" by virtue of paragraph (b).



Item 2 exempts goods for use by a person in "(a) a primary production activity,". By definition "primary production activity" includes "agriculture" (item 2). Paragraph (b) and (c) of item 2 cover first and second tier ancillary activities to the higher level primary production activities. The ancillary activities include such things as scheduling and controlling higher level activities and transporting goods used in carrying out higher level activities (section 10 of the Act).



Item 2 clearly distinguishes between primary production activities, such as cane farming, and activities ancillary to the main activities. However, Item 3 is more restrictive. It limits exemption to certain goods for use by a person in carrying out activities in agricultural industry. Furthermore, there is no exemption for goods used in ancillary activities.



We accept that activities such as actual harvesting of cane falls within the item 3 meaning of "in agricultural industry". But, activities such as transport and harvest control and scheduling by a cane inspector do not. They are not agricultural activities for the purpose of item 3, because the item does not cover activities that are in connection with an agricultural operation. Whereas productivity advice to farmers and provision of farm maps may, in certain cases, be ancillary activities, such activities are not covered by item 3.



Policy and past decisions



Before our DQ 27420/5 (14 March 1985), we had a longstanding policy that persons could not gain exemption under the old Item 13(1) for vehicles used in the course of supervising and advising primary producers in relation to their crops. Taxation Ruling ST(N.S.) 3001 then reviewed our interpretation of a number of provisions concerning agricultural industry. In particular, paragraph 3.5(iii) of the ruling explains that we do not consider activities such as bore sinking, fencing, professional shooting and machinery repair and maintenance undertaken by general contractors as being in agricultural industry. Cane inspectors perform functions at a similar level. Unfortunately, since ST (N.S.) 3005, some persons, such as cane inspectors, have obtained exemption for vehicles in circumstances not provided by law.



The Townsville office ruling STAX/419 of 19 January 1993 took the view that the vehicles in question were for use "in the manufacturing industry". Whilst this may be true, it may have been better to have explained, in more detail, why exemption did not apply.



Conclusion



Vehicles used by cane inspectors who carry out activities, such as scheduling and controlling on behalf of cane farmers and providing advice to the farmers do not qualify for exemption under item 3 of the First Schedule.



6. On 14 September 1994, Messrs Coopers & Lybrand sought an assessment on behalf of the taxpayer pursuant to sec 102 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 in respect of the four wheel drive vehicle now in dispute. In October 1994 the respondent issued the assessment, advising that the taxpayer was liable to pay sales tax with respect to the said vehicle in the amount of $4,350. On 5 May 1995 the taxpayer sought a review of that decision, setting out its reasons why, in its opinion, the vehicle was not subject to sales tax. The Notice of Objection sets out fully both the factual background and the relevant law, reference being made to the relevant Local Board Award 1995 (Q‘ld), the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992, the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992 and the Sugar Industry Act 1991 (Q’ld).



7. Turning to the law, paragraph (a) of Schedule 1, Item 3 of the Sales Tax (Exemption and Classification) Act 1992 specifies, inter alia, that “motor vehicles known as four wheel drive vehicles, with body type of jeep, platform, pick-up or utility” are exempt from sales tax if used “by a person mainly in carrying out activities in agricultural industry.” It was common ground that the vehicle met the description contained in paragraph (a) of Schedule 1; the dispute centers on the fact whether or not the vehicle is used by a person mainly, i.e. “to the extent of more than 50%”, in carrying out activities in agricultural industry. “Agriculture” is defined to include (a) “viticulture, horticulture, pasturage, apiculture, poultry farming and dairy farming; (b) other operations connected with the cultivation of the soil, the gathering of crops and the rearing of livestock. Item 4 in Schedule 1 states: “primary production activity” means “agriculture, forestry, fishing (including fish farming) or pearling”; “primary production business means a business carrying on primary production activities”.



8. At the hearing, Mr McGill, of Senior Counsel for the respondent, submitted that the applicant’s cane inspectors, when using the vehicle, were not “mainly” - if at all - carrying out activities in agricultural industry. Mr Morris QC, who appeared for the taxpayer, challenged this narrow construction, submitting that the vehicle was indeed used mainly in what he characterised “primary production activities”.



9. The only witness called for the taxpayer was its Chief Cane Inspector, who had come up from the ranks and was fully conversant with the nature of the duties of cane inspectors. Thus, it seems that approximately 90% of the vehicle’s use occurs during the crushing season (which generally lasts some 24 weeks). This was not challenged. I therefore have to decide whether the vehicle is used mainly in carrying out activities in agricultural industry during this period. Below is set out the relevant extract from the witness’ affidavit:



“The cane inspector is an important player in the operation of the sugar cane industry. He has an interest in both cane growing and milling operations and most of his activities are critical to both the grower and miller in order to maximise their returns. The area has 230 grower members. They expect regular visits from a cane inspector, to ensure that the cane harvest progresses smoothly and to negotiate estimate adjustments and any matters relating to their cane assignment.



The cane inspector's role has changed significantly since the advent of mechanical harvesters. Harvesting groups move rapidly from location to location, and the management of their cane bin delivery and collection with cane locomotives is now a major part of a cane inspector's daily routine. This operation in the cane harvesting sequence cannot be organised by individual growers, even though they determine the harvest rotation within their groups prior to the start of the harvesting season. The cane inspector facilitates the complex logistical problems of harvesting and cane transport. (my emphasis)



The cane inspector’s responsibilities are: Farm mapping, estimating, harvest grouping and daily allotments, cane transport and cane age, extraneous matter monitoring and control and the Prosper productivity database. These duties are discussed in detail below.



Farm Mapping



The cane inspector’s duties with respect to mapping include: Identifying and mapping new ground which is being farmed or has the potential for cane growing; organising photogrammetric control for aerial photographs. This job includes driving to cane growers properties throughout the mill district to establish photo controls.



Any changes in the boundary of an existing block are mapped each year. This ensures that block areas, estimates and varietal statistics are accurate. Farm maps derived from maps of individual cane fields establish the area of cane under production for each year. The area that can be harvested and crushed is established under the assignment system. Farm maps are the basis for on farm crop estimates.



Farm maps are used by the growers for a myriad of planning activities. One example: We often assist in layout planning for underground irrigation pipelines which can be preplanned on the farm map.



Whilst it is a requirement of the assignment system to map assigned areas, farm mapping's main purpose has developed to maintain equity between growers.



Estimating



The first estimate of the year is a major job for the field cane inspector. The work takes up to six weeks to complete. Estimating is done at the individual farm level. Each block on each farm will have an estimate of the expected crop. The block estimates are used to compile a farm estimate. This information is used by growers for financial planning and harvest rotation planning.



Farm estimates are checked once a month to determine trends. This involves the cane inspector travelling to the farm to determine which blocks have been harvested. Farm estimates are then adjusted up or down if the cane inspector believes that the trend will continue over the rest of the crop. If in doubt, a visual inspection will take place. At the completion of seventy percent of the harvest, all remaining blocks will be viewed and compared to the predicted estimate. Appropriate adjustments are made and the estimate is closely monitored until the end.



Harvest Grouping and Daily Allotment



Usually several growers will combine to form a harvesting group. There are two basic types - cooperatives and straight contractors. All groups must be formed under the guidelines of the Local Board Award.



Harvesting groups operate on a rotation system. This ensures equity among members. For instance, when the mill has 20% of the crop crushed, the group will be level with the mill and members of the group will be within close proximity to the mill percentage.



Harvest allotments are calculated by the cane inspectors: ie. Group tonnes/Mill days/average bin weight = bins/day. The cane inspectors monitor group to group equity each week and supply reports for the cane grower's benefit. Update of farm estimates flow to group estimates and are reflected in changing allotment of bins.



A complex allotment system maintains the group to group equity. The system above has been developed to maintain grower to grower equity. It is managed by the cane inspectors' department to facilitate an orderly harvest.



The maintenance of grower to grower equity ensures that no individual grower is left with unharvested cane at the end of the crushing season. This is particularly critical when the crushing finishes in wet conditions.



Cane Transport and Cane Age



The cane inspector works with the traffic officers to ensure a smooth bin delivery and collection system. This ensures that the cane is collected from the grower's siding, transported to the mill and crushed in priority according to its calculated cut to crush delay. As stated previously, cane transport is a complex logistical problem. The system operates by locomotive runs having ‘line limits’, ie a limit on the number of bins that can be delivered and collected each twenty four hours. Most cane sidings can have several cane harvesting contractors, who will require the facility to deliver harvested cane to the siding. The availability of sidings and a share of the ‘line limit’ of bins can only be coordinated by the cane inspector responsible for the loco run area. A major part of the work carried out each day requires a visit to most machines to coordinate harvester movement with siding and line limit restrictions. This ensures that harvesting progresses as smoothly and efficiently as possible. These arrangements, organised by the cane inspectors, need to be advised to the Traffic Officer who runs the locomotives. The efficient collection and transport of the cane is beneficial to both the grower and the mill in maximising the returns to the whole industry. (my emphasis)



The cane inspector’s duties include a responsibility to minimise cut to crush delays. Sugar cane is a highly perishable crop which deteriorates rapidly after it is harvested. The cane inspector’s role is to organise schedules to minimise the cut to crush delay to ensure that the grower does not suffer financially from a delay in transporting cane once it is harvested. When necessary, the cane inspector will rearrange crushing priorities to allow stale burns to be crushed first. An example of the need to rearrange crush priorities would be to cover likely losses from an accidental fire or wet weather. It is normal practice for the cane inspector to monitor the time that burnt cane remains uncut. He arranges machines to assist others to cut cane where there has been accidental fires or with high flotation equipment in wet, boggy conditions.



Extraneous Matter



The cane inspector monitors the cane coming from each harvester and determines whether the levels of extraneous matter are too high (extraneous matter includes the parts of the cane which cannot be used to make sugar, e.g. tops, leaves and dirt). If the extraneous matter levels are too high, the grower is then informed that the product is unacceptable. If the problem persists, the cane inspector will then discuss the matter with the grower. It is not in the best interest of the grower to send in cane which contains large amounts of extraneous matter because this results in a lower ccs [“commercial cane sugar”] which determines the grower's return and can also affect the stool of the ratoon crop.



Advice to harvesting contractors includes:



* Poorly maintained machines - eg blunt knives, worn extractor blades; etc.



* Base cutter too low, ie the machine is cutting under the ground and large amounts of dirt are being sent in with the cane and damaging the stool;



* Too much trash in cane;



* Not topping the cane well enough and large amount of extraneous matter being put in the cane bins.



If the cane inspector believes that the harvesting contractor is losing too much through aggressive harvesting (ie topping the cane too low or aggressive extractors), then the cane inspector would advise the contractor of his opinion and also the grower who owns the cane. This is to prevent financial loss to the cane grower.



Productivity Board Activities



The cane inspector assists the Cane Protection and Productivity Boards when they are short staffed and wish to perform an intensive research program, e.g. a program on ratoon stunting, or monitoring rat bites.



Productivity Database



A cane receival database is maintained to detail consignments of cane. Consignment notes are monitored for correct farm number, correct block number, variety of cane, etc. At the end of the harvest season, the database of information is transferred to the [local] database. This information is again tested for accuracy by the cane inspector and then made available to the Cane Productivity Board. Detailed productivity information and ranking is carried out by extension officers of that Board and sent to each grower.”



10. Is the above unchallenged evidence sufficient to conclude that the use of the vehicle during the crushing season (which is said to constitute the bulk of its usage) can be said to constitute activities “mainly in carrying out activities in agricultural industry”?

11. It is fair to say that the witness was closely cross-examined by Mr McGill, giving me an intimate awareness of the nature of the duties of the cane inspector. Thus

the witness was asked “to what extent would there be contact as a proportion based upon face-to-face contact - in other words, visiting growers by vehicle after the cane has been cut”? He replied:



It’s periodic during the season. When a harvester comes in and takes - let’s say it takes 20 per cent harvest off each individual grower - the cane inspector will have visited his contractor who’s cutting it; it may be a co-operative of growers that are cutting the cane, but he still visits the grower. He’s talking to the contractor at least every second day, I’d imagine, in relation to what’s happening on the farm - which blocks have been harvested so we can re-estimate. So we’re running a re-estimate program for the full season. We’re communicating with growers, either in the field where we’ll chase them up, or, if we can’t find them for various reasons, we do that by phone sometimes. And at the end of the season, when the harvest is finished, we then have to revisit again and spend time talking about the coming year - projections for the coming year.



Mr Morris: I may have to consider a proportion in terms of what is involved once cane has been cut and is organised into transport into the mill. Are you able to say whether this is the predominant part, or simply a part of the totality of your use of the vehicle?---Yes; it’s the dominant part.



12. On this evidence, it was strongly urged upon me by Mr Morris that a significant part of the cane inspector’s duties involve giving advice on the cultivation of soil and production and harvesting of sugar cane, which is said to constitute “other operations connected with ... the gathering in of crops” within the definition of “agriculture”. Thus Mr Morris placed considerable emphasis on the fact that cane transport is a major logistical problem, and that the cane inspector works closely with the traffic officers to ensure a smooth bin delivery and collection system, referring to the evidence that a major part of the work carried out each day requires a visit to most machines to coordinate harvester movement with siding and line limit restrictions to ensure that harvesting progresses as smoothly and efficiently as possible.



13. With all due respect to Mr Morris’ forceful submission, most persuasively put, I am nevertheless satisfied that to the extent that inspectors are involved in face-to-face contact with growers, advising on crops, harvesting and planning, as distinct from organising cane transport to the mill, farm mapping, estimating and monitoring the cane when it arrives at the mill, the former constitutes merely a small, relatively insignificant and peripheral activity of the total work commitment of the inspector’s daily duties, whereas the latter not only involves activities mainly concerned with the production of sugar by the mill, i.e. manufacture, but also constitutes the main use of the vehicle.



14. Since the sugar industry in Queensland is highly regulated, it is desirable to turn to the relevant statutory law and regulations to see whether the taxpayer can gain some comfort from any duty imposed by law on the cane inspector which may be said to involve a user of the four wheel drive in mainly carrying out activities in agricultural industry.



15. Turning to the relevant 1993 Award, published in the Queensland Government Gazette, a committee (The Group Harvesting Committee) is to be established, “consisting of three members appointed by the mill owner ... and three members appointed by the Mill Suppliers Committee, who shall meet regarding grouping of growers for harvesting and shall make recommendations to the Local Board”. The provision states, inter alia, that:



“Each group shall decide fair rotations and percentages for the harvest. Should any dispute arise, a sub-committee of the Group Harvesting Committee shall at the request of any grower in the group direct harvesting to obtain and maintain equity. The sub-committee shall consist of one member appointed by the mill owner, one member appointed by the Mill Suppliers' Committee, the Chief Cane Inspector and the Secretary of the Mill Suppliers' Committee”.



16. There is further provision to the effect that: - “The mill owner shall appoint three members of the Directorate, Manager and the Chief Cane Inspector who will oversee harvest equity between groups. The committee will ensure supply within the provisions of (i) to (vii) below ...”. Again, the Award states that there shall be a Clean Cane Committee consisting of three members appointed by the millowner and three members of the Mill Suppliers’ Committee. “Mill Cane Inspectors and B.S.E.S. [Bulk Sugar Experiment Station] Extension Officers shall be advisers to the Committee.



The objectives of the Committee will be to promote better farming and harvesting practices in order to produce cleaner delivered cane.



Whilst the Committee will endeavour to achieve this result by co-operation, the Committee has authority to:



(a) Reject cane containing unreasonable amounts of extraneous matter such as trash, tops, dead cane, grass or other foreign matter.



(b) Have rejected cane dumped and charge the grower for such costs.



(c) Suspend the harvesting of fields likely to yield contaminated cane.



(d) Suspended harvest allotments where operators make insufficient effort to produce sound billets.



17. Is a cane inspector, when transporting him or herself in the mill’s four wheel drive vehicle to the various cane farms in the exercise of his or her statutory obligations using this vehicle “mainly carrying out activities in agricultural industry”?

18. I am satisfied that the relevant statutory provisions include the carrying out activities in agricultural industry without that person being necessarily actually engaged in the industry. Thus crop dusters, agricultural experts and veterinarians who advise farmers on crops and animals, to name but a few, may well qualify for sales tax exemption if they use a motor vehicle falling within the description of the First Schedule mainly to transport themselves to the various farming properties. The fact that the vehicle itself is not used for agricultural purposes (e.g. carting cane or livestock) does not of itself, in my view, operate as a disqualification, nor the fact that these men or women may not be directly employed by the person actually engaged in the agricultural industry.



19. Where the taxpayer fails in this case is that it is unable to demonstrate that the motor vehicle is used mainly in agricultural activities. Indeed, the evidence has satisfied me that the main use of the vehicle is to carry cane inspectors to the various farm properties to ensure the smooth transfer of the cane to the mill, the management of cane bin delivery and collection by cane locomotives, a task undertaken for the efficient running of the mill rather than the grower - and the mill is not engaged in agricultural activities. I am furthermore satisfied on the evidence, as mentioned before, that to the extent that a cane inspector can be said to be engaged in activities which can be characterised as involving “agricultural industry” as defined, such activity is minor, and falls far short of the statutory requirement of more than the 50 per cent necessary for this application to succeed. If I were compelled to characterise just what a sugar mill does in converting cane to sugar, it comes close to “applying a treatment to foodstuffs as a process in preparing them for human consumption”, thus falling within the definition of “manufacture” (cf sec 5 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992).



20. Mr McGill, in a useful analysis of the case law, referred me to G Gramp & Sons v FCT [1965] HCA 53; (1965) 115 CLR 170, a case which, albeit involving different legislation, I consider nevertheless relevant to the issue I have to decide. The appellant in that case carried on an extensive business of wine-making. Although it used some grapes from its own vineyard, the bulk of the grapes used for making wine came from independent growers. One of the appellant’s properties consisted of some 750 acres, all of which - apart from the winery, which occupied some nine acres - was used for agricultural purposes, mainly as a vineyard. During the year in question, the appellant made certain structural additions and alterations to the winery and claimed that these should be regarded as “structural improvements situated on land used during the year of income for the purposes of agricultural or pastoral pursuits” within the meaning of sec 57AA of the Tax Act. It was argued on behalf of the appellant that wine-making was an agricultural pursuit in that it involves the preparation and treating a product of the soil; a fortiori where the wine-making plant is situated on the same parcel of land as the vineyard from which some of the produce is used, and where the whole of the area is owned by the vigneron. The respondent, on the other hand, submitted that whilst the production of grapes is an agricultural pursuit, their subsequent treatment in order to make wine is not. The latter argument succeeded, Windeyer J holding that “... making wine is a distinct industry in Australia. It is not now, if perhaps once it was, ordinarily an incidental part of viticulture”. His Honour referred to The Producers’ Co-operative Distributing Society Ltd v C of T (NSW) [1944] HCA 39; (1944) 69 CLR 523, where (at p 530), Latham CJ stated: “Today, the making of butter has become factory process, separated from the farm”. On appeal, the Privy Council noted:



“There are two industries, the farming industry and the butter-making industry. The industries are indeed closely related, but they are independent and the product of the latter industry is not in a real sense the product of the former industry” [1947] UKPCHCA 2; (1947) 75 CLR 134, at p 139.



21. Applying the legal analysis of the learned judge in that case to the instant facts, I am satisfied that when considering whether this four wheel vehicle is used by cane inspectors mainly in carrying out activities in agricultural industry - having regard to the total enterprise, from planting and growing cane, transporting it to the mill and producing sugar as the end product - I am satisfied that the vehicle in dispute is used mainly for purposes of the mill, which is engaged in the manufacture of sugar, and thus cannot be regarded as being mainly used in agricultural industry. Just as cows give milk and factories turn it into butter, so farmers grow cane and mills turn it into sugar - and that “the latter industry is not in a real sense the product of the former industry.”



22. It is with no disrespect to Mr McGill that I find it unnecessary to deal with the many cases he cited dealing with diesel fuel rebates where vehicles are engaged in the carriage of fertiliser to farms or grain to mills or silos, etc, and pose the question whether such operations can be said to be connected with the gathering-in of crops. Nor am I persuaded of the relevance of a copy letter from the Hon. Chris Schacht, Minister for Small Business, Customs and Construction to a Mr D, dated 22 August 1995, which states, inter alia, that: “I am advised that the Australian Customs Service has, since 1987, accepted that the gathering in of sugar does not cease until the cane arrives at the sugar mill, being the place of first and safe storage. Diesel fuel rebate will continue to be paid under paragraph (b) of the definition of ‘agriculture’ which refers to ‘the cultivation or gathering in of crops’”.



23. If diesel fuel rebate is payable on diesel fuel used to transport sugar cane from the field to the mill, so be it. I am not persuaded that it follows that four wheel drive vehicles used by cane inspectors are exempt from sales tax on a finding that they are not mainly “carrying out activities in agricultural industry”. To the extent that their duties are claimed to involve a substantial commitment to the gathering in of cane in an attempt to bring their duties within the four corners of the definition of “agriculture”, I have not been persuaded by the evidence that this commitment involves more than 50% of their overall duties.



24. Mr McGill’s final submission that the vehicle is, in any event, disqualified from sales tax exemption because Item 3 “contemplates the actual physical use of the vehicle in some way, rather than simply as a means of personal transport”, an argument based on the proposition that the statutory requirement demands that the various vehicles set out in the schedule must conform to certain specifications and/or adaptations before being eligible for sales tax exemption is, with respect, more enterprising than persuasive..

25. For the above reasons, the decision under review is affirmed.
