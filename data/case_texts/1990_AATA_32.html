Taxation - International Agreement with the United States of America - Article 17 Entertainers - payment by promoters of international and domestic airfares and hotel accommodation - whether reimbursed to applicant or borne on his behalf



Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953 Schedule 2, Article 17



In re Ross; Ex parte AG for Northern Territory [1980] HCA 2; (1980) 54 ALJR 145

REASONS FOR DECISION



16 February 1990 Deputy President R K Todd



1. This application for review is one of eight related matters before the Tribunal. It was agreed that this particular matter be heard and that the decision given in respect of it would administratively be determinative of the others. In two of the other seven cases there is a slight factual difference, but having regard to the conclusion to which I have come that will be of no moment.



2. In the year of income in question a United Kingdom entertainer, ("A"), came to Australia. With him on the tour came eight musicians, of whom the present applicant was one, from the United States of America. There was an Australian corporate promoter ("the ACP") of the tour, and it paid a United Kingdom company ("the UKC") a fee for the tour. The ACP paid the costs of the tour, including hotel accommodation, for the artists, their air fares within Australia, and certain other tour costs. The UKC paid what was called a salary to A; a salary to each of the eight musicians, and the air fares of A and the musicians to Australia and return. The musicians also received, apart from their salaries, some daily fees known as "per diems". The only expenses which the musicians were required to meet were hotel "extras", often called "incidentals". Diagram Omitted.



3. There was no written contract between the various parties or any of them, but there was no dispute that the arrangements set out above were those agreed between the parties and that they were consistent with standard agreements in the international music industry in the kind of situation which I have described. Evidence was given without demur of a similar agreement, so far as the musicians were concerned, that had been entered into three years previously. Even without that evidence, however, I would be satisfied, as I am, of the contractual arrangements that were entered into.



4. The problem in the present case arises because of the provisions of the Convention Between the Government of Australia and the Government of the United States of America For the Avoidance of Double Taxation and the Prevention of Fiscal Evasion with respect to Taxes on Income ("the Convention"). The Convention is Schedule 2 to the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953.



5. Article 17 of the Convention provides as follows:



"(1) Notwithstanding the provisions of Articles 14 (Independent Personal Services) and 15 (Dependent Personal Services), income derived by entertainers (such as theatrical, motion picture, radio or television artistes, musicians and athletes) from their personal activities as such may be taxed in the Contracting State in which these activities are exercised, except where the amount of the gross receipts derived by any such entertainer, including expenses reimbursed to him or borne on his behalf, from such activities does not exceed ten thousand United States dollars ($10,000) or its equivalent in Australian dollars for the taxable year or year of income concerned." (underlining supplied)



6. The facts in the present case are such that the applicant must be held to have derived gross receipts in excess of US$10,000, or its equivalent in Australian dollars, for the year of income concerned if there is taken into account any sums received over and above their salary and "per diems". In the case of the applicant (and in fact in the case of five others of the musicians) any one of the items of international air fares, domestic air fares or hotel accommodation would, when added to the items of salary and "per diems", take him and them through the US$10,000 doorway to assessability. In the case of the remaining two, one or other of the items of domestic air fares or hotel accommodation would have to be brought in to have that result, as they did not receive "per diems".



7. The questions in the case of the present applicant are whether the items in question were -



(i) "reimbursed to" the applicant, or

(ii) "borne on his behalf",

within the meaning of Article 17 of the Convention.



8. The question whether expenses had been reimbursed within the meaning of Article 17 was raised by the Commissioner's representative, but I see no basis upon which it could be found that expenses were reimbursed. For that to happen the applicant would have had to have himself paid the air fares or hotel accommodation in the first place. On the facts this was plainly not the case. I say no more about this point.



9. The question whether the air fares and/or hotel accommodation costs were "borne on (the applicant's) behalf" is more difficult, but not, in my opinion, unduly so. The applicant had an obligation to the UKC to perform at various concerts. For so performing he was entitled to be paid a salary and "per diems". In order to discharge the obligations that entitled him to be paid salary and per diems he had to be present at the concerts. To do that he had to travel to Australia and within Australia, and to be accommodated in Australia as he travelled around. That accommodation was arranged by the ACP, and so also was the domestic air travel. Likewise, the UKC arranged and paid for the applicant's air fares to and from Australia. The liability of the ACP and of the UKC was to discharge the costs relating to those items, and the sums so paid were paid in that behalf. Those sums were not the responsibility of the applicant. He was not under any obligation to pay them. Neither the public servant, nor even the statutory office holder, can surely be regarded as having had the cost of car transport provided by the Commonwealth to the airport, when he or she goes forth on circuit, paid for on his or her behalf. It is paid by the government, which requires the public servant to serve, and the statutory office holder to perform the duties of his or her office, in some place away from home base. It is paid on behalf of the government. In the same way the employer and the promoter, although for a finite period, undertook the responsibility of covering the travel and accommodation costs of the musician. The payments made in discharge of these responsibilities were their disbursements. They did not pay them because the musicians had failed to do so, but rather because they themselves had agreed to be liable therefor and thus to do so. They paid them on their own behalf.



10. For the Commissioner it was contended that the items in question were provided to the applicant pursuant to contracts for personal services. The situation was said to be comparable with that in which a sporting body contracts a person to play for it and agrees to pay his or her medical expenses in respect of sporting injuries. When the injury occurs and the club pays the doctor, it was said to be clear that if the sporting body were unable to pay the doctor the individual in question would remain responsible to discharge the debt to the doctor. He has had the benefit of the treatment of his broken leg, and would be responsible for the personal services carried out "on his behalf". That may well be the case, but the analogy is not perfect. In the case supposed, the sportsperson has to have his broken leg set in plaster, not just so that he can play sport but also so that he can live his ordinary life. The musicians here had no need to travel to and from, and in Australia, or to stay at hotels here, other than for the purpose of discharging their contracts to perform at concerts. The ACP and the UKC having contracted that the items in question were to be their legal responsibility, when they made the necessary payments they made them on their own behalf, in discharge of their own obligation.



11. It may be that if the accommodation had not been paid for by the ACP the hotels would have been able to recover from the musicians. But that would have been on a quantum meruit, and the situation is in any event unrelated to the present problem. That is because the problem is one in which it has to be presupposed that the amounts in question have in fact been borne by the ACP and the UKC.



12. The Commissioner's representative referred to the following passage in the judgment of the majority of the High Court in In re Ross; Ex parte AG for Northern Territory [1980] HCA 2; (1980) 54 ALJR 145 at 149:



"The phrase 'on behalf of' is, as Latham C J observed in The King v Portus: Ex parte Federated Clerks' Union of Australia [1949] HCA 53; (1949), 79 CLR 428, at p435, 'not an expression which has a strict legal meaning', it bears no single and constant significance. Instead it may be used in conjunction with a wide range of relationships, all however in some way concerned with the standing of one person as auxiliary to or representative of another person or thing.



In what is perhaps its least specific use, 'on behalf of' may be applied to someone who does no more than express support for persons or for a cause, as with one who speaks on behalf of the poor or on behalf of tolerance. It may be used when speaking of an agency relationship, but also of some quite ephemeral relationships, such as that which exists between a party to litigation and the witness he calls, a witness 'on behalf of' the defence. Again, it may, as the Northern Territory here contends, be used where the relationship is that of trustee and cestui que trust. It was of such a use that Lord Cairns LC spoke when he said, in Gillespie v City of Glasgow Bank, (1879), 4 App Cas 632, at p640 that the phrase could describe a relationship of trustee and cestui que trust 'if the circumstances of the case are consistent with that interpretation'. Context will always determine to which of the many possible relationships the phrase 'on behalf of' is in a particular case being applied: 'the context and subject matter' (per Dixon J in the Federated Clerks' Case at p438) will be determinative."



13. This passage was relied upon in support of a contention that the expression "on behalf of" should be construed widely. So no doubt it may be, but in my opinion it has in the present context a clear meaning that leads to the conclusion which I have expressed.



14. It was also submitted for the Commissioner that the Commonwealth of Australia and the United States of America as contracting parties had in mind a provision which, fastening on gross receipts, would "bring about a desirable administrative efficiency in the task of determining whether or not the visiting performer did have a tax liability, so that ... the taxation administration could make a quick and simple decision as to whether tax liability would arise". The rigours in this respect of attendance by ATO officers at golf courses, rock concerts and so on were referred to and are noted. I can however find nothing in this argument that is of assistance in deciding the meaning of the expression "on behalf of".



15. In my opinion the applicant is not caught by the provisions of Article 17. It follows that the applicant should not be found to have derived gross receipts exceeding US$10,000 or its equivalent in Australian dollars. The objection decision under review should be set aside and the matter remitted to the Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the objection be allowed in full.
