Strictly Stainless Pty Limited v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 534 (5 November 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

STRICTLY STAINLESS PTY LIMITED v. DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. NG 668 of 1993

FED No. 855/93

Number of pages - 5

Administrative Law

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

DAVIES J

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Law - Judicial Review - winding-up proceedings instituted by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation in the Supreme Court of New South Wales - offer of settlement arrangement refused - whether these steps are reviewable decisions under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review Act 1977 (Cth) - extent to which the institution of proceedings may be challenged by judicial review - public policy.

Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 (Cth)

Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) - s.39B

Newby v. Moodie (1988) 83 ALR 523

Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321

Smiles v. Commissioner of Taxation [1992] FCA 208; (1992) 35 FCR 405

General Newspapers Pty Limited v. Telstra Corporation, Davies,
Gummow and Einfeld JJ (unreported 22 September 1993)

Director of Public Prosecutions v. Humphrys (1977) AC 1

HEARING

SYDNEY, 5 November 1993

5:11:1993

Counsel for the applicant : T.S. Hale

Solicitors for the applicant : Rosier Associates

Counsel for the respondents : A. Robertson, N. Abadee

Solicitor for the respondents : Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The application be dismissed.

2. The applicant pay the respondent's costs.

Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

DAVIES J

The applicant, Strictly Stainless Pty Limited ("Strictly Stainless"), is indebted to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation in a sum which has not been quantified but appears to be in the vicinity of $120,000 to $130,000 arising from the non-payment of group tax during 1991,1992 and early 1993. There may also be some small amount of income tax due.

2. The Deputy Commissioner served a notice under the then s.460 of the Corporations Law on Strictly Stainless on 15 February of this year and subsequently, on 21 May, issued a summons in the Supreme Court of New South Wales seeking an order for the winding-up of Strictly Stainless.

3. These proceedings in the Federal Court were instituted on 27 August I need not decide whether they were instituted within a reasonable time as required by section 11 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 (Cth) ("the ADJR Act"). As to which see Stergis v. Boucher (1989)86 ALR 174.

4. The application seeks orders of review under the ADJR Act in respect of steps which are described as the decision of the Deputy Commissioner to commence the proceedings in the Supreme Court and the decision of the Deputy Commissioner to reject an arrangement to settle the proceedings in the Supreme Court and not consider any further proposals.

5. The first alleged decision is said to be a decision under s.209 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) and s.462 of the Corporations Law, and I take it that the second alleged decision is said also to have been made under those enactments.

6. As to the second alleged decision, I should comment that the facts do not seem to me to show that the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation has rejected entirely the consideration of any compromise. He has simply rejected the offers that Strictly Stainless has made up to this point of time.

7. The grounds relied upon by counsel for Strictly Stainless are that the decisions were manifestly unreasonable, that there was an exercise of discretion by reference to a policy or guideline without having regard to the merits of the particular case, and that procedural fairness was not provided in that Strictly Stainless was not given sufficient opportunity to explain its affairs.

8. In my opinion, the alleged decisions were not decisions for the purposes of the ADJR Act. It is true that, in Newby v. Moodie (1988) 83 ALR 523, it was held that a decision taken by the Director of Public Prosecutions to prosecute was a decision to which the ADJR Act applied, being a decision of an administrative character taken under the Director of Public Prosecutions Act 1983 (Cth). However, as I have already remarked in Smiles v. Commissioner of Taxation [1992] FCA 208; (1992) 35 FCR 405 at 407, I think that that authority cannot stand in the light of the subsequent decision of the High Court of Australia in Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321.

9. In the civil field, there have been decisions which have adopted a like view of the nature of a decision to that taken in Newby v. Moodie. In each of Australian Capital Territory Health Authority v. Berkeley Cleaning Group Pry Limited (1985) 7 FCR 575 and James Richardson Corporation Pry Limited v. Federal Airports Corporation (unreported, delivered 29 September 1992), it was held that while a certain contract was not a decision for the purposes of the ADJR Act, the resolution to enter into the contract was a decision which was reviewable under the ADJR Act. Those decisions proceeded upon similar reasoning to that which was applied in Newby v. Moodie. But, in General Newspapers Pty Limited v. Telstra Corporation, which was handed down on 22 September 1993, Einfeld J and I expressed the view that those decisions should not be followed having regard to what was said by the High Court in Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond. Our views in that respect had the support of Gummow J who was the third member of the court.

10. In Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond, the Chief Justice, Sir Anthony Mason, discussed the nature of a decision for the purposes of the ADJR Act. His Honour's remarks found agreement with the other members of the Court, Brennan Dean, Toohey and Gaudron JJ. At p.337 his Honour said:

"A reviewable 'decision' is one for which provision is made by

or under a statute. It will generally, but not always, entail a

decision which is final or operative and determinative, at least

in a practical sense, of the issue of fact falling for

consideration. A conclusion reached as a step along the way in a

course of reasoning leading to an ultimate decision would not

ordinarily amount to a reviewable decision unless the statute

prodded for the making of a finding or ruling on that point so

that the decision, though an intermediate decision, might

accurately be described as a decision under an enactment."

His Honour went on to observe further that:

"Another essential quality of a reviewable decision is that it be

a substantive determination."

11. In my view there is nothing in the nature of an ultimate or operative determination, or of a substantive determination, in the step of issuing a summons, which is the first procedural step in the course of proceedings in a court. And there is nothing ultimate or determinative or substantive in the carrying on of negotiations for settlement.

12. It seems to me that the reasoning of Mason CJ, which is now the law on the subject, is inconsistent with authorities such as Newby v. Moodie and the Berkeley Cleaning case.

13. That is not to say that there can be no part for judicial review in relation to the institution of proceedings. I would accept that, if there were exceptional and appropriate circumstances, relief under section 39B of the Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) could be granted. If for example, it were found that the taking of proceedings in a court was beyond the power of the person who took the proceedings, or that the proceedings were instituted for an improper purpose such as, for example, a private fraud, there may well be a place for the granting of an injunction or the making of a declaration under s.39B. Indeed, cases in which it has been sought to raise aspects of improper purpose have recently been removed to the High Court for its consideration as to whether or not the allegations constituted an arguable issue.

14. But the present is not such a case. There is no allegation of improper purpose. Principally, it is alleged that it was unreasonable for the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation to take the proceedings in the Supreme Court. It seems to me that that allegation does not provide a ground on which this Court would interfere, particularly as it is an important principle of public policy that a person should be entitled to approach a court and have his case determined according to law whether or not the institution of the proceedings be reasonable. A court must stand apart from the institution of the proceedings. A court must listen to people who wish to make a claim, whether the claim. is made reasonably or otherwise. Some litigants bring claims in circumstances which the courts from time think to be very harsh. But if a person brings a claim, and is entitled to relief, then the court will grant relief. I am not sure that this point has been stated as a matter of principle in relation to civil proceedings, but it is was certainly considered in cases concerning the institution of criminal prosecutions. Thus, in Director of Public Prosecutions v. Humphrys (1977) AC 1, Viscount Dilhorne said at 26:-

"A judge must keep out of the arena. He should not have or appear

to have any responsibility for the institution of a prosecution.

'The function of prosecutors and judges must not be blurted. If a

judge has power to decline to hear a case because he does not

think it should be brought, then it soon may be thought that the

cases he allows to proceed are cases brought with his consent or

approval."

His Lordship added:-

"If there is the power ... to stop a prosecution on indictment in

limine, it is in my view a power that should only be exercised in

the most exceptional circumstances."

15. Those observations dealt with a prosecution, but one can see the point of public policy involved. It seems to me to be quite inappropriate for this Court to consider whether or not it was reasonable for the Deputy Commissioner to institute wind-up proceedings in the Supreme Court. This Court should stand right away from the question and allow the Supreme Court to deal with the matter in the ordinary way.

16. I do not think it would be useful for me to make any comment on the facts of this present case. Indeed, I think it would be inappropriate for me to do so. The facts are for the Supreme Court to determine. If the Supreme Court wishes to consider any aspect of the matters which counsel has raised in this present case, that will be a matter for the Supreme Court. I ought not to express a view one way or the other.

17. Therefore, on the simple ground that the decisions sought to be reviewed are not reviewable decisions under the ADJR Act or, indeed, reviewable either under that Act or under s.39B of the Judiciary Act on any ground now put to this Court, I shall allow the objection to competency and order that the application be dismissed with costs.
