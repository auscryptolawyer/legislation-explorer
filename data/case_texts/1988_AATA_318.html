Re Mark Hastings Rohde and Commissioner of Superannuation [1988] AATA 318 (4 November 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: MARK HASTINGS ROHDE

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. S87/66

AAT No. 4723

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.A. Layton(1) (Deputy President), B.C. Lock(2) (Member) and Dr D.B. Williams(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - issue of Benefit Classification Certificate - anxiety state - whether symptoms of anxiety sufficient to find anxiety state - whether symptoms alone prevent medical standard being met - meaning of "not a real risk" - factors: degree of risk and personal characteristics - time at which risk assessed.

Words & Phrases: "not a real risk"

Superannuation Act 1976, paras.16(8)(a) and (c)

Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (No. 80 of 1986)

McMullen v Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 61 ALR 189

Re R.M. and Commissioner for Superannuation (1984) 6 ALD 272

Re Rowlands and Commissioner for Superannuation, A.87/30, delivered 12 August 1988 (Decision 4555)

Re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation S.87/163 delivered 4 November 1988

Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217

HEARING

CANBERRA

4:11:1988

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and in lieu thereof, a decision is substituted that the Benefit Classification Certificate issued by the Commissioner for Superannuation in respect of the applicant be revoked.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 10 May 1984 refusing to revoke a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") in respect of the condition of "anxiety state" pursuant to para. 16(8)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). This decision was confirmed by a delegate of the respondent on 7 April 1986.

2. The issue in this review is whether or not the applicant had suffered or is suffering from an "anxiety state" such that we are satisfied that there is not a real risk that he will not continue to be an eligible employee until his maximum retiring age (65 years) by virtue of that condition.

3. At the hearing, the applicant represented himself with the assistance of a union representative, Mr Thomas, and the respondent was represented by Mr Eadie. The documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the "T documents") were incorporated as part of the information before the Tribunal and further exhibits were tendered during the course of the hearing. At the hearing, the applicant gave oral evidence and called Dr Mills, a specialist in occupational medicine and a psychologist, Mr Tomlian. The respondent called Dr Barson, a general medical practitioner and Dr Burvill, a psychiatrist. The Tribunal of its own motion subpoenaed Dr Goldney, a psychiatrist, to give oral evidence, the circumstances of which are set out below.

4. The respondent submitted, and the Tribunal concurs, that the relevant legislation to be applied for the purpose of this hearing is the Superannuation Act 1976 as amended by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (No. 80 of 1986). The amendments effected by that Act were made retrospective to 1 July 1976 and therefore pre-date the decision under review, being the delegate's decision of 10 May 1984. It is not necessary for the purposes of this review to consider the amendments made by the subsequent Act No. 151 of 1986.

5. The background facts to this application are as follows. The applicant was born in South Australia on 24 August 1956 and is now 32 years of age. He matriculated in 1973 and the following year, commenced a Bachelor of Arts degree at Flinders University as a full-time student. The applicant described his university life as very hectic. He was under pressure to submit essays on time, he lived a varied lifestyle with a variable sleep pattern because he preferred to work at night, and at times he neglected his diet. Quite apart from study commitments, he was involved in various extra curricular student activities as well as joining the Citizens' Military Forces. The applicant described himself as a "sincere and committed person" at that time (p.22 of the transcript) but did not consider he was suffering from any "anxiety" during that period. This self- assessment is supported by the medical report of Dr Thomas from the Flinders Clinic dated 9 May 1988 who expressed the view that over the period September 1976 until August 1978, the applicant had not suffered from "anxiety state" (T1/66).

6. At the beginning of 1977, the applicant began working as a messenger for Australia Post. He was also employed for a short period in the workshop at the Phoenix Society and on shift work at the Port Stanvac Refinery.

7. On 2 May 1977, the applicant first became an "eligible employee" upon joining Telecom Australia as a clerk. That work involved processing of applications for telephone connections. He began as a Clerk Class 1 and then acted as a Class 2/3. The applicant gave evidence that no facet of the work caused him stress, although he "disliked the atmosphere and attitudes of the clerks milieu" (transcript, p 28).

8. On 2 June 1977, the applicant underwent a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr D.L. Davies. This examination was for the purpose of deciding whether the applicant should be accepted into the Superannuation Fund and to determine whether a BCC should be issued.

9. In completing the Statement of Personal Medical History, the applicant in answer to questions set down in Part B (T9/30) as to whether he was "now suffering" or had "ever suffered" from anxiety state, depression or difficulty in sleeping, migraine, recurrent pain in the chest or nasal antrum or sinus trouble, answered "yes" in relation to each of those conditions. So far as the anxiety state and depression or difficulty in sleeping were concerned, the applicant in Part C gave further details, namely "due to pressures, instabilities etc. of full-time university life" and in relation to migraine, gave the detail "haven't had one for years, never severe, stress-related probably also related to diet". It is not relevant to refer to the responses given in answer to questions about recurrent pain in the chest, nasal antrum or sinus trouble.

10. Dr Davies in making comments with regard to the examination stated as follows:

"Rather anxious expression. I think he lacks

confidence in himself and I get the impression that he

may be a "Stirrer". On several student committees at

Flinders during three years there. Is doing last 2 or

possibly last 3 Topics part-time this year having

withdrawn from them last year. He was rather evasive

about this matter. I consider he is an anxious

inadequate personality."

(T9/32)

Following his examination, Dr Davies recommended deferral for eleven months to assess performance of the applicant's work.

11. A delegate of the Commissioner, on the basis of the medical examination and report, issued a BCC stating "History of anxiety and depression". The applicant did not agree with that Certificate but chose not to challenge it at the time.

12. During 1977-78, the applicant attended at Flinders Clinic and the reasons for his attendance are set out in Dr Thomas' report as follows:

"13/4/77 U.R.T.I. Sudafed.

21/4/77 Dorsal strain. Indocid.

28/6/77 Herpes genitalis. Vibramycin.

5/7/77 Sore eyes. Sore knees.

3/8/77 Viral gastritis. Stemetil.

19/8/77 Pharyngitis. Pensig.

1/11/77 Malaise - viral. Catovit.

15/2/78 Malaise 2 weeks, enlarged lymph nodes neck

and axillae. Blood test showed glandular

fever.

From 1/3/78 Mr. Rohde saw me in consultation on 11

occasions up to 26/7/78. During this period he went

through the typical, long drawn out, slowly regressing

symptoms of glandular fever mainly lethargy and

tiredness. By 12/5/78 the blood test had returned to

normal."

(T21/66)

13. This information was not challenged by either party and the applicant stated that looking in retrospect, he was probably suffering from glandular fever late in 1977 and in his view he still had symptoms of that condition throughout 1978.

14. On 3 May 1979, the applicant resigned from Telecom Australia as a result of which the BCC ceased to have effect as he was no longer an eligible employee. His reasons for resignation were that he "... did not feel very healthy and working in a sedentary job seemed to (him) was not a very good idea ... and for (his) own health it was better to get something more physical and get outside" (transcript, p 29-30). In addition, he expressed the view that "I was not interested in the job. Originally it was just seen as a stop-gap and subsequently, as I was saying before, I found the millieu of clerks rather boring and not very stimulating .... More interesting things to do basically" (transcript, p 31).

15. We note at this stage in the chronological sequence that on 20 February 1979 and 7 March 1979, the applicant presented at his local medical practitioner, Dr Barson, with diarrhoea and we will refer in greater detail to those consultations later in these Reasons for Decision.

16. After the applicant resigned from Telecom Australia he endeavoured to find employment in a wide range of activities mostly of a labouring type. In addition, he became politically active in various organisations such as the Port Adelaide Unemployed People's Union, the Adelaide Unemployed Workers' Union and the Campaign Against Nuclear Energy. He did not obtain employment until in June 1980, when he undertook a test with Australia Post for what he believed to be a messenger/delivery job. He was successful in his test, however, and he was employed as a mail sorter initially on permanent late shift. Later, he was restored on a rotating shift from day to afternoon shift. The applicant again became an eligible employee on 4 August 1980.

17. Also early in 1980 he enrolled at Flinders University to complete his Arts degree which he had suspended in 1977. He initially enrolled for two subjects but found that his university studies conflicted with the training he was required to do as a mail sorter. As a result, he withdrew from one subject but retained the other and, as he explained at pp 37-38 of the transcript:

"I had to seek an extension because the training with

the Australia Post interfered with attending lectures

and writing essays, but I completed them which is

possibly when I took a few extra days off at that

time. I simply was burning the candle at both ends."

This description would appear to be consistent with a con sultation that he had with Dr Barson on 7 July 1980, the details of which we will refer to later in these Reasons for Decision.

18. On 6 October 1980, the applicant underwent a further medical examination with Mr Dhillon, a Commonwealth Medical Officer. The applicant completed Part B of the Medical History form at T11/37 in the following way. In answer to whether he was now suffering or had ever suffered from any of the following disabilities - mental or nervous conditions, anxiety state, the applicant answered "no" and in answer to whether or not he had ever suffered or was now suffering from depression or difficulty in sleeping, migraine and hay fever or allergic rhinitis, the applicant answered "yes". In explanation of those answers, the applicant stated in relation to depression or difficulty sleeping that it was "due to irregular lifestyle when unemployed (79-80) and when engaged in full-time study (74-6)" (T11/38). It is not relevant to consider the comments made with respect to migraine, hay fever or allergic rhinitis.

19. Upon examination, Mr Dhillon made the following comments with regard to the condition of "depression or difficulty in sleeping":

"States anxiety/depression as a result of glandular

fever in 1978. Earlier as a result of unemployment

and full-time studies at Flinders University. CMO

conclusions in 1977 - Anxious Inadequate personality.

Appears to be a person easily affected by stress."

(T11/38)

On the basis of these observations, Mr Dhillon took the view that the applicant did not meet the general medical standard as he was suffering from "anxiety state", but was fit nonetheless for employment. Accepting Mr Dhillon's report, the delegate to the Commissioner a year later on 27 October 1981 issued a BCC on the basis of a condition of "anxiety state". It is this certificate which is the subject of appeal.

20. During this time, the applicant completed the last of the requisite units to complete his studies and obtained his Arts degree at the end of 1981. In the following year, the applicant became involved with the Australian Postal and Telecommunications Union.

21. The applicant's assessment as to how he was coping with his employment at this time was expressed in the following terms:

"The work gets a bit boring and monotonous at times

but I find it very easy work to cope with. The social

situation there is quite stimulating. One can have

debates about philosophy and religion and politics and

whatever, music, whatever and there are all sorts of

different people at all sorts of different levels and

it is quite stimulating, the social side of it. The

work side of it you just go on with it."

(p.33 of transcript)

22. On 24 May 1982, the applicant presented at Dr Barson's rooms with a wart on his foot. This was treated and he was given a certificate for two days. Further detail of this consultation is referred to hereafter.

23. On 14 July 1982, the applicant again presented at the consulting rooms of Dr Barson but this time saw a locum, Dr Martha Kent. The records indicate that on that occasion, the applicant complained of:

" '...anxieties, will not discuss how or why. Works

as union rep, stressful, hedges about domestic

relationship. Lives in shared house with two women

and two children, incompatible. Wants a certificate

because could not sleep last night for anxiety.' I

gather that Dr Kent felt that he was not prepared to

discuss the details. She has made an entry here,

'Will not divulge any more details'. This would be

the first time he met Dr Kent."

(p 83 of transcript)

The applicant was given a certificate for incapacity for work for one day.

24. At the hearing, the applicant gave some explanatory details as to his personal situation during the period of these two consultations (p 37 of the transcript) when he stated:

"I was living with two single parents, two single

mothers. They had just split up with their relationships,

so I was subject to a lot of pressure with

them, taking out on me things that had gone wrong in

their relationships, because I was the only male handy

to take things out on. At that time there was also, I

believe - I was very busy with union work. As a

result of that I got rather tired and quite a bit 'run

down', and I think I saw a doctor once or twice and

asked for a day to rest."

25. In 1983 the applicant became the union representative in his workplace and, in addition, he subsequently became the workplace representative on the State Executive of his union. When asked as to whether he felt any stress or pressure as a result of his involvement in this union work, the applicant replied that the workplace representative is:

"... one of the most stressful jobs in the mail

exchange and recognized as such by most of the

supervisory and other staff in general. It involves a

lot of conflict, a lot of dealing with other people's

problems and other people's tempers and whatever. It

is fairly stressful. However, I believe that I coped

with that fairly well and operate very well under

fire."

(transcript, p 34-35)

Prior to his appointment as union representative, the applicant took annual leave and then leave without pay in order to travel around Australia and overseas, returning in July 1983. Whilst the applicant was overseas, he sustained an injury to his left knee and complications arose from this injury resulting in him taking time off work from 15 August 1983 until 26 August 1983.

26. On 24 February 1984, the applicant underwent a further medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr Verco. The purpose of that examination was to review the BCC as a result of the applicant's request that it be revoked. The applicant, in answer to questions in Section 4 of the Medical Report Form (T15/50) as to whether he was now suffering or had ever suffered from the conditions of: nervous fatigue or feelings of weakness or tiredness, and nasal antrum or sinus trouble, answered "yes". The applicant stated in explanation of the first condition that those symptoms of fatigue and tiredness were experienced in 1978 when he was diagnosed as having glandular fever. In relation to whether or not he was now suffering or had ever suffered from the condition of anxiety state, depression or difficulty in sleeping, mental or nervous condition, the applicant answered "no". Dr Verco formed the opinion that the applicant was not likely to continue to be an eligible employee until attaining the relevant maximum retiring age on the basis of "anxiety".

27. On 10 May 1984, a delegate of the Commissioner, on the basis of Dr Verco's medical report, refused to revoke the applicant's BCC. The applicant requested a review of the delegate's decision pursuant to sub-s.154(2) of the Act on 30 May 1984.

28. In mid 1984, the applicant provided various certificates relating to a strained trapezius muscle and/or pain in the shoulder resulting in various absences from work, mainly of short duration.

29. In July 1985, the applicant provided certificates for incapacity for work in relation to pain in his hands, wrists and elbows which seems to be related to a repetitive strain injury. This involved various weeks off from work during July and August 1985 and thereafter mainly short hourly or sometimes daily intervals of time away from the workplace.

30. On 22 August 1985, the applicant was sent by the respondent to see a psychiatrist, Dr Burvill. Dr Burvill in his report of 2 October 1985, provided the following summary:

"In conclusion, on the history given to me by

Mr. Rohde, and by my observations of him, I have no

evidence to support the diagnosis of anxiety state

currently, and the only events in the past appears to

have been Mr. Rohde's own indication that he suffered

from anxiety, as given when he filled out his initial

application for permanent employment. At that time he

undoubtedly was having some stress in his life in

coping with employment, university studies, and the

prolonged bout of glandular fever. His explanation of

his reasons for replying in the affirmative regarding

anxiety seem acceptable now. Without any further

evidence to the contrary, I would recommend that the

certificate of benefit classification be revoked."

(T25/101)

On the basis of this document, an Acting Senior Medical Officer, Dr Pamnany, recommended that the applicant's BCC be revoked (T27/103). Subsequently, the respondent wrote to Dr Burvill in a letter dated 10 December 1985 providing him with the following additional information:

"Dr M Barson, Mr Rohde's general practitioner, stated

in a report dated 16 Jan 1985, that in February 1979

Mr Rohde "was working hard and under considerable

stress". Dr Barson also commented that on 24 May 1982

"he was 'not coping too well' with a number of

stresses ... Six weeks later he saw my locum ... who

commented that he was complaining of anxiety ..."

In addition to Dr Barson's report, advice has been

received from Australia Post in relation to Mr Rohde's

sick leave record. Although none of Mr Rohde's

absences from work have been stated as "Anxiety", the

majority stating "illness" or "viral illness", the

number of absences is well above that which could be

considered as "average". I have to summarize below

Mr Rohde's sick leave taken over the period 1980-1985.

1980 - Jul-Sep 17 days

1981 - Mar-Sep 10-1/2 days

1982 - Feb-Oct 13 days

1983 - Jul-Sep 32-1/2 days

1984 - Feb-Nov 13-1/4 days plus 7 absences for

physiotherapy plus 24 days on

selected duties on medical advice.

1985 - Jan-Jun 12-1/2 days

In addition, several absences have been recorded for

which no medical certificate was provided.

Bearing in mind the above information, would you

please provide a further opinion on the question of

whether you consider Mr Rohde suffers from anxiety

state and whether he was likely to have suffered from

that condition on 10 May 1984."

(T28/104)

Upon consideration of this information, Dr Burvill without further consultation changed his previous recommendation stating as follows:

"Thank you for your further letter and its additional

information regarding Mr. Rohde and his application to

have the benefit classification certificate revoked

regarding anxiety state. As stated, the opinion given

by myself in the last paragraph of my report was

dependent on not having any further evidence to the

contrary regarding whether Mr. Rohde suffered from an

anxiety state. Mr. Rohde himself stated that he had

not got on well in his first interview with Dr. Davies

and subsequently with Dr. Dawes, and thought that all

doctors following that had been suspicious that he was

denying anxiety.

From the records of his general practitioner quoted by

yourself, it would appear that on the dates stated in

1979 and 1982 that he had been exhibiting inability to

cope with stress at that time. From his record of

sick days taken, he is taking above average days off

for illness, but as you state, the conclusion that he

suffers from an anxiety state cannot necessarily be

drawn from this record of illness, so much as that he

copes with life, including his work situation, by

having recourse to frequent episodes of physical

symptoms necessitating days off duty. Additional

evidence to support this could very well be drawn from

the fact that he has now shown symptoms of what he

calls the "R.S.I. thing". In conclusion, there is,

from the past records of his attending general

practitioners, clear evidence that he has suffered

from an anxiety state, and that currently he has

evidence of repeated episodes of illness quite above

usual for a young man and taken together, the anxiety

stated in the past and repeated physical illnesses and

now the claim of R.S.I., suggests that Mr. Rohde does

show from time to time evidence of neurosis of which

anxiety has been one presenting symptom.

Therefore, I would like to reverse the recommendation

made in my previous report and consider that the

certificate of benefit classification should stand."

(T29/106)

31. In 1986, the applicant enrolled at Flinders University to complete an Honours degree. Also in that same year, a woman with whom the applicant had previously had a de facto relationship became pregnant after they had separated and gave birth to a child by the applicant in April 1986. The applicant then took parental leave for a period of six months from 21 May 1986 until 31 October 1986. When asked whether he had been under any stress or strain as a result of this arrangement, the applicant replied:

"I enjoy having a child and am very pleased about it.

There is a bit of a problem, that I want to have more

access to my child than I am getting."

....

"It does not affect my health. It makes me a bit

unhappy, but it does not affect my health."

(transcript, p 38, 39-40)

32. On 7 April 1986, the delegate's decision of 10 May 1984 refusing to revoke the BCC was affirmed.

33. Some twelve months later, on 21 April 1987, the applicant was referred by Dr Colin Mills to Mr Marcus Tomlian of Adelaide Psychological Consultants for a psychological assessment. The applicant was required to perform certain tests, the results of which led Mr Tomlian to conclude that the applicant was not suffering from an anxiety state or neurosis at the time of his assessment. Mr Tomlian stated further:

"There is considerable evidence however that he is an

emotionally stable, intelligent, socially bold,

confident, analytical, precise and relaxed person.

With his need for precision and truth a superficial

examination may lead to a diagnosis of "anxiety" since

Mark will see any event or situation that provokes

disquiet in him as being "anxiety" eliciting."

(T34)

34. Dr Colin Mills, who had been seeing the applicant largely in relation to treatment for his repetitive strain injury, reported on 4 May 1987 (T35) that his assessment accorded with that of Mr Tomlian.

35. More recently the applicant was referred to a psychiatrist, Dr Robert Goldney, for examination, who saw him on 27 January 1988. Dr Goldney concluded as follows:

"Mr. Rohde gives a history of having been in general

good health throughout his life although he

acknowledged that he has had a number of infectious

illnesses, including Glandular Fever, and more recent

upper respiratory tract conditions which have caused

him to consult his general practitioner. However, he

certainly gives no history of definite psychiatric

illness, and he said that he has not consulted a

psychologist or psychiatrist for his nerves.

In considering his life history, it is of note that he

is a young man who has had some difficulties in

forming long standing interpersonal relationships. To

his credit he has been able to complete his university

degree whilst working, and he hopes to be able to

further his academic qualifications and possibly gain

a position within the Union movement.

Upon psychiatric examination at present there is no

evidence of any psychiatric disorder, and I also

consider that there is no firm evidence that he has

ever suffered from a psychiatric disorder.

On the basis of the reports which you presented to me

I have grave reservations about the conclusions drawn

by the Commonwealth Medical Officer in 1977, and also

those drawn by Dr. Burvill in his latter reports.

Having stated that those grave reservations exist, it

is my view that Mr. Rohde has a certain personality

style which at times causes him to have problems in

his personal relationships, but this personality style

certainly does not constitute an illness. This

personality style is simply one of a number of

personality styles which people may have, and it is

acknowledged that it may influence the nature of a

person's illness presentation. However, in

Mr. Rohde's particular case, in the absence of any

specific psychological illness episodes or treatments,

I am firmly of the view that one would be on very

shaky ground in asserting that it was of sufficient

severity to warrant a benefit classification.

From the psychiatric point of view I can see no reason

why an anxiety state should cause him to be unable to

work until his normal retirement age."

(T37)

36. In addition to the above facts, it is important to record some further factual history given by Dr Barson in his evidence. Dr Barson informed the Tribunal that over a period since February 1979 to date, the applicant has come to his consulting rooms on approximately 67 occasions and of those occasions, Dr Barson could identify only 7 which he considered were related to stress, depression or anxiety. The other conditions for which the applicant was treated were in the doctor's view unrelated to those conditions, and the symptoms complained of by the applicant were consistent with the physical medical conditions diagnosed.

37. We will now direct our attention to the consultations which Dr Barson considered were in some way related to stress, depression or anxiety suffered by the applicant. The first consultation was on 20 February 1979 when the applicant presented with diarrhoea. In the absence of being able to identify a specific cause and upon the applicant indicating that he was "under a significant amount of stress" (transcript, p 81), Dr Barson thought the condition may have had a causal relation ship with anxiety, although he could not arrive at a definite opinion.

38. The second consultation was two weeks later on 7 March 1979 when again the applicant presented with diarrhoea and abdominal pain. He told the doctor that he had "work pressures", was unable to sleep, and felt "worn out". The doctor's diagnosis on that occasion was "fatigue" (transcript, p 81).

39. The next consultation was eight months later on 7 July 1980 when the applicant presented again with gastro-intestinal symptoms. The applicant informed the doctor that:

"...he had been very energetic until recently but was

becoming depressed, with difficulty getting to sleep

for several days. He was worried about his job, about

some studies he was undertaking and about something I

have called political activities, which I think might

relate to union activities. We discussed several

lifestyle issues, among which was his lack of

exercise."

(transcript, p 81)

The doctor again attributed these symptoms to emotional problems.

40. The next consultation was one year later on 15 June 1981. The applicant presented with rectal bleeding being the major concern during the consultation, but also at that time, talked of being "edgy and depressed from time to time" (transcript, p 82).

41. Again almost one year later, on 24 May 1982, the applicant presented with a wart on his foot which had been treated by the doctor several weeks earlier. The doctor's notes record that during this consultation, the applicant told him that he was "not coping too well at work, union responsibilities, difficulties in his relationship in domestic setup". Dr Barson at that consultation discussed a psychiatric referral with the applicant and the applicant indicated he was not sure that that was appropriate. Dr Barson did not consider that medication was required. The doctor stated further:

"I would generally offer that (psychiatric referral)

to someone as one solution to their problems or

something they might consider. Had I thought it was

essential, I probably would have encouraged him to do

it but I imagine at the time - this is six years ago,

I have no recollection of the actual consultation - I

imagine at the time we discussed it it was one thing

he might do if he felt it might be useful."

(transcript, p 82)

42. The next consultation is 14 July 1982 when the applicant saw Dr Martha Kent (see previous notation para 23 hereof).

43. Four years later, on 2 July 1986, the applicant saw Dr Barson and on this occasion complained of difficulties with his arm which appear to be related to his repetitive strain injury. During this consultation, the applicant complained that "he was depressed, he could not see there would be any work he would be fit for, I presume, because of his arm and chronicity" (p 84-85 of transcript).

44. The sickness leave taken by the applicant may be summarised in the following manner. The leave records indicate on aggregation and rounding off the hours taken to the nearest day:

Period Days Special comments

6.8.80 - June 81 19

June 81 - June 82 13

June 82 - Dec 82 9

Dec 82 - Dec 83 19 Including 14 days due to knee injury

Dec 83 - Dec 84 17 Including short intervals of time with

symptoms associated with "RSI"

Dec 84 - Dec 85 60 Including 54 days associated with "RSI"

Dec 85 - Dec 86 11 Including 8 days associated with "RSI"

Dec 86 - Dec 87 7

Dec 87 - June 88 5 Still including short periods of "RSI"

45. The available sick leave to public servants over this period of time was two weeks per year. The applicant's attitude to sick leave is contained at p 36 of the transcript when he stated:

"Q: Are you suggesting that sick leave is normal sick

leave? --- I believe it is for a lot of people.

I know a lot of other people do not seem to get

the flu very often. I freely admit I am prone to

a bit of flu. I have had problems with hay fever

and sinusitis.

Q: In that respect your health has not been that

bad? --- I do not see it as abnormal, to get a

cold two or three a year. It seems normal to me.

Some people come to work with them and spread

them around. I stay at home and get over them."

.....

"Q: Where fellow workers have banks of (sick leave)?

--- Yes. That is more of a moral question. Do

you go to work with a cold, or do you not? I do

not feel compelled to go to work if I have a

cold. It is not fair to other people. I also

think it is silly, basically."

46. In addition, the applicant when summing up his case, gave further information which by agreement between the parties was regarded as evidence (p 187 of transcript):

"...perhaps I am being condemned for opting to use my

entitlement. I would also point out that one reason

that people do not take their sick leave is financial

reasons, and that is not a problem to me. Although I

am not rich I also do not spend much and my lifestyle

puts me in a good position to use up my sick leave."

(transcript, p 180)

47. Also in his evidence, the applicant told the Tribunal that he, together with other mail officers in his Department, were spoken to about their use of sick leave.

48. Before discussing the details of the medical evidence, we would firstly make the general comment that we found the medical evidence in this application difficult to reconcile. The two psychiatrists who gave evidence both later altered their opinion from their primary diagnosis and assessment. Dr Burvill initially considered that the applicant had not suffered from an anxiety state based on his assessment of the applicant on the one and only occasion of consultation (T25/100). 57. In considering the medical evidence, the first question which arises is what is an "anxiety state" such that it should be aptly characterised as a "mental condition" for the purposes of the Superannuation Act (supra). The words "anxiety" or "anxiety state" were interpreted differently by the witnesses, but it would be true to say that all were in agreement, that the layman's understanding of the words "anxiety state" would probably be different from the use and interpretation of those words by medical practitioners. The applicant suggested that a layman may confuse "anxiety state" with the feeling of being anxious, a view with which Dr Mills concurred. All medical witnesses were agreed that in general terms an "anxiety state" was one in which as a result of stress a person's bodily functions and ability to perform activities were disrupted. The two psychiatrists postulated refinements to this definition of "anxiety state".

58. It was submitted by the respondent that an "anxiety state" need not necessarily be a medical condition and reliance was placed on comments made by the Federal Court in McMullen v Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 61 ALR 189 when the court stated as follows at p 207:

"...the crux of counsel's contention on this aspect of

the matter was that there was clear evidence that it

was the paranoid personality of the applicant which

was the cause of her anxiety state on the two relevant

occasions, and thus provided the necessary "con

nection". Dr Merrifield's evidence, he said, had

clearly established that the applicant's paranoid

personality was not abnormal, was not an illness and

was not a "mental" condition. Counsel contended that

there was positive material that the connecting link,

i.e. the paranoid personality, was not a "mental"

condition and that there was no material to the

contrary.

In our opinion this submission must be rejected. Dr

Merrifield's evidence was that it was not a "medical"

condition. He did not specifically state that it was

not a "mental" condition. It is, in our view, quite

possible for a condition to be a mental condition, a

condition of the mind, without it necessarily being a

medical condition, a condition requiring medical

treatment."

59. The court, in considering the meaning of "condition", drew the distinction between a "condition" as compared to a "medical condition", the latter being interpreted as a condition requiring medical treatment.

60. We would agree that the words "physical or mental condition" in the Act need not necessarily be a condition which requires medical treatment, however, the word "condition" in connection with the word "mental" must mean something more than feelings which commonly occur to all people at some time in their lives, those feelings being encompassed by words such as unhappy, depressed, stressed, up-tight, tired, tense, sad, in conjunction with temporary common effects of sleeplessness, lethargy and gastric or gastroenteric disturbances.

61. There must be some dividing line, albeit on occasions blurred, when emotions, feelings and symptoms are such that by virtue of their chronicity, severity or longevity are beyond average or ordinary experience and such that they can properly be regarded as a mental condition. As the Tribunal in Re R.M. and Commissioner for Superannuation (1984) 6 ALD 272 at 284 said:

"It is a matter of common experience that personality

characteristics vary considerably between individuals.

In one sense, it is clearly correct, as Dr Merrifield

says, that paranoid personality is one of the normal

variants of personality. Many people probably display

such characteristics at some stage of their lives

without being characterised either as mentally ill or

as suffering from a "mental condition". But it

appears to us, having regard both to Dr Merrifield's

evidence and to the publications to which we have

referred, that it is ultimately a question of degree."

Further, as the Tribunal in Re Rowlands and Commissioner for Superannuation A. 87/30, delivered 12 August 1988 (Decision 4555) stated, "...the diagnosis of a condition is not the sole criterion for the issue of a BCC" and commented adversely on a dental caries condition being the subject of a BCC and for other minor conditions similarly being specified when coupled with the unlikelihood of such conditions leading to premature retirement.

62. Looking at the words "physical or mental condition" in the context of the Act and the role of a BCC, and addressing ourselves particularly to mental conditions as diagnosed on examination, we consider that the meaning of "anxiety state" should be that espoused by the specialty of psychiatry, which is a medical model also used by general medical practitioners, although probably applied by them in a less refined way than psychiatrists.

63. In the present case, Dr Burvill defined "anxiety state" in these terms:

"Anxiety state is a state of discomfort where a person

can experience - I use the word "discomfort" - I

really mean dysphoria. It means an alteration from a

normal state or feeling in a person which can be

either motor changes, autonomic changes, state of

apprehension or a state of vigilance. Motor change is

feeling tense, jittery, shaky, muscles will not relax.

Autonomic change is what we refer to as sweating,

trembling, sensations of abdominal pains, diarrhoea,

hot and cold sweats. The apprehension is a form of

feeling anxious and worried, and vigilance is a state

of feeling edgy and irritable. Now, anxiety state in

its generalised form takes in some variety of all of

those different components."

(transcript, p 105)

64. Dr Goldney, on the other hand, preferred the diagnostic criteria as set down in the Diagnostic Statistical Manual ("DSM") 3rd Edn. of the American Psychiatric Association (1987) (T39):

"Diagnostic criteria for 300.02 Generalized Anxiety

Disorder

A. Unrealistic or excessive anxiety and worry

(apprehension expectation) about two or more life

circumstances, e.g., worry about possible misfortune

to one's child (who is in no danger) and

worry about finances (for no good reason), for a

period of six months or longer, during which the

person has been bothered more days than not by

these concerns. In children and adolescents, this

may take the form of anxiety and worry about

academic, athletic, and social performance.

B. If another Axis I disorder is present, the focus of

the anxiety and worry in A is unrelated to it,

e.g., the anxiety or worry is not about having a

panic attack (as in Panic Disorder), being

embarrassed in public (as in Social Phobia), being

contaminated (as in Obsessive Compulsive Disorder),

or gaining weight (as in Anorexia Nervosa).

C. The disturbance does not occur only during the

course of a Mood Disorder or a psychotic disorder.

D. At least 6 of the following 18 symptoms are often

present when anxious (do not include symptoms

present only during panic attacks):

Motor tension

(1) trembling, twitching, or feeling shaky

(2) muscle tension, aches, or soreness

(3) restlessness

(4) easy fatigability

Autonomic hyperactivity

(5) shortness of breath or smothering sensations

(6) palpitations or accelerated heart rate

(tachycardia)

(7) sweating, or cold clammy hands

(8) dry mouth

(9) dizziness or lightheadedness

(10) nausea, diarrhoea, or other abdominal distress

(11) flushes (hot flashes) or chills

(12) frequent urination

(13) trouble swallowing or "lump in throat"

Vigilance and scanning

(14) feeling keyed up or on edge

(15) exaggerated startle response

(16) difficulty concentrating or "mind going blank"

because of anxiety

(17) trouble falling or staying asleep

(18) irritability

E. It cannot be established that an organic factor

initiated and maintained the disturbance, e.g.

hyperthyroidism, Caffeine Intoxication."

Dr Goldney made the qualifying comment that the above criteria did not have to be strictly adhered to, it was a general guide and not all of the symptoms were required to be present before a diagnosis of anxiety state could be made.

65. If the Tribunal applied the criteria used by Dr Goldney, particularly to the symptoms with which the applicant presented either to Dr Barson or Dr Kent, it is difficult to see how that criteria could lead to a diagnosis that the applicant had suffered from an anxiety state. One could also say the same of the criteria adopted by Dr Burvill.

66. Applying the criteria adopted by either of the two psychiatrists, the only symptoms which could possibly be attributed to an anxiety state were, at best, the applicant's complaints of diarrhoea and gastric disturbances in addition to a lack of sleep taken in conjunction with the applicant's comments as to work, union and domestic activities, being edgy and depressed and under stress at the time of consultation. Applying the diagnostic criteria, these appear to be very minimal symptoms on which to base a diagnosis of a psychiatric condition of "anxiety state". These symptoms complained of by the applicant would certainly indicate that the applicant was anxious and feeling low or stressed on these occasions and certainly in a general sense had physical symptoms which were related to those feelings. But, in our view, it is a much greater step to say that the applicant was suffering from a mental condition, such that it should be described at any one of those consultations or even collectively as an "anxiety state". It is also to be noted that the applicant's complaints and symptoms did not occur over any extended period of time, but rather over short periods of time and there was little time away from work as a consequence of the complaints or symptoms.

67. The other possible indicators of an anxiety state may be the sickness leave taken by the applicant which included many periods off from work with "URTI" (upper respiratory tract infection) and the applicant's alleged repetitive strain injury.

68. As to the applicant's sick leave, his record indicates that a large proportion of the leave was due to either a knee or repetitive strain injury. As to the balance, a number of days relate to URTI, which the applicant admitted he had a propensity to suffer from, and he also gave a reasonable explanation that he chose to stay home whereas others chose to work with such infections. This evidence, which we accept, in combination with the evidence of Dr Mills indicates an attitudinal approach to leave, and provides an explanation which persuades us that such leave is not indicative of an "anxiety state".

69. So far as repetitive strain injury is concerned, we are not satisfied that if the applicant is suffering or has suffered from that condition, that factor alone contributes to, or is an indication of, the existence of an "anxiety state" in the applicant as distinct from a quite normal response to injury and its resultant incapacity.

70. We do not therefore regard the applicant's sick leave record, nor his URTI, nor repetitive strain injury symptoms to be associated with any anxiety state given all of the other factors and circumstances. Further, we do not consider these symptoms, even if taken in conjunction with the occasions of his con sultations with Dr Barson, in all of the circumstances to indicate that the applicant has suffered from an "anxiety state" using the definitions provided by the psychiatrists.

71. If, instead of adopting the psychiatrists' definition, the Tribunal used the Department's General Medical Standard, even adopting the more recent restricted version issued in 1986 (T8/27), the Standard refers to "a chronic or recurrent psychotic or psychoneurotic illness" or "definite evidence of chronic personality disorder". The General Medical Standard also acknowledges that the necessary standard could still be met "even though a person has suffered from or shows symptoms of nervous, emotional or mental illnesses" and further, that freedom from recurrence of such illnesses for at least five years with no current signs and symptoms and no control by drugs, allows the standard to be met. Firstly, we note that these descriptions of mental disorder or illness fit well with the psychiatric definition of the "mental condition" of "anxiety state" and encompasses something more than occasional symptoms of unsettled emotions or anxious feelings. Secondly, the applicant appears to satisfy that standard. He has never had any time away from work for what was diagnosed specifically as "anxiety state", has had no psychiatric or psychological treatment for that condition and has had no medication for that condition, has had only a few short episodes which have been identified by Dr Barson as related to anxiety. The lack of chronic symptoms and no recurrence of symptoms support a finding that the General Medical Standard both in the past and up to the present time has been met. In reaching this conclusion, we note that Dr Goldney did not consider the applicant's complaint of depression on the consultation of 2 July 1986 relating to his RSI condition to be other than an acceptable reaction at that time. Therefore, the last anxiety symptoms complained of by the applicant date back to the consultation of 24 May 1982.

72. In summary, we are not satisfied that in the past, the applicant has suffered from an anxiety state. Further, on all of the evidence, we are confident that the applicant does not at the present time suffer from an anxiety state.

73. If, however, we are wrong, and the symptoms of anxiety which the applicant experienced when presenting to Dr Barson's rooms, together with his sick leave record and his repetitive strain injury are sufficient to conclude that the applicant had in the past suffered from an anxiety state, we will turn to consider whether or not the Commissioner and therefore this Tribunal should be satisfied there was not a real risk that as a result of that condition, the applicant will not continue to be an eligible employee.

74. The interpretation to be accorded the term "real risk" is canvassed in some detail in Re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation S.87/163 (delivered 4 November 1988). However, that application was largely concerned with the provisions of para.16(8)(c) of the Act as amended. The present review requires more detailed attention to para.16(8)(a) as amended as we must consider whether the delegate was correct in refusing to revoke the BCC under s.16(8)(c) or whether he should have instead revoked the BCC under para.16(8)(a).

75. Paragraph 16(8)(a) prior to the amendments effected by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (supra) provided that the Commissioner revoke the BCC if he was of the opinion "the person is not likely ... to cease to continue to be an eligible employee before he attains his maximum retiring age". The amendment to para.(a) now requires the Commissioner to be of the opinion that "there is not a real risk that the person ... will not continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age". (Tribunal emphasis).

76. The essential difference is a substitution of the words "not likely" by the words "there is not a real risk". It is important to note that both of the tests are expressed in the negative, that is, the Commissioner must be satisfied as to a negative condition in both instances.

77. In considering the various components that are contained within para.16(8)(a) of the Act, the Tribunal notes that the paragraph is concerned only with the revocation of a BCC and that the Commissioner is obliged to revoke the BCC only if the Commissioner (or on review, this Tribunal) is of the opinion that a negative situation exists, namely that there is "not a real risk" that the person will not continue employment until maximum retiring age by reason of a physical or mental condition contained in the BCC.

78. As to the meaning of "real risk", the views expressed by the Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe (supra) would also be applicable to the use of those words in para.16(8)(a), namely:

(1) The term "real risk" is not a term of art and

should be interpreted in accordance with its

dictionary meaning;

(2) "Real risk" refers to a chance of an occurrence

which is real and more than nominal, imaginary,

fictitious or fanciful but less than a probability;

(3) "Real risk" requires consideration and assessment

of the characteristics of the person, the nature,

gravamen and characteristics of the condition in

the context of employment to maximum retirement

age;

(4) Although percentages may sometimes assist in

assessing the seriousness or otherwise of certain

conditions in relation to employment, "real risk"

is not a quantitative assessment such that it can

be reduced to percentage terms in order to

determine its existence.

79. Having made these statements it is still necessary to consider what the negative expression "not a real risk" means as distinct from the positive, namely "real risk". The Tribunals in Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217 and Re Gelsthorpe (supra) considered the words "real risk" in sub-s.16(4) but neither Tribunal gave detailed consideration to the meaning of the phrase "not a real risk". Sub-section 16(4) and sub-para.16(8)(b)(ii) both require a decision-maker to be satisfied as to a positive, namely a "real risk", whereas para.16(8)(a) and sub-para. 16(8)(b)(i) require a decision-maker to be satisfied as to a negative, namely "not a real risk". Requiring a decision-maker to be satisfied as to a positive is different from being satisfied as to the same elements expressed in the negative, the two are not synonymous. The importance of the distinction will be most evident in a borderline case where a Commissioner or his delegate may be satisfied there was a real risk that the condition would give rise to premature retirement or death and therefore issue a BCC. Subsequently another delegate, if he had been called upon to consider the initial issuing of a BCC, may not have been satisfied that the necessary "real risk" existed but on consideration of a revocation, may be of the view that he was not satisfied there was "not a real risk" the person would prematurely retire from the condition and therefore hold that the BCC should not be revoked. In most cases, this will largely be a matter of semantics, but in borderline cases the difference in the expression between satisfaction as to the positive and satisfaction as to the negative may be the difference between success or failure for an applicant. Further, we note that the negative expression in relation to revocation means that it would be harder to have the BCC revoked than to issue it.

80. Turning now to address the medical evidence in the context of whether there is "not a real risk" of premature retirement by the applicant. Although Dr Burvill considered that the applicant had in the past suffered from an anxiety state, he assessed only a 10% risk that the applicant would prematurely retire as a result of that condition (p 122 of transcript).

81. Dr Goldney, on the other hand, who had previously expressed the view that the applicant would not prematurely retire from an anxiety state, expressed the contrary point of view in giving his evidence. He even went so far as to indicate that in his view, there was a 50% risk of the applicant prematurely retiring. However, on reviewing Dr Goldney's evidence, he would appear to be concentrating on what he sees as the boring nature of the applicant's work rather than looking at whether or not the reason for retirement would be attributable to an anxiety state and for that reason, we reject his assessment.

82. The other doctors, Dr Barson and Dr Mills, in concluding that the applicant would not prematurely retire due to an anxiety state, postulated on the basis that he did suffer from an anxiety state.

83. In considering the principles outlined in Re Gelsthorpe (supra) at para. 76 hereof, it is our view that the applicant appears to be a sensitive, philosophical and socially committed person who is presently employed in a position which is academically below his educational level but is work which, although mundane, is acceptable to him because it fulfils his political and social philosophies. The work is not stressful although his union activities at work may on occasion put pressure on him. Assuming for the purposes of this aspect of our consideration that he has in the past been anxious from time to time due to episodes of stress caused by his studies, union activities and domestic situation; those short periods of anxiousness have resulted in little loss of time from work and we also note the applicant has persevered with his work notwithstanding his alleged repetitive strain injury. The anxiousness suffered by the applicant over these periods, has not been sufficient to warrant specific medication, psychiatric attention nor extensive absences from work.

84. In considering para.16(8)(a), the decision-maker and this Tribunal are required to be satisfied not merely that the applicant may have periods of incapacity due to "anxiety state" or a related condition, but that the applicant will in fact prematurely retire as a result of that condition or an associated condition. On the evidence, we are satisfied that there is not a real risk that the applicant will not continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retirement age due to "anxiety state" or for a reason connected with that condition.

85. For these reasons, we consider that the decision of the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation should be set aside and in lieu thereof, a decision be substituted that the BCC issued in respect of the applicant be revoked.

I have read the Reasons for Decision of the majority in this matter. I am in agreement with them as to the meaning of "real risk". I accept the facts as set out in the majority decision. It is well known that facts in any matter admit of more than one interpretation, and in this matter, I find myself in disagreement with my colleagues. I find I am unable to accept the conclusion that the applicant presents no risk of early retirement because of a condition associated with his mental health or a condition connected with it or, more particularly, the condition of "anxiety state" specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC").

2. In this matter, the medical evidence has been conflicting to say the least. they are referred by general practitioners."

(transcript, p 151)

5. Both of the psychiatrists assessed the applicant according to their interpretation of what was "anxiety state" in the psychiatric sense. I find I am unable to go against that evidence.

6. I disagree with Dr Mills that the amount of sick leave taken was normal for a man of the applicant's age. That is not my experience, and indeed both psychiatrists take the view that the amount of sick leave, per se, irrespective of what was causing it, taken by this applicant, was a significant indicator of psychiatric illness.

7. Having heard both psychiatrists' and the applicant's evidence, I am of the opinion that not only is there a real risk (as we have defined that term) that the applicant will retire prematurely due to the condition specified in the BCC, indeed I would say it would be more probable than not that he will do so.

8. I would therefore affirm the decision under review.
