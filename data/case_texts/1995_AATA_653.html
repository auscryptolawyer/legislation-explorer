Decision Pursuant to s.4(7) of the Swimming Pools Tax Refund Act 1992, it is decided that the jurisdiction of the Tribunal is confined to the review of decisions made by the respondent to allow or not allow pool purchasers to make declarations.

.............................................

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

Jurisdiction - Taxation - Swimming Pools Tax Refund Act 1992 enacted to allow refunds of sales tax to pool builders and purchasers in certain circumstances following the decision of the High Court in Mutual Pools and Staff Pty Ltd and Anor v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1992] HCA 4; (1991) 104 ALR 545 - Whether the AAT only has jurisdiction to review decisions under s.4(7) of the SPTR Act or whether it can review other decisions of the respondent made in the course of processing applications for refund.



Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.3(3), s.30(1A)

Swimming Pools Tax Refund Act 1992 s.4



Mutual Pools and Staff Pty Ltd and Anor v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1992] HCA 4; (1991) 104 ALR 545

Precision Pools Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 109 ALR 670

Sales Tax Act (No. 1) of 1930

AAT No 10508



REASONS FOR DECISION

31 October 1995 Mr J. Handley, Senior Member



INTRODUCTION

  1. This is an interlocutory application to determine whether the Tribunal has power to review certain decisions made by the respondent under the Swimming Pools Tax Refund Act 1992 (“the SPTR Act”).
  2. At the directions hearing, Mr Mowbray appeared on behalf of the applicant and Mr Murphy of Counsel appeared on behalf of the respondent.
  3. The applications before the Tribunal are relatively novel in that they involve 51 persons who have been joined as parties pursuant to s.30(1A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”). These persons are pool purchasers in whose favour the respondent has decided to refund certain amounts of sales tax which was paid by them for swimming pools constructed in situ (refer paragraph 7). They applied to the AAT to be joined as parties when they learnt the applicant sought review of the decisions of the respondent to pay refunds to them. The basis of the purchasers being joined is that their interests are potentially affected by the applications for review. If the applicant is ultimately successful in its review before this Tribunal, their entitlement to refund is in jeopardy. Prior to the directions hearing the applicant and the respondent consented to all 51 parties being joined.
  4. The SPTR Act, assented to on 21 September 1992, was introduced to provide refunds of money paid as sales tax on swimming pools constructed in situ in certain circumstances. It was enacted following the decision in Mutual Pools and Staff Pty Ltd and Anor v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1992] HCA 4; (1991) 104 ALR 545. In that decision the High Court decided it was constitutionally invalid to deem swimming pools constructed in situ to be manufactured goods under the Sales Tax Act (No. 1) of 1930. The High Court decided the legislation which imposed the sales tax amounted to a tax not upon goods but upon land and was invalid because it contravened s.55 of the Federal Constitution. Accordingly, persons who paid sales tax on swimming pools constructed in situ are, subject to s.4 of the SPTR Act, entitled to a refund.
  5. The applicant in these proceedings did not construct swimming pools. The liquidators of the corporations Leisure Mutual Swimming Pool Company (Vic) Pty Ltd and Aquatic Pools and Spas Pty Ltd assigned to the applicant the right to claim refunds of sales tax under the SPTR Act.

THE LEGISLATION

  1. Section 4 of the Act says as follows-
“4.(1) Except as provided by this section, the Commonwealth is not liable to make any in situ pool tax refund payment.

(2) If, before the commencement of this Act or within 2 years after its commencement, the pool builder in respect of an in situ pool tax payment has made a declaration to the Commissioner, in a form approved by the Commissioner for the purpose, of either or both of the following kinds:

(a) that a specified amount, being the whole or part of the in situ pool tax concerned, was not passed on to the pool purchaser in relation to the swimming pool concerned;

(b) that a specified amount, being the whole or part of any of the in situ pool tax concerned that was passed on to the pool purchaser in relation to the swimming pool concerned, has been refunded to the pool purchaser;

then the Commonwealth is only liable to make the in situ pool tax refund payment to the pool builder to the extent that it equals the sum of:

(c) the amount of the tax that was not passed on; and

(d) the amount of the tax that was refunded.

(3) If, before the commencement of this Act or within 2 years after its commencement, the pool builder in respect of an in situ pool tax refund payment and the pool purchaser in relation to the swimming pool concerned have jointly made a declaration to the Commissioner, in a form approved by the Commissioner for the purpose, that a specified amount, being the whole or part of any of the in situ pool tax concerned that was passed on to the pool purchaser, has not been refunded to the pool purchaser, then subsection (4) applies.

(4) If a declaration is made under subsection (3), the Commonwealth is liable to make so much of the in situ pool tax refund payment as equals the amount of the tax that was passed on to the pool purchaser and not refunded, but is liable to make the payment to the pool purchaser instead of to the pool builder.

(5) Subject to subsection (6), if a declaration cannot reasonably be made under subsection (3) (because of the non-existence, death, incapacity or refusal of the pool builder, of for any other reason), the Commissioner must allow the pool purchaser, within the same period allowed under subsection (3), to make the declaration, but in a different form approved by the Commissioner for the purpose.

(6) Before the Commissioner allows the pool purchaser to make the declaration, the Commissioner must make reasonable efforts to contact the pool builder and obtain his or her views on the matter.

(7) The pool builder or the pool purchaser may apply to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of a decision of the Commissioner to allow, or not allow, the pool purchaser to make the declaration.

(8) If the pool purchaser makes the declaration, subsection (4) applies as if it were made under subsection (3).

(9) A person is not entitled to make more than one declaration for the purpose of each of subsections (2), (3) and (5) in respect of the same swimming pool.”


  1. Section 3 of the Act relevantly defines certain words and phrases appearing throughout s.4, as follows-
“in situ pool tax” means the tax referred to in the definition of “in situ pool tax provisions”;

“in situ pool tax payment” means an amount paid to the Commissioner in purported compliance with the requirements of the law relating to sales tax, or under an agreement, to pay in situ pool tax;

“in situ pool tax provisions” means the provisions of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930 that, when incorporated and read as one with the Sales Tax Act (No. 1) 1930, would have had the effect of imposing a tax on a sale value of so much of a swimming pool as is constructed in situ, but which, in accordance with the judgment of the High Court in Mutual Pools & Staff Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1992] HCA 4; (1992) 104 ALR 545, were of no effect;

“in situ pool tax refund payment” means any payment that the Commonwealth is liable to make by way of refund of an in situ pool tax payment, whether the liability arose as a result of a refund agreement or otherwise;

“pool builder” means the person to whom an in situ pool tax refund payment is liable to be made;

”pool purchaser” means a person for whom a swimming pool referred to in the definition of ‘in situ pool tax provisions’ was constructed;

“refund agreement” means an agreement having the effect that the Commonwealth is required to refund an in situ pool tax payment together with interest.”


  1. Section 3(3) of the AAT Act, which is relevant to the applicant’s submissions, says as follows-
“(3) A reference in this Act to a decision includes a reference to:

(a) making, suspending, revoking or refusing to make an order or determination;

(b) giving, suspending, revoking or refusing to give a certificate, direction, approval, consent or permission;

(c) issuing, suspending, revoking or refusing to issue a licence, authority or other instrument;

(d) imposing a condition or restriction;

(e) making a declaration, demand or requirement;

(f) retaining, or refusing to deliver up, an article; or

(g) doing or refusing to do any other act or thing.”


APPLICATIONS TO DATE UPON THE RESPONDENT





  1. The respondent notified the applicant on 25 May 1994 that it had decided to accept 17 declarations that were lodged claiming a refund of sales tax. No competing claim was made by any pool purchaser with respect to these 17 declarations. This decision is not the subject of any AAT proceedings.
  2. The applicant lodged 88 declarations for refund on 4 August 1994. The respondent decided that with respect to 81 of the declarations lodged he was not liable to make any refund to the applicant because he was not satisfied that the whole or any part of the in situ pool tax was not passed on to the pool purchaser. Of the remaining 7 declarations, the respondent decided to accept refund applications made by pool purchasers pursuant to s.4(5) of the SPTR Act because he could not conclude that the tax had not been passed on to the pool purchasers. The applicant has applied to review the decisions made with respect to these 88 declarations by application VT94/442.
  3. On 1 December 1994 the respondent advised the applicant that with respect to another 7 of the 81 declarations (refer para (ii) above) that he had decided to accept those declarations made by pool purchasers, pursuant to s.4(5) of the SPTR Act, but which competed with declarations initially made by the applicant. The respondent also decided he preferred the declarations of those purchasers in favour of the declarations of the applicant. The applicant has applied to review this decision by application VT95/30.
  4. On 23 February 1995 the respondent advised the applicant he had decided to accept another declaration from a pool purchaser being one of the 81 purchasers of a pool with respect to which the applicant had made a declaration per the above paragraphs. The applicant has not applied to review this decision.
  5. With respect to another 70 declarations lodged by the applicant, the respondent decided on 21 March 1995 that by reason of 13 competing declarations by pool purchasers, to make refunds to those purchasers because he was unable to conclude that the whole or part of any in situ pool tax was not passed on to the purchasers. Of the remaining 57 declarations, the respondent decided he was not liable to make refunds to the applicant because he was unable to be satisfied that the whole or part of any in situ pool tax was not passed on to the purchasers. The applicant has applied to review these decisions by application VT95/94.
  6. On 1 May 1995 the respondent advised the applicant that with respect to 37 competing declarations he had decided to accept the declarations of the 37 purchasers in favour of the applicant. The declarations by pool purchasers were made pursuant to s.4(5) of the SPTR Act. The applicant has applied to review this decision in application VT95/96.
  7. On 6 June 1995 the respondent decided not to accept 104 declarations lodged by the applicant for refund because he could not be satisfied that the whole or any part of in situ pool tax was not passed on to the pool purchaser. The applicant has not applied to review this decision.

Of the total number of declarations made by the applicant to the respondent, pursuant to s.4(2) of the SPTR Act, 175 concerned pools constructed by Leisure Mutual and 141 concerned pools constructed by Aquatic. As may be seen from the above chronology not all of the declarations are the subject of AAT review.





THE APPLICANT’S SUBMISSIONS

  1. Mr Mowbray, on behalf of the applicant, submitted the decision in Mutual Pools rendered the collection of sales tax on swimming pools constructed in situ as illegal and the intention therefore of the SPTR Act is to create a mechanism for refund to either a builder or a purchaser, depending on who paid the tax. He submitted some builders bore the sales tax and did not pass it on to purchasers. They are entitled, he argued, to have it refunded. This will be a matter for evidence at the hearing of the review. However, on his submitted construction of the SPTR Act, the builders are primarily liable for refund because they are responsible for the payment of the in situ pool tax.
  2. Whilst the applicant is challenging the decisions of the respondent to accept the declarations of the pool purchasers, Mr Mowbray submitted that there are two other types of applications before the Tribunal, namely-
    1. Where pool builders and pool purchasers have made competing claims for refund; and
    2. Where pool builders only have made declarations to the respondent and where the respondent has decided to refuse to make the refund applied for by the applicant.

  1. He agreed with the submissions also made by Mr Murphy that the liability of the respondent to make a refund is to the person or persons who paid the tax.
  2. With respect to pool builder only applications the issue then, he submitted, is whether the pool builder seeks review of a decision of the respondent not to pay or refuse to pay refund to the applicant in circumstances where no declaration is made by a pool purchaser. The effect of this, he submitted, is to allow the purchaser to make a declaration for refund.
  3. He argued that s.4(7), in builder only cases, permits pool builders to seek review by the AAT of decisions of the respondent not to pay refund to the applicant. The right of review, he submitted is given to builders because pool purchasers have done nothing to seek review by lodging a declaration. Pool purchasers are not therefore entitled to a refund, on his submission, without making a declaration. It followed, he said, that if a refund is not to be paid to a purchaser by the failure of a purchaser to make a declaration, that refund must be paid to a builder. To not make a refund to a builder is, in the circumstances, an error. The failure he said of the respondent to make a decision in favour of the applicant in the absence of a declaration by a pool purchaser amounts to a “decision” within the meaning of s.3(3) of the AAT Act and is therefore capable of review.
  4. Mr Mowbray made a number of comments concerning his client being frustrated by the respondent’s conduct, and labelled it on one occasion as a “device by the Commonwealth to hang on to its money”. He referred also to the legislation being “badly drafted”, but said it should be given “a generous interpretation”.

THE RESPONDENT’S SUBMISSIONS

  1. In its statement of facts and contentions filed on 28 June 1995, the respondent submitted the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to review applications VT94/442 and VT95/30 insofar as they relate to the respondent’s decision not to make an in situ pool tax refund to the applicant. It contended no amount of tax had been passed on to the purchasers or, if it had, it had not been refunded by the applicant. It further submitted that any AAT review should be confined to the jurisdiction conferred by s.4(7) of the SPTR Act namely, allowing pool purchasers to make declarations pursuant to s.4(5).
  2. At the directions hearing, Mr Murphy distinguished the present proceedings from the circumstances giving rise to the High Court decision in Precision Pools Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation and Anor [1992] FCA 445; (1992) 109 ALR 679. In Precision Pools, swimming pool builders paid money to the Commonwealth under the Sales Tax Act prior to the High Courts’ decision in Mutual Pools. The Commonwealth and Precision Pools Pty Ltd agreed in writing that if the High Court in Mutual Pools decided the tax on in situ pools was unconstitutional, it would refund money paid to the builders. He submitted that the right to recover money previously paid to the Commonwealth in that case was determined by a pre-existing contractual agreement, an issue not relevant to these proceedings. The SPTR Act alone, he submitted, was relevant to the current applications and the AAT’s jurisdiction is enabled by the SPTR Act only (refer also s.25 of the AAT Act).
  3. Mr Murphy submitted s.4(7) of the SPTR Act enables pool purchasers to apply to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to review a decision of the respondent to allow or not allow a pool purchaser to make a declaration and argued that before a refund can be made to a builder the two circumstances specified in s.4(2) need to be satisfied. These are-

  • (i) the builder either declares that he did not pass on the tax to a purchaser or, if he did, that he has refunded it; and
    • (ii) the refunded amount to the builder will not exceed the amount either not passed on or refunded.
  1. The obvious intent of the Act, he submitted, as evidenced by the foregoing, is that tax may only be refunded to the person who bore it. Of itself, a declaration by a builder will not satisfy the Commissioner without satisfactory evidence that he or she also paid it. Accordingly, in these applications, any decision made refusing or rejecting a claim for refund from a builder, when the Commissioner was not satisfied that the builder paid the tax, is not reviewable, he argued, because it is outside the ambit of s.4(7).
  2. Mr Murphy argued that only some of the decisions before the Tribunal are reviewable. In VT442/94, VT30/95 and VT94/95 the Commissioner made decisions to accept purchaser’s declarations which entitle them to refunds. Only these decisions, he said, are reviewable. The decisions which said that the Commissioner either did not accept, or was unable to be satisfied that the builder did not pass the tax on to purchasers, were not reviewable under s.4(7) as they were clearly not decisions with respect to allowing or not allowing a purchaser to make a declaration.
  3. Mr Murphy further argued that when the Administrative Appeals Tribunal eventually reviews these applications, even if it sets aside the decisions of the respondent in favour of the purchasers, it does not follow that the builder would be entitled to a refund. So as to reinforce the underlying intent of the SPTR Act, he argued that a refund is only payable to the person who paid the tax. If the Commissioner is not satisfied a builder did pay the tax he or she will not be entitled to a refund. Such a decision, Mr Murphy submitted, is not reviewable.
  4. Further submissions were made with respect to the issue of a purchaser’s qualification to make a declaration and what the Commissioner must take into account when allowing, if at all, the purchaser to make a declaration. Whilst these submissions were useful to assist in comprehending the SPTR Act they are not strictly relevant to the jurisdictional argument as they relate more to the issue for the Tribunal’s consideration at the substantive review of the decisions.

CONCLUSION

  1. The SPTR Act was enacted to create a mechanism for refund of sales tax previously paid, either by pool builders or pool purchasers, in circumstances the High Court in Mutual Pools said were illegal. The right of refund is dependent on the respondent being satisfied that refund, if payable, is passed on to the person who bore the tax.
  2. The right of refund to builders is clearly spelt out in s.4(2) of the SPTR Act. The rights afforded to pool purchasers to recover a refund is spelt out in ss.4(3) and (4). The right to ensure that a declaration can be made by a purchaser is given in s.4(5). An opportunity is given to a builder to scrutinise a purchaser’s declaration by s.4(6).
  3. The issue in these proceedings - as opposed to the substantive hearing of the applications - is the jurisdiction given to the AAT by s.4(7) of the SPTR Act. In my reading of the sub-section the language is clear and unambiguous. The AAT is enabled by s.4(7) of the SPTR Act-

  • to review a decision of the respondent
  • upon an application by a pool builder or a pool purchaser
  • to allow, or not allow, a pool purchaser to make a declaration.

This section is clearly designed to ensure that any refund is paid to the person who bore the payment of the in situ pool tax. The declaration contemplated by s.4(7) originates from s.4(5).

  1. To allay the fears of the applicant, s.4(7) does not refer to the AAT reviewing a decision of the respondent making a payment to a pool purchaser. It refers only to reviewing decisions of the respondent to allow or not allow purchasers to make declarations. It does not follow that an entitlement to refund automatically follows the making of a declaration. The respondent must still be satisfied as to who bore the tax, and whether it was in fact paid.
  2. Nor does it follow, as was submitted by the applicant, that if a purchaser does not make a declaration, a refund is necessarily payable to a builder. The respondent must first be satisfied that in situ pool tax was borne by the builder and then he or she is only entitled to a refund subject to the qualifications imposed by s.4(2)(c) and (d). Any quarrel by builders with a decision of the respondent to refuse or reject any application by them for refund are not within the Tribunals’ jurisdiction. The Tribunal’s jurisdiction, as I have indicated above, is created by the unambiguous language of s.4(7) of the SPTR Act.
  3. The argument as to the applicability of s.3(3) of the AAT Act and the alleged failure of the respondent to make a decision has no foundation in my opinion. The jurisdiction of the AAT to review decisions is provided in s.25 of the AAT Act. It permits review only when other enactments make provision for review and then only “for review of decisions made in the exercise of powers conferred by that enactment” (s.25(1)(a)). The Tribunal is not empowered to review each and every decision a decision maker makes and which may affect the rights of a person. The SPTR Act limits the right of review to a decision specifically described in s.4(7). Whilst the comments of Lockhart J., in Director General of Social Services v Hales [1983] FCA 81; (1983) 47 ALR 281 at 305-306 and Deane J., in Director General of Social Services v Chaney [1980] FCA 87; (1980) 31 ALR 571 at 591 are patently helpful in comprehending the meaning of the word “decision” in its applicability to AAT review, I am satisfied the only decisions capable of review by the AAT in these proceedings are the decisions of the respondent to allow, or not allow, a pool purchaser to make a declaration.
  4. The representatives of the parties referred to the Precision Pools decision in the course of their submissions. The circumstances giving rise to the ultimate dispute in Precision Pools occurred largely because the parties were aware of the challenge to a 1986 amendment to the Sales Tax Act (No. 1) of 1930 deeming swimming pools constructed in situ to be manufactured goods by the Mutual Pools’ case. Spender J., in Precision Pools did not consider the SPTR Act, nor did he need to. The decision does not, in my view, offer any assistance to either party in these proceedings.
  5. Accordingly, I am substantially in agreement with the respondent’s submission, that the AAT has no jurisdiction to review any of the decisions of the respondent in so far as they relate to the refusal or rejection of applications for refund of in situ pool tax made by pool builders. Of the four applications before the Tribunal, only the decisions made by the respondent to allow refunds to purchasers, as a consequence of the respondent accepting their declarations are reviewable.
  6. The applications will be adjourned to a Directions Hearing on 11 December at 9.30am where directions will be made for the future conduct of the hearing of these matters.
