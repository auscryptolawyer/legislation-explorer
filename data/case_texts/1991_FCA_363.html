Re Abraham Gilbert Saffron v the Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 363; 91 Atc 4646/22 Atr 307/102 ALR 19 30 FCR 578 (13 August 1991)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: ABRAHAM GILBERT SAFFRON

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. N G2021 of 1987

FED No. 473

Estoppel - Practice and Procedure

91 ATC 4646/22 ATR 307/102 ALR 19

[1991] FCA 363; 30 FCR 578

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Davies(1), Lockhart(2) and Beaumont(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Estoppel - "Collateral estoppel" - tax appeal - prior criminal conviction of conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth - whether evidence can be led by taxpayer to contradict matter of fact upon which conviction based.

Practice and Procedure - Abuse of process - tax appeal - prior criminal conviction of conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth - whether evidence can be led by taxpayer to contradict matter of fact upon which conviction based.

HEARING

SYDNEY

13:8:1991

Counsel for the Applicant : Mr D.H. Bloom QC with Mr F.P. Carnovale

and Mr M. Christie

Solicitors for the Applicant : J.S. Walker and D.K.L. Raphael

Counsel for the Respondent : Mr R.J. Burbidge QC with S.W. Gibb Mr

Gibb and Mr D.M. Mendelssohn

Solicitors for the Respondent : Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The question asked in the special case be answered as follows:-

Question: Is it open to the applicant to give the evidence described

in paragraph 6 of the stated case?

Answer: The applicant may conduct his case in the income tax

appeal presently before this Court for the purpose of

enabling the Court to determine the true liability of

the applicant to income tax in each of the relevant

years of income involved in the appeal; and for this

purpose the applicant may present a case denying that

he and James McCartney Anderson agreed that not all

the cash received from the conduct of businesses in

which they were involved, including the nightclub

businesses known as the Venus Room and the Carousel,

would be declared for income tax purposes. But the

applicant shall not conduct his case for the purpose

of challenging the propriety of his conviction for the

criminal offence of conspiring with Mr Anderson to

defraud the Commissioner or the fairness of the trial.

The respondent pay the applicant's costs of the stated case.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

This is a special case referred to the Full Court by a single judge of the Court. The issue arises in appeals which were brought by the applicant, Abraham Gilbert Saffron ("the taxpayer") against decisions of the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") on objections lodged with respect to income tax assessments for the income years 1977-1982.

2. The appeals were instituted well before 1987, when the taxpayer was tried on a charge of conspiring with James McCartney Anderson to defraud the Commonwealth. Many of the issues of fact arising in the trial and in the appeals are common. At the trial, the taxpayer did not give sworn evidence but made a statement from the dock. On 23 October 1987, the taxpayer was convicted and he was subsequently sentenced to 3 years imprisonment.

3. When the taxation appeals came on for hearing, counsel for the taxpayer announced that the taxpayer proposed to give sworn evidence, generally to the effect of the statement he had made from the dock. Counsel for the Commissioner stated that it would be an abuse of process to permit the taxpayer to put such a case or to give sworn evidence which conflicted with the facts which must necessarily have been found against him by the jury at his trial. Particulars of the contentions on behalf of the Commissioner, lodged for the purpose of the appeals, included:-

"3A. Having regard to the facts necessarily

determined in Reg. v Saffron (23/20/87;

Loveday, J. and jury of 12) and The Queen v

Saffron (1989) 17 NSWLR 395, it is not open to

the Applicant to contend that he did not derive

the amounts referred to in Schedules 1 and 2."

To test the point, the taxpayer gave this evidence in answer to one question put to him by his counsel:-

"Mr Saffron, do you deny that you and Mr Anderson made an

agreement to the effect that not all of the cash received

from the conduct of businesses in which you were involved

would be declared by an entity which conducted the business?

...

Do you so deny it?---I do deny it."

As it was agreed by counsel that this answer contradicted one of the matters of fact upon which the conviction was based, issues such as that considered in Beckner v Minister for Immigration Local Government and Ethnic Affairs (Davies J., 19 June 1991, unreported) need not be considered.

4. Accordingly, the question stated for the opinion of the Full Court is:-

"Whether it is open to the applicant (the taxpayer) to give

such evidence in the appeals before this Court."

5. Mr R.J. Burbidge QC, with whom Mr S.W. Gibb and Mr D.M. Mendelssohn appeared for the Commissioner, submitted that the evidence was not admissible. He relied solely upon an alleged principle of abuse of process. In my opinion, Mr Burbidge's contention is not supported by, indeed is inconsistent with, the law of this country, and of the United Kingdom. It would not be an abuse of process for the taxpayer in the taxation appeals, which were instituted prior to the trial and bona fide with a view to challenging the Commissioner's decisions on the objections lodged to income tax assessments, to give evidence as to the facts upon which the ascertainment of his income should be based.

6. English and Australian law deal with such an issue under the principles of res judicata and issue estoppel. See, e.g., Cross on Evidence, 4th Aust. Ed. at pp 130-182. As Cross states at 130:-

"When an estoppel binds a party to litigation, that party is

prevented from placing reliance on or denying the existence

of certain facts. ... From the point of view of the party

against whom it operates, the estoppel has the same effect

as if the subject facts were formally admitted."

7. A conviction is a decision in rem which establishes, while it stands, that the person convicted has been convicted of a certain crime. If the person has been convicted of a felony, it establishes that the person is a felon. Such a matter is one which the convicted person may challenge only by seeking to set aside the conviction. In the taxation appeals, the taxpayer may not challenge the fact that he has been convicted of conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth. But of course the taxpayer does not seek to do so and the fact of conviction is itself irrelevant. As is stated by Spencer Bower and Turner in the Doctrine of Res Judicata, 2nd Ed. at p 215, a conviction is conclusive merely of that which it establishes, namely the fact of conviction for the offence, but not of the facts lying behind that conviction.

8. Mr Burbidge did not rely upon issue estoppel, no doubt accepting that the parties in the criminal proceedings were not the same parties as those in the taxation appeals. See, e.g. Carl Zeiss Stiftung v Rayner and Keeler Ltd (No. 2) (1967) 1 AC 853; Ramsay v Pigram [1968] HCA 34; (1967-1968) 118 CLR 271. Moreover, issue estoppel has little or no application to criminal proceedings. See e.g. Director of Public Prosecutions v Humphrys (1977) AC 1; R v Storey [1978] HCA 39; (1978) 140 CLR 364.

9. Other principles of the law are likewise against Mr Burbidge's submission. Thus, the subject matter of the taxation appeals falls outside the first of the two categories enunciated by Viscount Simon in General Medical Council v Spackman (1943) AC 627 at 634-5. As to the first category, his Lordship said:-

"In the former case, the decision of the council is properly

based on the fact of the conviction, and the practitioner

cannot go behind it and endeavour to show that he was

innocent of the charge and should have been acquitted."

His Lordship distinguished that instance from the second category, in which a conviction may be a relevant factor but the decision is not based upon it. His Lordship's first category was applied by Full Courts of this Court in Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Daniele [1981] FCA 212; (1981) 39 ALR 649; Degerli v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1981) 4 ALN N39 and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Gungor [1982] FCA 99; (1982) 42 ALR 209 and by several decisions of single judges of the Court, including Sioeli Sim Lai v Minister for Immigration Local Government and Ethnic Affairs (Morling J., 5 April 1991, unreported) and Beckner v Minister for Immigration Local Government and Ethnic Affairs. Those cases establish that, where a conviction is the foundation for the exercise of a power, no challenge can be made to the fact of the conviction or to the essential facts on which it was based. But by making clear the circumstance in which no such challenge may be made, the cases establish that, where the exercise of the power is not founded on a criminal conviction, then, even if the conviction be relevant, a challenge may be made to the essential facts on which it was based. In Australia, an example is the decision of the High Court of Australia in Ziems v The Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of New South Wales [1957] HCA 46; (1957) 97 CLR 279.

10. The rationale for the distinction between the two categories is, of course, that in the first of the categories the exercise of the power arises out of, and is founded on, the conviction. The power conferred is not a power to reconsider that matter or the essential facts on which the conviction was based but a power to consider matters of discretion and like consequential matters which flow from the established fact of conviction. When the power is not so founded, then all relevant matters, including the facts on which the conviction was based, are open.

11. In the present taxation appeals, the conviction of the taxpayer is not a matter on which the ascertainment of the taxpayer's taxable income is founded. It is not even a relevant matter unless the conviction is admissible evidence of the essential facts on which it was based.

12. I am content to leave open the question of the admissibility of the conviction, as evidence in the taxation appeals, for it is not raised by the question put to the Court. In Hollington v F. Hewthorn and Co Ltd (1943) KB 587, the Court of Appeal held that a conviction is not, in civil proceedings, admissible evidence of the essential facts on which it was based. Likewise, in R v Seery [1914] HCA 70; (1914) 19 CLR 15 at 16-17 17, Griffith C.J. said:-

"There are, in my opinion, serious difficulties as to the

question whether a verdict in a criminal case either of

guilty or not guilty is admissible under any circumstances

as evidence in a civil case, and, if so, for what purpose.

According to the older authorities it was never admissible.

It may be that for some purposes it is admissible. But, if

it is, it can only be admitted as res judicata, and only

where it appears that the point determined by the verdict is

the same point which is in issue in the civil case."

See also Wing On and Co. Ltd. v Collector of Customs (NSW) [1938] HCA 71; (1937-1938) 60 CLR 97 per Starke J. at 100. Hollington v Hewthorn has, however, attracted much criticism, e.g. in Hunter v Chief Constable of the West Midlands Police [1981] UKHL 13; (1982) AC 529 at 543; Jorgensen v News Media (Auckland) Ltd (1969) NZLR 961. In the United Kingdom and in many States of Australia, but not in New South Wales, its effect has been abrogated by statutes which provide that a conviction is admissible evidence in other proceedings.

13. The controversy as to Hollington v Hewthorn is another illustration that the principle as enunciated by Mr Burbidge is not a principle of the law of this country or of the United Kingdom. Mr Burbidge is not seeking to put forward the conviction as evidence of the facts on which it was based. Mr Burbidge is seeking to establish that the conviction is conclusive proof of the essential facts on which the conviction was based. That is not the law.

14. That brings me to the question of abuse of process. Legal proceedings may not be instituted or carried on for the purpose of challenging by inappropriate means the judgment or order of a court made in either civil or criminal jurisdictions. See, e.g., Hunter v Chief Constable, in which proceedings were struck out as they sought to challenge in civil proceedings the order of a judge on a voir dire in criminal proceedings and the verdict of the jury therein. At 541, Lord Diplock said it was an abuse of process to initiate proceedings in a court:-

"for the purpose of mounting a collateral attack upon a

final decision ... which has been made by another court of

competent jurisdiction in previous proceedings in which the

intending plaintiff had a full opportunity of contesting the

decision in the court by which it was made."

But in such a case, it is the purpose of the proceedings which constitutes the abuse of process, as his Lordship made clear throughout his speech.

15. In the present case, so far as appears from the special case, the taxpayer merely wishes to continue with his taxation appeals which were instituted for the purpose of challenging the assessments which issued, which appeals were, moreover, instituted prior to the taxpayer's conviction. It has not been shown that there is any purpose connected with the taxation appeals as would constitute those appeals an abuse of process.

16. Another example of "abuse of process" is where an applicant seeks to institute a second set of proceedings in relation to matters raised or appropriately raised in other proceedings. See e.g. the decision of Mason J. in Moore v Inglis (1976) 9 ALR 509. But there is no such circumstance here. The term "abuse of process" has also been used in the context where an issue raised in one proceeding has so clearly been part of the subject matter of other litigation and so clearly could have been raised therein that it would be an abuse of process to allow the issue to be litigated in the new proceedings, though Gibbs C.J., Mason and Aickin JJ. commented in Port of Melbourne Authority v Anshun Pty Ltd (1981) 147 CLR at 602 that the abuse of process test in this context was "not one of great utility". No such principle of abuse of process applies in the present case.

17. I see no element of abuse of process in the taxation appeals. The taxpayer is merely exercising his statutory right of appeal and, indeed, it is in the public interest that he should be permitted to do so untrammelled by his conviction for conspiracy to defraud the Commonwealth. There was no element of abuse of process in the particular question asked and answered or in the evidence of that nature sought to be adduced. Such evidence is relevant to, indeed essential to, a proper consideration of the taxation appeals.

18. In support of his submissions, Mr Burbidge referred to the American principle of collateral estoppel, as enunciated in cases such as Teitelbaum Furs Inc v Dominion Insurance Company 375 P 2d 439 (1962); S.T. Grand Inc v City of New York 344 NYS 2d 938 at 941 (1973); Considine v United States 645 F.2d 925 (1981); Gray v Commissioner of Internal Revenue [1983] USCA6 840; 708 F.2d 243 (1983); Klein v Commissioner of Internal Revenue [1989] USCA10 198; 880 F.2d 260 at 262 (1989); Manzoli v Commissioner of Internal Revenue [1990] USCA1 239; 904 F.2d 101 at 105 (1990). But that principle is not a part of the law of this country.

19. In the American principle, the requirement of mutuality of estoppel has been abandoned. Identity of parties is not required. It is sufficient that there be an identity between an issue raised in the proceedings on foot and an issue which has necessarily been decided in prior proceedings. A further requirement of the estoppel is that a full and fair opportunity must have been afforded to the person alleged to be estopped to contest the decision said to be controlling the issue. Where the principle applies, a criminal conviction will afford conclusive proof of the facts necessarily decided by the verdict. I need not discuss the authorities to this effect.

20. However, even in the United States, trial courts have a broad discretion in the application of collateral estoppel. In Gray, Klein and Manzoli, which were tax cases, the principle was applied only to the finding of fraud which had been established by the conviction. Thus, in Manzoli, no mention of collateral estoppel appears in that part of the judgment that deals with the issue of unreported income. Rather, the report at p 104 discloses that the Manzolis had given evidence in their tax appeals and that their evidence had been weighed up in the usual way, that is to say, by taking into account the probabilities of the case, the demeanour of the witnesses and so on. Mr Burbidge did not refer to any any American case in which collateral estoppel was applied so as to preclude a taxpayer from giving evidence in a tax appeal as to what he alleged were the facts relevant to the assessment of his taxable income.

20. Mr Burbidge also relied upon the reasoning and dicta of Hill J. in Commissioner of Australian Federal Police v Butler [1989] FCA 408; (1989) 91 ALR 293 at 301-3. Insofar as his Honour's reasoning is to the contrary of mine, I do not accept it. I have not considered, however, the correctness of the decision itself.

21. For the reasons I have given, I would answer the question affirmatively in the terms propounded by my brother Lockhart J.

22. I would order that the Commissioner of Taxation pay the costs of the special case.

A Judge of the Court has stated a case pursuant to s. 25(6) of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 for the consideration of a Full Court of the Court. The essential question that arises is whether, in the course of appeals under Part V of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act") against assessments to income tax, the applicant, Abraham Gilbert Saffron, may adduce evidence which contradicts a matter necessarily decided against him by an earlier criminal conviction.

2. On 2 March 1984 the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation, issued notices of amended assessment with respect to the taxable income of the applicant for the financial years ended 30 June 1977 to 1982 inclusive being "default" assessments. The applicant objected to the amended assessments, the objections were disallowed and the applicant requested the respondent on 11 September 1984 to treat his notices of objections as appeals and to forward them to the Supreme Court of New South Wales, which then had jurisdiction in appeals under Part V of the Assessment Act. On 1 August 1985 the respondent transmitted the objections to the Supreme Court of New South Wales. Pursuant to s. 4(3) of the Jurisdiction of Courts (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 1987 (Cth) this Court obtained jurisdiction on 1 September 1987 with respect to the appeals.

3. The income tax appeals came before a single Judge of the Court on various occasions for directions; and the directions given included the filing and serving of statements of facts, issues and contentions by each party to the tax appeals.

4. Whilst the tax appeals were pending, the applicant was charged on 14 September 1987 before a Judge of the District Court of New South Wales in its criminal jurisdiction and a jury of twelve pursuant to s. 86A of the Crimes Act 1914 (Cth) with offences of conspiring with one James McCartney Anderson to defraud the Commonwealth. It is common ground between the parties that there is a close nexus between the facts involved in both the criminal trial and the tax appeals. The applicant pleaded not guilty.

5. The applicant did not give sworn evidence at his trial and made a statement from the dock. On 23 October 1987 he was convicted as charged and subsequently sentenced to imprisonment for three years. He was released from prison on 11 March 1990.

6. During the course of his trial, the applicant requested that certain questions of law be reserved for determination by the Court of Appeal of New South Wales pursuant to s. 72 of the Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) and, following his conviction, he appealed against it to the Court of Criminal Appeal of New South Wales. Submissions on the reserved questions of law and the grounds of appeal relating to his conviction were heard together by the Court of Criminal Appeal. On 7 October 1988 each reserved question was answered adversely to the applicant and his appeal against conviction was dismissed.

7. The tax appeals came on for hearing before a single Judge of this Court. The applicant gave evidence before the Court and verified his statement which is exhibit "A" to the Stated Case. It appears from the statement that the amended assessments are in respect of monies which the respondent claims were received by the applicant from the cash takings of certain nightclubs, restaurants and other sources including nightclubs known as the Venus Room, Carousel Cabaret, La Bastille and Showbiz all at Kings Cross, Sydney and a nightclub known as Gilligans at Bondi Junction, Sydney. During the course of his evidence in chief before the learned primary Judge the applicant was asked by his counsel this question:

"Q: Mr Saffron, do you deny that you and Mr

Anderson made an agreement to the effect that

not all of the cash received from the conduct of

businesses in which you were involved would be

declared by an entity which conducted the usiness?"

8. Counsel for the respondent objected to the question and it was the asking of this question which led to the case being stated to this Full Court. Paragraph 6 of the stated case says of that question:

"The answer to that question contradicts one of

the matters of fact upon which the conviction

... was based, or which was critical to that

conviction, namely, that the Applicant and James

McCartney Anderson agreed that not all the cash

received from the conduct of businesses in which

they were involved would be declared for income

tax purposes by an entity which conducted the business."

9. The case also states in paragraph 7:

"The respondent contends that it is not open for

the applicant to give such evidence in the

appeals before this Court on the basis that to

give such evidence amounts to a collateral

attack upon a final decision against the

Applicant by a criminal court of competent

jurisdiction."

10. The question for determination as stated in the case is as follows:

"The question for determination by the Full

Court is whether it is open to the Applicant to

give such evidence in the appeals before this Court."

11. There is a threshold difficulty in the hearing of this matter caused by the fact that the trial of the applicant before the District Court Judge and jury occupied many days and we do not have before us the transcript of the proceedings or of the Judge's charge to the jury. However, the reasons for judgment of the Court of Criminal Appeal of New South Wales, which heard the applicant's appeal from his conviction (R v Saffron (1988) 17 NSWLR 395), deals at length with various questions of fact. The reasons for judgment of Hope J.A. suggest that one of the matters of fact which was critical to the conviction of the applicant and which must therefore have been found against him by the jury, was that the applicant and Mr Anderson made an agreement to the effect that not all of the cash received from the conduct of two of the businesses in which they were involved (the Venus Room and the Carousel Cabaret) would be declared by the entity which conducted the business. It was not disputed before us that, in these circumstances, if the question which we are asked in the stated case is resolved against the applicant, he would not be barred from raising matters of fact in his case contrary to the question in respect of the other businesses. It is also common ground that the two businesses mentioned constitute most of the cash received from the businesses involved.

12. I also put to one side whether the question which the applicant was asked and which gave rise to the stated case mentioned above is admissible in form. It appears that the question was asked for the purpose of raising the question of law which has been submitted to this Full Court.

13. Counsel for the respondent argued before us that it is an abuse of the process of this Court to permit a moving (or any party) to civil litigation to adduce evidence contradictory of a matter necessarily decided against him by a conviction in a court of competent jurisdiction. Reliance was placed by counsel for the respondent on the doctrine developed in United States law of "collateral estoppel" or "issue preclusion" and, although the case was not argued on the basis that these doctrines should be imported into Australian law, they were relied upon by way of analogy to support the argument of the respondent in relation to abuse of process.

14. Counsel for the applicant submitted that not only is there no place in our law for "collateral estoppel" or "issue preclusion", but that it cannot be an abuse of process for the applicant to prove in the taxation appeals matters of fact even if they contradict matters essential to his criminal conviction. Reliance was placed by counsel for the applicant upon the special nature of appeals and reviews under the Assessment Act where the taxpayer is intended to be given full opportunity to contest his liability to tax. Reliance was also placed upon the fact that the taxation appeals commenced well before the applicant was charged with the criminal offences and convicted.

The Authorities

15. The discussion of the question before us should commence with Hunter v Chief Constable of the West Midlands Police [1981] UKHL 13; (1982) AC 529 which concerned the trial of the Birmingham Bombers. Bombs exploded in two Birmingham public houses on 21 November 1974 killing 21 people and injuring 161 others. Five of the six plaintiffs in subsequent civil proceedings, being Irish Republican supporters, were arrested by the Lancashire police and taken to a police station. The sixth plaintiff was also taken there after his subsequent arrest.

16. Four of the plaintiffs signed statements and two others made oral confessions regarding their parts in planting the bombs which had caused the explosion. All six plaintiffs were photographed. All six appeared before a Magistrate's Court and, although three of them complained to solicitors assigned to them of assaults by the police, no marks were noticed on their faces save for one plaintiff. All six were remanded in custody and taken to prison. Three days later they appeared again before the Magistrate's Court and their faces showed injuries which indicated they had been seriously assaulted. They were again remanded in custody and an inquiry was held as to how their injuries were sustained.

17. At their trial on twenty-one charges of murder their counsel objected to the admission into evidence of their statements, which were an essential part of the prosecution case, on the ground that they had been induced by violence and threats by the police. A voir dire examination, in the absence of the jury, took place for eight days during which the plaintiffs and the police officers gave evidence. The learned trial Judge held that the prosecution had discharged the burden of proving beyond reasonable doubt that the plaintiffs were not assaulted by police, that the statements were voluntary and should be admitted into evidence.

18. The trial then continued before the jury and the plaintiffs again alleged that their statements had been induced by violence by the police. The Judge warned the jury that if the plaintiff's allegations were or might reasonably be true the statements were worthless. All six plaintiffs were convicted by the jury and sentenced to imprisonment for life. Leave to appeal was refused by the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) on 30 March 1976.

19. The six plaintiffs then issued writs against the Chief Constables of the West Midlands and the Lancashire police and also against the Home Office. They claimed damages against the police for injuries caused by assaults which were the same allegations that had been made both at the voir dire and the trial, and also claimed damages against the Home Office, in respect of later assaults by police and prison officers. The Chief Constables applied for the statements of claim against them to be struck out under a rule of court which empowered the Court to strike out pleadings if they disclosed no reasonable cause of action or were scandalous, frivolous or vexatious or otherwise an abuse of the process of the Court. Cantley J. dismissed the applications but the Court of Appeal allowed an appeal by the Chief Constables and ordered that the statement of claim be struck out. The House of Lords affirmed the decision of the Court of Appeal. The Court of Appeal's judgment is reported in (1980) QB 283.

20. In the House of Lords, Lord Diplock gave the principal speech, with which the other four members of the House agreed. The House of Lords approached the case solely on the basis as to whether it was an abuse of the process of the High Court of Justice. The opening words of Lord Diplock's speech are:

"My Lords, this is a case about abuse of the

process of the High Court. It concerns the

inherent power which any court of justice must

possess to prevent misuse of its procedure in a

way which, although not inconsistent with the

literal application of its procedural rules,

would nevertheless be manifestly unfair to a

party to litigation before it, or would

otherwise bring the administration of justice

into disrepute among right-thinking people."

His Lordship referred to the fact that before Cantley J. it had been primarily argued that what had happened at the murder trial gave rise to an issue estoppel and that the abuse of process argument took second place; and that Cantley J. had dismissed the summons on the ground that there was no issue estoppel in the strict sense of that term and on the broader grounds that he ought not to dismiss the action as an abuse of the process of the Court if, in the light of evidence that was not called at the murder trial, (even though it had been available then) but which the plaintiffs intended to adduce in the civil action, it was "reasonably conceivable that another tribunal acting judicially might accept at least part of the plaintiff's case". All three members of the Court of Appeal held that Cantley J. was wrong on the broader ground.

21. Lord Denning M.R. and Sir George Baker were in favour of extending the description "issue estoppel" to cover the particular example of abuse of process of the Court presented in that case. Goff L.J. expressed his own view which had been shared by Cantley J. that such extension would involve a misuse of the expression "issue estoppel". The House of Lords approached the matter on the basis that the question before them was whether the civil proceedings were an abuse of the process of the Court. Lord Diplock said that in his view:

"it would be best, in order to avoid confusion,

if the use of the description 'issue estoppel'

in English law, at any rate (it does not appear

to have been adopted in the United States), were

restricted to that species of estoppel per rem

judicatam that may arise in civil actions

between the same parties or their privies, of

which the characteristics are stated in a

judgment of my own in Mills v Cooper (1967) 2 QB

459, 468-469 that was adopted and approved by

this House in Reg. v Humphrys (1977) AC 1, the

case in which it was also held that 'issue

estoppel' had no place in English criminal law."

22. His Lordship held that the case before the House exemplified abuse of process in the sense of the initiation of proceedings in a court of justice:

"for the purpose of mounting a collateral attack

upon a final decision against the intending

plaintiff which has been made by another court

of competent jurisdiction in previous

proceedings in which the intending plaintiff had

a full opportunity of contesting the decision in

the court by which it was made."

23. His Lordship held that the proper method of attacking the decision by the trial Judge in the murder trial that the plaintiffs were not assaulted by the police would have been to contend that the Judge's ruling that the confessions were admissible had been erroneous as a ground of his appeal against his conviction to the Criminal Division of the Court of Appeal, but this had not been done. His Lordship plainly held the view that the dominant purpose of the civil action was, not to recover damages, but to establish "long after the event when memories have faded and witnesses other than the Birmingham Bombers themselves may be difficult to trace, that the confessions on the evidence on which they were convicted were induced by police violence, with a view to putting pressure on the Home Secretary to release them from the life sentences that they are otherwise likely to continue to serve for many years to come" (541). His Lordship held that the civil action was a "collateral attack" upon the conviction of the plaintiffs at the criminal trial.

24. Hunter v Chief Constable is therefore authority from the highest source in England that, where a final decision has been made by a criminal court of competent jurisdiction, it is a general rule of public policy that a civil action cannot be used to initiate a collateral attack on that decision; and, if it does, it is an abuse of the process of the civil court. An exception may exist where fresh evidence is sought to be adduced in a civil action which was not available at the trial; but whether it is such an exception will depend on the facts of the particular case. As Lord Diplock noted, the English authorities are collected in the judgment of Goff L.J. in the Court of Appeal in Hunter v Chief Constable (at 324-339) where his Lordship carefully reviewed the earlier authorities in England and in the United States of America.

25. Hunter v Chief Constable is a very different case from the present one. The essence of the reasoning of the House of Lords was that the civil proceedings were a "collateral attack" upon the criminal conviction, with the dominant purpose of the civil action being, not to recover damages, but to challenge the foundation of their conviction at a time when it was likely that the principal witnesses called for the prosecution at the trial were either not available or their memories had faded, and that the only witnesses whose evidence would be clear would be that of the plaintiffs, the Birmingham Bombers themselves; that is, the abuse of process lay in the House's opinion that the proceedings were brought for the purpose of collateral attack on the final decision of a court of competent jurisdiction. The present case is a far cry from Hunter v Chief Constable.

26. Reliance was also placed by counsel for the respondent upon the judgment of the House of Lords in General Medical Council v Spackman (1943) AC 627 and especially the judgment of Viscount Simon L.C. at 634-635. His Lordship said with respect to the facts of that case that the medical practitioner concerned "cannot go behind it (i.e. the fact of the conviction) and endeavour to show that he was innocent of the charge and should have been acquitted".

27. An interesting case in this area is Lamari v Lamari (1988) 1 QdR 144. This was an action for damages for unlawful assault. In his defence the defendant put in issue that he had wrongfully assaulted the plaintiff. In an earlier criminal proceeding the defendant had been convicted of doing grievous bodily harm to the plaintiff, but it was not clear upon what basis the jury so decided. The plaintiff applied to have the defence struck out and for the entry of judgment against the defendant. Kneipp J. dismissed the summons and held that because one element of unlawful assault, namely, a wilful application of force, was not necessarily determined at the criminal trial, it could not be said that all the issues of the case before him were determined by the verdict of the jury; and that, because the defendant was putting in issue the lawfulness of the assault for the purpose of resisting the plaintiff's claim for damages and not merely reopening issues previously determined at the criminal trial, he was not abusing the process of the court. His Honour said at 147:

"It is not suggested here that the defendant

does not have a genuine interest in defending

the present proceedings. He is not defending

them merely for the purpose of re-opening issues

previously determined against him but for the

purpose of resisting the plaintiff's claim for

damages. I think that he is entitled to do this."

28. Counsel for the respondent also referred us to a decision of the Full Bench of this Court in Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Gungor [1982] FCA 99; (1982) 42 ALR 209. In that case the respondent was convicted of an offence under the Poisons Act 1966 (N.S.W.) for supplying Indian hemp and sentenced to one year's imprisonment. The Minister made an order for deportation of the respondent under the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) s. 12. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal, after reviewing the decision of the Minister, decided to recommend that the Minister revoke the deportation order and the matter was remitted to the Minister for reconsideration. The Minister then appealed to this Court pursuant to s. 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 on questions of law.

29. All members of the Court (Fox, Fisher and Sheppard JJ.) held that, when the Tribunal was reviewing a decision of the Minister to deport the respondent, the earlier conviction must be conclusive as to the guilt of the accused in relation to the offence charged and of the sentence imposed (per Fox J. at 213). Fox J. pointed out that "serious practical questions" would arise if the position were otherwise as the Tribunal could then arrive at its own decision as to whether the person concerned was or was not guilty of the offence and whether the sentence was merited; and that it would be doing this on material gathered and considered at what could be a long time after the trial, with all the attendant problems.

30. As was pointed out by the Court in Gungor's Case, the conviction is a necessary basis for the Minister's deportation order so that the Minister must, in deciding whether to deport or not, take the prior conviction as a starting point. It is plain from reading the judgments of Fox J. and Sheppard J. (Fisher J. agreed with both Fox J. and Sheppard J.) that it is because the conviction was the starting point for the Minister's order that they were of the opinion that no challenge could be made to the conviction or the sentence imposed upon the respondent. But both Fox J. and Sheppard J. were of the view that evidence could be led by the respondent which might raise matters important to the prior conviction (per Fox J. at 213 and Sheppard J. at 233-234). Fox J. gave the example of a prosecution witness who wished to tell the Tribunal on oath that he had in his evidence at the criminal trial committed perjury on vital matters.

31. Fox J. said he did not see why the Tribunal could not receive this new evidence as part of the material before it, but he stated that the Tribunal could not make a recommendation to the Minister influenced by any conclusion that the person concerned should not have been convicted. Fox J. said that it would be open for the Tribunal to not affirm the decision of the Minister and, indeed, to remit the matter to him for reconsideration with or without a recommendation arising from or related to the evidence of the self-confessed perjurer. There could even be, his Honour said (313-4):

"a recommendation that the order for deportation

be revoked until the position was clarified, on

the basis that, if appropriate, the Minister

could issue another. There is considerable

flexibility. What would be wrong, in my view,

would be to recommend revocation influenced by a

conclusion that the person concerned should not

have been convicted."

His Honour found support for his conclusion in the speeches of the members of the House of Lords in Spackman and the decision in Pochi v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1979] AATA 64; (1979) 26 ALR 247 especially per Brennan J. at 253-4. Both Fox J. at 215-216 and Sheppard J. at 230-231 approved the following passage from the judgment of Fisher and Lockhart JJ. in Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Daniele [1981] FCA 212; (1981) 39 ALR 649 at 653:

"The conviction is the genesis of the Minister's

power to deport. There are powerful reasons of

public policy why the Tribunal cannot ignore the

conviction or seek to set it at nought. That is

not to say that the circumstances surrounding

the commission of the offence or matters

relating to the trial itself cannot be examined

by the Tribunal. However, such examination is

for the purpose of enabling the Tribunal to make

its own assessment of the nature and gravity of

the applicant's criminal conduct and not for the

purpose of assessing the propriety of the

conviction or the fairness of the trial.

Whether these circumstances and matters are

susceptible of examination and the extent of

that enquiry will vary from case to case."

32. Other cases which concern the question before us include Bragg v Oceanus Mutual Underwriting Association (Bermuda) Limited (1982) 2 Ll LR 132; Commissioner of Australian Federal Police v Butler [1989] FCA 408; (1989) 91 ALR 293 (Hill J.); Sioeli Siu Lai v Minister for Immigration Local Government and Ethnic Affairs, (Morling J., unreported, 5 April 1991), and Beckner v Minister for Immigration Local Government and Ethnic Affairs (Davies J., unreported, 19 June 1991.)

33. Discussion on this subject would not be complete without reference to what has become known as the rule in Hollingworth v Hewthorn (1943) KB 587. That case is recognised as authority for the proposition that a finding of guilt is not evidence in a civil proceeding involving the same factual issues. In Hunter's Case the House of Lords was of the opinion that it was "generally considered to have been wrongly decided" (per Lord Diplock at 543); but this was in the context of the earlier enactment of legislation in the United Kingdom aimed at overcoming the perceived shortcomings of the rule. The Court of Appeal of New Zealand declined to apply the rule in Jorgensen v News Media (Auckland) Limited (1969) NZLR 961. But the rule has not been universally condemned; see Spencer-Bower and Turner's The Doctrine of Res Judicata 2nd edn 1969 at 221-227.

Findings

34. Appeals or reviews pursuant to Part V of the Assessment Act are of a special nature. Their purpose is to ascertain the true tax liability of the taxpayer under the Assessment Act. The onus is on the taxpayer to prove that the assessment is excessive (s. 190(b)). As Brennan J. observed in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1989) 168 CLR 614 at 621:

"After all, the purpose of the procedure of

assessment, objection and appeal or review is to

ascertain the true tax liability of the taxpayer

under the substantive provisions of the Act."

Although in one sense it is right to describe the taxpayer and the Commissioner in Part V proceedings as parties, they are parties in a special sense in that they are parties to a procedure for which the Assessment Act makes special provision to enable determination to be made of the true tax liability of the taxpayer according to the provisions of that Act.

35. In the present case, it is clear that the applicant has a genuine interest in challenging the assessments issued against him.

36. In my opinion it would not be open for the applicant to use the tax appeals before this Court for the purpose of a "collateral attack" upon his prior conviction; but there is nothing before us to suggest that this is the purpose of the present proceeding. The question of the true liability of the applicant to income tax has been alive for many years and existed well before he was charged with the criminal offence that led to the present point being raised. Indeed, the appeals against the assessments had been brought over three years before the applicant was charged with the criminal offence.

37. The tax appeals are proceedings of a different nature to those considered in the deportation cases. As Fisher J. and I said in Daniele's Case at 653:-

"The conviction is the genesis of the Minister's

power to deport. There are powerful reasons of

public policy why the Tribunal cannot ignore the

conviction or seek to set it at nought."

It is important to emphasise that the conviction of the applicant is not a necessary integer in the tax appeals. Indeed, it seems to me to be a wholly irrelevant matter to the determination of the issues in those appeals.

38. What the applicant seeks to do in the tax appeals, as I understand it, is to deny an agreement between himself and Mr Anderson to the effect that not all of the cash received from the conduct of various businesses in which they were involved would be declared for income tax purposes. This is an assertion of fact which is relevant to the tax appeals; and I can see no good reason why he cannot lead evidence to that effect if he wishes. The respondent may lead evidence to the contrary and in the result the primary Judge can determine the facts. But the Court cannot reach or express a view that the applicant was wrongly convicted of the offence of conspiring to defraud the Commonwealth. The processes of and respect for the criminal law would not be undermined by the applicant leading evidence of the kind which he seeks to lead. The position would be different if this evidence was lead for the purpose of impugning the applicant's conviction or the fairness of his trial.

39. The case was not argued before us on the basis of issue estoppel and nor could such an argument have succeeded. It was argued solely on the basis of abuse of process of this Court; though we were referred in argument by counsel for the respondent to a plenitude of American authority relating to "collateral estoppel" or "issue preclusion". There is no case of which I have knowledge in this country or in the United Kingdom where this American doctrine has been applied. I need refer to it no further.

40. I would answer the question raised by the stated case as follows:

The applicant may conduct his case in the income tax appeal

presently before this Court for the purpose of enabling the

Court to determine the true liability of the applicant to

income tax in each of the relevant years of income involved

in the appeal; and for this purpose the applicant may

present a case denying that he and James McCartney Anderson

agreed that not all the cash received from the conduct of

businesses in which they were involved, including the

nightclub businesses known as the Venus Room and the

Carousel, would be declared for income tax purposes. But

the applicant shall not conduct his case for the purpose of

challenging the propriety of his conviction for the criminal

offence of conspiring with Mr Anderson to defraud the

Commissioner or the fairness of the trial.

41. The respondent must pay the costs of the applicant of the Stated Case.

This matter comes before a Full Court, on a special case stated by a single judge.

The question stated by the special case

2. In the course of the hearing of several appeals brought in the original jurisdiction of the Court by the applicant, Abraham Gilbert Saffron, ("the taxpayer"), against decisions by the Commissioner of Taxation disallowing objections by the taxpayer against "default" assessments of income tax, certain evidence was sought to be lead from the taxpayer to which objection was taken by the Commissioner. The objection, said to be based upon public policy grounds, was that this evidence was not admissible because, in substance, it involved a collateral, and thus impermissible, attempt to set at nought, or challenge, a prior criminal conviction of the taxpayer. In order to understand the context of the question stated for the Full Court, which is whether it was open to the taxpayer to give this evidence in the appeals, it is necessary to refer to the history of the matter as follows.

The assessments

3. Original assessments for income tax were issued by the Commissioner for the years 1977-1982. They were amended on 2 March 1984, the major amendment being the inclusion of additional income, in substantial amounts, "based upon an examination of information held in this office". Additional tax for the filing of incorrect returns was also assessed. Two of the assessments had also been amended prior to this date. On 26 March 1987, further amended assessments for the years 1977-1979 were made. These included substantial additional income as follows:

"Based on further information received in this office the

following amounts of money have been determined to have been

- Venus Room

- Gilligans

- Carousel."

The objections to the assessments and their disallowance

4. Objections to the assessments were made on 1 May 1984. The grounds of objection included the following: that the assessments were invalid because they were not a bona fide exercise of the Commissioner's power and duty to assess; that the assessments were based on false information or information which did not relate to or form part of the financial records of the taxpayer; that the additional income was not assessable or not derived by the taxpayer; that the Commissioner had not properly exercised his judgment nor could an assessment be issued pursuant to s.167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act; that the assessments were not correctly made pursuant to ss.166 or 170 of the Income Tax Assessment Act as the Commissioner relied on false information which did not relate to or form part of the taxpayer's financial records, or (alternatively) without adequately ensuring that the information was truthful and reasonably related to the taxpayer; and that the Commissioner's reasons were inadequate or irrelevant and that the assessments were issued for a collateral purpose and without completion of the assessment process.

This was done in August 1985. The present appeals were transferred to this Court in September 1987 when this Court obtained jurisdiction.

The Commissioner's argument

18. The Commissioner seeks to base his argument on the power of the Court to prevent an abuse of its process. The source of the power is said to be implied, or inherent, or, alternatively, found in s.23 of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976. The Commissioner argues that, as a matter of public policy, it is an abuse of process to permit a party to civil litigation to adduce evidence contradictory of a matter necessarily decided against him by a court of competent jurisdiction. The conviction, the Commissioner submits, is conclusive of the facts on which it is based and those findings cannot be challenged in a subsequent tax appeal. Although the Commissioner acknowledges that the taxpayer may lawfully pursue these appeals, what he now seeks to challenge is the manner in which the taxpayer proposes to conduct his appeals. He contends that the finding of the Court of Criminal Appeal was that the jury must have accepted that there was an agreement to deprive the Commonwealth of its revenue at least in relation to two of the five businesses, viz. the Venus Room and Carousel. The Commissioner says that, by the question put to the taxpayer by his counsel, the taxpayer is seeking to set at nought his conviction.

The authorities relied upon by the Commissioner

19. On behalf of the Commissioner, particular reliance is placed upon the reasoning of Lord Diplock in Hunter v The Chief Constable of the West Midlands Police [1981] UKHL 13; (1982) AC 529. There, at the trial of six men on charges of murder, objection was taken to the admission in evidence of statements given to the police on the ground that they had been induced by violence by the police. Bridge J., in the absence of the jury, held, on a voir dire examination, that the prosecution had discharged the burden of proving beyond reasonable doubt that the men had not been assaulted by the police and admitted the statements. The jury convicted all six. Subsequently, the six men instituted civil proceedings against the police claiming damages for the alleged assaults. It was held by the House of Lords that where a final decision had been made by a criminal court of competent jurisdiction, it was a general rule of public policy that the use of a civil action to initiate a collateral attack on that decision was an abuse of the process of the court. Lord Diplock said (at 541):

"The abuse of process which the instant case exemplifies is

the initiation of proceedings in a court of justice for the

purpose of mounting a collateral attack upon a final

decision against the intending plaintiff which has been made

by another court of competent jurisdiction in previous

proceedings in which the intending plaintiff had a full

opportunity of contesting the decision in the court by which

it was made."

He went on to say (at 541):

"It would call for a degree of credulity too extreme to be

expected even from judicial members of your Lordships' House

to fail to recognise that the dominant purpose of this

action, and the parallel actions brought by the other

Birmingham Bombers so far as they are brought against the

police, has not been to recover damages but is brought in an

endeavour to establish, long after the event when memories

have faded and witnesses other than the Birmingham Bombers

themselves may be difficult to trace, that the confessions

on the evidence on which they were convicted were induced by

police violence, with a view to putting pressure on the Home

Secretary to release them from the life sentences that they

are otherwise likely to continue to serve for many years to come."

20. It has also been recently held in England, applying the reasoning of Lord Diplock, that where issues of fact have been litigated exhaustively in sample cases, it is an abuse of process for a litigant, who was not a party in one of the sample cases, to re-litigate all the issues of fact on the same, or substantially the same, evidence (see Ashmore v British Coal Corporation (1990) 2 QB 338; House of Spring Gardens Ltd. v Waite (1991) 1 QB 241 at 254-5).

21. In Minister for Immigration v Daniele [1981] FCA 212; (1981) 39 ALR 649, Fisher and Lockhart JJ., in considering the role of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in reviewing a deportation decision, said (at 653):

"The conviction is the genesis of the Minister's power to

deport. There are powerful reasons of public policy why the

Tribunal cannot ignore the conviction or seek to set it at

nought. That is not to say that the circumstances

surrounding the commission of the offence or matters

relating to the trial itself cannot be examined by the

Tribunal. However, such examination is for the purpose of

enabling the Tribunal to make its own assessment of the

nature and gravity of the applicant's criminal conduct and

not for the purpose of assessing the propriety of the

conviction or the fairness of the trial. Whether these

circumstances and matters are susceptible of examination,

and the extent of that inquiry will vary from case to case.

The matters which the Tribunal is called upon to determine

are wider than and different from the questions which a

trial judge and jury must determine."

22. In Minister for Immigration v Gungor [1982] FCA 99; (1982) 42 ALR 209, Fox J. and Sheppard J. each referred with approval to the above passage. Fox J. said (at 213):

"While it stands, the conviction must be conclusive, so far

at least as concerns a Tribunal reviewing a decision which

takes the conviction and the Minister's decision as its

starting point. When I say conclusive, I mean conclusive as

to the guilt of the accused in relation to the offence

charged, and of the sentence imposed...Quite obviously,

serious practical questions arise if the position is

otherwise. The Tribunal could presumably arrive at its own

decision as to whether the person concerned did what he was

charged with doing, and for that matter, what sentence his

offence merited. It would be doing so on material gathered

and considered at what could be a long time after the trial,

when some witnesses for the prosecution were unavailable and

memories were in any event dimmed. Accepted trial

procedures would be absent. The Crown, as repository of the

function of criminal prosecutions, would not be a party.

The Tribunal might in the end find itself substituting its

own view for that of the jury."

23. Sheppard J. said that the Tribunal should not allow evidence to be led which had the effect of "going behind the conviction and setting it at nought" (at 233). He said (at 233-4)"

"In my opinion it was not open to the respondent to lead

evidence for the purpose of showing that he was a principal

in the second degree rather than in the first. Certainly it

was not open to the Tribunal to treat him as convicted on

the basis of that conduct rather than upon the basis of

conduct which made him a principal in the first degree. To

proceed as it did involved it in an error of law.

I do not believe that such a conclusion is greatly

restrictive of the Tribunal's difficult and important

function in deportation cases. If the view I have proposed

prevails, the Tribunal remains entitled, indeed bound, to

examine for itself what was involved in the entirety of the

conduct of the applicant before it. This examination will

include the receiving of evidence to put the tribunal in a

position to weigh and to make its own assessment of the

seriousness of the conduct which has led to the conviction.

Nevertheless, I concede that the view I favour may, in some

cases, have an inhibiting effect on the Tribunal. It may

also create a degree of difficulty for it, because it will

need to determine whether particular evidence sheds light on

the way conduct on the part of an applicant for review

should properly be viewed or whether its real effect, if

accepted, would be to go behind the conviction. It will be

necessary for the Tribunal to be aware at all times of the

purpose for which it receives (or has received) the evidence

and for which it uses the evidence. If the evidence in

question is in truth evidence which involves the applicant

in seeking to go behind the conviction or to have the

conviction set at nought, it ought to be put aside;

otherwise it will need to be weighed along with all other

evidence for the purpose of deciding whether to remit the

matter to the Minister for reconsideration in accordance

with the Tribunal's recommendation."

24. The Commissioner also relies upon the decision of Hill J. in Commissioner of Police v Butler [1989] FCA 408; (1989) 91 ALR 293 and Sioeli Siu Lai v Minister for Immigration (Morling J. unreported, 5 April 1992). As well, New Zealand and Canadian authorities were relied upon (see for example, Brant v Collector of Customs (1984) 1 NZLR 280 and Demeter v British Pacific Insurance 13 DLR (4th) 318).

25. The public policy considerations in this area were recently considered in the High Court of Australia in Giannarelli v Wraith [1988] HCA 52; (1988) 165 CLR 543 at 558, per Mason C.J.; see also per Wilson J. at 574.

26. Counsel for the Commissioner also referred to the United States doctrine of "collateral estoppel" or "issue preclusion". Under this doctrine, an issue necessarily decided in a cause of action determined by a court of competent jurisdiction, is conclusively determined if it is involved in a subsequent, different cause of action. The requirements for the doctrine are first, that there be an identity of issues between the two actions and secondly, that the party against whom the estoppel is asserted must have had a full and fair opportunity to contest the prior action. Mutuality of estoppel is no longer a prerequisite. A litigant may use collateral estoppel "offensively" in a suit against the party who lost on the decided issue in the first case (see Parklane Hosiery Co. v Shore [1979] USSC 8; 439 US 322 (1979)). The doctrine is justified by considerations of judicial efficiency and consistency of result. A recent statement of the doctrine was made in Klein v Commissioner of Internal Revenue 88O F 2d 260 (10th Cir., 1989) by Holloway C.J. at 262-3 as follows:

"Whether the application of collateral estoppel is

appropriate necessitates four inquiries: first, whether the

party to be estopped was a party to or assumed control of

the prior litigation; second, whether the issues presented

are in substance the same as those resolved in the earlier

litigation; third, whether the controlling facts or legal

principles have changed significantly since the earlier

judgment; and finally, whether other special circumstances

warrant an exception to the normal rules of preclusion."

27. The American position thus seems to be close to the Anglo-Australian "abuse of process" doctrine.

Was there an abuse of process in the present case?

28. To determine whether process has been abused in the present context is essentially a question of fact: is the process of the Court being used for a legitimate purpose or is it being used for a foreign or ulterior object? (see I.O.C. Australia Pty. Ltd. v Mobil Oil Australia Ltd. (1975) 49 ALJR 176 at 182.) As Sheppard J. pointed out in Gungor, the relevant inquiry is with respect to the real purpose or object in carrying out the examination of the facts underlying the conviction. For the present, the question is whether the matter put to the taxpayer by his counsel was something done with the purpose or object of merely challenging the Commissioner's assessment, on the one hand; or, on the other hand, was it something done with the purpose or object of setting at nought the conviction?

29. The matter, I think, should be considered objectively. So viewed, I have come to the conclusion that, in all the circumstances, (and having regard in particular to the fact that the taxpayer's statement, Ex. A, has been tendered and received in evidence without objection), the putting of the additional question to the taxpayer was an abuse of the process of the Court and, thus, impermissible.

30. It is common ground that the taxpayer may legitimately pursue his statutory right of appeal against the assessments. By not objecting to the tender of Ex. A, the Commissioner has accepted that the material (now evidence) in that document may properly be advanced by the taxpayer in support. By that evidence, the taxpayer seeks to demonstrate that he did not derive the income assessed. This is the central question in the appeals which, it is common ground, may lawfully be agitated. But the additional matter sought to be put to the taxpayer by his counsel in the course of his examination in chief raises, I think, a question which is collateral to the tax appeals yet, as counsel for the taxpayer now concedes, it was critical to his conviction. In my opinion, there is no legitimate reason for the taxpayer's now pursuing this matter, collateral as it is, in the exercise of his statutory right to challenge the assessments by way of appeal to this Court. The proper inference is that the matter has been raised with a view to setting his conviction at nought.

31. I would answer the question raised by the special case accordingly.

32. Since writing the foregoing, I have had the advantage of reading the judgments of Davies J. and of Lockhart J. I agree with Lockhart J. that -

"The applicant may conduct his case in the income tax

appeal presently before this Court for the purpose of

enabling the Court to determine the true liability of

the applicant to income tax in each of the relevant

years of income involved in the appeal;"

I also agree with Lockhart J.'s statement that -

"But the applicant shall not conduct his case for the

purpose of challenging the propriety of his conviction for

the criminal offence of conspiring with Mr Anderson to

defraud the Commission or the fairness of the trial."

However, Lockhart J. has also held that

"...and for this purpose (i.e. for the purpose of

prosecuting his tax appeal) the applicant may present

a case denying that he and James McCartney Anderson

agreed that not all the cash received from the conduct

of businesses in which they were involved, including

the nightclub businesses known as the Venus Room and

the Carousel, would be declared for income tax purposes."<`

33. I regret that I cannot agree that the presentation of such a case involves a legitimate purpose. It will be recalled that the subject of the special case was a question put by counsel for the taxpayer in the course of his examination of the taxpayer in chief. In substance as well as in form, the question put asked whether the taxpayer had conspired not to declare income, i.e. to defraud the Commonwealth. It did not, for instance, ask whether the parties were in partnership, which would be a different matter. The question put is, in my view, merely a paraphrase or restatement of the terms of the indictment. As I have already said, on its face the question put by counsel was not directed to the issues in the tax appeal, but rather was directed to a collateral matter, viz., the setting at nought of his conviction. (It will be recalled that the issue of conspiracy was not raised by either party in the statements of issues.) Having so characterised the question, it must follow, in my view, that the putting of this question in chief was not a proper use of the Court's process. It was something done, not with the object of contesting the issue whether the taxpayer derived the income assessed, but with the different object of contradicting the fact that the taxpayer had been guilty of a conspiracy.

34. In short, in my view, counsel for the taxpayer could not, in chief at least, have asked the taxpayer whether he had conspired to defraud the Commonwealth. The question now put, in my opinion, is in substance the same question.

35. In this respect, I differ from Lockhart J. only in the application of the relevant legal principle. We are agreed on the principle itself.
