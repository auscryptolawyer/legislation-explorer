ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - share of partnership net loss - transfer of trading stock - sale of land for developers to associated partnership - subsequent sale to Hong Kong company - section 36A(2) elections by parties - whether elections apply - sham transactions - whether deduction allowable for bad debt on non payment by purchaser.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – Ss 36,36A,51,63.

FC of T v Westraders Pty Ltd (1980) 140 CLR 55; 121 ATR 24; 80 ATC 4357

Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd [1967] 2 QB 786

FC of T v Gulland 85 ATC 4765

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant company was, at the relevant tine, a member of a partnership established for the purpose of trading in land. As a result of the alleged sale of the trading stock of the partnership, the partners lodged a declaration in terms of section 36A of the Income Tax Assessment Act so as to obtain the benefit of sub-section 36A(2). The respondent denies that the declaration was effective. The applicant also claims that the partnership incurred a bad debt as a result of the sale of the land to the purchaser and claims its share of the bad debt. The applicant claims a deduction amounting to $92,298 in respect of the net loss of the partnership.

2. The relevant provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act were (as at 30 June 1974):

"36(1) Subject to this section. Where -

(a) the taxpayer disposes fey sale ... property being trading stock, ..,

(b) that property constitutes or constituted the whole or part of the assets of a business which is or was carried on by the taxpayer; and

(c) the disposal was not in the ordinary course of carrying on that business,

the value of that property shall be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer, and the person acquiring that property shall be deemed to have purchased it at a price equal to that value."

"36(8) For the purposes of this section ... -

(a) the value of any property ... shall be -

(i) the value of the property ... on the day of the disposal; or

(ii) ...;

(b) ... ."

"36A(1) where, for any reason, including -

(a) the formation or dissolution of a partnership; or

(b) the variation in the constitution of a partnership, or in the interests of partners,

a change has occurred in the ownership of, or in the interests of persons in, property constituing the whole or part of the assets of a business and being trading stock, ... and the person, or one or more of the persons, who owned the property before the change has or have an interest in the property after the change, section 36 applies as if the person or persons who owned the property before the change had, on the day on which the change occurred, disposed of the whole of the property to the person, or all the persons, by whom the property is owned after the change."

"36A(2) Where -

(a) a property in relation to which the last preceding sub-section applies has become, upon the change in ownership or interests, an asset of a business carried on by the person or persons by whom the property is owned after the change;

(b) the person or persons by whom the property was owned before the change holds or hold, after the change, an interest or interests in the property of a value equal to not less than one quarter of the value of the property; and

(c) the person or persons by whom the property was owned before the change together with the person or persons by whom the property is owned after the change give notice to the Commissioner, In accordance with this section, that they have agreed that this sub-section shall apply in respect of the property, the value of the property, for the purposes of section 36, shall be, instead of the value specified in paragraph (a) of sub-section (8) of that section, the value (if any) that would have been taken into account at the end of the year of income if no disposal had taken place and the year of income had ended on the date of the change."
  1. The applicant company was a member of a partnership known as "Land Company". There were 20 members of the partnership, each member being an associated company.
  2. The applicant company was incorporated in 1971.
  3. The memorandum of association of the company stated, inter alia, that its objects were to carry on business as land developers, sub-dividers, dealers in improved and unimproved lands, earthmovers, road builders, drainage contractors, sewerage contractors, electrical contractors and general contractors.
  4. The company's business was described in its annual income tax returns as "developer". The income tax returns of Land Company described the business of the partnership as "land trading".
  5. The Land Company partnership began to carry on business from 1 July 1971 under a partnership agreement dated 9 July 1971. The business of the partnership was described in the partnership agreement as real estate sub-dividers, land developers and builders. The capital and assets of the partnership were held in equal shares by the partners. It is apparent from the partnership agreement that the real and effective control of the partnership's business was exercised by one R, who also who had real and effective control of each of the 20 companies constituting the Land Company partnership. R also held the controlling "A" class share in each company and, for good measure, was also nominated to represent each company in the business of the Land Company partnership.

8. The accounts of Land Company Partnership lodged with the partnership's income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1974 disclosed, inter alia, the following (cents ignored) in respect of the period 1 July 1973 to 7 June 1974:

After bringing into account certain miscellaneous amounts of income and expenses including a substantial expense in respect of interest charges the net profit for the period was shown as $1,836,423.

  1. This loss was shown as distributed to each partner in an amount of $25,667. He have set out the dates above as they are set out in the return. As will appear later, the inconsistency between 7 and 8 June was shown in evidence to be of no consequence, because the relevant date is in fact 14 June 1974.
  2. In a schedule to the partnership return lodged by Land Company, the following document appeared (edited to maintain confidentiality):

"To: The Commissioner of Taxation

... ...

THE COMPANIES the names of which are listed in the First Schedule hereto (herein called "Land Company") all of which companies are duly incorporated in the State of ... and have their registered offices at ... and N LIMITED a company duly incorporated and having its registered office in ... .

HEREBY notify you pursuant to Section 36A of The Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936-1973 as amended that Land Company Group and S Limited have agreed that 36A(2) of the said Act shall apply in respect of the property described in the Second Schedule hereto and the change of ownership of that property occurring pursuant to a Deed bearing date the 14th June, 1974 and made between the Land Company Group of the one part and the Land Company Group other than P Pty Limited and N Limited of the other part pursuant to which the Land Company Group sold and transferred all their right title and interest in the said property to N Limited and the Land Company Group other than P Pty Limited."

  1. P Pty Ltd was a member of the Land Company partnership, but not of the C-N Partnership.
  2. By a deed dated 14 June 1986 and executed in Hong Kong, the partners of Land Company except P Pty Ltd (C Group) agreed to enter into partnership with N Ltd, a company incorporated in Hong Kong and having its registered office in Hong Kong. N Ltd is registered as a foreign company in Queensland. The business of the partnership was stated to be land traders and dealers in real estate. The capital of the partnership was agreed to consist of the sum of $100, to be contributed by the parties as to one quarter thereof by N Ltd and as to each of the Land Company partners joining the partnership, three eightieths thereof, except W Pty Ltd which was to hold three fortieths thereof. Prima facie then, sub-section 36A(2) applied and the partnerships were entitled to make the election.
  3. That means the new partnership was to be enabled to bring the land to account for tax purposes at its value for tax purposes in the old partnership. In other words, the profit made by Land Company in selling the land to the C-N Partnership was not assessable in the hands of Land Company. The deemed sale price in accordance with the sub-section 36A(2) election was shown in the return of the partnerships as $1,858,770 and the actual sale price was $3,250,000.

16. The Commissioner refused to accept that section 36A applied to the sale from Land Company to C-N Partnership and in his statement required to be furnished in accordance with Income Tax sub-regulation 35(1), the Commissioner asserted, inter alia:

"The provisions of section 36A of the Act have no application to the purported transfer of the trading stock (i.e. the land in question) by Land Company to the C-N Partnership and the value of the land was properly included in the assessable income of Land Company in accordance with section 36 of the Act."

17. The Commissioner treated the proceeds of the sale from Land Company to the C-N Partnership as assessable income and allowed a deduction for the cost of the land.

18. The land in question was not held as to legal ownership by all the partners in Land Company, although it is beyond doubt that Land Company owned the land beneficially.

19. The C-N Partnership return of income for the year ended 30 June 1974 was the first income tax return lodged by the partnership, it having commenced to carry on business on 14 June 1974. The trading accounts of the partnership were made up for the period 7 June 1974 to 30 June 1974 while the copy of the balance sheet lodged with the return was as at 25 June 1974. Exhibit F is a partnership agreement duly stamped by the Stamps Office Hong Kong and dated 14 June 1974. Clause 1 of this partnership agreement states that the partnership was to commence on the date of the partnership agreement, i.e. 14 June 1974. Clause 2 provides for the business to be carried on under the name of "Land Company" - the same name as the partnership from which the C-N partnership purchased the land.

20. Clause 21 of the partnership agreement provides:-

"If upon the determination of the partnership there shall be any assets remaining in the ownership of the partnership then the same shall be purchased from the partnership by the C Group in equal shares as tenants-in-common absolutely at a value to be agreed upon by all of the partners or failing such agreement at a value to be certified by Messrs Blank, Chartered Accountants, Black Stump, as the cost price to the partnership of those assets."

  1. The C Group comprises those members of the C-N Partnership other than N Ltd. Although the partnership agreement was apparently executed in Hong Kong, the law of the State of Queensland was nominated to be the proper law of the contract.
  2. Counsel for the taxpayer conceded that the dates shown in the partnership's return were inaccurate on the basis that 14 June 1974 was the date on which the C-N Partnership came into being.
  3. Exhibit G is an agreement dated 14 June 1974 between Properties Pty Ltd, a company incorporated in Queensland (and one of the partners in the Land Company partnership and the C-N Partnership) of the first part, the companies constituting the Land Company partnership of the second part and the companies constituting the C-N Partnership of the third part.
  4. The deed recites that Properties Pty Ltd is the registered proprietor in fee simple of the land set out in the Third Schedule to the Agreement. The Third Schedule discloses the same details with respect to the land as the Second Schedule to the section 36A election, referred to at para 13 above, and also details the values attributable to each parcel of land listed, with the total consideration being shown as $3,278,000, whereas Land Company disclosed in its return that the consideration was $3,250,000.
  5. Certain parcels of land included in the Third Schedule were subject to registered first mortgages securing a principal sum of $859,510 as set out in the Fourth Schedule to the agreement.
  6. Consideration for the sale of the land from Land Company to the C-N Partnership was stated to be $2,390,490, the recitals making it clear that this amount was arrived at after allowing for the $859,510 secured by first mortgage over certain parcels of land. The debt due by the C-N Partnership was repayable on demand and interest was to be charged at 12% per annum.
  7. The deed stated that Properties Pty Ltd held the land in trust for the C-N Partnership. In so doing the company was subject to the general directions of the partnership through a management committee of the partnership.
  8. We have already referred to the fact that counsel for the applicant accepted that the C-N Partnership came into being on 14 June 1974.
  9. Exhibit J is the journal and ledger cards of the C-N Partnership. The journal shows an entry dated June 7 as follows:

"Stock (A) $3,250,000.00

Creditors Land Company $2,437,500.00

N Ltd 812,500.00

Creditors Land Company 644,632.50

N Ltd 214.877.50

Loans 859,510.00

being purchase of interest in all land being trading stock of Land company on formation of partnership between Land Company 3/4 interest and N Ltd 1/4 interest."

The notation to the journal entries also sets out the value of each parcel of land as well as details relating to the loans.

  1. The journal also discloses that on June 14 1974, sales were apparently made of two blocks of land to arms-length purchasers. The journal also shows that on June 18, a further block of land was sold to another arms-length purchaser. Total consideration on sale of these three blocks was $18,610, while the journal recorded the cost of sales as being $15,900. We will return to these transactions.
  2. As already noted. Properties Pty Ltd held the various parcels of land in trust for Land Company until 14 June 1974 when it held the land in trust for C-N Partnership. Exhibit K is a certified copy of an agreement dated 14 July 1974 between Properties Pty Ltd of the first part, the Land Company partnership of the second part and a company listed on the Stock Exchange which we will describe as "Public Company" of the third part. The deed was stamped by the relevant stamp duties office on 10 July 1974. In essence, the deed provided that the land previously held on trust by Properties Pty Ltd for Land Company would now be held on trust by Public Company for Land Company. Acting in pursuance of this agreement. Properties Pty Ltd transferred by memorandum of transfer dated 22 August 1974 all its estate and interest in the land described in the memorandum of transfer to Public Company. That transfer was registered in September 1974.
  3. Exhibit P is a copy of an unsigned deed dated 25 June 1974 between Public Company of the first part, the companies forming the C-N Partnership of the second part and N Ltd of the third part. The document recites that Public Company is the registered proprietor of the land set out in the Second Schedule (in the same terms as mentioned in para 24 above), which is held in trust for the C-N Partnership, described in the deed as the Vendors. It states that the Vendors have unanimously agreed to dissolve the C-N Partnership, having agreed to sell the subject parcels of land to N Ltd upon the terms and conditions set out in the agreement for a consideration of $3,250,000, the purchaser assuming the liability for $859,510 in respect of the mortgages.
  4. Public Company declared it would henceforth hold the land in trust for N Ltd, subject to the mortgages and covenanted to carry out promptly and obey all directions of the purchaser (N Ltd) in relation to the said lands subject to being indemnified by N Ltd.
  5. The vendors and the purchaser agreed that sub-section 36A(2) of the Act applied to the sale and that a notice of election in accordance with section 36A would be given to the Commissioner of Taxation.
  6. Exhibit 0 is deed dated 25 June 1974. This deed effects dissolution of the C-N Partnership at the request of the partners other than N, with effect from 25 June 1974.
  7. Exhibit Q is a copy of a deed dated 26 June 1974 whereby Public Company and N Ltd agreed that N Ltd convey its beneficial interest in the subject land to Public Company for a consideration of $3,278,000.
  8. By a telex message dated 28 June 1974, C Group demanded immediate payment by N Ltd. That was followed by a further telex of the same date from C Group to N Ltd requesting confirmation that the payment demanded had been forwarded by telegraphic transfer. A third telex followed from C Group to an individual, referring to the earlier telex message and advising that unless a reply was received within 30 minutes, C Group would proceed for recovery of the monies due. Those three telex messages brought forth a response from N Ltd wherein it advised that it was totally unable to repay the amount due (Exhibit S).
  9. By a document dated 28 June 1974, R instructed Land Company's accountant to write off as a bad debt the amount of $2,362,490 owing by N Ltd (Exhibit U).
  10. On 1 July 1974 R wrote on behalf of Land Company to a firm of solicitors advising that N Ltd was insolvent and unable to pay for the purchase of the lands sold to it by the C Group (not the C-N Partnership) and instructing the solicitors to take all steps possible to obtain payment of the funds owing (Exhibit T). A letter of demand dated 25 July 1974 was sent by the solicitors (Exhibit U).
  11. R explained in evidence that Land Company had substantial unrealised profits in its land holdings at the relevant time. These had a book value of approximately $1.8 million and a market value in the vicinity of $3.2 million. Land Company also had substantial current year realised profits of approximately $1.8 million (Exhibit A, Folio 43) for the period to 14 June 1974. It is clear that R had formed the intention of transferring the land held by Land Company to Public Company without realisation of profits for tax purposes. It is also apparent and not denied that it was proposed to ensure that substantial allowable deductions became available to Land Company so as to reduce its taxable income. The documents exhibited and referred to above form the basis of the arrangements and we will now put flesh on the bare bones by summarising our findings of fact.
  12. R sought advice from the principals of a company known as Speedo Mining. It appears those persons advised R to follow certain arrangements which would have the effect of reducing Land Company's liability for income tax and at the same time ensuring that no state stamp duty was payable. The transactions entered into were designed to convert Land Company's net profit into a loss for taxation purposes. The evidence of R on this point was quite clear and unequivocal.
  13. Running side by side with the taxation arrangements was a scheme to transfer the land trading activities of Land Company to Public Company, so that the land trading activities of R, previously conducted through Land Company, would in future be conducted through Public Company. However, the land was not transferred direct from Land Company to Public Company because of the expressed desire to avoid or minimise taxation.

43. The C-N Partnership and N Ltd were interposed into the transfer of the land from Land Company to Public Company because of that desire to avoid or minimise taxation.

44. Exhibit 2 is a copy of a notice dated 11 June 1974 whereby the Secretary of Public Company notifed the holding of an extraordinary general meeting on 28 June 1974 in accordance with the resolution of directors dated 10 June 1974 (Exhibit E). The resolutions to be proposed at the general meeting of Public Company were set out in the notice as follows (edited to maintain confidentiality):

"1. The approval be and is hereby granted to the Agreement entered into by the Directors on the 11th day June, 1974, for the acquisition by the Company of certain lands from Land Company.

2. That approval be and is hereby granted to the Agreement entered into by the Directors on the 25th day of June, 1974, for the acquisition by the Company of certain lands from N Ltd."

  1. During cross examination, R acknowledged that he had caused this notice to be sent out by Public Company. However, he was unable to explain why the directors had decided to take this proposed course although he was present at the meeting of directors which proposed the resolutions for the extraordinary general meeting. We find the answers by R on this matter evasive, and we find that on the probabilities, the directors of Public Company took this course of action because they were aware of the predetermined arrangement for allegedly selling the land to N Ltd on the basis that it would, in fact, be sold to Public Company. In making their decision to hold the extraordinary general meeting at their meeting of 10 June 1974 (Exhibit E,) the directors of Public Company must have been aware of the proposed scheme for sale of the land from Land Company to Public Company, and we so find. It follows that R, as the controlling mind of Land Company and as the chairman of the meeting held on 10 June 1974, must also have been aware of the proposed arrangements.
  2. We should add that the first resolution proposed for the meeting of 28 June 1974 refers to a matter which is claimed not to be in contention before the Tribunal. However, R was unable to explain this first resolution, and we are therefore left to speculate as to its significance, if any, to the issues in dispute before the Tribunal. Notwithstanding the attempts by counsel for the applicant to explain that the first resolution had no relevance to these proceedings (pages 8 and 64 of transcript), we are satisfied, on the evidence, that the resolution is relevant for the following reasons:

(a) Exhibit C is a circular to shareholders of Public company dated 2 May 1974 and issued by a partnership of 13 companies of which R was apparently the controlling mind. The circular was apparently signed by R or at least issued in his name. It included the following:

"PROPOSED ACTIVITIES OF THE GROUP

The purpose of the acquisition by the R family of a controlling interest in Public Company is to provide the public with an interest in the real estate activities of the family. Until recently the real estate activities were carried on by 66 private companies operating as four partnerships. Plans for the re-arranging of the group are summarised below.

Land Company (20 companies in partnership) has specialised in the subdivision of broad acres into residential, industrial, and acreage lots. It is intended that the stock of Land Company will be sold to Public Company. This transaction will occur at or below an independent real estate valuation of the land involved, and will be subject to approval by shareholders in general meeting. The R family will not vote at such meeting. The amount of land involved will be in excess of $3.5 million and will be financed partly from normal sources and the balance by a medium term unsecured loan from the R family at 9%".

(b) As we will go on to show the legal and equitable interests in the land owned by Land Company were separated and passed, according to the documents, by separate and different routes into the hands of Public Company.

  1. We are therefore satisfied that it was the intention of R, as the controlling mind of Land Company to sell the legal and equitable interests in the land holdings of the partnership to Public Company, and that the directors of Public Company were of the same mind, hence the first proposed resolution in the minutes of the meeting of 10 June 1974 and the notice to members of Public Company dated 11 June 1974 (Exhibits E and 2 respectively). It has already been noted that Land Company's stock of land was held on trust by one of the partners in Land Company - Properties Pty Ltd. It is not open on the evidence to suggest that Public Company was only acquiring the legal interest in the land. The shareholders were clearly advised that the stock of land would be sold to Public Company.
  2. As noted above, Properties Pty Ltd and Land Company agreed in writing on 14 June 1974 whereby Public Company would hold the subject land on trust for Land Company, its administrators and assigns absolutely. Exhibit K includes a copy of a memorandum of transfer dated 22 August 1974 whereby Properties transferred the land to Public Company.
  3. On the same day (ie 14 June 1974), there was a meeting of the directors of N Ltd held in Hong Kong at which the draft partnership agreement for the C-N Partnership was tabled (Exhibit F). Also before that meeting of directors was the deed dated 14 June 1974 between Properties Pty Ltd and the C-B partnership (Exhibit G). It appears, although there is only hearsay evidence, that both of these documents were executed in Hong Kong on that day. We infer that to be the fact.
  4. Exhibit G provides for the sale of the subject land to the C-N Partnership by Land Company for a consideration of $2,390,490 after allowance from the present value of the lands of $859,510 secured thereon by first mortgages.
  5. Exhibit F, as we have noted earlier, provides for the creation of the C-N Partnership, which according to Clause 2 of the Agreement was to trade under the name of Land Company. It will be apparent from this that no outward change was intended to occur so anyone outside the R empire, such as persons purchasing land, would ostensibly be dealing with the same organisation. It is also relevant to note that the bank nominated for the C-N Partnership was an Australian bank.
  6. The sale agreement executed on 14 June 1974 by Properties Pty Ltd, Land Company partners ("the old partners") and the C-N partners ("the new partners") (Exhibit G) for sale of the beneficial interest contains clauses which depend for their operation on the fact of Properties Pty Ltd is the registered proprietor of the subject lands. By Clause 7, Properties Pty Ltd warrants to the new partners that it is the registered proprietor in fee simple of the said land, subject to the first mortgages set out in the deed. In Clause 2, Properties Pty Ltd declares it will hold the land for the new partners absolutely by the direction of the new partners.
  7. As we have already noted, the legal ownership of the lands was agreed to be transferred by Properties Pty Ltd to Public Company by a deed dated 14 June 1974 (Exhibit K) in which Properties Pty Ltd acknowledges that it holds the subject lands in trust for Land Company. The deed further declares that Land Company has requested that Public Company become the registered proprietor of the subject lands as trustee for Land Company. Properties Pty Ltd declared that it would hold the said lands as and when they are transferred to it upon trust for Land Company, its administrators and assigns absolutely.
  8. It seems to be clear that Properties Pty Ltd remained the legal owner of the lands until the memorandum of transfer was executed on 22 August 1974 (Exhibit K), whereby Properties Pty Ltd transferred the subject lands to Public Company. That transfer was registered by the Titles Office on 23 September 1974.
  9. We return to the transfer of the beneficial ownership from Land Company to the C-N Partnership effected by the deed dated 14 June 1974 (Exhibit G). That deed did not set out the title description of the land being sold, but used general terms designed to identify the tracts of land, using the name of the vendor to Land Company to identify the particular tracts.
  10. The books of account of the C-N Partnership were in evidence before the Tribunal (Exhibit J). These reveal that three blocks of land were sold to customers between June 14 and June 18 for a total consideration of $18,610. The cost of these sales is shown as $15,900, leaving a gross profit on these sales of $2,710. The description of this land in the accounts of the ON Partnership does not allow the Tribunal to determine whether such land was part of the land transferred by Land Company to the C-N partnership.
  11. For present purposes we will refer to these three blocks of land as lots 5, 6 and 13.
  12. Lot 5 was sold to Mr and Mrs 8 pursuant to a conditional contract executed on 28 May 1974. The contract was between Properties Pty Ltd and Mr and Mrs B. It was conditional upon Mr and Mrs B obtaining finance (Exhibit 3). Mr and Mrs B obtained an offer of finance by a letter from a finance company dated 28 June 1974 and accepted that offer in writing on 10 July 1974 (Exhibit 4). R, on behalf of Properties Pty Ltd, executed a memorandum of transfer dated 25 June 1974 in favour of Mr and Mrs B. The memorandum of transfer was stamped on 19 September 1974.
  13. In cross examination, R agreed that it was not normal procedure to execute a memorandum of transfer in respect of a contract of sale which was still conditional on finance being obtained by the purchaser.
  14. The same position prevailed with respect to lot 6, which was sold to one T by a conditional contract executed on 27 May 1974. This contract was also conditional upon finance being obtained by the purchaser which was offered by the financier on 28 June 1974, accepted on 9 July 1974, with the land being transferred by a memorandum of transfer, apparently dated 21 August 1974 (the day in August is unclear on the copy before the Tribunal), stamped by the Stamp Duties Office on 21 August 1974, with settlement on 27 September 1974 (Exhibit 5).
  15. The sale of lot 13 to one "S" followed a similar course with the contract of sale being signed on 1 June 1974 by the purchaser and Properties Pty Ltd, with settlement within 30 days. Notwithstanding that the memorandum of transfer was signed by Properties Pty Ltd on 24 June 1974, the memorandum is stamped on 5 September 1974, with settlement apparently taking place on 24 September 1974. R described this sale as a cash sale, with settlement on 30 June 1974 according to the contract.
  16. We are satisfied that lots 5, 6 and 13 were not sold on account of the C-N Partrnership, notwithstanding the entries in the partnership's books of account. He are supported in this view by a schedule attached to the income tax return of Land Company for the year of income ended 30 June 1974. This schedule listed debtors of Land Company as at 30 June 1974 and included "B", "T", and "S" as debtors for the full amounts of the consideration payable under the contracts. Although the point was not established before us, we infer that the deposits paid by the respective purchasers were held by a stakeholder as at 30 June 1974.
  17. Exhibit 8 is a copy of the C-N Partnership bank account ledger sheet from 25 June 1974 - when the account was opened - to 30 October 1974. It does not disclose receipt by the partnership of the proceeds of sale of the blocks of land to "B", "T" and "S".
  18. For good measure, cheques for expenses paid and claimed by the C-N Partnerships are dated 18-19-20 June 1974 on cheque forms issued by the bank on 25 June 1974 (the day the partnership purported to sell the land to N Ltd) and which is confirmed by the chequebook register of the bank (Exhibit 7) and the debit to the bank account (Exhibit 8). The first of the cheques drawn by the partnership was cleared by the bank on 2 July 1974. R, in effect, conceded in cross-examination that the cheques had been backdated.
  19. As might be imagined, there is another side to these arrangements to which we have not yet alluded. As seems to be standard procedure in these types of cases, a flurry of activity in the movement of funds around a chain of companies was necessary to apply the necessary "gilding" so as to make gold out of straw. R displayed a general lack of detailed knowledge of the financial arrangements, notwithstanding that he was the controlling mind of most of the entities. It seems he relied to a considerable extent on arrangements made by the principals of Speedo Mining and R's own professional advisers. Certainly one can well believe that if R had momentarily blinked on 28 June 1974, he would have missed the whole series of contemporaneous transactions.

66. It is not necessary for us to describe these financial transactions in detail (which are set out in graphic form in Exhibit 15, although not all the transactions shown on that exhibit were formally proved at the hearing of this matter). Suffice it for present purposes to say:

(a) on 28 June 1974 N Ltd paid C-N Partnership $28,000;
  • (b) on 28 June 1974 Public Company paid N Ltd $2,418,490 being the net value of the lands sold by N Ltd to Public Company;
  • (c) on 28 June 1974 N Ltd paid $2,390,490 to Finance Pty Ltd a company controlled by R, which had loaned $3,250,000 to N Ltd;
  • (d) on 28 June 1974 Finance Pty Ltd paid $2,240,490 to another company controlled by R and $150,000 to a company controlled, so it seems to us, by the principals of Speedo Mining; and
  • (e) other than for the $28,000 mentioned in (a) above N Ltd made no further payment to the C-N partnership and that partnership made no payment to Land Company.

67. The quite extraordinary aspect of this is that Public Company was prepared to pay N Ltd for the land when it must have known that N Ltd was insolvent and could not pay the C-N Partnership, which in turn could not pay Land Company.

  1. It further appears from the evidence of an investigating accountant, called on behalf of the applicant, that the transactions relating to the purchase and sale of the land do not appear in the accounting records of N Ltd held in Hong Kong (transcript page 129).
  2. The amount of $3,250,000 loaned by Finance Pty Ltd to N Ltd was apparently expended on acquiring shares in companies associated with R and Speedo Mining. He do not think it is necessary to explore these transactions, but they explain why N Ltd was devoid of funds when it purported to purchase the land from C-N Partnership and why, when N Ltd received funds from Public Company, it repaid portion of the loan made by Finance Pty Ltd rather than pay C-N partnership for the land.
  3. It will be clear from what we have said so far that the whole efficacy of the arrangements entered into by the applicants depends upon a finding that there was an effective transfer of the equitable ownership of the subject lands to N Ltd, firstly in its capacity as a partner in C-N Partnership, and secondly, in its own right absolutely.
  4. So far as the creation of the C-N Partnership is concerned we find that there was a deliberate plan to take advantage of the provisions of section 36A so as to ameliorate the operation of section 36 by the transfer of the subject lands from Land Company to Public Company. That plan was intended to reduce the incidence of tax in accordance with specific provisions of the taxing Act. It follows that because the C-N Partnership was intended to produce the very effect for which it was created, what was done cannot be regarded as a sham. Neither can the transfer of the equitable interest in the land to the C-N Partnership be construed as a sham. There was a deliberate intent to transfer the equitable interest in the land to the partnership and this was effected. What was done was clearly within the terms of the decision of the High Court in FC of T v Westraders Pty Ltd [1980] HCA 24; (1980) 144 CLR 55; 11 ATR 24; 80 ATC 4357.
  5. Land Company was carrying on a business of trading in land and that land formed part of the trading stock of Land Company up to the time of the disposition to C-N Partnership. The sale to C-N Partnership is fully explicable and consistent with the intention to transfer the lands from Land Company to Public Company and to take advantage of the (then) provisions of sections 36 and 36A of the Act. Be that as it may, such a transfer can hardly be regarded as in the ordinary course of the business of Land Company.
  6. He are therefore satisfied that section 36A(2) operates to deem the sale price of the land, when sold by Land Company to C-N Partnership, as being the value that would have been taken into account at the end of the year of income if no disposal had taken place and the year of income had ended on the date of the change. That means that Land Company correctly treated the stock of land as sold for $1,858,770 for tax purposes, rather than the sale price of $3,250,000 shown in its trading profit and loss account.
  7. That brings us to a consideration of the declaration made under sub-section 36A(2) by the members of the C-N Partnership upon the sale of the land to N Ltd. He have already concluded that the three blocks of land sold for $18,610 were the subject of conditional contracts between Land Company and the purchasers up until 30 June 1974 inclusive. Because of this, the C-N Partnership did not derive assessable income from this source. There are two reasons for this view. The first is that, as the contracts were still conditional as at 30 June 1974, no assessable income had been derived by any person up to that date. The second reason is that the assessable income was derived by either Land Company or Public Company, but not by the C-N Partnership.
  8. It has not been established to our satisfaction that the C-N Partnership ever carried on any business of trading in land. It is established that it made no sales of land. As for outgoings, it paid various expenses on its last day of operation, which we are satisfied must have been incurred by Land Company. Certainly the fact that C-N Partnership purported to carry on business under Land Company's name indicates to us that the reality of the situation is that Land Company did carry on the business through Properties Pty Ltd right up to the time when ownership in the land passed to Public Company. This finding is enough to deny the C-N Partnership the benefit of the election under sub-section 36A(2).
  9. However, even if that view is wrong, we are not satisfied that the C-N Partnership cones within the terms of section 36, because the only sale of land transacted by the Partnership is the alleged sale to N-Ltd. Given the clear intention of R as the controlling mind of, inter alia, C-N Partnership, to sell the subject land to Public Company, the only view open on the facts is that C-N Partnership was established for the purpose of appearing to sell the land to N Ltd. C-N Partnership effectively did very little, if anything else. We have therefore concluded that the alleged sale of land was in the ordinary course of the partnership's business because that was the only business the partnership had - to be a conduit through which the land could be passed to its ultimate destination.
  10. We have dealt with the sub-section 36A(2) election separately before dealing with the transactions between C-N Partnership and N Ltd. In the alternative, we have concluded that the purported sale to N Ltd was a sham. In Snook v. London and West Riding Investments Ltd [1967] 2 QB 786 at 802, Diplock LJ considered the nature of a sham in the following dicta:

"As regards the contention of the plaintiff that the transactions between himself, Auto Finance and the defendants were a 'sham', it is, I think, necessary to consider what, if any legal concept is involved in the use of this popular and pejorative word. I apprehend that, if it has any meaning in law, it means acts done or documents executed by the parties to the 'sham' which are intended by them to give to third parties or to the court the appearance of creating between the parties legal rights and obligations different from the actual legal rights and obligations (if any) which the parties intend to create. But one thing, I think, is clear in legal principle, morality and the authorities (see Yorkshire Railway Wagon Co v. Maclure (1882) 21 Ch D 309 CA and Stoneleigh Finance Ltd v Phillips [1965] 2 QB 537) that for acts or documents to be a 'sham', with whatever legal consequences follow from this, all the parties thereto must have a common intention that the acts or documents are not to create the legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating. No unexpressed intentions of a 'shammer' affect the rights of a party whom he deceived. There is an express finding in this case that the defendants were not parties to the alleged 'sham'. So this contention fails."

78. Our reasons for finding "sham" are:

(a) at all relevant times it was the intention of R, as the controlling mind, to sell the subject land to Public Company;

(b) legal ownership of the land remained at all relevant times with Properties Pty Ltd;

(c) N Ltd was for all intents and purposes insolvent at the relevant time, and R must have been aware of this state of affairs through the dealings of Finance Pty Ltd with N Ltd;

(d) the alleged sale to N Ltd having taken place on 26 June 1974, Land Company demanded payment on 28 June 1974;

(e) telex advice from N Ltd dated 28 June 1974, to the effect that N Ltd was "unable to repay the amount due to C-N Partnership", was accepted;

(f) the amount of $2,362,490 said to be owing by N Ltd was written off by C-N Partnership as a bad debt on 28 June 1974;

(g) on 26 June 1974 Public Company and N Ltd agreed to transfer the equity of redemption and beneficial interest in the said lands to Public Company and released it from its obligations as trustee of the said lands;

(h) on 28 June 1974, the shareholders of Public Company resolved (on the motion of R) that the lands be acquired from N Ltd; and

(i) on 28 June 1974, Public Company paid N Ltd for the lands acquired, whilst on the same day the C-N Partnership was writing off as a bad debt the amount alleged to be owing by N Ltd over the same land.

  1. These and other resolutions "were intended to create the appearance of creating between the parties legal rights and obligations different from the legal rights and obligations (if any) which the parties intended to create", insofar as they purported to transfer the beneficial ownership of the lands to N Ltd. The purported sale by C-N Partnership to N Ltd is therefore deprived of legal effect because it has no reality in law (cf per Dawson J; FC of T v Gulland 85 ATC 4765 at 4793.
  2. Having found that the purported sale of the land to N Ltd was a sham, what are the consequences of that finding. The subject land was purportedly sold to Public Company by H Ltd on 26 June 1974. It is beyond doubt that at all relevant times it was intended that Public Company become the owner of the land subject to the rights of the mortgagees. It follows that the sale to Public Copmpany was not a sham and that either N Ltd sold the land to Public Company as agent for C-N Partnership, or C-N Partnership sold the land under the guise of N Ltd. We think the latter is the only rational explanation. There is therefore no relevant declaration for the purposes of section 36 and therefore the sale must be brought to account at full value in the ascertainment of the taxable income of the C-N Partnership.

81. Before the Tribunal the applicant led evidence designed to show that although the land was sold to Public Company for $3,278,000 before allowing for the mortgages amounting to $859,510 the market value of that land was only $1,721,336 and that is the amount which should be adopted for the purposes of sub-section 36(8).

82. Mr Hanger QC, of senior counsel for the Commissioner, submitted that the notice of objection did not embrace the question of value. In our view grounds 4(a) and 5(c) of the notice of objection raise the question of value of the land, albeit those grounds claim that the value should be the original cost of the land. While, with respect, we see some basis in Mr Hanger's submission, we are inclined to follow the dicta of Mansfield J in D v. Commissioner of Taxes (Old) (1940) 6 ATD 25 where his Honour stated: (at p 41)

"Any doubt as to wheher a ground is included in a notice of objection should be decided in favour of the taxpayer, but although I incline to the opinion that the grounds as stated in the notice of objection do not set out fully and in detail this objection, my decision upon the alternative contention of the Commissioner renders it unnecessary for me to make a decision on the preliminary objection."

We find ourselves in the same position as his Honour. As we find for the Commissioner on the valuation point it is not necessary for us to make a decision on Mr Hanger's objection.

83. To adopt a hypothetical valuation in the face of evidence from the market place would require more courage than we possess. The proven fact is that the land was sold to a public company for an amount described in the minutes of a meeting of shareholders of Public Company held on 28 June 1974, at which R was the Chairman. Four other directors, the secretary and three shareholders are recorded as being present. The relevant part of the minutes (edited to maintain confidentiality) reads as follows (Exhibit R):

"The Chairman drew attention to the special statement of information circularised to shareholders prior to the acquisition of ... which stated that Public Company intended to purchase all the trading stock held by Land Company. The purpose of this, he continued, was to enable a large bank of land to be maintained by the company in order to produce saleable lots as and when required.

Hence, the following resolutions were designed to enable the purchase of lands described in Schedule A and Schedule B to be enacted. The purchase price is set out in Column 1 and shown in Column 2. The total valuation is $4,020,100 and the total purchase price is $3,741,934.50. Before moving the resolutions the Chairman announced that he would not vote due to his involvement on both sides of the transaction, however he stated that the other directors recommended the proposals."

The relevant lands were included in Schedule A. That schedule shows the purchase price as being $3,278,000 and the valuation as $3,466,650. He think the purchase price is the market value of the lands transferred on the day of disposal whether that day be 25 June 1974 or 26 June 1974 and if it were necessary to do so we would be of the opinion that the value of $3,278,000 is fair and reasonable. The amount of $3,278,000 less $859,510 is assessable income in the hands of the C-N Partnership.

  1. We turn now to the claim in respect of a bad debt because of the non-payment by N Ltd for the alleged purchase of the land. As we have concluded that the sale to N Ltd was a sham and therefore of no effect, it follows that there was no relevant debt existing which could be written off as a bad debt. It also follows that the amount of $28,000 paid to the C-N Partnership by N Ltd was a windfall gain and not assessable income. We would therefor exclude this amount from the assessable income of C-N Partnership.
  2. The Tribunal will set aside the objection decision and remit the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the following directions with liberty to apply:
    • (1) the section 36A notice lodged with the Commissioner in respect of the sale of land from Land Company to C-N Partnership was a valid notice within the terms of section 36A(2) and fixed the value for the purposes of section 36;
    • (2) the purported sale of land by C-N Partnership to N Ltd was a sham and therefore of no effect, the real sale being to Public Company for a net consideration of $2,418,490 (after allowing for bad debts secured amounting to $859,510 in favour of mortgagees);
(3) alternatively, section 36 did not apply to the alleged sale of land by C-N Partnership to N Ltd;

(4) the sales of lots 5, 6 and 13 were made by Land Company and the income derived by that partnership;

(5) the various expenses paid by C-N Partnership on 25 June 1974 were incurred by Land Company;

(6) the C-N Partnership is not entitled to any deduction in respect of the bad debt claimed in respect of the alleged debt owing by N Ltd;

(7) the C-N Partnership is not entitled to any deduction in respect of the loss said to have arisen on the sale of the land to N Ltd; and

(8) the payment of $28,000 received by the C-N Partnership was not assessable income in the hands of C-N Partnership.
