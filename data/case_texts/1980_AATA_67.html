Board of Control of Michigan Technological University and Deputy Commissioner of Patents [1980] AATA 67 (19 November 1980)

Last Updated: 13 March 2007

Application for review of the decisions of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents refusing an extension of time to lodge a patent application and restoration of same

Decision refusing application for extension of time set aside and time extended - S160(2) Patents Act 1952, s.151 Patents Act 1952, reg.7B(6) Patents Regulations

Decision refusing restoration affirmed - meaning of undue delay - reg7B(8)(a) - (c) , -reg.92, s3(3) Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

Re: BOARD OF CONTROL OF MICHIGAN TECHNOLOGICAL UNIVERSITY

And: THE DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF PATENTS

No. 78/12033

AAT Decision No 419

Davies J.

19 November 1980

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. 78/12033

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: BOARD OF CONTROL OF

MICHIGAN TECHNOLOGICAL

UNIVERSITY

Applicant
And: THE DEPUTY COMMISSIONER

OF PATENTS
Respondent

DECISION

The Tribunal hereby :

(1)sets aside the decision of Mr P.F. Kildea, Deputy Commissioner of Patents, delivered 3 November 1978, in so far as the same refused an application by the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University under S160(2) of the Patents Act 1952 for an extension of time within which to make an application under reg7B(6) of the Patents Regulations and, in lieu thereof, extends the time for the making of such application under reg7B(6) to the extent necessary to validate the application for restoration of patent application no.80103/75 made by written application dated 3 March 1978;
(2)affirms the decision of Mr P.F. Kildea, Deputy Commissioner of Patents, given on 3 November 1978, in so far as the same refused the application by the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University for the restoration of lapsed patent application no.80103/75.

DATED this nineteenth day of November 1980.

J.D. DAVIES, J President

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. 78/12033

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: BOARD OF CONTROL OF

MICHIGAN TECHNOLOGICAL

UNIVERSITY

Applicant
And: THE DEPUTY COMMISSIONER

OF PATENTS
Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

19 November 1980 J.D. DAVIES, J

This is a review of a decision of Mr P.F. Kildea, Deputy Commissioner of Patents, refusing an application by the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University under S160(2) of the Patents Act 1952 for an extension of time within which to make an application under reg.7B(6) of the Patents Regulations and refusing an application by the same applicant under reg.7B(8) for the restoration of lapsed patent application no.80103/75. The decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents was given on 3 November 1978. Both the Act and Regulations have since been amended and, in this review, it is necessary to consider the Act and Regulations in the form in which they then stood.

An application for the grant of a patent for an invention entitled "Method for Agglomerating Steel Plant Waste Dusts" was lodged on behalf of the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University by Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co., registered patent attorneys. The application, which was given the number 80103/75, was dated 10 April 1975 and was received in the Patent Office on 14 April 1975. The application was accompanied by a complete specification. On 10 April 1975, Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. advised the US patent attorneys for the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University that the application had been filed. On 21 April 1975, Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. advised the US patent attorneys that the complete specification had been lodged on 14 April 1975 and that the application had been allocated number 80103/75. Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. advised that examination of the application should be requested within five years and that continuation fees to maintain the application in force would become payable annually from 14 April 1977.

On 8 May 1975, a direction was given under reg7B(3) of the Patents Regulations requiring that a substitute specification complying with the provisions of Part II of the Third Schedule of the Patents Regulations be lodged. On 26 May 1975, Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. wrote to the Commissioner of Patents requesting that the examiner advise the specific pages required to be replaced by substitute pages. A note endorsed on that letter on 24 June 1975 by an acting Formalities Officer records an opinion that:

"In my opinion pages, la, 6a to 8, 12, 13, 25A, 32, have to be substituted as they contain, broken letters or exceed the maximum length, or contain alterations, or a combination of all three reasons. The remainder of the specification is of marginal quality with the majority of pages containing broken letters in the top 5 lines to a lesser degree than those mentioned above. Recommended that pages la, 6a to 8, 12, 13, 25A and 32 be substituted."

On 26 May 1975, Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. wrote to the US patent attorneys:

"Re: BOARD OF CONTROL OF MICHIGAN TECHNOLOGICAL UNIVERSITY - U.S. 287,346. Australian Patent Application No. 80,103/75 entitled: 'Method for Agglomerating Steel Plant Waste Dusts'.

We have received the enclosed notice from the Commissioner to file a retyped specification as the specification originally filed is a xerox copy and some of the pages lack sufficient clarity for the patent office reproduction requirements.

We have noted your request of 16th April, 1975 to file new pages 26A and this will be attended to with the filing of the new specification. No loss of priority is occasioned with this requirement.

In page 12 received by us there are a number of manuscript alterations and it would be appreciated if you could let us have details of amendment A and B."

The US patent attorneys responded on 4 June 1975:

"Re: Docket 1790-A - Australian Patent Application No. 80,103/75 corresponding to U.S. 287,346 'METHOD FOR AGGLOMERATING STEEL PLANT WASTE DUSTS' - Board of Control of Michigan Technological University

Gentlemen:

We received your May 26, 1975 letter forwarding a notice from the Commissioner to file a retyped specification.

As you have noted, some amendments were made to the original U.S. application during prosecution. The purpose of this letter is to inquire whether the retyped specification should include these amendments or be an exact copy of the original application."

Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. wrote on 11 June 1975:

"Re: Australian Patent Application No. 80,103/75 corresponding to U.S. 287,346 in the name of BOARD OF CONTROL OF MICHIGAN TECHNOLOGICAL UNIVERSITY entitled: 'Method for Agglomerating Steel Plant Waste Dusts'.

We refer to your letter of 4th June, 1975 and suggest that you let us have a draft of the proposed amendments. We will prepare the specification and the Statement of Amendments which will accompany same and the specification will be examined with the amended specification. The requirement is that I have to certify that the refiled specification is a true and correct copy of the original specification."

On 24 June 1975, the US patent attorneys wrote to Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. advising of a small number of amendments to be made on page 12 of the original specification. Thus, the provision of the substitute specification was left in the hands of Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. who had advised on 11 June 1975 that:

"We will prepare the specification and the Statement of Amendments which will accompany same and the specification will be examined with the amended specification. The requirement is that I have to certify that the refiled specification is a true and correct copy of the original specification."

By letter dated 30 June 1975, Messrs RT Gregory & Co. was informed by the Assistant Commissioner (Administration) that the direction of 8 May 1975 had been reconsidered and still stood and that the specification contained broken letters and was thus not suitable for reproduction by photographic means, further that many pages exceeded the maximum length and contained alterations. Messrs RT Gregory & Co. thereafter failed to attend to the direction or to the amendments requested by the US patent attorneys and failed to lodge a substitute specification.

Sub-regulations (1), (3) and (4) of reg7B of the Patents Regulations read as follows:

"(1) Where a complete specification received at the Patent Office does not comply with the requirements referred to in regulation 7, or the requirements of sub-regulation (2) of regulation 13, of these Regulations, the Commissioner may treat the complete specification as not having been lodged at the Patent Office or treat it as having been so lodged.

....

(3) If under sub-regulation (1) of this regulation the Commissioner treats a complete specification as having been lodged at the Patent Office, he may, within three months after the date on which it was lodged, direct the applicant to do such things as are necessary to ensure that the complete specification complies with the requirements referred to in that sub-regulation.

(4) If an applicant to whom a direction is given under the last preceding sub-regulation in relation to a complete specification fails to comply with the direction within six months after the date on which it was given, the application to which the complete specification relates shall lapse."

Pursuant to these provisions, the application for the grant of a patent lapsed. The lapsing was due to the failure to comply with the direction which required, in effect, that a copy of the complete specification typed so as to accord with the requirements of Part II of the Third Schedule to the Patents Regulations be substituted for the complete specification lodged with the application.

Sub-regulation (5) of reg7B provided:

"(5) Where an application has lapsed under the last preceding sub-regulation, the Commissioner shall advertise that fact in the Official Journal."

By letter dated 9 March 1976, Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. were advised by the Patent Office that the application would be advertised as "Lapsed, Not Open to Public Inspection" in the Official Journal on 11 March 1976. The lapsing was so advertised. Sub-regulation (6) of reg.7B provided:

"(6) The applicant may, within three months after the date of the advertisement under the last preceding sub-regulation, make an application (in this regulation referred to as 'the application for restoration1) to the Commissioner for the restoration of the lapsed application."

Again, however, no application for restoration was made within this three months' period. Indeed, no step at all was taken in the matter by Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co..

On 15 March 1977, the US patent attorneys wrote to Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co.:

"We have not heard from you since June 11, 1975. What is the status of the above case?"

On 31 March 1977, the US patent attorneys wrote again to Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co.:

"The above case has come up on our records for payment of the continuation fee due April 14, 1977. You may take this as your authorisation to pay the fee. Please acknowledge receipt of these instructions."

On 7 April 1977, Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. wrote to the US patent attorneys:

"We advise that in accordance with your instructions we have attended to the payment of the continuation fee on the above application and enclose our account. The next fee will become payable on or before 14 April 1978."

However, the decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents records and I accept that:

"The records of this Office show that a continuation fee was forwarded to this Office on 12 April 1977 but was returned to the original Australian patent attorney on 20 April 1977 accompanied by a Patent Office Form SX and a Treasury Refund Form 17. In accordance with normal practice, the letter from the original Australian patent attorney which accompanied the fee would have been endorsed to the effect that the fee was not receivable for the reason that the application had lapsed by virtue of regulation 7B and that the fee was available for refund."

Notwithstanding this refund, no action was taken by Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. with respect to the application.

In November 1977, as a result of doubts which had arisen in the minds of the US patent attorneys as to the progress of a number of applications which were being handled by Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co., Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons, registered patent attorneys, were instructed to establish the status of the subject patent application and others and to amend the address for service to their firm from that of Messrs RT Gregory & Co.. On a telephone enquiry to the Patent Office, Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons were advised that the application had lapsed. That firm sent a telex to the Patent Office advising change of address for service and requesting, "...advise last official action which lead to lapsing".

By letter dated 9 November 1977, the Patent Office advised Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons that the application had lapsed, not open for public inspection, pursuant to reg7B(4) and that the lapsing had been advertised in the Official Journal on 11 March 1976. Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons were informed that a continuation fee would have been due on 14 April 1977. On 4 November 1977, Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons wrote advising that they were now the address for service. On 5 December 1977, Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons by telex requested a copy of the specification which had been lodged. On 3 March 1978, Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons forwarded to the Commissioner of Patents an application for extension of time under s160(2) (a) and an application for restoration of the lapsed application. The applications, which were received on 7 March 1978, were accompanied by supporting evidence.

The applications for extension of time and for restoration were refused by the Deputy Commissioner of Patents. In his decision, the Deputy Commissioner expressed the view that the provisions of s160 were not applicable to the lodgment of an application for restoration under sub-reg7B(6) and that, as the application for restoration was not lodged within the prescribed time, three months, it was not possible to restore the application for the grant of a patent. These refusals are the subject of this review.

Section 160 of the Patents Act provided, inter alia:

"(2) Where, by reason of -

(a)an error or omission on the part of the person concerned or of his agent or attorney; or
(b)circumstances beyond the control of the person concerned,

an act or step in relation to an application for a patent or in proceedings under this Act (not being proceedings in a court) required to be done or taken within a certain time has not been so done or taken, the Commissioner may, upon application by the person concerned, but subject to this section, extend the time for doing the act or taking the step.

(3) The time for the doing of an act or the taking of a step may be extended under either of the last two preceding sub-sections although that time has expired."

Before me it was submitted on behalf of the respondent that S160(2) permitted only an extension of time for the doing of an act or the taking of a step in relation to an application for a patent which was subsisting. It was submitted that an application which had lapsed was not subsisting though it might be restored. Dr J.McL. Emmerson, who appeared for the Deputy Commissioner, submitted that an application for restoration of the lapsed patent was not "an act or step in relation to an application for a patent". In support of his submission, Dr Emmerson relied upon the decision of Kitto J in The Eimco Corporation v. Commissioner of Patents [1960] HCA 1; (1959-60) 103 CLR 521 and my decision in Re Toyo Pulp Co. Ltd. and Deputy Commissioner of Patents, 2 ALD 177. However, both those cases were concerned with a situation where the act sought to be done was an act which was required to be done by "the applicant". Kitto J said that the words, "the applicant" sufficed to give a clear indication that the sub-section with which he was concerned referred only to an application which was still an application in the course of being prosecuted. At p 182 of the Toyo Pulp decision, I said:

"A divisional application may be made at any time, but it must be made by 'the applicant'; A person who does not hold this character may not make a divisional application. To act pursuant to s.160 to authorize a person who is not 'the applicant' to make a divisional application would be to act inconsistently with the clear legislative intent expressed by s49A."

Both those decisions were dealing with contexts different from that with which we are concerned.

In my view, an application for the restoration of a lapsed patent application is "an act or step in relation to an application for a patent". In the context of reg7B(6) the words "the applicant" referred to the person who was the applicant for the grant of a patent under the application which has lapsed. The words did not refer to the applicant under an application for the grant of a patent which was still subsisting. I see nothing in the terms of s160 itself which required that the extension of time be granted in relation to a subsisting application. Rather, the extension of time might be granted for the doing of "an act or step in relation to an application for a patent" whether or not the prescribed time had expired. In my view, an act or step required to be taken for the restoration of an application for a patent is "an act or step in relation to an application for patent". In neither the Eimco case nor the Toyo Pulp case was it said that s160 itself was limited to an act or step in relation to a subsisting application. Rather, it was said that, in these cases, time could not be relevantly extended because, even if time were extended, the person granted the concession would not hold the character of "the applicant". In the present instance, if time is extended, the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University will hold the necessary character of "the applicant". In their context, the words "the applicant" in reg7B(6) referred to the person who was the applicant under the application for the grant of a patent which has lapsed.

It was next submitted by Dr Emmerson that the provisions of reg7B constituted a code and thereby excluded the operation of the general provisions of s160. Dr Emmerson relied upon my decision in the Toyo Pulp case where I said that the granting of an extension of time under s.160 so as to affect the time limits provided by s54 would be inconsistent with the provisions of s53. However, reg7B was different from s53. There was nothing in reg7B, other than the prescription of the time limit, from which an intent that s.160 should not apply could be inferred. And the mere prescription of a time limit did not of itself give rise to such an inference for s160 did not operate until an act or step required to be done or taken within a certain time had not been so done or taken. Section 160 was a provision of the Act having a general operation in relation to time limits prescribed by the Act and the Regulations. An intention that it should not apply to a particular time limit should not be readily inferred and I see nothing in reg7B from which such an inference could or should be drawn.

It follows, in my opinion, that the Commissioner of Patents had authority under s160 (2) to extend the time prescribed by reg7B(6) for the making of the application for the restoration of the lapsed application for the grant of the patent. That authority is exercisable by this Tribunal. Section 151 of the Patents Act provides that an application may be made to this Tribunal for a review of a decision of the Commissioner made under s160 of the Patents Act. Accordingly, the. question arises whether, in all the circumstances of the case, time should be extended so as to permit the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University now to make application for restoration of its lapsed patent.

From the applicant's point of view, there would clearly be substantial hardship if the extension of time were not granted. Its application for a patent was filed on 14 April 1975 and should have proceeded in the ordinary way. No blame can be attributed to the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University or its US patent attorneys for the lapsing of the application. The latter were attentive to the application and the application was in the hands of an Australian patent attorney. The event which occurred was a most unfortunate incident which should not have occurred. In my view, time should be extended in favour of the applicant unless the public interest can be seen to prevail over the applicant's interests. But a consideration of reg.7B shows that the public interest is, in this case, sufficiently protected by sub-reg.(8) for that sub-regulation precludes the restoration of an application for a patent in a case where there has been undue delay. The grant of an extension of time will not adversely affect the public interest for it will not enable the restoration of the lapsed patent application if there has been such delay. No element of public interest other than that arising out of undue delay here suggests itself. I am therefore of the view that time should be extended to the extent necessary to validate the application for restoration of patent application no.80103/75 made by written application dated 3 March 1978.

I turn now to consider the application that application no.80103/75 for the grant of a patent should be restored. Dr Emmerson submitted that I should not enter upon a consideration of this question. He submitted that the decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents was restricted to the question of the applicability of s160 to the provisions of reg.7B. He said it was not open to this Tribunal to go beyond the decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents and to deal not only with the application under s160 but also with the application under reg7B(8). This submission repeated part of a submission made to me when this matter first came on for hearing. It was then submitted that the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review the decision of the Deputy Commissioner for Patents for, it was said, the Deputy Commissioner had simply held that he had no power to grant the extension of time or to exercise the authority conferred upon him by reg7B(8). The present submission is based on the latter part of that argument.

Regulation 7B(8) provided that, on application being made, if the Commissioner was satisfied as to compliance with certain conditions he should restore the lapsed application, but, if he was not so satisfied, he should refuse the application for restoration. Regulation 92 provides that an application may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for the review of a decision made by the Commissioner in the exercise of powers conferred upon him by reg7B(8). Therefore, a decision by the Commissioner refusing an application for restoration is a decision which is reviewable by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. And s3(3) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act provides that a reference in the Act to a decision includes a reference to "refusing to make an order or determination" and to "refusing to do any other act or thing". In my decision on jurisdiction, delivered 11 May 1979, I said:

"The decision in that case (Collector of Customs (New South Wales) v. Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd, [1979] FCA 21; 2 ALD 1) confirmed that the exercise of jurisdiction by this Tribunal is not dependent upon the existence of a valid decision made by the decision maker and that jurisdiction is conferred in cases where the decision, whether valid or not, was made or purported to be made under the relevant enactment."

My decision on jurisdiction was the subject of appeal to the Federal Court in Deputy Commissioner of Patents v. Board of Control of Michigan Technological University 28 ALR 551. The Court disallowed the appeal. At pp.562-3 Smithers J said:

"He (the Deputy Commissioner) entertained, heard argument upon, and finally ruled upon two applications. Whether he granted the applications or, as in this case, rejected them, he acted in the exercise of the power conferred upon him, in the one case by s160(2), and in the other by sub-reg 7B(8). What he actually did is appropriately described in the conclusion of the learned Deputy President in the following terms : 'When Mr Kildea considered and refused the application ... he was exercising or purporting to exercise the authority conferred upon him by s160(2) so to do and his decision was both a decision under s.160 of the Patents Act and a decision made in the exercise or purported exercise of powers conferred by s160 of the Patents Act. Similarly, his decision to refuse to restore the lapsed application, being a decision made on an application to him to do so, was a decision made in the exercise or purported exercise of the powers conferred upon him by reg7B(8).' "

Franki J, expressing a similar view, said, at p.569:

"Once again for the same reasons I consider that reg92, where the words are decision made in exercise of the powers' under reg7B(8), should be interpreted to cover a decision where the Commissioner has said he had no power to exercise the power conferred upon him by reg7B(8). If one adopted any other construction it would mean that in many cases the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review decisions of the Commissioner not to exercise a power given to him under reg7B(8). This interpretation in my opinion would be contrary to the approach taken by the majority of this court in Collector of Customs (NSW) v. Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd, supra."

Keely J expressed a similar view.

The Deputy Commissioner of Patents had before him two applications, both in proper form, the one being an application for an extension of time and the other being an application for restoration of a lapsed application. Although the applications were not advertised as required by reg 7B(5), both nevertheless were brought on for hearing before the Deputy Commissioner for Patents. His decision by its heading expressed itself to be a decision in the matter of lapsed application no.80103/75, in the matter of an application for restoration thereof and in the matter of an application for an extension of time. The decision considered relevant aspects of both extension of time and restoration and, at pp12-13, considered the requirement contained in reg.7B(8) that the Commissioner be satisfied that there has been no undue delay in the making of the application for restoration. At pp17-21, the Deputy Commissioner discussed the evidence which had been lodged in support of both applications and, at the conclusion of his decision, said:

"Leaving aside deficiencies in the evidence and the other difficulties to which I have referred, because I have reached the conclusion that I have no legal power to do so, I am not authorised to grant the applications for an extension of time and for restoration of the lapsed application."

In my view, that decision was a decision refusing both applications including the application for restoration of the lapsed patent application. Although the decision was expressed to result from lack of legal power to grant the applications, the decision nevertheless was a decision which considered both applications and refused them.

Thus, this Tribunal has adequate power to review the decision in so far as it refused the application for the restoration of the lapsed patent application. And, indeed, the decision of the Federal Court concludes that matter. I have given consideration as to whether, in this review, if I were of the opinion that an appropriate extension of time should be granted, I should remit the matter back to the Commissioner for his consideration of the merits of the application for restoration of the lapsed patent. It seems to me, however, that this would not be an appropriate course. In my view, the decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents considered in detail the merits of the application for restoration of the lapsed patent. The views expressed were adverse to the applicant's interests. This is not a case where the substance of the matter was not considered by the decision under review. The substance of the matter was considered and the application was refused, notwithstanding that the formal ground of the decision was expressed to be no legal power to grant the applications. In the circumstances, I think it is appropriate that this Tribunal should itself exercise the. decision-making power conferred by reg 7B(8). I therefore turn to consider the terms of that provision.

The granting of an extension of time so as to permit the application for restoration to be made does not determine that application. It simply permits the application to be made. Regulation 7B(8) specified its own criteria. The first matter on which the Tribunal must be satisfied is that:

"(a) the failure to comply with the direction within the prescribed time was unintentional and resulted from an error or omission on the part of the applicant or of his agent or attorney or from circumstances beyond the control of the applicant;".

The direction with which there was failure to comply was a direction as to the filing of a substitute specification. It is clear that the failure to comply was, so far as the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University, its US patent attorneys and Mr RT Gregory himself were concerned, unintentional and, further, that it resulted from circumstances beyond the control of the applicant and from an omission which occurred in the office of Messrs RT Gregory & Co. The evidence does not disclose the precise circumstances as to how the omission came about, but the evidence discloses clearly that there was inadequate control of the office staff, that there was insufficient surveillance by Mr Gregory of the work of his staff and that, as a result, omissions occurred which, in a well-conducted office, ought not to occur. The likelihood that the failure to comply with the direction arose from inefficiency, lack of diligence or oversight is so high that I think that this is not a case where any further investigation of the facts is required. Deliberate omission on somebody's part is so unlikely that I think it need not be considered as a real possibility. I am therefore satisfied that the omission was unintentional. It is unnecessary for me to consider whether condition (a) would have been satisfied had the facts been that a member of the staff of Messrs RT Gregory & Co had knowingly let time pass without complying with the direction. It may well be that in such a case it would still be proper to be satisfied that the omission was unintentional. The person who had undertaken the responsibility of complying with the request was Mr RT Gregory himself. The US patent attorneys and the applicant understandably left the compliance with such a formality in his hands. Mr Gregory has given evidence that the omission was unintentional so far as he was concerned. I am inclined to think that that would be enough. Nevertheless, however the omission came about, I am satisfied that it was unintentional.

The Tribunal must next be satisfied that the applicant has complied with the direction. The evidence shows that this was done. A retyped specification accompanied a letter from Edwd Waters & Sons to the Commissioner of Patents dated 3 March 1978.

The next requirement is that "there has been no undue delay in the making of the application for restoration". It was on this requirement that most of the argument centred. Dr Emitter son contended for the view that the effect of sub-regs.(6) and (8) was that an application for restoration of a lapsed patent application must not only be made within three months after the date of the advertisement but that it must also be made without undue delay. Dr Emmerson submitted that undue delay could occur before the expiration of the three months' period and necessarily occurred if no application was made by the time of the expiration of the period. Dr Emmerson alternatively submitted that the delay which occurred until the application for restoration of the patent was made, from 11 March 1976 to 7 March 1978, was undue.

Mr RK Searby, QC, senior counsel for the applicant, submitted, first, that the time did not start to run for the purpose of undue delay until Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons became aware in November 1977 that the application for the patent had lapsed and that, having regard to the complexities of the problem, there was no undue delay while the matter was in the hands of Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons. He submitted, alternatively, that, as there was no known prejudice to any other person, the time which elapsed from 11 March 1976 to 7 March 1978 was not undue.

In my opinion the words "undue delay" were not required by their context to denote a period of less than three months. I have already said that, in my opinion, in an appropriate case, the period of three months prescribed by sub-reg(6) might be extended. I think that, in an appropriate case, a period of more than three months from the advertisement in the Official Journal might be shown to have elapsed without there having been undue delay. Nevertheless, the prescription of the period of three months was not without significance. It was part of a scheme laid down by the Act and Regulations which created a structure for the grant of monopolies in respect of inventions. Necessarily, the structure contained a pattern of time limits which are intended to achieve the systematic processing of applications for letters patent. Thus, s 41 dealt with the time for the lodging of the complete specification. Section 45 provided for the priority date to be given to claims. Sections 47 and 47A made provision for the lodging of a request for the making of an examination of an application and the complete specification and s47C provided that, where an applicant did not request the making of an examination of the application and specification before the expiration of five years from the date of lodgment of the complete specification or, where he had been directed by the Commissioner to make the request, before the expiration of six months after the date on which the direction was given, the application should lapse. Section 47D provided for the payment of a continuation fee and for the lapsing of an application if the continuation fee was not paid. Section 54 provided that the period within which an application and complete specification might be accepted was twelve months after the date on which the first report of the examiner arising out of the examination of the application and specification was sent to the applicant. The section further provided that the Commissioner might extend the period within which the application and complete specification could be accepted but an extension of time in this respect should not be granted for a period expiring more than twenty-one months after the date otherwise specified for acceptance. The above provisions are examples of the structure against which the requirement of undue delay is to be tested.

The delay which occurred from the date of the advertisement in the Official Journal was a period of two years. During this period, on 14 April 1977, a continuation fee would have become payable had the application not already have lapsed. Payment of that fee was not accepted by the Patent Office, or was refunded by the Patent Office, for the reason that the patent had lapsed. At that point of time, members of the staff of Messrs RT Gregory & Co, although not Mr RT Gregory himself, were again reminded of the position with respect to this patent. But no action was taken.

Persons in the office of Messrs RT Gregory & Co. must have known from the Official Journal in March 1976 that the application had lapsed. It is the duty of patent attorneys carefully to read the Official Journal. I think that the proper conclusion to draw is that the Official Journal was read and that no action was taken. Again, in April 1977, the office of Messrs RT Gregory & Co. was advised by the refund of the continuation fee that the application had lapsed. No action was taken. In my view, the delay that occurred was undue or excessive. We are not considering a matter which involved complexity. The lapsing of the application was the result of a failure to comply with a direction to lodge a complete specification properly typed so as to comply with the requirements of Part II of the Third Schedule. On Mr Gregory's evidence, this was a simple matter. The application for restoration was not a matter which would have involved difficulty or the obtaining of instructions from overseas. All relevant information was in the hands of Messrs RT Gregory & Co. All that was required was action on the part of that firm. But the firm did nothing. The delay was, in my view, undue. It was a long delay and an inexcusable delay. I take undue delay to be delay which is unreasonable or unnecessary in the circumstances of the case. The delay which occurred in this case was unreasonable and unnecessary.

The principal issue which arises is whether the delay which was occasioned by Messrs R.T. Gregory & Co. and of which the US patent attorneys and the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University were unaware was delay which should be taken to the prejudice of the applicant. The applicant itself was not aware of the delay. Nor should it have been aware of it. Nor were the US patent attorneys at fault. Nor was the four months' delay which occurred from the time when Messrs Edwd Waters & Sons undertook the matter to the lodging of the application an unreasonable delay, particularly keeping in mind the novelty of the application and the fact that the Christmas vacation intervened.

In some fields of law, negligent delay on the part of professional advisers has been held to be relevant delay. In Allen v. Sir Alfred McAlpine & Sons, Ltd and Sternberg and Another v. Hammond and Another (1968) 1 AllER 543 it was held that proceedings should stand dismissed because there had been inexcusable delay due to the negligence of the plaintiffs' solicitors.

At p.560, Salmon LJ said,

"Delay is not by any means always inexcusable. .... The real evil, however, lies in those cases which, for no reason at all, save sheer dilatoriness on the part of the plaintiff's solicitor, do not come on for trial until many years, sometimes five, six or even ten years after the accident out of which they arose. Such dilatoriness undoubtedly amounts to negligence. Once it is generally recognised, as I have no doubt it now will be, that inordinate and inexcusable delay may and probably will result in the action being dismissed for want of prosecution and the liability to pay damages being transferred on to the shoulders of those responsible for the delay, there will, I am. confident, be a remarkable improvement - an improvement which it has apparently been impossible to achieve by any other means."

In other fields of law, particularly in the area of limitation of actions, delay or negligence on the part of a solicitor has not been attributed to the claimant personally. See Quinlivan v Portland Harbour Trust [1963] VicRp 5; (1963) VR 25, Black v City of South Melbourne [1963] VicRp 7; (1963) VR 34, and Anisiena v H. Crane Haulage Pty Ltd [1974] VicRp 81; (1974) VR 670. However, within the context in which they appeared in reg 7B, the words "undue delay" were not, I think, looking to delay which was personal to the applicant. I think the words were looking to delay affecting the making of the application for restoration. Sub-reg(6) provided for the making of an application within three months and sub-reg(8) provided a further condition, namely, that the application was not to be granted unless the Commissioner was satisfied that there had been no undue delay in the making of the application. Thus, the sub-reg(8) imposed a special condition of promptitude. The application was not to be granted if there had been unnecessary or unreasonable delay. In these circumstances, the applicant's personal knowledge of the matter seems irrelevant. Unreasonable or unnecessary delay may be due as much to the fault of a patent attorney as to the fault of the applicant personally.

This view is also supported by the consideration that the application was required to be made without undue delay after advertisement in the Official Journal. Advertisement in the Journal may not be notice to an applicant personally but, nevertheless, it was notice to the world at large including the applicant. Its effect was to give public notification of the lapsing of the patent. If an application for restoration was to be made thereafter, it had to be made without undue delay. The failure on the part of the applicant personally to read the Official Journal and thus to become aware of the lapsing was not, in my view, reasonable excuse for the delay.

In other jurisdictions, the question of undue or excessive delay which may preclude the favourable exercise of a discretionary power of extension of time has often been tested by reference to the question whether the position of other persons has changed by virtue of the lapse of time. Extension of time has been refused where it has been thought to be unfair to other persons to extend the time. In the present case, there is no evidence of hardship occurring to any other person by virtue of the lapse of time. However, we are dealing with an application for the grant of a patent of which the only public knowledge is the fact that an application was made for the grant of a patent for an invention entitled "Method for Agglomerating Steel Plant Waste Dusts". At no time has the complete specification been available for public inspection. Prior to the commencement of the hearing I directed that:

".... the Deputy Commissioner of Patents advertise in the Official Journal, as soon as practicable,

(i) the application, dated 3 March 1978, of the Board of Control of Michign Technological University for extension of time to apply for restoration of the lapsed application No. 80103/75 for letters patent;

(ii) his decision, dated 3 November 1978, refusing that application;

(iii) the application, dated 30 November 1978, of the Board of Control of Michigan Technological University to this Tribunal for review of that decision;

(iv) the provisions of s.30 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act which allow a party whose interests are affected by the decision under review to apply to the Tribunal to be made a party to the proceeding for review."

Such advertisement subsequently appeared in the Official Journal.

No person has sought to join in these proceedings with a view to opposing the grant of an extension of time. Nevertheless, the priority date of a claim is the date of lodgment of the complete specification, in this case 14 March 1975, and time runs from the priority date of the claim in favour of the monopoly if granted. Although no person presently has come forward to say that he would be prejudiced by the delay which has occurred, that is not to say that no such person exists. I therefore think that this review is to be determined by testing the delay by reference to the known facts and the overall time structure laid down by the Act rather than by seeking to ascertain whether any other particular person has been prejudiced by it.

Therefore, for the reasons I have given, I am satisfied that there was undue delay in making the application for restoration. It follows that the lapsed application for the grant of a patent should not be restored and that I should affirm the decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Patents on this point.
