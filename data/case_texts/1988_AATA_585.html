AT87/314 Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 585 (28 July 1988)

Last Updated: 30 June 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - deductions - home office - deductibility of interest

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.51(1)

F.C. of T. v Faichney 72 ATC 4245

Thomas v F.C. of T. 72 ATC 4094

Handley v F.C. of T. 81 ATC 4165

F.C. of T. v Forsyth 81 ATC 4157

Re Inglis & Anor. v F.C. of T. 87 ATC 2037

Swinford v F.C. of T. 84 ATC 4803

AAT Decision No 4512

Deputy President R.K. Todd

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

28 July 1988 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. The applicant in the present matter claimed inter alia a deduction of $5795 in the year of income ended 30 June 1986 under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA") in respect of interest on money borrowed for the construction of a study as an extension of his home. The claim was rejected both on assessment and on objection. The applicant holds a very senior position at a tertiary institution. He has many interests, and many contacts, including contacts with overseas institutions. He is obviously a man of energy and determination, and he is one for whom time does not stand still while he seeks to busy himself in his work well beyond the call of duty.
  2. The applicant's principal source of income in the year in question was derived from his salary, which is paid for the duties which he performs at the tertiary institution. He also was in receipt of interest. The house that is in question in this review is situated on a small rural block just out of the capital city where the applicant lives. From this he derived some income from a business of primary production. The business however resulted in a small overall loss.
    1. The applicant's case was presented in detail and with great clarity and care. It however boils down to this: The applicant, with the help of an architect, had built a house of a distinct architectural style which complied with an environmental philosophy of which the applicant is a strong adherent. At one end of the house was a small study. Photographs of this study in its then state were tendered in evidence, and they are reminiscent of Tristram Shandy's description of the table in his uncle Toby's room, which he referred to as "being somewhat of the smallest for that infinity of great and small instruments of knowledge which usually lay upon it". The applicant's instruments include not only books but also equipment for, inter alia, viewing slides and showing videos. He is involved in that ultimate art of teaching teachers and would-be teachers to teach, and the books and equipment that he needs to keep at home are substantial, and are accompanied by a great deal of archival material which the applicant has accumulated and of which he is reluctant to dispose.
  3. The applicant, his former study having become quite inadequate, embarked upon a project to build for himself a new study or office. He did not stint himself in expense on this, partly because it was substantial, amounting to some five squares on the old scale, and partly because he insisted on the house being extended in such a way as to be as consonant as possible with the architectural and environmental standards that he had set himself in relation to the original construction.
  4. The applicant borrowed substantially in order to bring this project into being, and in the result now claims a deduction of $5,795 in respect of interest paid by him in the year of income on a mortgage securing the loan that was made to him by a bank. The applicant emphasised a number of points.
  5. First, it was put that the added room was not part of the applicant's home. The whole project was claimed to have involved building a structure which, while attached to the house, was an office facility and not an integral part of the house. Reference was made to some awkwardness that, it was claimed, would be involved if a purchaser of the house wished to incorporate the office fully into the life-style of a purchaser and his family. Access to the office is through the kitchen, and the awkwardness referred to appeared to relate to the heater and to the telephone. I am not, I am afraid, much impressed with this submission. The room is accessed directly from the kitchen, and with some minor adjustments could in my opinion serve as a family room or as a reception area. I make this finding over the applicant's objections, for he says that it would involve the removal of walls, and a change in the heating system of the house.

7. Secondly, the applicant emphasised that he did not use the room solely for his own individual use and that students visit the house for about two hours per week on average.

8. Thirdly, it was said that the applicant was carrying on a business from the office. This argument was based on his having derived some income in the past from writing for journals and from consultancies, and that he was moving towards a "commercial orientation". This argument I entirely reject. On the facts, there was no evidence put before me that he derived business income in the year in question. I can see therefore no basis whatsoever for the proposition that the applicant was in the year of income carrying on a business from the office or from anywhere.

9. Fourthly, it was put that the applicant had two bases of operation, one from the tertiary institution and one from the office at his home. As a salaried employee he was required to complete his work, and in order to do so it was often requisite, rather than a mere preference, that he work from the office at his home. There was a factual basis for this submission in the rigorous schedule set for the applicant by himself. He found himself rising at 4.30 a.m., working from about 5 a.m. to 8.30 a.m. , with breakfast taken on the run; working at the tertiary institution from 9 a.m. until 7.30 or 8 p.m., dining at home around 8.30 to 9 p.m. and working again till retiring at 11.30 p.m.

  1. Up to around 15 years ago it was no doubt generally thought, and perhaps the Commissioner accepted, that a professional man or academic who maintained a separate room at his or her home which was used exclusively, or nearly so, for the purpose of doing work in relation to his or her business or employment was entitled to a number of deductions in respect of costs associated with maintaining such a room. The costs of heating and lighting such a room are still allowed in appropriate circumstances, no doubt in reliance on what was said by Mason J., as he then was, in F.C. of T. v. Faichney 72 ATC 4245. With, however, the decision in Thomas v F.C. of T. 72 ATC 4094 the scene changed, culminating in the decisions of the High Court in Handley v F.C. of T. 81 ATC 4165 and FC of T v Forsyth 81 ATC 4157.
  2. When one considers that the barrister taxpayers in Handley and in Forsyth were, unlike the present applicant, undoubtedly carrying on a business, and that in the study maintained at their homes they consistently did a substantial amount of work related to the carrying on of that business and yet were denied deductions, it can be seen how difficult is the path which the applicant must tread in this case. The taxpayers in the cases decided in the High Court were unsuccessful because they failed to establish that the claimed expenses were not of a capital, private or domestic nature. That being so, the only hope for an applicant in a case such as the present is, it seems to me, to convince a court or tribunal that the office used at home is the applicant's place of business. I have said before (see Re Inglis & Anor. v F.C. of T. 87 ATC 2037) that unless an applicant can establish the kind of facts found to exist in Swinford v FC of T 84 ATC 4803, or unless an applicant can establish that the office or study was the base of the applicant's business operations, such claims are now really doomed to failure and should no longer be taken on to a hearing before this Tribunal. I say this not out of criticism of the present applicant, but out of an attempt to persuade future applicants that, unless there is compliance with the rigid tests which I have mentioned, they are in truth wasting their time, and for that matter the time of the Tribunal and of the Commissioner by proceeding to a hearing.

12. In all the circumstances the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
