STAMP DUTY - transfers of Crown leases - leases held in trust for applicant pursuant to a trust deed - trustees executed transfers of leases - title to leases registered pursuant to Real Property Ordinance 1925 - consideration of effect of a transfer when registered - value of interest in the land transferred - whether deed polls also constituted agreements to transfer leases to applicant - whether deed required trustees to execute transfers in fact or in law.

REASONS FOR DECISION

18 August 1987 Deputy President C J Bannon QC

In both of these matters the applicant seeks reviews of decisions by the Commissioner of Taxation ("the respondent") notified pursuant to section 66 of the Australian Capital Territory Taxation (Administration) Act 1969 (Cth.) on 18th March 1982 assessing stamp duty on two transfers of Crown leases of blocks of land in the Stromlo District of the Australian Capital Territory, the transfers both being dated 24th September 1979.

Objections to the assessments were disallowed on 1st December 1982 and requests for reference to a Taxation Board of Review were made on 24th January 1983. As explained in the Tribunal's decision in Re Elders Resources Oil Investments Ptv. Limited and Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 87 ATC 2014 those applications come before this Tribunal for review as a result of legislation having effect from 1st July 1986.

In each case the Crown lease was held in trust for the applicant, pursuant to a deed of trust executed on 20th March 1956 by former trustees (part of Exhibit C). That deed was made between the applicant and the then trustees and acknowledged that the applicant was the sole beneficial owner of both leases. Title to the leases of the blocks of land devolved separately but in August 1968 the then trustees of each lease executed and delivered deeds poll declaring that each lease was held in trust for the applicant herein and authorising the applicant "to do all things and sign all documents necessary to convert its equitable title to a legal title in respect of the said property." However those steps were not taken, but the separate trustees themselves executed the separate transfers, copies of which are set out in Exhibit A, T51 and T52, in each case transferring "all the estate and interest in all the land" vested in them as proprietors of the Crown leases abovementioned. It is to be noted that the deeds of 1956 and of 1968 were executed prior to the coming into operation on 20th June 1969 of the Australian Capital Territory Stamp Duty Act 1969 ("the Act"). The title to each lease is registered pursuant to the Real Property Ordinance 1925 and the transfers follow the Torrens system.

Although the transfers have crossed out the printed reference to "consideration" and substituted the words "in pursuance of the provision of the Administration and Probate Ordinance 1929 as amended", they are clearly transfers and not transmission applications pursuant to clauses 135 and 136 of the Real Property Ordinance 1925. That ordinance applies to Crown leases and provides for paramountcy of the registered title in clause 52. The registered title is conclusive except for certain exceptions (clause 58). Registration of a transfer is required to give it effect (clause 57). Clause 138 provides that although the registered proprietor holds the property subject to any trusts at law, for the purposes of a dealing the registered proprietor is deemed the absolute proprietor.

The effect of a transfer, when registered, is therefore to transfer the whole of the registered title and the whole of the registered estate or interest set forth in the instrument. The instrument does not deal with trusts because trusts do not appear on the face of the register and indeed may be defeated by registration of a dealing in favour of a bona fide purchaser for value without notice. The effect of a transfer of a lease under the Torrens system is well set forth in Stonham on Vendor and Purchaser, paragraph 1799 at p 912. In my opinion the interest transferred pursuant to the instrument when registered is the whole of the registered title. Reference to the position of the transferor as a trustee is irrelevant to a consideration of the interest transferred upon registration of a Torrens system transfer.

Section 5 of the Act provides that the rate of duty to be applied is the rate specified in the third column of the First Schedule to the Act opposite to the reference to the class of instruments. It is agreed the appropriate reference is in item 5 which provides for a rate of stamp duty proportionate "to the value of the interest in the land transferred or agreed to be transferred". Item 4 uses a similar expression. "Interest" in property is nowhere defined, as it is for example in section 4 of the Gift Duty Assessment Act 1941 (Cth.)

No argument was presented to suggest that the transfers were exempt from duty pursuant to the Second Schedule to the Act nor does it appear to me that there is any provision for such an exemption. The transfers do not purport to transfer the bare legal estate of bare trustees, but transfer the whole registered estate of the leases. It appears to me that it is consonant with the observations of the High Court in DKLR Holding Co (No 2) Pty Limited v Commissioner of Stamp Duties (NSW) [1982] HCA 14; (1982) 149 CLR 431, particularly per Gibbs CJ at p 442, Mason J, as he then was, at pp 450, 451 and Brennan J at p 474 that I should hold that the interest transferred in each case is the full value of the unexpired portion of each lease with all its concomitant rights and not a bare legal title.

Learned senior counsel for the applicant however raised a further argument which has occasioned me some difficulty. Section 6(9) of the Act is in the following terms:-

"(9.) Stamp duty is not imposed on a lease, or on an instrument of transfer of an estate in fee simple in land or of a lease, for which an agreement in writing has been executed before the commencement of this Act."

Although the declarations of trust made in August 1968 were by deed poll they were nevertheless fully constituted express trusts (Jordan, Chapters on Equity, 6th edition, pp 22, 23). Counsel argued that upon delivery to the beneficiary (as happened) they also constituted agreements to transfer the leases to the applicant. It is true they authorise the applicant to sign all documents to convert the equitable title to a legal title. It is true they involve an acknowledgment that in Equity the trustees are obliged to transfer the legal title if requested, but do they amount to an agreement to transfer within the meaning of section 6(9) of the Act. The declaration of trust of 1956 has the advantage of being contained in an agreement between the applicant and the then trustees, but the same problem arises with regard to section 6(9) of the Act.

While there is no express term contained in the deed of 20th March 1956 (Exhibit C) requiring the trustees to transfer the Crown leases to the applicant, nevertheless it may be suggested that such an obligation is implied. I do not consider such a term is implied in fact upon the principles considered in BP Refinery (Westernport) Pty Ltd v Shire of Hastings [1977] HCA 40; (1977) 16 ALR 363 at 376, but is it a term implied by law, treating "law" in these days of parallel or confused streams of law as encompassing equity. Under the terms of the deed of 1956 the trustees were not bare trustees but trading trustees. (See Jacobs' Law of Trusts in Australia, 5th edition, paragraphs 317, 318). But it is not clear that under the 1956 deed the beneficiary had a right to extinguish the trust and call for the registered estate at least until the trustees duties of management were completed and the trustees indemnified (see Jacobs ibid, paragraph 2308). I do not consider that it is open to me to decide that by implication of fact or of law the deed of 1956 contains an agreement in writing to execute instruments of transfer of the Crown leases. No doubt such instruments would be directed to be executed by a court exercising equitable jurisdiction in certain circumstances and subject to conditions as to indemnity, but that is a different proposition from saying the deed exhibits a consensus for execution of such transfers.

For such a term to be implied by law the declaration of trust would have to also sound in undertaking, but although the deed of 1956 is bilateral, the acknowledgment of trust therein is not a consensual obligation, but has effect only in courts exercising equitable jurisdiction. I do not consider that an undertaking to execute a transfer would be imposed by law. (See Simonius Vischer & Co. v Holt & Thompson [1979] 2 NSWLR 322, 348 per Samuels J.A.).

Senior Counsel drew my attention to item 18A introduced into the Act by amendment, but properly conceded it has no application to these antecedent transfers. With some regret I disallow the objections and affirm the assessments under review.
