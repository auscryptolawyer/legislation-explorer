Daphne Helene Cocks and Commissioner of Superannuation [1989] AATA 179 (25 July 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DAPHNE HELENE COCKS v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q87/386

AAT No. 10340

Number of pages - 23

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

K L BEDDOE (Senior Member), B C LOCK (Member) AND H M PAVLIN (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Provident Account contributor - benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force - retirement due to condition specified in certificate - whether condition substantially contributed.

Superannuation Act 1976 Ss. 66, 184

John Steel v. Commissioner for Superannuation (unreported)

Burr v,. Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1

Whiteford v. Commissioner For Superannuation )1987) 14 ALD 321

HEARING

BRISBANE

25:7:1989

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that the decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

K L BEDDOE, B C LOCK AND H M PAVLIN This is a review of a decision made by a delegate of the respondent in respect of the abovenamed applicant pursuant to s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") to:

(i) VARY the decision of the delegate on 26 November 1981 to

issue pursuant to sub-section 184(2) of the Act in respect

of Mrs Cocks, a benefit classification certificate

specifying the condition "Symptoms of Anxiety and

Depression", by SUBSTITUTING for that condition the

condition "Personality Disorder", and by adding the

condition "Diabetes Mellitus".

(ii) CONFIRM the decision also taken by a delegate on 26

November 1981 to be of the opinion under paragraph 66(2)(c)

of the Act that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs

Cocks' retirement was caused, or was substantially

contributed to, by a condition or conditions specified in

the benefit classification certificate or by a condition or

conditions connected with such conditions.

2. In this matter the Tribunal sat on six sitting days and took oral evidence from six medical witnesses and five other witnesses. In addition there were 20 medical reports in the T documents and two more were tendered as exhibits.

3. The applicant is a 63 year old former telephonist previously employed by Telecom at the Lismore Telephone Exchange, New South Wales.

4. She first worked as a telephonist for the Post Master General's Department from 1943 to 1947. We are not concerned with that period.

5. In 1969 she again sought employment as a telephonist with the Postmaster General's Department at the Lismore Telephone Exchange. She was examined as to her medical fitness for employment as an exempt (temporary) telephonist on 22 April 1970 and commenced duty in that capacity on 19 August 1971. Some nine months later she sought to be reappointed as a permanent officer and she was examined on 1 May 1972 by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr K G Lawrence, to determine her fitness for permanent employment and as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act"). A number of medical examinations were conducted at the time, the end result of which was that she was approved medically fit for permanent appointment but only as a contributor to the Provident Account and not as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund.

6. The applicant continued to work as a telephonist until 1979. On 8 January 1979 she was involved in a motor vehicle accident on the way to work. She was totally incapacitated from 8 January 1979 to 15 January 1979. She resumed work after this absence and continued working until July 1979 when she was admitted to the Lismore Base Hospital suffering from severe back pains. She was awarded compensation for the absence in January and for the period 10 July 1979 to 19 January 1980 when according to certain medical reports she should have been fit for duty. She did not, however, resume duty as a telephonist and some 17 months later, on 28 May 1981, by a determination of the Workers' Compensation Commission of New South Wales she was declared totally incapacitated from 20 January 1980 and has been receiving compensation payments under the Compensation (Commonwalth Government Employees) Act 1971 since that date.

7. On 7 July 1981 Telecom retired the applicant on the grounds of invalidity due to the condition "spinal disorder with spondylitis 100%" pursuant to s.56(1)(c) of the Telecommunciations Act 1975.

8. On 16 July 1981 Telecom advised the Commissioner for Superannuation that Telecom had retired Mrs Cocks on the grounds of invalidity whereupon the Commissioner considered the applicant's pension entitlements under the Act. Because she had been a contributor to the Provident Account a benefit classification certificate was deemed to be in force and the provisions of s.184 of the Act apply.

9. On 26 November 1981 the Commissioner for Superannuation issued a benefit classification certificate specifying "symptoms of anxiety and depression". Stated briefly, a benefit classification certificate shall be issued in circumstances where the respondent Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for retirement of the applicant was caused by, substantially contributed to, or connected with the same condition which was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act whereby the applicant was deemed medically unfit to be a contributor to the Superannuation Fund.

10. The effect of this decision to issue the benefit classification certificate in the applicant's case was to substantially reduce her pension entitlements. On 11 October 1981 she lodged an appeal against the decision and between that date and 24 June 1986 the respondent reviewed the decision and on 24 June 1986 varied the decision as set out in paragraph (1) of these reasons, the effect of which was to leave the applicant with the same reduced pension entitlements. In effect the respondent Commissioner confirmed that a benefit classification certificate should be issued but that the condition "symptoms of anxiety and depression" should be substituted by the condition "personality disorder" and adding the condition "Diabetes Mellitus".

11. In essence the applicant's case rests on her assertion that at the time of her admission to the Provident Account she did not suffer from any personality disorder and if there was anything at all wrong with her it was the Diabetes Mellitus from which she suffered throughout four pregnancies. She denied that she had any psychiatric problems and she denied that her retirement was in any way connected with mental disorders but was entirely due to the effects of the motor vehicle accident in 1979 and, that being the case the benefit classification certificate should not have been issued and she should be entitled to full pension entitlements.

13. Section 184 of the Act as far as it applies to this case reads as follows:

"184(2) ... where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately

before the commencing day, was ... a contributor to the

Provident Account ceases to be an eligible employee by reason

of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years

and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee,

he has not attained his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the

person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement

of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to -

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person THAT WAS

RELEVANT for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was

more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more

of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the

person connected with the physical or mental condition of the

person THAT WAS RELEVANT for the purposes of the superseded Act

or, if there was more than one such condition, with those

conditions or one or more of those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit

classification certificate in which there is or are specified

the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person

that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act

and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be

deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have

been in force in respect of the person immediately before his

death or retirement.

(7) In this section -

(a) a reference to a contributor to the Fund shall be read as a

reference to a contributor to the existing Fund under Part III

of the superseded Act;

(b) a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account shall

be read as a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account

established under the superseded Act; and

(c) a reference to the phsyical or mental condition or

conditions of a person that WAS OR WERE RELEVANT for the

purposes of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to

the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person

that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the

physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by

reason of which -

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section

5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness

of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a

contriburor to the Fund;

other than -

(iii) a physical or mental condition that, in the opinion of the

Commissioner, did not exist

(A) at the time the person became a contributor to the Fund or a

contributor to the Provident Account;"

(emphasis added)

14. Section 184(2) is therefore the substantive provision for the issue of a benefit classification certificate retrospectively in certain circumstances. These circumstances are:

(a) the person was a Provident Account Contributor;

(b) The person ceased to be an eligible employee by reason

of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(c) the person's contributory service is less than 20 years;

and

(d) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity

which was the ground for retirement was caused or

substantially contributed to by the condition (1) which, in

the opinion of the Commissioner, was the cause of the

contributor being in the Provident Account or (2) by a

condition connected with that condition.

It is beyond argument that (a), (b) and (c) apply to the applicant.

15. There are three basic decisions to be decided. First, what is the relevant condition for the purpose of s.184(7). Second, what is the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. And thirdly, what were the physical or mental conditions which caused or substantially contributed to that incapacity.

16. Once we decide these questions of fact, there are four other questions which arise:

(a) Was the incapacity caused by the relevant condition?

(b) Was the incapacity substantially contributed to by the

relevant condition?

(c) Was the condition causing the incapacity connected with

the relevant condition?

(d) Was the condition substantially contributing to by the

incapacity connected with the relevant condition?

(S.s.184(2))

17. Section 184(7)(c) is the provision of the Act which applies and it refers to a decision made under s.5(1) of the superseded Act. The difficulty in this and in all other cases where similar cases have been before the Tribunal is that under the superseded Act the delegate of the Superannuation Board did not record his reasons for his decision to place the employee in the Provident Account. This issue arose in Re Bailey (No. 352 of 1979) dated 24 December 1979 where the Tribunal stated:

"In a case such as the present, where there is no direct

evidence to establish the physical or mental condition or

conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not

accept the applicant as a contributor to the Superannuation

Fund, it appears to us that the proper test to apply when

considering the material before the Board when it reached its

decision is - what condition or conditions must have influenced

the Board to accept the person into the Provident Account rather

than the Fund. Unless, on a fair consideration of that material,

the Tribunal can feel satisfied that a particular physical or

mental condition was, in fact, a reason which influenced the

Board's decision, the Tribunal would not, in our view, be

justified in finding as a fact that the condition should be

taken as being specified in the benefit classification

certificate which is deemed, by section 184(2) of the Act, to

be in force in respect of the applicant."

18. Paragraph 184(7) (c) provides an exhaustive definition of the phrase "condition - relevant for the purpose of the superseded Act". The definition requires the formation of opinion by the Tribunal as to the condition that was actually taken into account by the Superannuation Board for the purpose of coming to a conclusion about the health and phsyical fitness of the person.

19. A recent case is that of Re John Steel v. Commissioner for Superannuation (V86/425 of 9 February 1988; No. 4137). It traverses the cases concerning s.184(7) and concludes that what the Tribunal must do is satisfy itself what was, on all the evidence before it and on the balance of probabilities, the matters which were taken into account by the delegate of the Superannuation Board in accepting the applicant for Provident Account rather than the Superannuation Fund. The Tribunal stated in the course of its reasons:

"However, it is clear from the authorities cited above (and

indeed from the terms of sub-paragraph 184(7)(c)(i) that what

must be ascertained for present purposes is not what was the

applicant's medical condition at the time, but what was, rightly

or wrongly, in the mind of the delegate of the Superannuation

Board. In Re Hegedues and Commissioner for Superannuation (1982)

4 ALN N139 the Tribunal, presided over by Mr Ewart Smith, Senior

Member, said:

'What has to be examined, therefore, is not what physical or

mental conditions the applicant was in fact suffering from

at the time he underwent the medical examination for the

purposes of a s.5 of the superseded Act, but (in the absence

of any withholding or any information or furnishing of false

information, which was not alleged in this case) what were

the conditions that caused the Superannuation Board (through

its delegate) not to be satisfied that his health and

phsyical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted

as a contributor to the superannuation fund. For this

purpose, it seems to us clear that what must be examined is

the evidence that was available to the Board or its delegate

at the time when the decision was made to exclude him from

that fund, and not evidence that became available only

subsequently and which could not have affected the decision

that was taken at that time. In other words, what has to be

considered is what was before the Board or its delegate when

the decision was taken in 1975, not what has happened or

been learnt since which, if it had been available to the

Board or its delegate, would have been taken into account."'

20. In the present application, a strong case was argued on behalf of the applicant that the condition personality disorder was not a condition which was taken into account when the decision was made in 1973 as to whether or not the applicant should contribute to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account.

25. On 21 June 1973 the Delegate of the Superannuation Board determined under s.5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the applicant were such that she should contribute to the Provident Account and not the Superannuation Fund.

26. That delegate of the Superannuation Board was Denis Albert Hennessy. He gave evidence before the Tribunal. He identified as his own the signature on T8/1, which is a form (PSB9A) addressed to the Secretary, Superannuation Board, Canberra, ACT. It is headed "Determination of Medical fitness on Appointment" under which are set out two alternatives Superannuation Fund/Provident Account with an asterisk. The asterisk is explained - "delete whichever is not applicable". Mr Hennessy informed the Tribunal that in 1973 he was officer in charge of the "appointments area". He explained his duties in this position, as delegate of the Superannuation Board and of the Public Service Board, required taking decisions in respect of appointees as to their acceptance into the Public Service and as contributors to the Superannuation Fund or Provident Account. He physically performed this latter task by crossing out on the form PSB9A the alternative which was not to apply and signing the form. Beneath his signature his designation is described "Delegate of the Superannuation Board and the Public Service Board".

27. Mr Hennessy had no independent recollection of the applicant's case and that is not surprising considering the fact that at that time the Post Master General's Department in New South Wales was making 1,000 new appointments per year. He did, however, explain the procedure at page 389 in the course of examination-in-chief, as follows:

Q. Well, Mr Hennessy, on the basis of the documents that you

have been able to examine in relation to Mrs Cocks, are you able

to explain what happened in Mrs Cocks' case immediately leading

up to you taking your decision?

A. Once we got the decision from Dr Byrne, the Senior Medical

Officer, and his rejection of the applicant, the papers are then

referred to the Public Service Inspector's office or Public

Service Board and we await advice from them. Now, during that

process, they can request the person to undergo further medical

examinations. They also have access to the Senior Director of

Health in the Department of Health at that time and they

regularly go down and liaise and discuss it with the Director of

Health. Then we await the decision or their advice to come back

to us which would say either acceptance in the superannuation

scheme or acceptance into the Provident Account. Now, when we

get that advice, as I am the delegate, I have got to exercise my

delegations responsibility and if I agree then I would

automatically go ahead and issue the PSB9A which is that form in

front of you. If I still had reservations about it, I would then

take it up with the Department of Health direct or with the

Public Service Inspector which may involve me going over there

and speaking to them about the case and may go down to the

Department of Health and discuss it further. In this case, I was

in agreement with their advice and proceeded to sign the

document which was known as PSB9A."

In doing so he crossed out the words "Superannuation Fund" and signed the form and it was sent to the Superannuation Board.

28. When reviewing the decision under review the Commissioner reached the conclusion that the relevant conditions for purposes of s.184(7) are "Diabetes Mellitus" and "personality disorder".

29. Bearing in mind that Mr Hennessy had no personal recollection of what prompted him to decide that Mrs Cocks should be in the Provident Account but taking into account the sequence of events, the date of medical examinations and the medical evidence arising from previously listed notes on Dr Greenberg's letter made by Dr Byrne, Dr Slaughter's report and the evidence before the Tribunal and the guidelines set out in the Handbook for Guidance of Medical Officers, we have formed the opinion that diagnosed Diabetes Mellitus and a history of some psychiatric illness, however described, were the reasons why Mr Hennessy as delegate of the Superannuation Board decided to accept the applicant into the Provident Account and not the Superannuation Fund. We therefore are of the opinion that the relevant condition or conditions for the purpose of s.184(7) are "Diabetes Mellitus" and "personality disorder". That deals with the first of the basis questions referred to in paragraph 15 above.

30. We now turn to the second and third questions, what was the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement, and what were the physical or mental conditions which caused or substantially contributed to that incapacity?

31. The applicant continued working as a telephonist at the Lismore Telephone Exchange until 1979. On 8 January 1979 whilst travelling to work she was involved in a motor vehicle accident and suffered back injuries. She claimed compensation and liability was accepted. She was granted compensation leave from 8 January 1979 to 15 January 1979. She resumed duty and worked until July 1979. She was again granted compensation leave from 10 July 1979 to 19 January 1980. She did not return to work and remained on sick leave. On 10 July 1979 she was admitted to hospital suffering severe back pains. She was in hospital for three weeks receiving treatment. During this time she came into contact with Dr Watson, orthopaedic surgeon, and Dr Petroff, psychiatrist. On 27 March 1980 a delegate of the Commissioner for Compensation issued a determination covering the periods of sick leave from 10 July 1979 to 19 January 1980 and noting that the personal injury sustained was "back injury and compensation neurosis". On 2 July 1980 she was examined as to her fitness to resume duties or be retired, by a Commonwealth Medical Officer. Dr Harvey, who reported that she was fit to resume duty (T9/2) and referred to two other medical reports. The first dated 4 July was from Dr James A Gatenby who said in his report:

"Mrs Cocks has suffered from a genuine depressive reaction with

dissociative features following on her injury in a motor vehicle

accident early last year. I consider she has now recovered from

that period of depression, and from a psychiatric viewpoint she

is now fit to return to her usual duties at work with Telecom

Australia. She is now definitely motivated towards returning to

work and therefore I would not recommend consideration of her

retirement on medical grounds. Any restriction of her neck

movements and possible interference with her usual duties at

work would, of course, be assessed separately by the Orthopaedic

Surgeon."

32. The second report is from Dr Hedberg, orthopedic surgeon, who also examined the applicant on 3 July 1980. He said:

"...There was on 8 January 1979 an aggravation of a mild

degenerative cervical spondylosis. There was possibly a

lumbar sprain. There have been concurrent psychological and

medical problems which are apparently resolving. She is bright

and cheerful and forward looking today.

There is no significant residual orthopaedic diability. On

orthopaedic grounds she is fit to resume her duties as a

Telephonist."

33. The applicant did not, however, resume duty, but it appears that on the basis of these reports, which were both directed to Telecom Australia, the worker's compensation payments were stopped.

34. The applicant pursued her right to compensation and in a decision handed down in the Workers' Compensation Commission of New South Wales on 28 May 1981 his Honour Judge Ferrari found:

"(a) The applicant suffered injury on 8 Jan 1979 arising out

of and in the course of her employment by the respondent

whilst on a journey to her employment by the respondent.

(b) Such injury included the following

(i) intervertebral disc lesions at levels C4/5, C5/6 and

C6/Da;

(ii) intervertebral disc lesion at L4/5.

(c) As a result of the said injury the applicant was totally

(d) On and from 20 Jan 1980 the applicant has remained

The applicant has been in receipt of weekly payments of compensation for total incapacity since that date.

35. Although a lot was said before the Tribunal about attempts being made by the Commonwealth to reverse this decision by the Commission, such is not the case. The respondent Commissioner is only concerned with this decision insofar as it helps him decide the question "what was the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement and what were the physical or mental conditions which caused or substantially contributed to it", and likewise the Tribunal is only concerned with the decision for the same reason.

36. On 7 July 1981 approval was given for the applicant to be retired on the ground of invalidity due to the condition "Spinal Disc Disorder with Spondylitis 100%". The retirement was to be effective from close of business 6 July 1981 as the applicant had been on continuous sick leave/compensation for the maximum period allowable.

38. As can be seen from the chronology and from an examination of the correspondence in the T documents, the Commissioner conducted an exhaustive, indeed exhausting, re-examination of all the medical evidence in this matter. It has led to the point where Telecom altered the description of conditions causing the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement from "spinal disc disorder with spondylitis 100% to "spinal disc disorder with spondylitis and symptoms of anxiety and depression".

39. In addition to the report of Dr Bell, which Telecom had but which is not before us, Telecom would have had the reports of the orthopaedic specialists mentioned above and of the psychiatrists Dr Petroff, Dr Gatenby and Dr Tym. The Tribunal accepts the findings of the Workers Compensation Commission of New south Wales, dated 28 May 1981, that the applicant suffered a compensable back injury and that she was totally incapacitated from 20 January 1980, as found by that Commission. We are not convinced, however, that the back injury alone contributed to the incapacity.

40. The evidence of the two orthopaedic specialists who gave evidence before the Tribunal was conflicting. Dr Watson, who treated the applicant following the accident, was of the view that there was nothing seriously wrong with the applicant's back. Dr M F Guirius in his report dated 30 March 1981 (T14/4) expressed the following opinion:

"Mrs Cocks has sustained the following injuries, all of which

could result from the accident mentioned above:

1. An injury to the intervertebral disc at the levels of C4/4,

C5/6/1 in the form of a prolapse of the intervertebral on these

levels, more marked at the C5/6 level.

2. A soft tissue injury to the thoracic spine, in the form of a

sprain of the thoracic spine.

3. A disc injury at the level of L4/5 in the form of a prolapse

of the disc.

As regards to the prognosis, I am afraid that the outlook in Mrs

Cocks' case is rather gloomy, and I would be very reluctant to

offer her any for surgical treatment (sic) for this eventually

will not help her, due to the hi ... (sic) of the nervous

breakdown and intensive psychological treatment she has received

since the accident.

I think the only way to tackle her problems now would be to

carry on with the conservative measures and to learn how to live

with it. I have arranged for her a course of physiotherapy which

was unfortunately delayed, because of another course of

intensive psychological treatment she received. Mrs Cocks gave

me a history of being off work for the past 20 months or so and

in my opinion she remains unfit to do any duties tht applies

(sic) stresses to her cervical and lumbar spines. In some

circumstances it is very difficult (sic) to assess the permanent

disability resulting, but, as described above, she is greatly

disabled with her cervical and lumbar injuries and the

psychological effects inflicted by them."

Dr Guirius gave evidence before the Tribunal. Questioned in cross-examination about the nervous breakdown and the psychological effects referred to in his opinion, he gave the following evidence at pages 460-461:

"Q. Now you say that you would have suggested surgical treatment

except for the applicant suffering a nervous breakdown. What

surgical treatment would you have suggested?

A. Because I saw her in such agony I was going to suggest a

discogram on the neck to localise, to make sure that it is the

C5-6 that was producing the problem and then an antecervical

fusion. For the back it will be a discectomy and either a fusion

or not, it depends on the result after the operation. Obviously

to get a good surgical result from a spinal operation you have

to have a prepared patient.

Q. And how did the nervous breakdown interfere with - how would

it interfere with an operation?

A. An anxiety to the patient makes people feel more pain.

Surgery in itself is an insult on the body and when you get a

tired horse with a nervous breakdown and then whip him, whip the

horse, the horse will collapse.

Good, thank you. Now, you talk about, 'she is greatly disabled

by her cervical and lumbar injuries and the psychological

effects inflicted by them', what do you mean by the

psychological effects?

A. If somebody has got a sore tooth, he knows that this will last

for five or six days; maximum seven days, and if you you will go

to the dentist and either the dentist will deal with it or take

it out and the problem is finished. So the patient knows that

there is an end to the suffering he is dealing with. When

somebody has an injury to the neck or the back regardless

whether it is a profusion or a muscle, ligament injury or

whatever he is in pain or she is in pain, she goes to the doctor

- when am I going to be cured, doctor, God knows, if I operation

will I be better, God knows, can you give me a guarantee. God

knows, take these tablets, try these tablets, do this, do

physiotherapy, do chiropractor, do, do, do, and all this

empirical treatment, and it is something that is not there.

People who get that sort of pain without hope for relief over a

period of time, they can accommodate it for six months, they can

accommodate it for a year, but once it starts and passes beyond

the one year mark, now the International Society of Pain, they

recognise one year as a landmark: definite changes appear in

their psychology, they become apprehensive about it, they become

angry about it and they become obsessed about it and they become

anxious and depressed; and this is what we call a chronic pain

syndrome; and unfortuantely, there is no cure for that. And,

unfortunately I could give any - if she asked me to do the

operation regardless of the nervous breakdown and she asked me,

would I be better after the operation, would the pain in the

neck disappear, I would say, no, I could not give you any

guarantees.

Q. And, how important was the psychological problems in her

incapacity at the time that you saw her?

A. It was important.

Q. Do you think that it was a substantial factor?

A. A substantial factor in its production was the neck pain and

back pain that she suffered."

Faced with this evidence we are convinced that there was some psychological condition associated with the back injury that lead to the conclusion that the applicant was totally incapacitated in May 1981 when the compensation issue was finalised.

41. All three psychiatrists reported that the applicant had suffered significant psychiatric illness following the accident. Dr Petroff was of the view in February 1980 that the applicant should be retired on psychiatric grounds. In July 1980, however, Dr Gatenby reported that she presented with no evidence of depression and at that time would not recommend retirement. In November 1980 Dr Tym reported as above. It is apparent that Dr Tym was not aware of the visits to the psychiatrists Drs Slaughter and Greenberg before the accident or of the report from Miss Orr reporting the applicant's suggestions of suicide (Exhibit 3).

42. Neither Dr Tym nor Dr Gatenby was called to give evidence.

43. Dr Petroff did give evidence before the Tribunal and his reports of 19 December 1979 (T9/8) and 20 December 1980 (T9/7) were also before the Tribunal. He had been the applicant's treating psychiatrist since July 1979 and had seen her on numerous occasions. In his evidence he was adamant that the motor vehicle accident was the final straw - the straw which broke the camel's back - the back being a psychiatric disorder from which the applicant always suffered. We note at this stage that Dr Slaughter described the condition as "life-long personality problems" with "periods of neurotic depession". Dr Petroff described the condition as "histrionic personality disorder". In his report dated 17 December, Dr Petroff stated that the applicant had compensation neurosis. He was asked to explain the meaning of this term:

Q. Could you explain what you mean by 'compensation neurosis'?

A. Where a person seeks either dependency, or revenge, or

financial gain by focussing on an incident - an event, and

there is generally a lot of other factors that have added to

their demise." (p.269)

44. He was further asked to clarify his use elsewhere in his report of the term "de-compensation" which he described as "the loss of the will to keep on fighting" (page 270):

"Q. And then you go on to say that the accident has brought

about decompensation. What do you mean by 'decompensation',

doctor?

A. The will to keep on fighting. (page 269-70)

Q. ... Could you tell the Tribunal what you actually mean by

A. Well, I mean, Elaine (sic) has a histrionic personality

disorder where she tends to see things in black and white; where

Asked to describe anxiety depression, Dr Petroff said that when you talk about depression it really refers to three separate entities - there was depression that is a disease as in manic depressive condition where it is a physical condition, and then you have the much more common reactive depression which is something that every body has suffered (it is depression after a loss), "and the third is depression as a lifestyle where the person, because of their personality problems, is constantly having losses ... It is a combination of second plus the personality problems... the second one we have all had - reaction; we have lost something. And the third is that the person is constantly in a situation plus have a propensity to look on things and expect more from life than they are getting, so they always see the glass as being half empty rather than half full." (p.275)

"Q. And which category does Mrs Cocks fall into?

A. The lifestyle, the third." (p.275)

46. Faced with this evidence we are compelled to the conclusion that the condition which has caused the applicant's retirement incapacity was a combination of the physical injury to her back and the association of the psychiatric disorder which has always been part of her makeup, i.e. her personality disorder.

47. It was described by Telecom as "spinal disc disorder with spondylitis and symptoms of anxiety and depression".

48. Her incapacity was her inability to perform the duties of a telephonist. The word "incapacity" has been interpreted as the outward result of the physical or mental condition which leads to the person being unable to perform work (Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1 at pt.(ii).

49. The incapacity which was the grounds for the applicant's retirement was her inability to perform her duties as a telephonist by reason of her back injury and symptoms of anxiety and depression which came to the fore at the time of the accident.

50. That answers questions 2 and 3 referred to in paragraph 15.

51. The question now is whether that incapacity was caused "or substantially contributed to" by her pre-existing personality disorder or by a physical condition connected with that disorder. We are of the opinion that the "personality disorder" and the symptoms of "anxiety and depression" are in fact the same condition and that it substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant.

52. As to the phrase "substantially contributed to" we adopt the dicta in Re Whiteford (1987) 14 ALD 321 at 329 where the Tribunal said

"Essentially, we are satisfied, the legislation intended that,

for a condition to be regarded as substantially contributing to

incapacity for the purposes of s.66(2)(c), it must be of such

significance in relation to that incapacity as to warrant

depriving the employee of a significant part of the benefits

which he would otherwise receive under the provisions of the

Act. The meaning of 'substantially' must, therefore, be

considerably higher up the scale of substantiality than 'not

trivial, minimal or nominal'. On the other hand, in some cases

the incapacity may be caused by two or three conditions with

each contributing equally to it. In such a case, we have no

doubt, each of them must be regarded as substantially

contributing to the incapacity. So the meaning of

'substantially' in s.66(2)(c) is not as far up the scale as 'in

the main' or 'in the greater part'."

53. We therefore find that the personality disorder substantially contributed to the retirement incapacity. It follows that the decision of the respondent Commissioner to issue the benefits classification certificate and to determine invalidity benefits in accordance with para 66(2)(c) should be affirmed.
