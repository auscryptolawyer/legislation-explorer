Stephen Patrick Saunders and Commissioner of Superannuation [1994] AATA 14 (21 January 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

STEPHEN PATRICK SAUNDERS v. COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

No. A93/81

AAT No. 9263

Number of pages - 8

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.J. MCMAHON (Deputy President), D.B. TRAVERS (Member) AND N.J. ATTWOOD

(Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - transfer of eligible employee from Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS) to Public Sector Scheme (PSS) pursuant to ss 244 and 245 - applicant completed election to transfer form - document mislaid - document received by respondent and acted upon - later election to remain in CSS rejected by respondent because of earlier election to transfer - review of decision not to treat earlier election as ineffective - whether earlier election was ineffective because the applicant did not have the necessary intention to transfer at the time the document was signed and that he was not responsible for transmitting it to the respondent - explanation for transmission of election speculative and inherently unbelievable - evidence that applicant caused the delivery of the document - unreliable memory - decision affirmed

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ss 154, 157, 244, 245

Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1981) 35 ALR 151

HEARING

CANBERRA, 16 December 1993

21:1:1994 The Applicant represented himself.

Ms T. Munoz (Departmental Advocate) appeared for the Respondent.

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

B.J. MCMAHON, D.B. TRAVERS AND N.J. ATTWOOD This is an application to review a decision made under the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") which was affirmed under sub-section 154(4) of that Act. The decision was not to treat the paper tendered as Exhibit 2 as ineffective for the purposes of s 244 of the Act.

2. The applicant was a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS) as an eligible employee in the Department of Employment, Education and Training (DEET). On 1 July 1990, the Public Sector Scheme (PSS) commenced operation and the Act was amended to provide for transfers of eligible employees from the CSS to the PSS.

3. The relevant parts of ss 244 and 245 governing the method of transfer are in the following terms -

"s244(1) Subject to this section, an eligible employee who is

not precluded by or under the Superannuation Act 1990 (other

than by paragraph 6(2)(a) of that Act) from being a member of

the Superannuation (1990) Scheme may, in writing addressed to

the Commissioner:

(a) declare that he or she wishes to become a member of that

scheme; and

(b) elect to cease to be an eligible employee.

....................

s 245 A person who makes a declaration and election under

section 244 is taken to have ceased to be an eligible employee

at the end of the day on which the declaration and election are

made."

4. In addition to providing the mechanism for transfers, s 244 dealt with the time within which a declaration and election provided for in sub-section (1) was to be made. Generally it was between 1 July 1990 and 30 June 1991.

5. For the purpose of assisting eligible employees in either making the declaration and election to transfer, or in confirming that it was their intention to remain in the CSS, a form SE1 was produced by the Commissioner's office and was circulated widely through the personnel areas of departments and other public authorities approved for purposes of the Act. Evidence was given before us by Mr Palmer, a Senior Officer in the pay and conditions section of DEET that the form SE1 formed part of a package of materials in a kit which was circulated by desk drop to every officer through DEET's central registry. He said that there was a good deal of publicity about it at the time. Public servants were reminded that if any person had not received one of these kits, they should apply to their personnel areas. Mr Palmer said that he did receive some complaints from officers who said they did not receive these kits, but he received no such complaint from Mr Saunders.

6. The form SE1 is divided into 2 parts; one coloured blue and the other pink. The blue part, Part A, is headed "NOTIFICATION OF DECISION TO REMAIN IN

THE CSS". The pink part, Part B, is headed ELECTION TO CEASE MEMBERSHIP OF THE CSS and TO BECOME A MEMBER OF THE NEW PSS".

7. Mr Saunders completed Part B of a form SE1 in the following terms -

"Superannuation Act 1976

ELECTION TO CEASE MEMBERSHIP OF THE CSS

and

TO BECOME A MEMBER OF THE NEW PSS

TO: Commissioner for Superannuation

In accordance with Section 244 of the Superannuation Act 1976,

Given Names Surname

I, STEPHEN PATRICK SAUNDERS

whose AGS No. is 238 54674 and date of birth is 17/9/49

DECLARE that I wish to become a member of the Superannuation

(1990) Scheme (known as the PSS) and accordingly elect to cease

to be a member (ie an "eligible employee) of the CSS;

ACKNOWLEDGE that my decision cannot be reversed;

ACKNOWLEDGE that I have received sufficient information to

enable me to make an informed decision.

(Signed) SP Saunders 15/3/91

Signature Date"

This document was tendered as Exhibit 2 and is the document referred to in paragraph 1 of these reasons.

8. It is common ground that Mr Saunders had completed his Australian Government Service number, had written in the date of his birth and had signed the form. It bears a date in his hand writing. Mr Saunders could not, however, recall whether he held the form for a period before he signed it. He was therefore unable to say whether he in fact signed the form on 15 March 1991.

9. He agreed that he had received the form as part of a kit, but when shown the contents of the kit that was identified by Mr Palmer as the one circulated, he looked at them and was unable to recall any particular documents. He said that in particular he could not remember seeing the graphics that appear in the information pamphlets. He said: "They could have been part of my paraphernalia. I got a kit but I don't know what was in it. These could have been elements of it but I don't think so. They just don't gel."

10. Mr Saunders could not recall how long he had the form before he signed it. He recalled that he held it for a period during which time it was left in the kit folder on his desk "laid out in horizontal fashion". He thought that he could have had the form for a couple of months but he could not be sure. He said "it seemed to me to be important".

11. During that couple of months, he remembers reading material relating to the CSS and the PSS which presented comparisons and contrasts to assist him in making up his mind whether to transfer to the PSS or not. He recalled going to one formal discussion in DEET which was arranged to inform officers of the advantages and disadvantages of the alternatives. He also remembered discussing the question in general with his colleagues. He did not recall discussing his proposed transfer with the Commissioner's office and he was not aware who the Commissioner's delegate was in his Department. He also remembers discussing the proposed transfer with "my personnel people".

12. He gave evidence that he filled out Form B which purports to be an election to cease membership of the CSS and to transfer to the PSS. After he signed it, he recalls folding it and putting it in a yellow envelope, which is a characteristic envelope for internal use within the department. He then said that he put the paper and envelope in his weekly diary planner. He can not recall whether he did this on the same day as the date shown on the form. He had not recently checked his diary but as far as he could remember he was in Canberra on 15 March 1991. In that year, Good Friday was on 29 March. He could not remember whether he had sealed the envelope but he does remember addressing the envelope to "the personnel people".

13. His practice was to deal with all matters put in his diary planner within 2 weeks. If at the end of one week he had not dealt with any matter, or document inserted in the diary, he would put it in for the following week. He does not recall moving the yellow envelope containing Exhibit 2 to any subsequent week. In fact he does not recall seeing Exhibit 2 and the yellow envelope again.

14. He said that sometimes he takes his planner diary home with him but normally he has it on the top of his desk next to an in tray so that staff can have access to the diary for the purpose of writing in appointments.

15. He has an out tray on his desk but this is not regularly cleared. There can be "sporadic" clearances. In addition, there is a general out tray for distribution of mail throughout the Department which is located some 20 metres from his office. No records are kept of any documents left in the general out tray for distribution.

16. Although the diary was taken home from time to time, it was not his practice, he said, to take papers out of his diary. He has no recollection of any other papers ever going missing, except that he may have lost minor bills from time to time. Certainly, he agreed, nothing like an addressed envelope had previously disappeared.

17. Mr Saunders holds an Honours Degree in Mathematics and a Masters Degree in Urban Geography. He holds the position of Senior Officer, Grade A, and has, he told us, frequently acted as Branch Head in the Senior Executive Service. His capacity to inform himself about the PSS and the transfer arrangements can not, therefore, be doubted. He had every opportunity to satisfy himself about the benefits and perceived defects of the PSS scheme so far as it affected him. Indeed his evidence is that he was fully briefed by the written material and through the discussions to which we have referred. The form, Exhibit 2, contains on the reverse side further information as to the method of making a choice. This includes instructions as to the dispatch of the choice, instructions as to when it has to be made and advice of the impossibility of changing after an election has been made.

18. When asked directly, Mr Saunders could not say why he had signed the form except that at the time he thought that he was going to make the decision in favour of the PSS. He said: "I was still uncertain. What's wrong with signing the form?" He said that he often signs and dates documents and puts them in his planner to review later.

19. He signed a subsequent Part A election in June 1991 which was rejected because of the existence of the earlier Part B election. That had been received in the Personnel Section of DEET, according to a date stamp endorsed upon it, on 9 April 1991. Mr Saunders said that when he signed the June election "I knew I had signed an earlier one. I thought it had disappeared or I had chucked it out. I did not make any enquiries of my personnel people". Mr Saunders could offer no explanation of the fact that his Part B document was received by the personnel section on 9 April 1991. He speculated that: "I think it most likely that it became mixed up with other work papers and that someone, quite reasonably, may have sent it off to the area to which it was addressed. They would not have had a return address on the back of the envelope as is not uncommon with internal mail in the department. A reasonable officer finding it might well have sent it off rather than pry by opening the envelope. It is surely not uncommon or unusual for people to send on an addressed mail they find as a courtesy. I have done it myself".

20. On these facts, Mr Saunders contends that the March Part B document was ineffective as an election because he did not have the necessary intention to elect. He referred to a number of authorities to support the proposition that for an election to be valid, in general terms, there must be clear proof that the person put to his election was aware of the nature and extent of his rights and that having that knowledge, he intended to elect. Mr Saunders agrees that he signed and dated a form addressed to the Commissioner, but because it was not delivered by him with the intention of exercising an election, it was an invalid election. In view of the conclusions to which we have come, it will not be necessary to examine these authorities. One can readily accept the proposition that there must be a necessary intention in an act of election as in all other acts affecting legal rights and responsibilities. We have come to the conclusion that in this case the necessary intention existed.

21. Mr Saunders made it clear that he did not wish to amend the March election. Submissions from the Commissioner as to the legal non-availability of amendment were therefore irrelevant. Mr Saunder's case was that he had not made an election. He did not therefore wish to retract the March document or have it amended in any way. The June document, having been signed prior to 30 June 1991 was, in his submission, the only valid election.

22. Section 244 requires a declaration and an election, both made in writing and addressed to the Commissioner. The declaration and election need not be made on the form SE1. On a strict reading of the section, the constituent acts of the election would have been completed upon signing the document. There is nothing in the section requiring the document to be delivered to the Commissioner. Section 245 makes the declaration effective at the end of the day on which the declaration and election "are made". Nevertheless we consider that a court would read into the section a requirement for receipt of the election by the Commissioner before it became effective.

23. In Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1981) 35 ALR 151 the High Court inserted words in their reading of an Act to avoid a drafting oversight and to achieve a result consistent with what they perceived to be Parliament's manifest intentions. In that case, their Honours observed that the literal meaning of the words used would have produced a result that was "incongruous" (at 157) "contrary to the objects of the Act". (at 162) and "capricious and irrational" (at 170). The system of transferring from CSS to PSS would be unworkable if declarations were made privately and were kept by the declarant. We consider that a court would read into the terms of s 244 a requirement that the election be received by the Commissioner before it becomes effective. Consequently s 245 would be read to mean that the declaration would be effective at the end of the day on which the declaration and the election were received by the Commissioner.

24. As we have observed, it will not be necessary to examine the legal effect of the receipt of such a document forwarded without the knowledge of the declarant. We have come to the view that on the balance of probabilities, the applicant was aware that the document was to be delivered and probably assisted in the process of its delivery.

25. Mr Saunders was not a satisfactory witness. His loquacious responses were often not addressed to the questions asked. When pressed to give direct answers, we found that he was evasive. Furthermore, the evidence indicates a faulty recall in many important areas and, in some cases, a total inability to remember the receipt of important documents. We are satisfied on the evidence of Mr Palmer, and on the practices described by him, that Mr Saunders received the whole of the documents in the kit Exhibit 3. The fact that he could not recognise some of them is an indication to us of his unreliable memory. We have concluded that it would be unsafe to decide this application on the basis of Mr Saunder's evidence alone, without any corroboration.

26. What corroboration exists tends to detract from the account given by Mr Saunders. The applicant's imagined explanation of delivery of the election is speculation and depicts a sequence of events which is inherently unbelievable. On the other hand, we are satisfied that it was perfectly normal and natural for Mr Saunders to sign the Form B after he had informed himself through extensive enquiries as to his future rights. Signing a document (notwithstanding Mr Saunders' surprise at this fact) does have specific legal consequences and certainly indicates a concluded affirmation of the contents of the document by the signatory. The fact that the document was then dated, the fact that personal details were inserted, the fact that it was then folded and placed in an appropriate envelope and that the envelope was addressed, indicates to us that all the elements necessary to make a s 244 election were present, except delivery of the election to the Commissioner's representative (in this case the personnel area of DEET). On the balance of probabilities we are of the view that Mr Saunders arranged for this delivery.

27. There is no evidence that the established practice which he described has in any way been departed from in previous instances. Papers have not been taken from his desk, from his diary planner, or (except sporadically) from his out tray without his knowledge and consent. There is a presumption that established practices will continue to be adhered to unless there is clear evidence to the contrary. In this case we can not accept that the envelope left the applicant's desk without his knowledge.

28. We do not say that Mr Saunders deliberately misstated the position. We have simply concluded that he has an unreliable memory. The fact that he may have put the envelope in the out tray on his desk, or in the general out tray 20 metres from his office, is inherently more believable than that the envelope should be misplaced in some area away from his office so as to attract the attention of a perfect stranger who would then direct the envelope to its destination. The likelihood of an error occurring in the way assumed by Mr Saunders is so remote and improbable, in our view, as to be completely outweighed in the balance by the probable events to which we have referred.

29. Mr Saunders did not seek to argue that his Part B statement should be amended or that there was power in s 244 or in s 157, setting out the general provisions applicable to elections under the Act, to amend his statement. He was content with the submission that the statement did not amount to an election because there was not the necessary mental element in the act. As we have come to the conclusion that there was such an element, it will not be necessary to examine the provisions of the sections to which we have referred in relation to the power of amending or rescinding declarations already made. For these reasons the decision under review is affirmed.
