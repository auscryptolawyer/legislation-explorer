Re Arnold Bloch, Leibler and Company and Commissioner of Taxation [1984] AATA 123 (18 April 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ARNOLD BLOCH, LEIBLER AND COMPANY

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. V83/198

Freedom of Information

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Freedom of Information - claim of exemption - applicant seeking inspection by counsel of exempt documents for proper opportunity of presenting submissions - s.64(1) FOI Act - legislative attitude

Practice and Procedure - directions hearing - request to inspect exempt documents - present practice - index of documents - supporting affidavits justifying exemptions claimed

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, ss.37, 39 Freedom of Information Act 1982, ss.58, 64

Mobil Oil Australia Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 41; (1963) 113 CLR 475

Alister v R (1983) 50 ALR 41

The News Corporation Limited & Ors v National Companies and Securities Commission (Full Court of the Federal Court, 1 March 1984)

Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (A83/32, 26 August 1983)

Vaughn v Rosen [1973] USCADC 464; 157 US App DC 340, 484 F.2d 820

HEARING

CANBERRA

18:4:1984

DECISION

At the conclusion of a Directions Hearing in the above matter held on 18 April 1984 the Tribunal gave orally its reasons for declining to make a particular direction sought by counsel for the applicants. Those reasons are now reduced to writing and appear below.

1. This is a directions hearing at which a direction has been sought by counsel for the applicants, Messrs Arnold Bloch Leibler and Company, who are a firm of solicitors carrying on practice as such in Melbourne. The direction is sought in relation to the provision of access to counsel for the applicants to certain documents that are the subject of these proceedings, being proceedings brought by the applicants before this Tribunal pursuant to the provisions of the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("FOI Act"). That Act has been amended since the making of the application for review, but so far as I am aware it has been amended in no relevant respect.

2. The applicants sought access to a manual in the possession of the respondent entitled "Investigation Training and Reference Text". The respondent provided access to the manual so described but subject to a number of deletions, and in respect of those deletions a claim to exemption is made pursuant to a number of different sections of the FOI Act, namely ss.37(1) (a), 37(1) (b), 37(2) (b), 38, 40(c) and 45, thus covering a wide range of exemptions.

3. The applicant seeks a direction that counsel may be permitted to have access to the documents in order that, as I shall expand in a moment, the applicants may have a proper opportunity of presenting their case. Specifically, the direction sought was as follows:

"1. That the respondent lodge at the Registry not less than 14 days before the date for hearing this application two copies of the documents for which the respondent claims exemption ('the relevant documents').

2. Upon Counsel for the applicant giving an undertaking in writing in the following terms -

(a) That he will not copy or permit to be copied the relevant documents or any part thereof;

(b) That save for the purpose of obtaining instructions from his instructing solicitors herein regarding this application he will not disclose or permit to be disclosed to any person for any purpose whatsoever any of the contents of the relevant documents;

(c) That at the conclusion of the proceedings herein he will deliver up to the Registry the relevant documents,

one copy of the relevant documents shall be delivered to Counsel for the applicant."

4. In a decision given in August last year by the Tribunal in Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (No.A83/32) the Tribunal said at page 13 of the Reasons:

"Before referring to the documents remaining in question, it should be mentioned that at the hearing an order was made pursuant to s.35 of the AAT Act prohibiting disclosure of the documents remaining in dispute to the applicant, who appeared in person. The documents were then produced and evidence was taken and submissions made in relation to those documents without the applicant having the documents before him. Awkward as this procedure may seem, the Tribunal is satisfied that this is clearly preferable to the only other alternative available, namely the exclusion of the applicant from the hearing while the particular documents are dealt with. Where an applicant is represented by counsel it may be that in appropriate circumstances the Tribunal may feel justified in permitting the documents to be seen by counsel subject to the making of an order under s.35."

The possibility of inspection by counsel was thus raised by the Tribunal in that case and the Tribunal now has the opportunity to consider the matter in the light of submissions made to it by counsel for both the applicant and for the respondent.

5. For the applicants, Mr Myers placed at the forefront of his application the provisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("AAT Act") itself. He referred to a number of provisions but in particular I think it is fair to say that he emphasized the provisions of s.37 in relation to the lodging of documents with the Tribunal, and emphasised even more the provisions of s.39 as follows:

"Subject to sections 35 and 36 the Tribunal shall ensure that every party to a proceeding before the Tribunal is given a reasonable opportunity to present his case and, in particular, to inspect any documents to which the Tribunal proposes to have regard in reaching a decision in the proceeding and to make submissions in relation to those documents."

6. So there are really three requirements there: a reasonable opportunity to present the case, a reasonable opportunity to inspect any documents to which the Tribunal proposes to have regard, and a reasonable opportunity to make submissions in relation to those documents. My Myers adds to the scales the submission that in these proceedings counsel will, in all likelihood, appear on both sides, that a detailed examination of the documents will be required, that there is likely to be examination also of the evidence adduced by the respondent in relation to questions of public interest and the like, and that there will be cross-examination.

7. In view of all those features of the matter, he submits that, unless some opportunity is given to the applicants' counsel to inspect the documents a reasonable time before the commencement of the hearing, the Tribunal will not have ensured that the applicant has had a reasonable opportunity to present his case, a reasonable opportunity to inspect relevant documents, or a reasonable opportunity to make submissions in relation to those documents.

8. If one left the matter there, and if indeed one went to familiar principles in relation to the grant of natural justice to parties in a proceeding of this nature, there would of course be very great force in what Mr Myers put to the Tribunal. What is natural justice in a situation before a Tribunal of this kind and what are the limitations on the relevant principles are set out clearly in Mobil Oil Australia Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 41; (1963) 113 CLR 475, in particular at pp.502-504. At p.502 Kitto J commenced by a consideration of the position of a Taxation Board of Review and in many ways what he says about a Board of Review would of course apply to this Tribunal in like manner. He said that a Board's:

". . . function is merely to do over again (within the limits of the taxpayer's objection) what the Commissioner did in making the assessment - not to give a decision affecting the taxpayer's legal situation, but to work out, as a step in administration, what it considers that situation to be. The Board is 'in the same position as the Commissioner himself', as the Privy Council said in Shell Co of Australia Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1931) AC 275 at p.298; (1930) 44 CLR 530 at p.545). It is 'only another executive body in an administrative hierarchy': Jolly v Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1935) [1935] HCA 21; 53 CLR 206), per Rich and Dixon JJ ((1935) 53 CLR at p.214)."

He considered the matter on alternative bases, first that a Board of Review is bound by the rules of natural justice and second that it is not, but on p.504 he said:

"Even if the Board is bound in law to act 'in the spirit and with the sense of responsibility of a tribunal whose duty is to mete out justice' to quote Lord Haldane's words in Local Government Board v Arlidge ((1915) AC 120, at p.132) it does not follow (and his Lordship proceeded immediately to say so) that the procedure of each such tribunal must be the same: 'what that procedure is to be in detail must depend on the nature of the tribunal' ((1915) AC at p.132)."

On pp.503-504 he said:

". . . the books are full of cases which illustrate both the impossibility of laying down a universally valid test by which to ascertain what may constitute such an opportunity in the infinite variety of circumstances that may exist, and the necessity of allowing full effect in every case to the particular statutory framework within which the proceeding takes place. By the statutory framework I mean the express and implied provisions of the relevant Act and the inferences of legislative intention to be drawn from the circumstances to which the Act was directed and from its subjectmatter."

9. What of course Mr Moshinsky said for the respondent is that that is the very thing we have to do here and that, notwithstanding the provisions of the AAT Act, what has primary application here are specific provisions of the FOI Act. That Act of course is later in time than the AAT Act, and it also deals with the specific question of the grant of access to documents pursuant to that Act. What is of the highest importance for present purposes is that in particular it provides, in s.64(1), as follows:

"Where there are proceedings before the Tribunal under this Act in relation to a document that is claimed to be an exempt document, section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 does not apply in relation to the document but if the Tribunal is not satisfied, by evidence on affidavit or otherwise, that the document is an exempt document it may require the document to be produced for inspection by members of the Tribunal only and if, upon the inspection, the Tribunal is satisfied that the document is an exempt document, the Tribunal shall return the document to the person by whom it was produced without permitting any person other than a member of the Tribunal as constituted for the purposes of the proceeding, or a member of the staff of the Tribunal in the course of the performance of his duties as a member of that staff, to have access to the document or disclosing the contents of the document to any such person."

Pausing at that point, it can in my opinion be said that there is a modification of the provisions of s.37 of the AAT Act. It is in no way repealed, it is in no way set aside, but in the context of freedom of information part of s.37 no longer applies, being the part which provides that every document or part of a document that is in the possession or under the control of the decision maker and considered by him to be relevant for review of a decision by the Tribunal must be lodged with the Tribunal. So the first point is that the respondent in this case is under no obligation to lodge that document with the AAT, and I make the point of course that s.37 of the AAT Act is dealing with relevant documents generally, whereas under the FOI Act what we see is a statutory dealing with a document that is itself the foundation of the dispute.

10. The critical part of s.64(1) of the FOI Act is as follows:

". . . if the Tribunal is not satisfied, by evidence on affidavit or otherwise, that the document is an exempt document it may require the document to be produced for inspection by members of the Tribunal only . . . . " (underlining supplied)

This appears to me to be a quite clear provision, with no ambiguity in it, emphasising that the document may be produced for inspection by members of the Tribunal "only" and even if it stopped there, the inference would be clear, I believe, that inspection by members of the Tribunal "only" means just that.

11. The point is made clearer, however, when one reads on and sees that:

". . . if, upon the inspection, the Tribunal is satisfied that the document is an exempt document, the Tribunal shall return the document to the person by whom it was produced without permitting any person other than a member of the Tribunal as constituted for the purposes of the proceeding, or a member of the staff of the Tribunal in the course of the performance of his duties as a member of that staff, to have access to the document or disclosing the contents of the document to any such person."

That provision appears to me to provide a clear, legislative direction that access to the document for the purpose of the proceedings is to be to members of the Tribunal only and to staff of the Tribunal who are obliged to see it for the purpose of their duties.

12. Mr Moshinsky also referred to s.58(2). That section, I think, does reinforce the apparent legislative attitude to the point in dispute because it provides that:

"Where, in proceedings under this section, it is established that a document is an exempt document, the Tribunal does not have power to decide that access to the document, so far as it contains exempt mater, is to be granted."

That provision of course emphasises that the Tribunal does have a rather different role under the FOI Act from that which it has in other proceedings. It is in the present context reviewing a claim to an exemption where we are dealing with exemptions, and the Tribunal has power to decide whether the document is an exempt document. It does not decide that access shall or shall not be granted.

13. It would also be a very strange thing if the Tribunal could not make an order for access to a document when the question of exemption has been finalised but could do so at an interlocutory stage. But the principal difficulty lies in s.64(1), which as I have said in my opinion makes the matter quite clear. I might add that the amendments to the Act consequent upon the repeal of the provisions relating to the Document Review Tribunal put in place even more special provisions in relation to the production to the Tribunal of documents where a certificate has been given under ss.33, 33(a), 34, 35 or 36. We are not concerned with those provisions here, but I think that they do emphasise the close attention given in the legislation to the manner in which, and the occasions upon which, the Tribunal itself may inspect the document for the purposes of the Act. So the matter resolves itself at this stage, in my opinion, simply by an observance of the provisions of s.64(1), which I see as a clear staturory indication limiting the persons who may inspect documents and to that extent modifying the provisions of the AAT Act.

14. A number of other matters were raised before me. Mr Myers, as I have already mentioned, referred to these being adversarial proceedings in which counsel will be engaged and so on. I have noted what he says, but of course the precise form taken by the proceedings from time to time will not enable the Tribunal to be influenced in its attitude to the provisions of s.64(1). I have come to the conclusion that whether an applicant be represented or not the position remains the same, that the Tribunal itself has only a limited ability to inspect the documents and that it is not empowered to provide access to those documents to an applicant or to a representative of an applicant.

15. Reference was made to the position at common law, and Mr Moshinsky referred me to a number of well known authorities, but as I said in the course of discussion I do not find the position at common law an altogether satisfactory analogy. The position at common law in relation to the right of a Judge hearing a matter to inspect the document where a question of public interest immunity, for example, arises, is quite different. The fact is here, of course, that it is the document that is at the heart of the matter that is in question, not some document that may be relevant at the periphery of relevance, and here we have in the FOI Act a specific provision in relation to the right of the Tribunal to inspect the document.

16. Nevertheless, having said that, the authorities to which I was referred by Mr Moshinsky, in particular the decision of the High Court in Alister v R (1983) 50 ALR 41 appear to leave the present law in a position that is entirely consistent with the notion that there will be occasions where the law regards it as appropriate for a Tribunal to inspect a document without a similar right being conferred upon a party to the litigation. The very form in which I have expressed that however leaves me nevertheless remaining uncomfortable as to the relevance of that common law position in the statutory situation in which it arises for consideration before the Tribunal.

17. I was also referred by Mr Moshinsky to the report of the Senate Standing Committee on Constitutional and Legal Affairs on Freedom of Information Bill 1978 (Parliamentary Paper No.272/1979) where the question of discovery of documents claimed to be exempt is referred to at pp.315-315. Once again, what is said there is consistent with the position I have taken here. I could not, however, rely on that Report. The winds of change in relation to reference to extrinsic materials may be blowing into a gale, but at present it is simply, in my view, unnecessary to go to that Report having regard to the clear way in which the legislation is expressed. I do note, however, that the Full Court of the Federal Court did not, I think, regard it as appropriate to rely upon that Report in The News Corporation Limited & Ors v National Companies and Securities Commission (1 March 1984). There were a number of other authorities referred to but I think it unnecessary to mention them.

18. We did mention during the course of discussion the American authority of Vaughn v Rosen [1973] USCADC 464; (158 US App DC 340, 484 F.2d 820) which is in fact referred to in the Senate Report, and it is interesting that the procedure which we have adopted in this and other cases of making a direction as to the filing of affidavits, and in effect as to the provision of an index of the withheld documents accompanied by a specific justification for its action, as it is put in the Senate Report, accords with the procedure adopted in Vaughn v Rosen.

19. I acknowledge that to those brought up in the customs of the common law the situation in which a Tribunal looks at a document of which one party is fully cognizant and has a copy before it, while the party seeking access to the document does not have it before him, is an awkward and, on its face and on one view, an unfair proceeding, but it is a question of the balance of fairness and obviously the legislature has decided that applicants are to be only able to know in a general way what are the terms of the disputed document for the purpose of deciding a claim for exemption. I therefore decline to make the direction sought.
