WCVB and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2021] AATA 5684 (16 December 2021)

Catchwords

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – objection to summons – whether summons’ are relevant to proceedings – whether summons’ are overly oppressive – summons’ to be amended and re-issued

Legislation

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth)



Cases

Cosco Holdings Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1997] FCA 1504; (1997) 37 ATR 432

Eldridge v Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 369

South Pacific Hotel Services Pty Inc v Southern Pacific Hotel Corporation Ltd [1983] 1 NSWLR 710

Trade Practices Commission v Arnotts [1989] FCA 248



REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. In Eldridge v Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 369, Foster J reflected on the nature of the Tribunal as an executive decision-maker. His Honour pointed out (at [41]):
    Although it could be said to be part of an administrative hierarchy, its functions partake far more of the Court than of the office desk.
  2. One of the Tribunal’s court-like features is the power to issue a summons. The recipient of a summons is compelled to produce documents or attend and give evidence. When used correctly, the summons power enables the Tribunal (or, more commonly the party asking the Tribunal to issue the summons) to gather information the Tribunal needs to make the correct or preferable decision. Like any coercive power, the summons power should be exercised deliberately and with appropriate restraint.
  3. This decision and reasons relate to an interlocutory application by WCVB, the applicant in these proceedings. WCVB has challenged the breadth and relevance of summonses issued at the request of the Commissioner of Taxation. The Commissioner has defended the summonses that were issued at his request. He says he needs the information that he expects to be provided to complete his statement of facts, issues and contentions (SFIC), and to assist the Tribunal.

Background

  1. WCVB is a member of a corporate group. It sold three adjoining properties in 2010. The contracts for sale were completed in August 2010. It is agreed the proceeds of sale should have been included as revenue in the income year ending 30 June 2011. Following an audit, the Commissioner issued an amended assessment that:
    (a) included an additional amount as assessable income in the 2011 year which represented the difference between the reported sale price of the properties and the amount which the Commissioner says should have been included pursuant to s 6-5 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA97) (the ‘sale price issue’); and

    (b) disallowed deductions that had been claimed pursuant to s 8-1 of ITAA97 in relation to interest expenses incurred (the ‘interest deduction’ issue) and the costs of construction that were billed to the applicant (the ‘cost of construction’ issue).

  2. WCVB objected to the amended assessments. It has now commenced review proceedings under Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (the TAA). WCVB has already filed a SFIC in these proceedings in which it articulated its case. It has also produced evidentiary material upon which it proposes relying at the hearing. The Commissioner has not yet filed a SFIC. The Commissioner argues this is a substantiation case and there are significant gaps in the applicant’s evidence. The Commissioner says he can best assist the Tribunal by obtaining further documents from the applicant and others (including associated entities) that might shed light on the three issues that lie at the heart of the dispute. To that end, he requested the issue of eight summonses. Those summonses were subsequently issued, but the applicant has demurred.
  3. The applicant says I should set aside or require substantial amendments to several of the summonses. The applicant argues some of the requests for documents are improper because they are overbroad and – importantly - do not relate to matters or periods that are before the Tribunal. The applicant also argues some of the requests are oppressive and place an undue burden on the recipients of the summons. Some of those recipients are unrelated third parties. As it happens, the National Australia Bank, Suncorp-Metway and the liquidator of another related entity have already provided documents pursuant to a summons each of them received as part of this process. I will not address the arguments about oppression in relation to those summonses given those entities have already done what was required of them. At the interlocutory hearing, the Commissioner made some minor concessions about amendments that might be made to the terms of several of the summonses, and it was also agreed the Tribunal should set aside and re-issue a summons directed to the Australian Securities and Investments Commission after the applicant indicated it would not press its objection to that summons.

General principles governing a summons to produce documents in Tribunal proceedings

  1. The power to issue a summons is now contained in s 40A of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) (the AAT Act). A summons is typically issued at the request of a party to the proceedings. In the past, a summons would be issued by the Tribunal’s registry when a request was received if the request was regular on its face. If there was a dispute about the summons, it was generally left to the other party to raise its concerns. These days, in the Taxation & Commercial and Small Business Taxation Divisions of the Tribunal, the Divisions’ Practice Registrar or one of the Tribunal’s Conference Registrars reviews requests when they are filed. If a Registrar is satisfied the request is appropriate and the terms of the summons are sufficiently clear and precise, the summons will issue without further deliberation – although the other party to the proceedings remains free to ask the Tribunal to set the summons aside.
  2. A Registrar does not have the power to refuse a summons, but – by virtue of their specialist experience – they are in a good position to screen applications. If the Registrar believes the documents sought are irrelevant to the issues in dispute, or worries that the summons is overbroad or oppressive, the Registrar will discuss the concerns with the party making the request to see if appropriate changes can be made. If a Registrar is not persuaded to issue the summons, the matter will be brought on for an interlocutory hearing before a member who will make a decision on the request after hearing from the parties.
  3. The power to issue a summons is discretionary. Section 40A does not specify the circumstances in which the discretion should be exercised, nor does it articulate the principles which govern the scope and limitations of a summons. The summons power has come to be regulated according to the same principles which govern the issue and scope of subpoenas in courts: see generally Cosco Holdings Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1997] FCA 1504; (1997) 37 ATR 432. In that case, Spender J referred to a range of authorities including Trade Practices Commission v Arnotts [1989] FCA 248 that discussed the principles regulating the issue of a subpoena. In Arnotts, Beaumont J explained the Court was concerned to ensure (a) each subpoena was issued for a proper purpose, and (b) the subpoena did not have an unjust or oppressive effect on the recipient. His Honour suggested at least two questions presented themselves for consideration (at [44]):

  1. Does the material sought have an apparent relevance to the issues in the principal proceedings, ie, is adjectival, as distinct from substantive, relevance established? Does the subpoena have a legitimate forensic purpose to this extent? This involves a consideration of the matter from the standpoint of [the entity requesting issue of the subpoena];
  2. Is the subpoena seriously and unfairly burdensome or prejudicial?
  3. Beaumont J concluded the first question was resolved in favour of the party issuing the subpoena in that case because the party established ‘adjectival relevance’. As Spender J explained in Cosco, that expression meant, in effect, the Court was satisfied:
    ...the material sought could reasonably be expected to throw light on some of the issues in the principal proceedings.
  4. Spender J emphasised the power was not to be used speculatively. There must be a reasonable expectation that potentially useful evidence was available. A party will not be permitted to issue a summons in the hope that something might turn up. A speculative enquiry like that would be a classic ‘fishing expedition’, which would be objectionable in any event because it would be burdensome and disproportionate.
  5. His Honour also cited authorities explaining that a summons would likely be regarded as oppressive or an abuse of process if it is not “couched in terms of reasonable particularity”, especially where it is directed to a third party. A stranger to the litigation should not be unreasonably burdened by the obligation to produce large numbers of documents. What is unreasonable will depend on all the circumstances, including the interest of the recipient of the summons, the likely value of the documents in the dispute, and the public interest in the due administration of justice: see South Pacific Hotel Services Pty Inc v Southern Pacific Hotel Corporation Ltd [1983] 1 NSWLR 710 at 710 per Clarke J.
  6. It will typically be easier to make a judgment about the adjectival relevance of documents sought under a summons if the party making the request has already prepared a SFIC which succinctly identifies the issues in dispute. The Commissioner has not done that yet in this case. The parties were nonetheless able to agree on the three issues I have already identified. They did not agree on much else. Ms Kovacs, the Commissioner’s counsel, pointed out the Commissioner was at an informational disadvantage: while the Commissioner was not persuaded by the applicant’s arguments at the audit and objection stages, he needed additional information from the applicant and others to properly assist the Tribunal in evaluating the (limited) evidence the applicant has now produced. Ms Kovacs conceded, in effect, that the Commissioner wanted to access documents held by the applicants and others to assist the Commissioner – and in due course, the Tribunal – to get to the bottom of what the applicant had done in relation to the transactions which lie at the heart of this dispute.
  7. When the rationale of the request is explained in those terms, the warnings about fishing spring to mind. Ms Kaur-Bains, counsel for the applicant, said this is not the time for the Commissioner to be embarking on such a broad-ranging enquiry. She said the Commissioner should have used his extensive powers at audit to obtain the information he needed to form a view. If the Commissioner was only now seeking information to defend the amended assessment, there might be a question over whether that assessment should have issued in the first place.
  8. At first glance, it might appear surprising that the Commissioner is only now asking for information that would presumably have been relevant during the audit and at the time he decided to issue the amended assessment. But Ms Kovacs pointed out the Commissioner is not confined in the Tribunal review to the reasons he gave for issuing the amended assessment. While he is obliged to put the other side on notice of any fresh contentions he wishes to make in the course of the Tribunal’s review in a timely way – something that is typically done in the SFIC – the review occurs de novo (albeit the applicant is confined to the original grounds of objection unless he obtains leave to amend). If the concept of a de novo review is to mean anything, Ms Kovacs argued, the Commissioner must be free to seek additional information that may assist me to understand what really occurred in circumstances where the Commissioner doubts the applicant has been (or is being) completely forthcoming.
  9. Ms Kovacs’ submissions draw attention to the unusual dynamics in the relationship that exists between the Commissioner and taxpayers. The incidence of many taxes depends on the individual circumstances of the taxpayer, or upon facts that are best known to the individual taxpayer. The Commissioner is almost always guessing at the affairs of taxpayers – whereas individuals are presumably in the best position to know and justify their own affairs. Our self-assessment system can only work if taxpayers accept the burden of establishing the facts. Many of those facts may be peculiarly within their own knowledge. The alternative to this system would be to give the Commissioner much more intrusive powers to monitor and intervene in the affairs of individuals so the Commissioner can be sure the correct amount of tax is being paid.
  10. Ms Kovacs said there was nothing unusual or improper in the Commissioner’s request when understood in the context I have just described. The Commissioner should not be unreasonably limited in making legitimate forensic choices when presenting his case. In appropriate cases, he might be content to test the evidence through cross-examination and compare it to documents that have already been produced. In such a case, he might simply argue the taxpayer has not discharged the taxpayer’s burden of establishing the amount by which the assessment was excessive. But there will be cases where the evidence being presented by the applicant is potentially misleading if it is not properly contextualised or tested with respect to evidence the Commissioner has not already uncovered, but which he reasonably believes may exist. Indeed, there may be some cases where the Commissioner will be failing in his statutory duty to assist the Tribunal if he does not actively probe the applicant’s affairs with a view to gathering evidence that completed the record the Tribunal needs to make the correct or preferable decision. The process for screening summons’ requests and potentially setting aside a summons ensures the Commissioner can justify those forensic choices and avoid an undue burden on the taxpayer or others.
  11. Ms Kovacs pointed out the test I must apply when considering a summons is not one of relevance. That is the criterion I apply when considering whether to admit evidence. At this stage of our inquiry, the test (or one of the tests, at any rate) is adjectival relevance – and Ms Kovacs says each summons that has been issued seeks documents which I can reasonably expect will shed light on the issues (or perhaps, more accurately, shed light on the applicant’s case).
  12. With that said, I turn to the individual summons’. In these reasons, I will refer to the summons consecutively as ‘summons A’, ‘summons B’, and so on, because these proceedings are being held in private. The key to the identity of the recipient of each summons will be included in annexure one to these reasons. In order to give effect to the privacy regime in the TAA, I will direct pursuant to s 35 of the AAT Act that the names in the annexure be redacted in the version of these reasons which is published to the public. I will also make an order under s 35 in the text of these reasons to conceal the identify of an entity whose identity should not be disclosed if the Tribunal is to give effect to its obligations.

Summons A

  1. The first summons I will discuss is the summons addressed to the applicant company. It contains seven paragraphs.
  2. The first paragraph asks for “all documents which refer to the sale of land [i.e. the identified parcels which lie at the heart of these proceedings]...in 2010...”.
  3. Ms Kaur-Bains pointed out the proceedings relate to the amended assessment that was issued in the income year concluding on 30 June 2011. Some of the documents sought in the relevant paragraph might be dated in the first half of that year. She questions the potential relevance of those documents. Ms Kovacs argued the sale price achieved on the transaction lies at the heart of the dispute, and documents that refer to but pre-date the transaction were therefore adjectively relevant. Ms Kovacs is right. At this point, there is a reasonable expectation that the documents might shed light on an issue in the proceedings, and the terms of the request is sufficiently particular that the applicant company will not be unduly inconvenienced.
  4. The second paragraph asks for “All correspondence relating to the [named properties]...during the period from 1 April 2010 to 1 October 2010 between [the applicant’s director]” and a list of named entities, including related parties, lawyers and financiers, who have some connection to the transaction. Ms Kaur-Bains says the relevance of the documents is unclear, but she is also concerned about the generality of the description. Ms Kovacs points out the director provided a statement in which he refers (at [36]) to negotiations with some of these individuals over price of the land. That is potentially relevant to the core of the issue before the Tribunal. I am satisfied there is a reasonable expectation that the material generated prior to the settlement of the sales in July might shed light on the issues before me. I do have some concerns about the precision of the request, however. In order to prevent the request being oppressive, the request ought to be limited to documents containing any discussion of price or consideration for the property.
  5. There is no contest in relation to paragraphs three, four, five and seven of the summons. The applicant remains concerned about the relevance and breadth of the request in paragraph six, which refers to:
    All documents which refer to the sale of development documents relating to [the named property]... including correspondence from the period 1 April 2010 to 1 October 2010 between [the applicant’s director and range of related parties, lawyers and financiers].
  6. The Commissioner says he expects the documents will shed light on how the transaction involving the development documents relates to the contracts for sale of land. The Commissioner admits that, at this stage, the relationship is unclear – but he says there is good reason to believe the documents will help sort that out. I accept the documents have adjectival relevance even though some of the documents might pre-date the year of income in question, and some of them post-date the settlement of the land transactions. Ms Kaur-Bains also pointed out that one of the entities referred to – [a related entity] or its representatives – did not appear to be formally involved in the transaction – but I understand it is related to an entity that is involved. I am satisfied that adjectival relevance is established and the scope and specificity of the request is not such that it could be said to be oppressive in all the circumstances.

Summons B

  1. The second summons is addressed to the applicant’s director. Numbered paragraphs one and two correspond with paragraphs one and two in Summons A. It seems to me the same reasons apply, which means there must be an amendment to paragraph two of Summons B. Paragraph three is in the same terms as paragraph six in Summons A; it is unobjectionable for the same reasons.
  2. The applicant does not take any objection to paragraph four, but it does take exception to the request for bank statements held by the company which also had an ownership interest in the land (sought in paragraph five) between 1 July 2009 and 30 June 2011 and a request for bank statements for two accounts of another company (sought in paragraph six) over the same period. Ms Kaur-Bains said the request for bank statements over such an extended period prior to the year of income in question cannot be related to any legitimate forensic purpose.
  3. Ms Kovacs said it was appropriate to seek bank accounts from the co-owner of the land in circumstances where the entity may have received income from the sale transaction. While the extended timeframe of the request might seem very wide at first glance, it was explained that the property was held over a long period and that costs were incurred over that time even though they did not show up for present purposes until the 2011 year of income. The same logic applies to suggest the account statements referred to in paragraph six over a longer period might also have adjectival relevance in that they might shed some light on the final price. I do not regard the requests as being oppressive. Requiring an individual to reproduce bank statements over a defined period is not onerous in the circumstances.
  4. The Commissioner does not press paragraph seven but says the documents referred to in paragraph 8 are adjectivally relevant. Ms Kaur-Bains repeats the concern that the documents do not appear to relate to the 2011 year of income but I accept they have adjectival relevance at this point for reasons I have explained. Ms Kovacs referred to evidence that the director had numerous conversations with the bank officer concerned about the value of the property over a long period. The request is not oppressive in the circumstances.

Summons C

  1. Summons C is directed to an accounting firm. Ms Kovacs points out the applicant has already provided a statement from its accountant incorporating a spreadsheet referring to some of the data the Commissioner seeks. She also points to the Commissioner’s belief that there were otherwise undocumented intercompany transactions amongst parties related in various ways that were likely to show up in financial records. She argues all of the documents sought plainly have adjectival relevance in the circumstances. She says the applicant should not be permitted to disclose some documents but not others where those others might provide a more comprehensive picture. I agree, although I should address the individual paragraphs.
  2. Paragraph one requests the firm to disclose documents relating to the 2002-2012 income years for the applicant company. Ms Kaur-Bains argued the request was oppressive because it refers to accounting records in the broadest of terms. She also argued the extended timeframe of the request is not for a legitimate forensic purpose. Ms Kovacs pointed out the final transaction which lies at the heart of the dispute was potentially shaped by what occurred earlier, so there is adjectival relevance. While I accept the request for records defines those records widely, it is not apparent to me why those records could not be accessed and reproduced without undue burden (assuming they still exist). It is open to the accounting firm to explain any unexpected difficulties in complying with the request.
  3. The documents sought in paragraph two also have adjectival relevance. The documents in question are records of a related company that was involved in the construction and improvement of the properties in question. The activities of that company may well bear upon the construction cost issue in particular. Given those activities occurred over a longer period, the timeframe of the request is not unreasonable in all the circumstances.
  4. Ms Kovacs argues the financial documents sought from the company in paragraph three which was involved in the development document transaction also has adjectival relevance given the Commissioner’s legitimate objective of exploring the relationship between the property sales and the sale of the development documents. The extended time frame and the scope of the request does not strike me as unreasonable in the circumstances.
  5. I was told the documents sought in paragraph four had adjectival relevance because the company in question acquired the work in progress from another construction company and those costs manifested in the 2011 year of income when the company invoiced the applicant. At this stage of the inquiry, that is enough to warrant the Commissioner asking for documents – and the request does not otherwise appear oppressive.
  6. The documents referred to in paragraph five relate to the income tax returns (including the working papers) of the applicant in the 2008 to 2011 income years. For the reasons I have already explained, documents recording financial interactions and transactions surrounding the transactions occurring in late 2010 have adjectival relevance – and given the dealings with the property appear to have occurred over a lengthy period, it is appropriate that the requests extend to a number of years preceding the 2011 year of income.
  7. Paragraph six asks for the income tax returns and supporting material for the 2010 and 2011 years of income for the company involved in the development documents transaction. I am satisfied the Commissioner has established adjectival relevance in that he says there is a reasonable prospect the information contained in the income tax returns and working papers can be compared against the information yielded by the financial statements sought from the same company under paragraph two of the summons. I am not satisfied it would be unreasonable or oppressive to require production of the documents in all the circumstances.
  8. The last paragraph of the summons asks for all versions of an excel spreadsheet that was annexed to the accountant’s statement. Ms Kovacs said the Commissioner is aware there may be several different versions of the spreadsheet which disclose different information. I am satisfied the Commissioner has established adjectival relevance, and that it would not be oppressive to require production of the documents.
  9. I acknowledge there may yet be a claim for privilege in respect of some classes of documents. That claim can be made when the documents are produced to the Tribunal and inspected by the applicant.

Summons D

  1. This summons was issued to the National Australia Bank. I understand the documents have already been produced. In those circumstances, I will not address any of the arguments about oppression of the recipient.
  2. The Commissioner has agreed not to press paragraph six, so the documents produced pursuant to that request may be returned. He has also agreed he could only justify seeing bank statements in paragraph four for the period 30 June 2010 to 30 June 2011. The summons should be amended to that extent and the documents falling outside the amended period should be returned to the bank.
  3. The remaining requests all refer to bank statements of companies that I understand are in the group or involved in transactions, such as the company involved in the development documents transaction. The Commissioner has demonstrated adjectival relevance for now: there is reason to believe those documents will shed light on the issues which appear to arise out of a complicated set of interactions between different companies that manifested in the 2011 income year.

Summons E

  1. This summons was directed to a law firm which held documents referring to the sale of the parcels of land, including correspondence between the firm and named individuals. Ms Kaur-Bains foreshadowed there might be some claims of privilege, but it seems to me that claim should be made when the documents are produced and identified on inspection. She agreed there was no difficulty asking for copies of the contracts of sale, since they were of central importance in the proceedings. Ms Kaur-Bains did object to the requests contained in numbered paragraph five which referred to documents (including correspondence with named individuals) that referred to the sale of the development documents. I note paragraph six also seeks correspondence referring to the Deed of Agreement.
  2. Ms Kovacs said all of the documents would all assist in understanding the circumstances of the transactions and interactions over a period that led up to the year of income. I accept the Commissioner has established adjectival relevance.

Summons F

  1. The next summons was issued to Suncorp-Metway. I understand the bank has already responded to the summons and the documents have been produced. Ms Kaur-Bains did argue the terms of the request were oppressively wide, but I can leave that objection to one side in the circumstances. The summons requests documents and correspondence relating to the sales of the land. Given those transactions lie at the heart of the dispute, I have no difficulty accepting the Commissioner has established adjectival relevance.

Conclusion

  1. The respondent shall amend the summonses in accordance with these reasons so that they may be reissued by the Tribunal.
