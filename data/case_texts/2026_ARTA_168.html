Yelda and Commissioner of Taxation (Practice and procedure) [2026] ARTA 168 (22 January 2026)

Applicant: Reem Yelda

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2025/3648

Tribunal: General Member C. Willis

Place: Melbourne

Date: 22/01/2026

Decision: The Tribunal refuses the application for reinstatement under section 102 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth).



...............................[SGD].........................................

General Member C. Willis

Catchwords

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – application for reinstatement – application previously dismissed on basis that no reviewable decision – applicant seeking review of decision made by Respondent under Compensation for Detriment caused by Defective Administration (CDDA) scheme – application for reinstatement refused

Legislation

Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth), sections 97, 102

Statement of Reasons

BACKGROUND

Introduction

  1. Ms Yelda (the Applicant) is seeking reinstatement of an application she previously made to the Tribunal on 8 May 2025. That application was dismissed by the Tribunal on 14 October 2025 (the Dismissal Decision) on the basis that the decision which the Applicant had identified in her application was not one that the Tribunal was able to review.
  2. The Applicant wrote to the Tribunal on 9 November 2025 to say that she was seeking reinstatement:
    ‘on several grounds, particularly that the General Member made errors of facts and law in her decision. I believe she failed to properly consider all the issues, my personal circumstances and all the relevant evidence.’
  3. The Applicant included with her request for reinstatement a bundle of documents and further written submissions as to why she believed the Dismissal Decision was made in error.
  4. The reinstatement application was listed for a hearing by telephone on 12 January 2026. This hearing was conducted by a different General Member to the one who had made the Dismissal Decision.

The original Tribunal application and Dismissal Decision

  1. In her original application to the Tribunal the Applicant identified the decision she wanted reviewed as one made by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) on 16 April 2025 (the Respondent Decision). The Applicant had made a claim in late 2024[1] for compensation under the ‘Compensation for Detriment caused by Defective Administration Scheme’ (CDDA Scheme). The Respondent Decision was to the effect that compensation was not payable to the Applicant.
  2. The Tribunal sets out below at some length certain events which led up to the Applicant’s claim for compensation. To be clear, this does not constitute a review by the Tribunal of these events or the parties involved in them. The Applicant has said that in making the Dismissal Decision the Tribunal did not properly consider her personal circumstances and all relevant evidence and made errors of fact. In addressing the Applicant’s reinstatement application, the Tribunal wishes to assure the Applicant that the matters she has brought to the Tribunal have been carefully considered.
  3. At a high level the Applicant’s claim for compensation arose from an underlying dispute between the parties over a tax debt and cancellation of the Applicant’s Australian Business Number (ABN). Processes that have occurred during this dispute have given rise to further matters of contention between the parties, including processes which involved the Taxation Ombudsman.[2]
  4. The Applicant told the Tribunal that around April 2016 there was an incident at her former workplace which led to her making a workers compensation claim. She became unwell and unable to work and felt that she was being harassed and bullied by her former employer, which was a NSW government agency. Her workers compensation payments stopped in 2017 leaving her in financial difficulties, such that she needed to sell an investment property to pay her essential living expenses. She said that in 2018 or 2019 she called the Respondent to speak to them about a tax debt and her circumstances at the time. She recalled that an officer of the Respondent told her that they would put her tax debt on hold, but she was to contact them if her circumstances changed.
  5. The Applicant was involved in litigation against her former employer and another party between 2019 and 2021 in the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal and Supreme Court of New South Wales. She received an amount of $100,000 by way of damages. Although the decision relating to the damages was made in June 2021, she ultimately did not receive the amount until September 2021. She contacted the Respondent in June 2021 to inquire about her tax debt and tax return. During this call she said that she and the officer of the Respondent with whom she was speaking could see on her myGov account a notation from July 2019 which said ‘Non-pursuit – uneconomical to review.’ The ‘balance’ of her existing tax debt on her myGov account was shown as reduced from over $45,000 to nil on that date.[3]
  6. The Applicant said that a dispute subsequently arose with the Respondent as to how much tax she should be paying for the 2021 income year. The Applicant had also received a payout of her long service leave entitlements in late 2020 but believed that she had already paid tax on the amount of that payout. She said that her accountant at the time, Mr S, initially told her that she had to pay the tax however she sought further advice from a tax barrister, Mr HP. Mr S subsequently lodged her tax returns for the 2021 and 2022 income years on the basis of Mr HP’s more favourable advice. The Respondent then commenced an audit of the Applicant’s tax affairs and withheld the tax refunds that she had been expecting.
  7. The Applicant told the Tribunal that she had been working as an Uber driver when she was not sick and had an ABN for this purpose. This was her only source of income since she left her previous employer. However in early 2023 the Respondent advised that it intended to cancel her ABN on the basis of inactivity. She said that she agreed to cancel it at the time because her accountant (Mr S) told her that she could reactivate the ABN if she needed it in the future. Later in 2023 she tried to do so and found that her application to reactivate her ABN was rejected.
  8. The Applicant formed the view that the Respondent’s staff were intentionally trying to prevent or delay her from working and that she was being targeted by another government organisation. She told the Tribunal that the actions of the Respondent were similar to those of her former employer and she wanted the staff involved to be held accountable for their actions.
  9. Mr S made a complaint to the Taxation Ombudsman on the Applicant’s behalf in December 2023. On 28 March 2024 the Taxation Ombudsman responded to the complaint.[4] The Applicant was dissatisfied with the Taxation Ombudsman’s response. Mr S indicated that he was unable to assist the Applicant any further so the Applicant then contacted the Taxation Ombudsman directly seeking a review by the Taxation Ombudsman of its previous investigation of her complaint. The Taxation Ombudsman further investigated her complaint and wrote again to the Applicant on 21 June 2024 advising that although an officer of the Respondent had apparently provided her with incorrect advice about her tax debt during the telephone call in June 2021, the Respondent was nevertheless obliged by law to offset her tax refunds against her tax debt.[5] The Applicant had also complained that she had not been able to view in her myGov account some of the Respondent’s correspondence as referenced by the Taxation Ombudsman. The Taxation Ombdudsman explained that not all documentation from the Respondent was visible in myGov accounts and that the Applicant could make an FOI request.
  10. The Taxation Ombudsman further advised the Applicant that it was not empowered to recommend that the Respondent pay the Applicant compensation, nor was it able to waive tax debts. The Applicant expressed her dissatisfaction that the Taxation Ombudsman did not have the power to grant her compensation or otherwise resolve her dispute with the Respondent.
  11. Following information from the Taxation Ombudsman the Applicant then submitted a claim to the Respondent for compensation under the CDDA Scheme. According to a summary of her claim prepared by the Respondent she sought monetary compensation for the fees she paid to Mr HP (the tax barrister) for his advice[6], fees paid to her accountant Mr S and to a law firm[7] and an amount of $44,590.46 for ‘false statements’.[8] The Applicant also sought release of the tax refunds owing for the 2021 and 2022 income years in accordance with Mr HP’s advice, reimbursement or removal of any general interest charge (GIC) that had been imposed on her and for the Respondent to not withhold any of her future tax refunds.
  12. The Respondent provided the Applicant with a ‘preliminary view’ letter dated 19 March 2025 in which it advised its view that compensation was not payable to the Applicant under the CDDA Scheme and that the Respondent was unable to provide the other remedies sought by the Applicant. The Respondent invited the Applicant to provide any further information she wished the Respondent to consider before the decision was finalised. The Respondent Decision of 16 April 2025 represents that final decision.
  13. On 8 May 2025 the Applicant applied to the Tribunal for a review of the Respondent Decision.
  14. In her application to the Tribunal the Applicant said that the Respondent Decision was wrong because:
    ‘Defective administration, mala administration and breach of the (Taxation Administration ACT 1953).

    The ATO’s rejection of my claim is unfair because it failed to acknowledge its own administrative errors and the serious impact those errors had on my personal wellbeing over several years.

    Despite clear evidence of false statements, misuse of power, and lack of transparency, the ATO has avoided accountability, while I have been left to bear the consequences and costs.

    It is unjust for the ATO to hold taxpayers to the highest standards of compliance while exempting itself from the same expectations of accuracy, fairness, and ethical conduct.’

  15. On 21 May 2025 the Tribunal wrote to the Applicant advising that it could not find any Commonwealth law which said that the Applicant could seek review of the Respondent Decision. The Tribunal therefore believed that it could not review the Respondent Decision and asked the Applicant to contact the Tribunal within 14 days if she disagreed.
  16. The Applicant wrote to the Tribunal on 23 May 2025 asking for details of which laws prevented the Tribunal from reviewing her claim and stating that she believed there were grounds for review under section 14ZZ of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 1953) or ‘another applicable provision’. She asked that the Tribunal consider whether jurisdiction existed ‘based on the facts and context of this matter and my circumstances.’ The Applicant outlined certain circumstances including:
    (a) She said that errors of the Respondent had caused a significant financial and emotional impact on her. Administrative errors by the Respondent since June 2021 had resulted in significant financial loss and stress to her.

    (b) The Respondent and the Taxation Ombudsman had failed to resolve her issues which left her without recourse. The Respondent and Taxation Ombudsman had failed to consider her personal circumstances and had dismissed her claim without proper consideration of her circumstances. It was therefore important for the Tribunal to consider her personal circumstances.

    (c) She believed that the Respondent and Taxation Ombudsman had failed to deliver accountability and service which raised concerns for her about their effectiveness and integrity. She believed that they had acted to protect their own actions, had failed to comply with their own systems and processes and did not consider her personal circumstances.

    (d) When she made her complaint to the Taxation Ombudsman she said that she was misled into believing they had the power to resolve her claim and was only told a year later they did not.

    (e) She believes that the CDDA Scheme is ‘unfair and one sided’, that the Respondent made no attempt to settle with her and that the Respondent’s conduct was an abuse of administrative power and a waste of taxpayer money.

  17. The Tribunal listed an interlocutory hearing on the jurisdiction question of whether there was a decision which the Tribunal could review. This gave rise to the Dismissal Decision, with the Tribunal finding that:
    (a) To the extent that the Applicant complained or sought review of actions of the Taxation Ombudsman, the Taxation Ombudsman was not a respondent to the Tribunal proceeding and the Tribunal could not comment further.

    (b) The CDDA Scheme is an administrative scheme which is not established under an enactment. There was no basis for review by the Tribunal of decisions made under the CDDA Scheme. Any review of a decision under the CDDA Scheme would be under section 39B of the Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth).

    (c) Cancellation of, and refusal to issue, an ABN arise under the A New Tax System (Australian Business Number) Act 1999 (Cth) which does provide for an objection process. However, the Applicant had not made an objection in relation to the cancellation of her ABN or subsequent refusal to issue her with an ABN. Absent an objection decision, there was no ‘reviewable objection decision’ for review under the ART Act.

    (d) In circumstances where there was no reviewable decision for the purposes of the ART Act the Tribunal was obliged under section 97 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (the ART Act) to dismiss the matter, which is what the Tribunal did.

  18. In making her request for reinstatement the Applicant provided the Tribunal with a bundle of materials, including a copy of the Dismissal Decision, further copies of materials previously filed with the Tribunal and a copy of the Department of Finance’s guidance to Commonwealth agencies on the CDDA Scheme.[9] The Applicant also filed a six page document of submissions in which she said that the Tribunal did have jurisdiction to review the Respondent Decision because:
    (a) The Respondent failed to properly apply the CDDA Scheme criteria. She had demonstrated that she had met the criteria for compensation under the CDDA Scheme. The CDDA Scheme was her last resort and she had no other remedy available to her.

    (b) The Member who made the Dismissal Decision had failed to consider material grounds that were in her initial application for review, including the failure of the Respondent to follow proper administrative procedures, incorrect or misleading advice given by the Respondent and the Taxation Ombudsman, the loss of her tax refunds, the failure of the Taxation Ombudsman to act, her need to pay a barrister and other advisers substantial amounts for advice and a lack of transparency by the Respondent.

    (c) The failure of the Member to address these matters constituted an error of law and denial of procedural fairness.

    (d) The Member failed to consider the full scope of the Applicant’s complaint and narrowly defined the dispute as relating to two matters only.

    (e) The Member failed to understand that the Applicant’s claim was about both the Respondent and the Taxation Ombudsman. The Applicant understood the Taxation Ombudsman to be part of the Respondent and her claim was about the whole process.

  19. The Applicant also repeated her allegations about the conduct of staff employed by the Respondent and Taxation Ombudsman, claims of misuse of the administrative processes and ‘systemic procedural abuse.’ She said that the Respondent was vicariously liable for the actions of its employees and contractors which had caused her loss and harm. She told the Tribunal that after the Dismissal Decision she had made further unsuccessful attempts to settle with the Respondent.

SHOULD THE APPLICATION BE REINSTATED?

Relevant law

  1. Section 102 of the ART Act applies if the Tribunal dismisses an application: subsection 102(1).
  2. Where an application for review has previously been dismissed under section 97, the Tribunal may reinstate the application if it ‘considers that the application was dismissed in error’. This may be done upon a reinstatement application by a party, or the Tribunal may reinstate on its own initiative: subsections 102(3), 102(5), 102(6).[10]
  3. Section 97 provides that the Tribunal must dismiss an application for a review of a decision if the Tribunal is satisfied that the decision is not reviewable by the Tribunal. This was the basis upon which the Tribunal made the Dismissal Decision.
  4. The question for the Tribunal in this reinstatement application is, therefore, whether the Applicant’s application for review was dismissed in error on 14 October 2025, that is, whether the Dismissal Decision was incorrect. This requires a consideration of whether the Applicant has identified a decision that is reviewable by the Tribunal.
  5. It is important to understand that the Tribunal does not have the power to review every decision made by a government department, agency or entity. The ART Act sets out which decisions the Tribunal is able (by law) to review.
  6. Section 17 of the ART Act provides that a person whose interests are affected by a ‘reviewable decision’ may apply to the Tribunal for a review of the decision.
  7. Subsection 12(1) of the ART Act identifies what is a reviewable decision for this purpose and provides that:

‘A decision is a reviewable decision if an Act or a legislative instrument provides for an application to be made to the Tribunal for review of the decision.’

  1. Therefore, where an application is made to the Tribunal for review of a decision, the Tribunal must be satisfied that an Act or legislative instrument says that an application can be made for such a review.
  2. In her written submissions the Applicant did not directly engage with the provisions of the ART Act relating to reviewable decisions, as set out in the Dismissal Decision, other than to assert that a ‘reviewable objection decision’ for the purposes of the TAA 1953 had been made and that the Tribunal had the power to review her matter or ‘should consider it.’ There may have been some confusion following the Dismissal Decision as to what a ‘reviewable decision’ comprised, particularly in the context of decisions made by the Respondent or in relation to taxation matters.
  3. The Tribunal therefore asked the Applicant at the reinstatement hearing to clarify what reviewable objection decision she said had been made in relation to her and/or where a reviewable decision otherwise arose for the purposes of the ART Act. The Applicant told the Tribunal that:
    (a) The Respondent had made a number of errors in relation to the tax debt it said she owed and made further errors, which she believed were intentional, after she questioned their position. The Respondent’s behaviour in this regard was not fair and was not reasonable. She was dissatisfied with the actions of the Taxation Ombudsman following her complaint. For example, the Respondent’s recording of its telephone conversation with her could not be found.

    (b) She did not agree with, and objected to, the Respondent’s position in relation to a loan.[11] It was incorrect for the Respondent to ask her to pay tax on this basis and the Respondent was asking her to pay more tax than she should.

    (c) Her application for compensation under the CDDA Scheme was sound and she had met the Department of Finance’s guidelines in relation to the CDDA Scheme. The CDDA Scheme was not immune to review, having regard to High Court authority.

    (d) She also objected to the Dismissal Decision, which she disagreed with. She did not believe it was reasonable to suggest she take her matter to the Federal Court, which would be costly for both parties. The Tribunal’s objective included the requirement to resolve matters with as little expense as appropriate. The Tribunal should be able to make a decision as to where the Respondent had breached the CDDA Scheme and the law.

No Act or legislative instrument providing for review of a CDDA Scheme decision

  1. The CDDA Scheme is a ‘discretionary mechanism’ and ‘operates on the basis of authority provided to individual portfolio ministers under the executive power of sections 61 and 64 of the Constitution.’[12] As such, there is no Act or legislative instrument which provides for an application to be made to the Tribunal for review of a decision made in relation to a CDDA Scheme claim for compensation. This means that the requirements of subsection 12(1) of the ART Act are not met in relation to such a decision.
  2. This matter alone is sufficient for the Tribunal to find that the Respondent Decision is not a reviewable decision for the purposes of the ART Act, and the Tribunal cannot conduct a review of the Respondent Decision.
  3. The Department of Finance’s Resource Management Guide relating to the CDDA Scheme (RMG 409) states that avenues of review of CDDA Scheme decisions are as follows[13]:
    (a) An entity which determines claims under the CDDA Scheme may put in place its own formal internal review mechanisms and may choose whether to determine reconsideration on a case-by-case basis.

    (b) The Commonwealth Ombudsman may review a decision made under the CDDA Scheme, following a complaint by a claimant or of its own volition. However, the Ombudsman has no power to overturn or vary an entity’s decision.

    (c) As CDDA Scheme decisions are not made under an enactment, they are not subject to judicial review under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 but may be subject to judicial review under section 75 of the Constitution or section 39B(1) of the Judiciary Act 1903.

  4. The Applicant has already followed the Respondent’s internal review mechanisms, the outcome of which was the Respondent Decision. Although the Applicant made a complaint to the Taxation Ombudsman about the Respondent’s processes in relation to her tax debt prior to making her CDDA Scheme claim, she has not sought review of the Respondent Decision (that is, the Respondent’s decision under the CDDA Scheme) by the Commonwealth Ombudsman.[14] In this regard, the Tribunal notes that RMG 409 says that the Commonwealth Ombudsman does not have the power to overturn or vary the Respondent Decision.
  5. The Applicant referred to the decision of the High Court in Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v Li [2013] HCA 18 in support of her contention that just because the CDDA Scheme was discretionary, it did not make the Respondent Decision ‘immune from review or legal error.’ The Li matter involved a review by the former Migration Review Tribunal (MRT) under the Migration Act 1958 of a decision made under that Act to refuse Ms Li’s application for a visa. The particular issues considered by the High Court related to the MRT’s decision not to exercise a discretion to adjourn or defer its review while Ms Li obtained further evidence from a third party, which led to the MRT affirming the Minister’s decision in a manner adverse to Ms Li. The High Court found that the MRT had not made its decision to refuse an adjournment of the review on a proper basis. There was no issue as to whether the MRT had jurisdiction to review the visa refusal decision in the first place. The visa refusal decision and the application to the MRT were made under an Act, being the Migration Act 1958. The Tribunal does not believe the Li decision provides any basis for the Tribunal being required or permitted to review a decision made by the Respondent under or for the purposes of the CDDA Scheme.

Is there otherwise a reviewable objection decision?

  1. Despite the absence of an enactment or legislative instrument providing for review of a CDDA Scheme decision by the Tribunal, the Applicant submitted that there was nevertheless an objection decision made by the Respondent and the Tribunal has the power under section 14ZZ of the TAA 1953 (or another provision) to review that decision.
  2. In the context of taxation matters and decisions made by the Respondent generally, subsection 14ZZA(1) of the TAA 1953 provides that:
(1) The ART Act applies in relation to:
(a) the review of reviewable objection decisions; and

(b) the review of extension of time refusal decisions; and

(c) ART extension applications.

subject to this Division.

  1. This also means that not every decision made by the Respondent, even if made under a taxation related Act or legislative instrument, is able to be reviewed by the Tribunal.
  2. The Applicant’s present circumstances do not involve an extension of time refusal decision[15] or ART extension application. [16] It is therefore necessary to consider whether there is a ‘reviewable objection decision.’
  3. Section 14ZQ of the TAA 1953 provides that a ‘reviewable objection decision’ means ‘an objection decision that is not an ineligible income tax remission decision.’ The Applicant’s circumstances also do not involve an ineligible income tax remission decision[17], therefore the Tribunal must consider whether her application relates to an ‘objection decision.’
  4. Section 14ZY of the TAA 1953 defines an ‘objection decision’ by reference to decisions that the Respondent can or must make after receiving a taxation objection.[18] A taxation objection can only be made if a provision of an Act or a legislative instrument provides that a person who is dissatisfied with an assessment, determination, notice or decision, or with a failure to make a private ruling, may object.[19]
  5. The making of a decision under the CDDA Scheme, even when made by the Respondent in relation to taxation matters, does not give rise to any right of objection under an Act or legislative instrument. Therefore, there cannot be any objection decision following the CDDA Scheme decision. The refusal by the Respondent to provide compensation or other remedies to the Applicant under the CDDA Scheme is not an objection decision, and is therefore not a reviewable objection decision for the purposes of the ART Act.
  6. In her reinstatement application the Applicant referred to section 14ZZ of the TAA 1953 in support of her contention that she had valid grounds for review by the Tribunal. Section 14ZZ is the provision which gives a person who is ‘dissatisfied with the Commissioner’s objection decision’ rights to apply to the Tribunal for a review or to appeal to the Federal Court. Again, review and appeal rights under this provision are dependent on the existence of an objection decision: subsection 14ZZ(1). The Tribunal is unable to identify an objection decision in the Applicant’s application, despite her assertion that an objection decision exists.[20]
  7. As explained at paragraph 8 of the Dismissal Decision, there are taxation law provisions which allow a person to object against a cancellation of, or refusal to issue, an ABN. Had the Applicant lodged an objection and the Respondent made an objection decision, that objection decision would likely be subject to review by the Tribunal. However, that is not what occurred and there is no objection decision relating to the ABN matters. In its Dismissal Decision the Tribunal explained how the Applicant might make a request to lodge a late objection. In her reinstatement submission to the Tribunal, the Applicant has advised that she has since obtained her ABN. However, she still wished to seek some form of review of the processes of the Respondent and its employees, even if the ABN cancellation itself had been dealt with.
  8. The Applicant told the Tribunal at the reinstatement hearing that she objected to the Respondent’s position in relation to a loan, in the sense of disagreeing with the Respondent’s position, although she had not lodged an objection for the purposes of the TAA 1953. Therefore no objection decision exists in relation to this matter as yet.
  9. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that she objected to the Dismissal Decision, as set out in paragraph 10 of her written submissions of 9 November 2025. The Tribunal explained that this was not an ‘objection decision’ for the purposes of the TAA 1953 or ART Act. Any ‘reviewable objection decision’ would need to be made by the Respondent. The Applicant’s disagreement with the Dismissal Decision is the basis for making a reinstatement application under section 102, which is the subject of this interlocutory hearing and further Tribunal decision.
  10. The Applicant was therefore not able to identify any reviewable objection decision existing at this time.

Did the Tribunal dismiss the application in error?

  1. In summary, the Tribunal finds that the application was not dismissed in error. This is because:
    (a) In relation to the Respondent Decision, being a decision made under or in relation to the CDDA Scheme, there is no Act or legislative instrument which provides for an application for review by the Tribunal of that decision. Therefore it is not a reviewable decision for the purposes of the ART Act.

    (b) Further, and for the avoidance of doubt, although the Respondent Decision is made by the Respondent (the Commissioner of Taxation) it is not an ‘objection decision’ for the purposes of the TAA 1953 and therefore not a ‘reviewable objection decision’ to which the ART Act applies.

    (c) As the Tribunal is satisfied that the decision as identified by the Applicant is not reviewable (and the Applicant has not identified any other reviewable decision in her application) the Tribunal must dismiss her application under section 97 of the ART Act.

  2. The Tribunal cannot identify any reviewable decision in the Respondent Decision. The Tribunal therefore does not consider that the application was dismissed in error on 14 October 2025 and for this reason the Tribunal is unable to reinstate the application.
  3. In her request for reinstatement the Applicant said that the Member who made the Dismissal Decision made errors of fact and law, as well as failing to take into account relevant evidence and the Applicant’s personal circumstances. The Tribunal does not agree that the Dismissal Decision contained errors of fact or law, however, during the course of the reinstatement hearing, the Tribunal formed the impression that there may have been some misunderstanding of the Dismissal Decision. In this reinstatement decision the Tribunal has set out at length and considered the matters of evidence and personal circumstances identified by the Applicant in her submissions to the Tribunal.
  4. The Applicant identified a number of matters which she said the Member who made the Dismissal Decision failed to consider, giving rise to an error of law and a denial of procedural fairness.[21] Many of these matters go to the processes of the Respondent and the merits of the Respondent Decision. The Tribunal does not believe that this represented any error in the Dismissal Decision. This is because the application was dismissed on the basis of an absence of any reviewable decision and a merits review of the Respondent Decision could therefore not be undertaken by the Tribunal.
  5. Where there is no reviewable decision for the purposes of the ART Act the Tribunal does not have any discretion to take into account personal circumstances as the basis for a review by the Tribunal or reinstatement of a review application that has previously been dismissed. The Tribunal acknowledges that the Applicant is clearly distressed by a number of events that have occurred and is not suggesting that the Applicant has been untruthful in her submissions to the Tribunal or that the events have not had a significant impact on her. However, in the absence of a ‘reviewable decision’, any review that the Tribunal purported to undertake and any decision that the Tribunal purported to make, would have no legal effect.

Other matters

Processes of the Taxation Ombudsman

  1. In the Dismissal Decision the Tribunal noted that the Taxation Ombudsman was not a party or respondent to the application and did not intend to comment further on Taxation Ombudsman matters. This did not represent an error on the part of the Member who made the Dismissal Decision. To address a point made by the Applicant in her reinstatement submissions,[22] the Taxation Ombudsman is not part of the Respondent. It is a separate Commonwealth statutory office established and with powers arising under the Inspector General of Taxation Act 2003 (Cth) and Ombudsman Act 1976 (Cth). It is independent of the Respondent and tasked with reviewing broad taxation system issues and addressing taxpayer complaints.
  2. The Applicant submitted that she wished to challenge ‘systematic mismanagement and lack of good faith ... as a breach of administrative law principles.’[23] For the reasons given above, the Tribunal is only empowered to conduct a review of a reviewable decision.

Remedies and outcomes of the Tribunal

  1. The Applicant also referred to the Respondent being ‘vicariously liable’ for the actions of it’s employees and contractors, which she said had caused her loss. The Tribunal is unable to award compensation or require the Respondent to pay compensation.
  2. The Applicant asserted that the Tribunal had jurisdiction ‘to review whether a Commonwealth agency properly exercised its discretion under an administrative scheme, especially where I believe the criteria in RMG 409 were met but ignored.’ However in the alternative, the Applicant said ‘the Tribunal should have referred the matter to the appropriate forum rather than dismissing it outright.’[24] The Tribunal has explained above why it is unable to review the Respondent Decision. The Tribunal has also explained that where there is no reviewable decision, section 97 of the ART Act requires the Tribunal to dismiss the application. The Tribunal is unable to refer the application to another forum in lieu of dismissal. The Tribunal noted judicial review under section 39B of the Judiciary Act 1903 at paragraph 7 of the Dismissal Decision and recommended that the Applicant seek legal advice. The Tribunal also provided information about lodging a late objection in paragraph 8 of the Dismissal Decision. RMG 409, a copy of which the Applicant included with her reinstatement application also describes options for review of CDDA Scheme decisions.

Further objection processes

  1. During the reinstatement hearing, the Applicant stated that she ‘objected’ to the Respondent’s position relating to a loan and asked for clarification as to whether this type of objection could be the basis of a review by the Tribunal as a ‘reviewable objection decision.’ The Tribunal is unable to provide legal or taxation advice to parties although it is accurate to observe that decisions made by the Respondent under Division 3 of Part IVC of the TAA 1953 (‘Taxation objections’) are likely to constitute reviewable objection decisions.[25] A common example of an objection decision which is subject to review by the Tribunal is where a person is dissatisfied with an assessment made by the Respondent, that assessment having been made based on the Respondent’s view of how taxation law applies to the person and the person disagreeing with the Respondent’s view.
  2. The Applicant said that she had not lodged a formal objection under Part IVC. Therefore the Tribunal’s view is that there could be no ‘reviewable objection decision’ (as yet). The Applicant also told the Tribunal that after she had sought to resolve her dispute directly with the Respondent, the Respondent had sent her information on how to lodge an objection, although the information referred to eligibility requirements including the time within which an objection should be made. If the Applicant sought to lodge an objection in relation to an assessment of tax issued to her some years ago by the Respondent, that objection would likely be treated as having been lodged late and the Applicant would also need to make a request to lodge her objection late. These matters, and a reference to the Respondent’s guidance on late objections, were set out at paragraph 8 of the Dismissal Decision (albeit there in the context of an objection against ABN cancellation).
  3. The Applicant asked whether the Tribunal could place her current application on hold pending her lodging an objection with the Respondent. The Tribunal is unable to do this. The Respondent Decision the subject of her application is not a reviewable decision and therefore the Tribunal must dismiss her application under section 97. If the Applicant lodges a valid objection (for example, in relation to a tax assessment issued to her) and the Respondent provides an objection decision under Part IVC of the TAA 1953, that objection decision will be a separate and new decision from the Respondent Decision under the CDDA Scheme, even if it involves some or many of the same background facts. The Applicant could then consider whether she wanted to make a fresh application to the Tribunal to seek review of that new objection decision.
  4. The Tribunal made it clear that it was unable to give the Applicant any guarantee that the Respondent would agree to accept a request to lodge a late objection. This is a matter she must address with the Respondent. However the Tribunal notes that subsection 14ZZA(1) also applies the ART Act to ‘review of extension of time refusal decisions’ which means that in addition to ‘reviewable objection decisions’ the Tribunal may also be able to review a decision of the Respondent to refuse an extension of time for a person to lodge an objection: see also section 14ZX of the TAA 1953.
  5. In relation to obtaining further taxation advice, the Applicant explained her concerns relating to the significant expense that she had previously incurred in obtaining professional advice. The Respondent’s representatives referred the Tribunal and the Applicant to the National Tax Clinic program which is funded by the Commonwealth government and provides independent and free tax help to eligible people.[26]

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal has found that the Applicant’s application does not relate to a decision that is reviewable by the Tribunal. Therefore the Tribunal must dismiss her application under section 97, and the Dismissal Decision was not made in error. Therefore, the Tribunal must refuse the Applicant’s request under section 102 for reinstatement of her application.
