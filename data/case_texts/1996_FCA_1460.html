Edward Geoffrey Stuart v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1996] FCA 1460 (10 May 1996)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

EDWARD GEOFFREY STUART v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE

COMMONWEALTH OF

AUSTRALIA

No. WAG 117 OF 1995

FED No. 338/96

Number of pages - 9

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

WESTERN AUSTRALIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

R.D. NICHOLSON J

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - objections and appeals - Administrative Appeals Tribunal - whether procedural fairness denied by refusal of adjournment - whether error of law in failing to consider submissions relating to provisional tax credit.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), s207, s221YA, s227, s264,

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth), s34s, 42A, s42B, s44

Dietrich v The Queen [1992] HCA 57; (1992) 177 CLR 292

Kioa v West [1985] HCA 81; (1995) 159 CLR 550

Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Teoh [1995] HCA 20; (1995) 128 ALR 353

HEARING

PERTH, 2 May 1996

10:5:1996

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr J Hockley

Solicitors for the Applicant: A C Thorpe and Co

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr P Macliver

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The "appeal" be dismissed.

2. The applicant pay the respondent's costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

R.D. NICHOLSON J The applicant "appeals" from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal") constituted by Associate Professor R D Fayle, Senior Member, given on 2 August 1995. The amended notice of appeal maintains that two questions of law are raised namely, whether the Tribunal erred in law in failing to grant an adjournment sought by applicant's counsel and so denying the applicant procedural fairness and, further, whether it erred in law in failing to consider the applicant's written submissions in regard to an issue concerning a provisional tax credit.

2. In order to understand the circumstances in which the adjournment was refused it is necessary to trace the history of the matter which was before the Tribunal at the time the adjournment was refused.

3. On 30 October 1991 notices of assessment were issued addressed to the applicant in respect of the financial years ended 30 June 1985 to 30 June 1988. The applicant lodged an objection to the assessments on 20 December 1991. The assessments had issued as a result of an audit of the applicant's income tax affairs, the results of which had been communicated to him.

4. On 8 April 1992 the respondent wrote to the applicant requesting that he identify the issues which he wished to object against. On 15 April 1992, the applicant advised the respondent that, for him to lodge a complete objection, he had made a request for the tax audit file under the Freedom of Information Act 1992 (Cth). This in turn led to an application by the applicant to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal which handed down its decision on the applicant's freedom of information request on 12 February 1993.

5. On 17 June 1993, the respondent wrote to the applicant again requesting details of the issues which he wished to object against.

6. On 14 January 1992, the applicant had written to the respondent requesting details of the basis for the assessments. On 17 February 1992, he had written again in the same tenor. On 24 December 1993, he again wrote requesting a response to the two earlier letters. On 5 January 1994, the respondent wrote to the applicant stating that the information requested in the letter of 14 February 1992 had been provided in the response of 8 April 1992. In addition it was stated it was no longer the policy of the respondent to issue adjustment sheets but that letters from the respondent to the applicant dated 7 November 1990 and 8 August 1991 explained the basis of the adjustment made to Assessments for the financial years ended 30 June 1985-88. On 7 December 1993 the applicant requested a statement of reasons in respect of the decision not to issue those adjustment sheets. This was provided by letter dated 25 January 1994 from the respondent.

7. On 1 August 1994 the respondent made a decision on the objections. In doing so it addressed three principal "questions at issue" namely, whether the income derived from Stuart Financial Services was derived in his own right or as a member of a partnership; whether the adjustments to income received by the taxpayer were correct; and whether the adjustments to the deductions claimed were correct. In the objection report the sub-issues arising from the "questions at issue" are delineated by headings. None of these headings addresses the question of provisional tax raised in the financial year to 30 June 1988.

8. On 30 September 1994 the applicant lodged an application for review of the respondent's decisions on objection with the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. On 21 October 1994, the respondent filed its statement of findings.

9. On 10 November 1994 the Tribunal directed that within twenty one days the applicant filed a detailed statement of the grounds upon which he relied. On a subsequent application from the applicant made on 17 November 1994 the Tribunal extended the time for compliance with the direction to 31 January 1995.

10. On 25 January 1995 the applicant filed a letter with the Tribunal purporting to provide a detailed statement of grounds upon which he relied. The respondent advised the Tribunal by letter dated 17 February 1995 that the statement of grounds did not comply with the directions of the Tribunal and that it would apply for the application to be dismissed pursuant to s42A of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) ("the AAT Act") at a Directions Hearing scheduled for 27 February 1995. In the event the directions made on that date were that the hearing of the application be listed for 18 and 19 May 1995 and that summaries of evidence be filed and served before 4 May 1995 together with a list of witnesses and authorities. Furthermore, it was directed that "at the conclusion of the case for the applicant, the Tribunal will be prepared to consider an application by the respondent for an adjournment if it appears that justice requires that the respondent be given time to respondent to the applicant's case".

11. On subsequent advice from the Tribunal to the applicant and respondent the hearing date was moved from 18 and 19 May to 25 and 26 May 1995.

12. On 13 March 1995 a direction was made by the Tribunal permitting the applicant to extend his grounds of objection to include the question of penalties.

13. On 24 May 1995 the solicitors for the applicant wrote to the Tribunal requesting an adjournment of the hearing. This was objected to by the respondent. Accordingly, the hearing commenced on 25 May 1995, that is two months after the Directions Hearing at which the application was listed.

14. The course of the hearing was as follows.

15. At the hearing counsel announced his appearance for the applicant and then sought an adjournment. He said:

"The applicant's grounds for requesting an adjournment are

such that as counsel for the applicant we have not had the

sufficient time to evaluate and consider the strength and

weaknesses of the applicant's position and to advice the

applicant as to the merits of the applicant's case. Our firm

received instructions to act on the applicant's behalf on

11 May and this is indeed the first occasion where we

understand that the applicant has throughout the course of

this matter retained legal counsel."

16. In response to a question from the Senior Member counsel advised that on that date the applicant had provided him with all the files, the T documents and correspondence. In addition, he had been in contact with the respondent. He said:

"...Our instructions were to attempt to reach a settlement

position with the respondent prior to this hearing, so our

mind has really been turned just to those elements of the

dispute as to the quantum which is involved establishing the

proportion of penalties, the tax in dispute and those sort of

issues. Not actually to the legal or substantive issues

associated with the question of whether a partnership exists

and those sort of things."

17. He further advised it was his view the applicant should receive competent advice and he did not feel he was in a position to offer this to the applicant so, if the applicant's request for adjournment was denied, he would be seeking leave to withdraw from acting on the applicant's behalf. He accepted, apart from the question of time, he was otherwise competent. Later he said if he did not withdraw the applicant would just be incurring legal fees without him being able to offer any real assistance in the conduct of the proceedings.

18. It was accepted by counsel for the applicant when put to him by the Senior Member that the applicant had been practicing for a number of years as a Registered Tax Agent and had university qualifications.

19. The application for adjournment was "vehemently opposed" on behalf of the respondent. Counsel for the respondent said the applicant had had ample opportunity to prepare his case and that this was another example of the applicant trying to stall the proceedings. He maintained the applicant had refused to provide information and documentation during the audit and was prosecuted and found guilty of failing to comply with an order pursuant to s264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) ("the ITA"). He took the Tribunal to two instances where he alleged there had been unsuccessful endeavours to obtain information from the applicant. He pointed to the fact that on 10 November 1994 directions had been made that the applicant file detailed grounds within twenty one days, the time had been extended for compliance and the directions made on 27 February had been ignored by the applicant. He submitted that the applicant was a delinquent debtor and it was not in the public interest to grant any adjournment. In his submission the taxpayer had had six months to discharge his onus of proof in relation to the issue of partnership but had chosen to delay progress of the matter. He asked the Tribunal to dismiss the matter either in accordance with s42A or 42B of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act.

20. In response, counsel for the applicant said it was clear the respondent had agreed there were issues in dispute. There was therefore no reasonable basis for dismissing the matter under those sections. The applicant had been acting bona fides, the matter was not untenable or groundless and the applicant rejected any assertion of deliberate delay.

21. The Senior Member then said:

"Well, I am not going to give an adjournment, and I guess my

reasons are fairly much encapsulated in the submissions of

Mr Maloney. I have spent considerable time on this file

myself, in coming to this hearing today, and it seems to me

that there has been delays, extraordinary delays and

extraordinary requests by the applicant for information, more

than once, in this and also I would go further and say

extraordinary responses by the respondent to provide

information and assist the applicant in gaining all the

information that he would need to get into the mind of the

respondent in the way in which the respondent has come to

these conclusions."

22. The Senior Member then turned to the applicant's letter of 25 January 1995 and, in conjunction with counsel, isolated seven issues which were the issues to be treated as the grounds of objection arising before the Tribunal. The seventh issue was identified as being that of penalties and that raised the question of whether the Tribunal had power to review some of the penalties and whether or not it ought to exercise a different discretion in terms of s227(3) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) ("the ITA").

23. In addition, the Tribunal referred to the statement in the letter that "all matters are in dispute" and a further statement that anything else was in dispute. The Tribunal then ruled there were no other grounds of which proper grounds had been provided in terms of ITA s185 so that nothing further arose pursuant to this purported general item. The question of provisional tax was not included in the first six issues nor expressly referred to as arising under the matter of penalties.

24. Following discussion of other matters not material here, the Tribunal took a short adjournment, in particular to enable counsel to consult with the applicant on the question of whether there would be any witnesses called by the applicant. Upon resumption, counsel for the applicant requested the Tribunal to be excused, for which leave was given.

25. The applicant thereupon requested an adjournment on the basis that he did not have legal representation and, although he had experience as a tax agent, he was not a qualified legal practitioner and there were fairly complex legal issues involved. The application was refused for the reasons earlier given. The refusal of that adjournment is not within the grounds relied upon in the amended notice of appeal.

26. The applicant then requested the presiding Member to exclude himself on the ground that he had presided in a previous conference. The Senior Member accepted such a request could be made provided it was made before the hearing: AAT Act s34(4). The application was refused on the ground it was not made as required by that section. The refusal of this application is not the subject of the appeal, although the applicant seeks to have it taken into account as a circumstance relevant to the refusal of the grant of adjournment. In my opinion it is a circumstance which arose subsequent to the refusal of the grant of adjournment and has not been raised in a manner which accords it any status in consideration with issues of procedural fairness raised on the applicant's behalf.

27. The applicant then applied to the Tribunal for an adjournment to enable him to obtain legal representation. The application was refused.

28. The Tribunal made apparent to the applicant that as a consequence of him having no legal representation counsel for the respondent was under a duty to assist the Tribunal and the Tribunal had a duty to make sure he received justice. It was also pointed out to him by the Tribunal that it was not necessary that he should have legal representation and that it was sufficient that he was present before the Tribunal.

29. Following the luncheon adjournment the applicant advised the Tribunal that he was required to leave at 2.45 pm in order to pick-up his children from their school bus stop. He addressed the Tribunal during the course of which he tendered three documents which were allowed in as exhibits. The applicant did not give evidence or call witnesses although it is asserted in the grounds of appeal that counsel had advised the "Court" six witnesses would be called. Counsel, however, was on the applicant's case patently uninstructed on the nature of the case apart from the limited instructions given to him. At 2.45 pm counsel for the respondent commenced his submissions and shortly thereafter the applicant asked to be excused. Counsel continued to address the Tribunal concluding with remarks on the issue of penalties to which reference will later be made.

30. At the conclusion of those submissions the Senior Member said that a copy of the transcript from the time counsel for the respondent began his submissions would be sent to the applicant who would have seven days to respond with written submissions, with a copy to the respondent who had a further seven days to reply.

31. On 15 June 1995 the applicant responded in writing to the respondent's oral submissions made at the Tribunal hearing. On 23 June 1995 the respondent replied to the applicant's written submissions.

32. The decision of the Tribunal was handed down on 2 August 1995. In those reasons the Senior Member said:

"The Tribunal is sympathetic to the submission made by

Mr Maloney that this rather late request for adjournment

was but another "antic" engaged in by the applicant to delay

the due process of this matter."

Refusal of adjournment

33. The grounds in the amended notice of appeal put the applicant's case in relation to the refusal of adjournment in a number of ways. Leaving aside for the moment the general issue of procedural fairness, the first is that "the applicant was forced to continue at the hearing when counsel refused to continue in view of the refusal of the application for an adjournment to enable the case to be adequately prepared". In my opinion the inference from the evidence before the Tribunal is that the applicant instructed his counsel not to continue so that the appearance of the applicant without counsel was as a result of his own doing.

34. Next it is said the applicant was placed in the invidious position of being at the hearing of his case without counsel when he was not in a position to conduct the case and the case was one of complexity which required competent counsel. The hearing date was fixed by direction on 27 February 1995. The applicant had every opportunity to engage counsel to properly represent him. He did not instruct counsel to do so until 11 May 1995 and then only instructed counsel in terms of negotiation for settlement. If it was the applicant's appreciation of his case it required counsel, it is patent he did not take the opportunity to provide himself with counsel for the occasion.

35. Next it is said the applicant was unsure and uncertain of what to do and the conduct of his case was left to the Tribunal. The first is an assertion of fact and the second is an expression only of the duty cast on the Tribunal in the absence of the applicant having representation.

36. The final thrust of the grounds is that generally the applicant was denied procedural fairness and was thereby severely prejudiced. It certainly is not the case that by his absence from part of the hearing the applicant was occasioned any prejudice because he was provided with the relevant transcript and took advantage of the opportunity to make further written submissions. The only question remaining in the applicant's contentions on this matter is whether as part of the concept of procedural fairness he was entitled to be legally represented.

37. In Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Teoh [1995] HCA 20; (1995) 128 ALR 353 at 381, McHugh J summarised the present content of the law of procedural fairness in terms of what was said in Kioa v West [1985] HCA 81; (1995) 159 CLR 550 at 585. There it was said that, in the absence of a clear contrary legislative intention, the rules of procedural fairness require a decision-maker "to bring to a persons attention the critical issue or factor on which the administrative decision is likely to turn so that he may have an opportunity of dealing with it". The central issue is the provision of an opportunity to the person to deal with the critical issue or factor.

38. In the present case the critical issues or factors were known to the applicant better than anyone. It was the applicant who since 10 November 1994 had been subject to a direction to make known those critical issues or factors.

39. Nor does the notion of opportunity carry with it the right to have counsel make oral submissions. What procedural fairness requires, subject to contrary legislative intention, is the opportunity of dealing with the matter. This was presented to the applicant both by his presence after the departure of counsel at the hearing and by the opportunity provided to him following his departure to respond to the written transcript of submissions made in his absence, an opportunity of which he fully availed himself. The common law of procedural fairness does not recognize the right to be represented by counsel on the occasion of the opportunity to be heard: cf Dietrich v The Queen (1992) [1992] HCA 57; 177 CLR 292.

40. The applicant was aware from 27 February 1995 of the impending hearing date. There was presented to him by the direction given on that date full knowledge of the opportunity which faced him to call witnesses and present his case. The applicant was provided with all the opportunity necessary to satisfy the requirements of procedural fairness, the requirements of which do not dictate the opportunity either be taken or be exercised in a particular way.

41. The description by the Senior Member of the application for adjournment as "another antic" on the part of the applicant was but a characterisation by him based on the vehement submissions in opposition for the respondent and in no way denies the applicant procedural fairness.

42. In my opinion, there is no substance in the applicant's contention he was denied procedural fairness by the refusal of the adjournment sought by his counsel.

Provisional tax credit

43. In relation to the question of provisional tax credit, it is contended in the grounds of appeal the Tribunal should have allowed the applicant's submissions for such a credit and should have allowed a reduction in penalty tax imposed as a result of the failure to give the applicant a credit for provisional tax in the 1988 taxation return.

44. The issues before the Tribunal at the hearing were those which were identified by the Senior Member in conjunction with counsel for both the applicant and respondent at the commencement of the hearing. As has been seen, they did not include the issue of provisional tax in the first six issues.

45. The seventh issue relating to penalties was address by counsel for the respondent in terms which included the statement "there is provisional tax on the later year - on the 1988 assessment of $13,722". This statement was in the transcript forwarded to the applicant for his written submission.

46. In his written submission the applicant therefore addressed the subject of provisional tax, asking the question how provisional tax in that amount could be raised when the assessment itself was invalid and stating further that the taxpayer's return for the financial year ended 30 June 1989 had been lodged as non-taxable but the provisional tax in the year to 30 June 1988 had not been dealt with.

47. There are two answers to the applicant's ground that the issue of provisional tax credit was not considered. In the first place it was not an issue which formed the subject of objections in relation to the years ended 30 June 1985-88 before the Tribunal. In the second place, it was not a matter which could have been the subject of an objection either in the year to 30 June 1988 (when the provisional tax was required to be raised) or at all: see ITA, s221YA(3).

48. The applicant's further ground seeks a reduction of penalty tax. The applicant was granted a credit for provisional tax of $13,722. The applicant objects to paying penalty tax which includes a penalty for that provisional tax, prior to the receipt of the credit for it. The amount in issue is $4,101.19 being additional tax for late payment pursuant to ITA, s207 computed from 6 December 1991 (the day after the raising of the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1988) to 5 August 1993 (the date upon which the taxpayer lodged his return showing nil income for the year ended 30 June 1989).

49. The provisional tax credit was given on 13 September 1995. No additional tax was charged from 5 August 1993 when the taxpayer lodged the relevant return to the date of that credit. In short, the facts disclose that the provisional tax issue was not dealt with at the time of the 1988 return because at that time the taxpayer had not lodged the nil assessment for the relevant year. That was a matter which did not occur until 5 August 1993.

50. In relation to the written submissions of the taxpayer which included the submissions on provisional tax, the Tribunal stated it had considered those submissions but, as they did not address any of the grounds of objection nor any of the respondent's submissions, they did not assist the applicant nor the Tribunal in any respect. In my opinion, the Tribunal did not err in reaching that conclusion. It was not argued whether the ground did, in any event, raise a question of law being a necessary precondition to the right to appeal: AAT Act, s44.

51. For these reasons I consider that the "appeal" should be dismissed.
