Bonsall and Commissioner for Superannuation [1999] AATA 674 (10 September 1999)

Last Updated: 13 September 1999

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 674

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No N98/796

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re PETER BONSALL

Applicant

And COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President) Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member) Mr G.D. Stanford (Member)

Date 10 September 1999

Place Sydney

Decision The decision under review is affirmed.

(Sgd) BJ McMahon

..............................................

Deputy President

CATCHWORDS

SUPERANNUATION - application for late election - special circumstances - impact of delay - employee file destroyed - whether there was information available and provided at initial election - adequate consultation - 6 year delay between notice of late election and actual election - not 'unusual, uncommon or exceptional'

WORDS OR PHRASES 'unusual, uncommon or exceptional'

Superannuation Act 1976 s 157

Superannuation Act 1922 ss 119, 119J(1), 119J(2), 119U(1), 119U(2)

Beadle v Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; (1985) 60 ALR 225

Re Beadle and Director-General of Social Security (1984) 6 ALD 1

Eastman and Commissioner for Superannuation unreported No.570 of 2 April 1982

Secretary, Department of Social Security and Lane (1993) 31 ALD 105

Re Marshall and Commissioner for Superannuation (1992) 29 ALD 69

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President) Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member) Mr G.D. Stanford (Member)

1. This is an application to review a decision made under the Superannuation Act 1976 concerning events, which took place during the currency of the Superannuation Act 1922. The decision relates to a refusal to allow the applicant to make a late election.

2. Mr Bonsall was born on 20 April 1952. On joining the former Postmaster General's Department, he was accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account with effect from 20 January 1969. Because of congenital health problems, he was not then accepted as a member of the Superannuation Fund. Both the Provident Account and the Superannuation Fund had been constituted under the Superannuation Act 1922.

3. Five years later, a medical examination established that the former reservations concerning his health were no longer valid and he was therefore permitted to transfer to the Superannuation Fund on 24 January 1974. It was in his interest to transfer, as the contributions required were lower than those which he was obliged to make to the Provident Account.

4. On 2 April 1974 a letter was sent to him by the secretary of the Fund explaining the transfer procedure that could be implemented when he transferred from one fund to the other. He could either have his accumulated entitlements in the Provident Account paid to the Fund for his future benefit, or he could be paid out his accumulated entitlements in cash and start his membership with the Fund from the date of acceptance.

5. The relevant parts of the letter are as follows:

"Option 1 You can elect to transport your Provident Account equity to the Pension Scheme in which case a Transfer Value will become payable in respect of you. The amount of Transfer Value in your case will be the total of your contributions plus interest in the Provident Account (C + I), plus an employers component equal to twice (C + I). When the Transfer-Value is paid into the Pension Scheme, it will purchase for you a number of fully paid units i.e. units which attract full pension benefits but in respect of which no fortnightly contributions are required to be made. Your actual contributions to the Pension Scheme will then be for such number of units as represents the "difference" between your normal unit entitlement according to salary, and the number of units determined to be fully paid in respect of the Transfer Value applicable to you. In electing to take advantage of this option, you will have full pension entitlement in respect of the maximum number of units available under the Superannuation Act.

Option 2 This option gives you the opportunity to elect to receive a refund of your contributions plus interest in the Provident Account. However, in taking this course of action the restrictive provisions of the Superannuation Act will have effect and your normal eligibility to contribute for full unit entitlement according to salary will be diminished. In effect, the amount of your contributions plus interest in the Provident Account will be equated as being equivalent to a number of units in the Pension Scheme, and the number of units so determined will be applied in relation to you as a unit restriction i.e. units forfeited for the whole of your term as a contributor under the Superannuation Act. It is also mentioned that a contributor with a unit restriction is not permitted to hold reserve units of pension.

In practice, the number of units determined to be fully paid according to a Transfer Value payable in respect of you is equal to the number of units determined to be a unit restriction if you elected to receive a refund of your contributions plus interest in the Provident Account, i.e. if for instance your Transfer Value represented 20 fully paid units, but you rejected this option in favour of receiving a refund, you would forfeit 20 units of your full unit entitlement according to salary.

Attached hereto is a schedule which gives full details of the contributory position applicable in relation to you as at the date of the Superannuation Board's decision to allow you to contribute to the Pension Scheme. The schedule should be examined in conjunction with the foregoing information and you should ensure that you comply with any instructions contained therein with regard to the making and forwarding of elections.

I have enclosed a copy of a brochure entitled "Commonwealth Superannuation" which I trust will be of further assistance to you."

6. In evidence before us, Mr Bonsall recalled receiving this letter. He said that he discussed it with his wife and later with his supervisor. At the time, he was employed at the Homebush exchange. He did not seek to discuss the letter with any personnel officer. His evidence before us was that he did not know where the personnel department was located. There was, however, evidence given by the respondent that at the relevant time, there were personnel units in Homebush and in Burwood, four of them being within a short distance from the applicant's place of employment. It appears that he did not enquire as to where they were located.

7. He elected to accept option 2. In doing so, he completed a form which required him to strike out the option which he rejected. His decision to accept a refund of his contributions was therefore a conscious and considered decision arrived at after discussions with his wife and supervisor (but not with others who might have been better qualified to advise him) and was evidenced in a quite deliberate way.

8. Mr Bonsall now says that he misunderstood the terms of option 2 as set out in the letter. The author of that letter referred in the second sentence of the option 2 paragraph to the effect of a diminution of Mr Bonsall's entitlement. As the applicant now understands the position, what he lost was more than a diminution. Nevertheless what he received must have been known to him from the time of his transfer. Since at least 1982, he received annual statements informing him, among other things, that his membership dated from 1974.

9. Mr Bonsall could not recall receiving the brochure referred to in the last paragraph of the letter although he had no reason to doubt that it would have been sent to him. A brochure, no doubt, would have made it clear what the applicant's benefits would be if he chose one or other of the two options. If Mr Bonsall was still in doubt after reading that brochure, he could have consulted with personnel officers at any one of four nearby locations. Alternatively, he could have consulted his own union, as he later did. None of these courses was followed at the time.

10. Mr Bonsall received his payout, invested the money and continued on with membership of the superannuation fund from 1974. In 1976, he automatically became a member of the scheme (the CSS) established by the Superannuation Act 1976. On 26 August 1992 he ceased to be a member of that scheme by virtue of his election to transfer to the Telstra Superannuation Scheme (the TSS). At that time, a transfer benefit of $130,819.37 was paid from the CSS to the TSS. On 17 December 1997, the applicant resigned from his employment with Telstra and accordingly also ceased to be a member of the TSS. A benefit of $207,604.30 was paid to him, of which $50,961.98 was paid as a cash benefit and $156,041.40 was transferred to a rollover fund.

11. Mr Bonsall now seeks to reverse the process that occurred in 1974 and to have his membership of the fund backdated to 1969 through the transfer to the Fund of an amount equal to the cash payout, which he received in 1974. Had he chosen option 1, this would have been the normal course of events. He now seeks to choose that option retrospectively. He seeks to make a late election under section 119 of the 1922 Act. Such an election should have been made within the prescribed period of 3 months. Subsection 119U(2) empowers the then Board (and by a series of legal provisions, this Tribunal) to extend that period of 3 months if it is satisfied that there are "special circumstances that justify it in so doing". The applicant contends that there are special circumstances relating to his case.

12. He first raised this matter in a letter dated 9 December 1991. The original of that letter has since been legitimately destroyed and Mr Bonsall has not kept a copy of it. The reply to his letter, however, is still extant and is in these terms:

"I refer to your letter dated 9 Dec 1991 requesting consideration of a late election under section 157 of the Superannuation Act 1976 to preserve your prior period of membership of the Provident Account. I apologise for the delay in forwarding this reply.

2. At the outset I should explain that section 157 of the Superannuation Act 1976 (the 1976 Act) is a general provision applicable to elections made under the 1976 Act. (See attached copy). For example where an election is made after the expiration of the period allowed by the 1976 Act the election may, subject to the conditions set out in subsection 157(1), be treated as though it had been made within the period allowed.

3. An election to transfer your Provident Account equity to the Superannuation Fund should have been made in accordance with subsection 119J(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 (the 1922 Act). Consequently the provisions contained in subsection 157 of the 1976 Act cannot apply in your case. However subsection 119J(2) of the 1922 Act, which is quoted below, did permit the Board, in special circumstances, to extend the period for the making of an election under subsection 119J(1) an on from 1 Jul 1976 a reference to the Board is now read as including a reference to the Commissioner for Superannuation.

"(2) The Board may, if it is satisfied that there are special circumstances that justify it in doing so, extend the period for the making of an election and the payment of an amount to the Board under sub-section (1)."

In other words for a late election under subsection 119J(1) of the 1922 Act to crystallise, and for the preservation provisions of the Act to apply, the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation must be satisfied that there are special circumstances that would justify his extending the period for the making of an election.

4. Therefore you may still submit a late election under subsection 119J(1) of the 1922 Act. However, if you do, it will also be necessary for you to provide a statement setting out the reasons in support of your late election. I have already noted that in your letter dated 9 Dec 1991 you state that the reason for submitting a late election is that you were unaware that your equity in the Provident Account could have been preserved. However according to copies of correspondence retained on your contributor file the Superannuation Board, after approving your application to transfer from the Provident Account to the Superannuation Fund (ie the superseded scheme) wrote to inform you that you had two options with regard to your equity in the Provident Account. These options were set out in the Board's letter to you dated 2 Apr 1974. (See attached copy).

6. You were advised that Option 1 involved the making of an election in which case a transfer value would have been paid from the Provident Account into the Superannuation Fund (ie the Pension Scheme). That transfer value represented a lump sum equivalent to your own contributions and interest in the Provident Account plus an employer component equal to twice the value of your own contributions and interest. On payment of the transfer value into the Pension Scheme it would have purchased for you a number of fully paid units. Your actual contributions to the Pension scheme would have then been for the number of units representing the "difference" between your normal unit entitlement according to your salary and the number of units determined to be fully paid. By taking advantage of this option you would have had entitlement to the maximum number of units available under the Pension Scheme.

7. On the other hand Option 2 enabled you to obtain a refund of your contributions and interest. However the Board's letter clearly informed you that in taking this course of action your normal eligibility to contribute for the maximum number of units based on your salary would be diminished. In this instance the number of restricted units would have been equal to the number of units that would have treated as fully paid under Option 1.

8. In the attachment which accompanied this letter you were also advised that if you were electing for a Transfer Value (Option 1) then your election had to be made by not later than 27 May 1974. Alternatively if you applied for a refund of your contributions and interest (Option 2) then you would have to complete a Form SB2A. On 27 May 1974 the Superannuation Board received your written request for a refund of your Provident Account contributions (see attached copy) together with a completed Form SB2.

9. I trust this explanation has been able to clarify your position with regard to the making of a late election to preserve your equity in the former Provident Account. It is also advised that unless a formal late election under subsection 119J(1) of the 1922 Act is submitted, together with a statement of reasons in support of that election, this Office will not take any further action in this matter."

13. Nothing further was heard from Mr Bonsall concerning this proposed application for a late election until 15 September 1997, when he applied in contemplation of his retirement from the Telstra Fund later that year. In his letter he said:

"I wish to back date my superannuation membership date to my eligible service date of 20/1/69 not as is now 24/1/74.

In 1974 after being in the Provident Fund and converting into the Superannuation Scheme, I took option 2 as stated on attached sheets and received a payment.

Option 2 states "Full unit entitlement according to salary will be deminished". [sic]

I believe the entitlement would still be there and excess money only would be given to me at this time, therefore allowing my previous date of joining to stand.

My reasons for this request are as follow:

* I received no verbal or written advice as to my various options.

* If I had been informed about my rights to preserve my Superannuation Benefits I would have done so as the benefit of this to myself and family is obvious

* It is also relevant to take into account that there was not any Superannuation Manual available.

I consider that special circumstances justify my request and I look forward to hearing from you soon."

14. The refusal of this application is the decision under review.

15. In his request to have the decision internally reviewed, additional grounds were put forward by Mr Bonsall in a letter dated 12 November 1997 in these terms:

"I am dissatisfied with the delegate's decision on the back dating of my Superannuation membership date, and I would like the Commissioner to reconsider.

I [sic] your letter dated 13 th October 1997 it is stated that Personnel sections had superannuation manuals available. This information was not told to me at the time of this decision and Personnel sections were remote to my place of work at this time.

The funds, which were paid out, were used as a deposit on a second block of land. This land was sold in 1981 at a profit and has since been invested in a home unit at 3/2-6 Morton Pd at Nowra.

In the letter sent to me 2 April 1974 I do not believe the options were clearly set out Option 2 states "Full unit entitlement according to salary will be deminished".

I believe the entitlement would still be there and excess money only would be given to me at this time, therefore allowing my previous date of joining to stand.

If I had been informed about my rights to preserve my Superannuation Benefits I would have done so as the benefit of this to myself and my family is obvious.

I consider that special circumstances justify my request and I look forward to hearing from you soon."

16. The question to be determined is whether there are special circumstances that justify this Tribunal extending the period for making the relevant election.

17. There have been some observations from Courts and Tribunals on the meaning of this formula of words. In Beadle v Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; (1985) 60 ALR 225, a Full Federal Court observed at 228:

"The Director-General has power to fix a longer period in special circumstances. Presumably in this context special circumstances must include events which would render the six months unfair or inappropriate. For example, where the delay beyond six months was due to the claimant's being misled by a departmental officer or was due to the negligence of a third party, it might be thought the normal six months would be inappropriate; that special circumstances had been shown which warranted a longer period. More difficult would be questions of ignorance, illiteracy, isolation, illness and the like. It would depend upon the circumstances of the particular case whether these would constitute special circumstances. We do not think it is possible to lay down precise limits or precise rules. The matter is one for the Director-General bearing in mind the purpose for which the power is given. The phrase 'special circumstances' although lacking precision is sufficiently understood in our view not to require judicial gloss.

18. The Tribunal decision (in which Toohey J presided) reported at (1984) 6 ALD 1, added at page 3 these words which were approved by the Full Court:

"An expression such as "special circumstances" is by its very nature incapable of precise or exhaustive definition. The qualifying adjective looks to circumstances that are unusual, uncommon or exceptional. Whether circumstances answer any of these descriptions must depend upon the context in which they occur. For it is the context which allows one to say that the circumstances in one case are markedly different from the usual run of cases. This is not to say that the circumstances must be unique but they must have a particular quality of unusualness that permits them to be described as special."

19. In Eastman and Commissioner for Superannuation (unreported No.570 of 2 April 1982) the Tribunal observed that in determining whether "special circumstances" existed, it was necessary to have regard to whether, by exercising the discretion, the decision maker would be achieving or frustrating end or objects which are conformable with the scope of the legislation (see also Secretary, Department of Social Security and Lane (1993) 31 ALD 105).

20. The reconsideration decision of the delegate of the Commissioner dated 27 May 1998 (T2/2) sets out some general principles which have been drawn from the authorities in relation to considering whether or not special circumstances exist:

"(i) it is not possible to lay down precise limits or rules as to what constitutes special circumstances. All of the circumstances of the individual case must be looked at in determining whether special circumstances exist;

(ii) the discretion must be exercised in a way that is consistent with the needs or objects of the legislation;

(iii) the period of time allowed in the enactment is relevant, as it was considered sufficient by the legislature in the normal case;

(iv) "special circumstances" warranting extension of time must include events which would render the normal period allowed unfair or inappropriate. Relevant factors include the extent of the applicant's knowledge of the relevant provisions, the adequacy of the information conveyed to the applicant, the diligence of the applicant and whether the circumstances were of the applicant's own making;

(v) the circumstances must be unusual, uncommon or exceptional; and

(vi) in the case of lengthy delay, weighty facts would be required to establish special circumstances.

21. In Re Marshall and Commissioner for Superannuation (1992) 29 ALD 69 at 82, the Tribunal endorsed this summary. We also agree that it correctly summarises the relevant principles.

22. In his first request on 15 September 1997, the applicant alleged that he had received no advice as to his various options and that there was no superannuation manual available. This is clearly contrary to the evidence. The applicant was given advice in the 1974 letter and in the enclosed brochure. Further advice was available to him at any of the personnel units surrounding his place of work. The applicant is an intelligent, educated man. He undertook a five-year course before becoming a qualified technician and worked as a technical officer during the course of his employment with Telstra. In our view, he would have been quite capable of understanding any of the advice that had been given to him or which was available to him and not sought by him.

23. In his second letter requesting reconsideration, no special circumstance is alleged except a possible misunderstanding of a phrase in the letter of 1974, a misunderstanding which was not alleged until some 23 years after the event. Furthermore, if there had been a misunderstanding in 1974, it could not have continued for more than a few years, in view of the membership information regularly made known to the applicant by the Fund.

24. Subsection 119U(1) prescribes a period of 21 days in which a person can elect to preserve their benefit. Upon preserving and a transfer payment-becoming payable, the person, pursuant to subsection 119J(1), has 3 months in which to elect and pay the transfer value to the Board. Obviously regard must be paid to the relevant period fixed by Parliament as an indication of what was considered appropriate. A period of 3 weeks followed by a period of 3 months in which to seek advice and possibly to reorganise finances, indicates that only a short further period was in contemplation when the Board was given a discretion to extend time. The applicant is now seeking an extension of time of at least 17 years (if the 1991 application is to be taken in account) or more realistically, 23 years. The period that has expired demands that the exigencies of any special circumstances, which are alleged, must be quite pressing. We do not see any relevant circumstance, which should require a benign exercise of the discretion when one considers the structure of the legislation and the effect, at this late stage, of allowing any such election. Some of the funds through which he has passed have been wound up. He is no longer a member of any of the relevant funds. The affairs of members of both the CSS and the TSS funds would be affected by any decision to allow the applicant to increase his beneficial interest so long after the event.

25. None of the events surrounding the applicant's case are unusual, uncommon or exceptional so as to constitute special circumstances. None of them is special to the applicant. The fact that he realises now that he would have been better off if he had transferred his benefit into the Fund does not constitute something which is unusual, uncommon or exceptional. There is certainly no hardship alleged. Choosing the wrong option in an informed situation and continuing for 23 years upon the basis of the option chosen should not give the applicant an advantage over all other members of the Fund. The delay is compounded by the fact that nothing was done between 1991 and 1997 to advance the application.

26. We do not consider that a case for special circumstances has been made out. Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed.

I certify that the 26 preceding paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President), Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member) and Mr G.D. Stanford (Member).
