Re Commissioner of Superannuation v Christine Perrett [1989] FCA 348; 90 ALR 385 23 FCR 425 (1 September 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

And: CHRISTINE PERRETT

No. V G363 of 1988

FED No. 562

Public Service

90 ALR 385

[1989] FCA 348; 23 FCR 425

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIAN DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Jenkinson J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Public Service - Superannuation (Cth) - Matters affecting level of benefits - Condition "connected with" pre-existing condition.

Superannuation Act 1976 - ss. 16, 66

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Benham (unreported, judgment 4 April 1989)

HEARING

MELBOURNE

1:9:1989

Counsel for the Applicant : Mr. R.R.S. Tracey

Counsel for the Respondent : Mr. T.P. Tobin

Solicitor for the Applicant : Australian Government Solicitor

Solicitors for the Respondent : Slater & Gordon

ORDER

The appeal be allowed.

The decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal made 24 August 1988 be set aside.

The matter of the application to the said Tribunal for review of the applicant's decision made by his delegate on 15 December 1987 be remitted to the said Tribunal and be heard and determined by the said Tribunal according to law.

The said Tribunal be at liberty to receive further evidence on the hearing of the said matter.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

Appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal.

2. The respondent was at relevant times an eligible employee within the meaning of that expression in the Superannuation Act 1976. On 28 June 1985 she ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of "retirement on the ground of invalidity", a phrase which sub-section 7(1) of that Act requires to be read, in the Act, as a reference to the employee's having been retired, or her services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform her duties. She had not then attained her maximum retiring age. Sub-section 66(1) of the Act makes provision for the entitlement of such a prematurely retired person to benefit under the Act, except in a case where sub-section 66(2) applies. The latter sub-section also makes provision for the entitlement of such a person to benefit - a lesser benefit - under the Act. Sub-section 66(2) applies

"where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible

employee by reason of retirement on the

ground of invalidity before attaining his

maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the

person, immediately before the person's

retirement, a benefit classification

certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that

the incapacity which was the ground for

his retirement was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a

physical or mental condition or

conditions specified in the certificate

or by a physical or mental condition or

conditions connected with such a

condition or such conditions."

Section 16 provides for the issue by the applicant Commissioner of "a benefit classification certificate". There was such a certificate in force in respect of the respondent immediately before her retirement. In the certificate the medical condition called psoriasis was specified. The Commissioner had, by his delegate, formed in respect of that condition an opinion of a description contained in paragraph 66(2)(c), but on review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal a contrary opinion was formed. The decision of the Tribunal which is the subject of the appeal to this court gave effect to that contrary opinion by setting aside the Commissioner's delegate's decision that he formed an opinion satisfying the requirements of paragraph 66(2)(c), and by remitting the matter to the Commissioner "for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's invalidity retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate that was in force in respect of the applicant immediately before her retirement, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with that condition."

3. It has been accepted by the parties and by the Tribunal that the incapacity which was the ground for the respondent's retirement was caused by a physical condition which is designated psoriatic arthritis in medical nomenclature. It has also been accepted by the parties and by the Tribunal that, notwithstanding that suggestive name, the evidence did not justify a finding that the respondent's incapacity was - or, generally, that the condition known as psoriatic arthritis is - caused, or contributed to, by psoriasis. The Tribunal was concerned, as is the court in this appeal, with the question whether psoriatic arthritis is "connected with" psoriasis in the sense in which that expression is, on the proper construction of paragraph 66(2)(c), to be understood.

4. The evidence before the Tribunal in my opinion required, and the Tribunal in fact made, the findings which the Tribunal thus expressed : "that the cause of psoriasis is unknown, and the cause of psoriatic arthritis is unknown; that people who suffer from psoriasis may develop arthritis of a particular kind, which is easily recognisable on clinical examination, and is known as psoriatic arthritis; that some people without a previous history of psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis, and in such people it is described as 'psoriatic arthritis sine psoriasis'; and that of these people, some subsequently develop psoriasis, and some do not." Further, the particularity to which reference is made by the Tribunal in the passage quoted consists merely of clinically obvious or radiographically demonstrable signs and characteristic symptoms, the evidence showed, not of any biological or chemical singularity. The Tribunal had no evidence - and did not find - that the psoriatic arthritis was caused by the psoriasis or that psoriasis substantially contributed to the development of the psoriatic arthritis. Mr. Tracey of counsel for the applicant Commissioner did not submit that the Tribunal had erred in failing to reach either of those factual conclusions. His case was that a causal relationship between specified condition and incapacitating condition was not the only relationship which could satisfy the words "connected with" in paragraph 66(2)(c).

5. It was the understandable belief of the members of the Tribunal that the reasoning of two members of a Full Court of this court in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153, Davies and Pincus JJ., had established that only if the condition specified in the certificate had contributed to cause the incapacitating condition was the latter condition "connected with" the former. However, after the Tribunal's decision had been given, another Full Court of this court, in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Benham (unreported; judgment 4 April 1989), observed of the judgment of the third member of the court in Miller's Case, Beaumont J. : "His appears to us to be the majority view, since Davies J., while adding the comments to which reference has been made, expressed general agreement with it." Beaumont J. had in Miller's Case expressly refrained from asserting that the relationship required by the words "connected with" must necessarily be causal, as the Full Court pointed out in Benham's Case. Beaumont J. had in Miller's Case observed (8 FCR at 160):

"In its ordinary meaning, 'connected with'

suggests a link or an association. In the

present context, in my opinion, the phrase is

intended to have its ordinary meaning and thus

to pick up those cases where a link or an

association exists between two medical

conditions. That is a question of fact, to be

determined in the light of the expert evidence.

It may be accepted that, in some cases,

difficult questions of fact will arise for

resolution in determining whether the

requisite nexus between the two conditions has

been established. In this respect, since the

Commissioner bears the onus of proof (see

Minister for Health v. Thomson (1985) 60 ALR

701 at 712), if the expert evidence available

does not permit of a conclusion that the two

conditions are linked or associated in a

relevant medical sense, the provisions of

s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c) will not apply.

Further, a question of law, one of

construction which it is presently unnecessary

to resolve, could arise in some cases as to

whether the nexus required by these provisions

is one of causal connection or something short

of that.

In the present case, the requisite link or

association has, in my view, been established,

even if it were necessary for the Commissioner

to establish a causal connection for this

purpose. The respondent's hormonal mastopathy

required surgery; as a result of the surgery,

the prosthesis was inserted; the prosthesis

caused the fibrous reaction. It follows, in

my opinion, that the reaction was 'connected

with' the earlier hormonal disorder for the

purposes of both s.16(1)(b) and s.66(2)(c)."

The "fibrous reaction" to which Beaumont J. referred was the incapacitating condition. In both Miller's Case and Benham's Case the only connection suggested between the condition specified in the certificate and the incapacitating condition was a causal connection. I understand the Full Court in Benham's Case to have denied - as Mr. Tracey submitted it had denied - that Miller's Case provided authority for the proposition that only a causal connection could satisfy the requirement of paragraph 66(2)(c) that the incapacitating condition, if it were not a specified condition, should be connected with a specified condition. The question then is whether in Benham's Case itself authority for or against that proposition is to be found.

6. The benefit classification certificate in which a physical or mental condition may be specified, and upon which the operation of sub-section 66(2) depends, may be first issued by the Commissioner when the person to whom it relates becomes an eligible employee (sub-sections 16(2), 16(3) and 16(4)), or after the occurrence of certain subsequent events which are specified in sub-sections 16(10) and 16(11). One of the events contemplated by sub-section 16(10) is a lack of satisfaction on the part of the Commissioner, and, on review, on the part of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, that a person's death or incapacity "was not caused, and was not substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that existed at the time the person became an eligible employee, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions". If that lack of satisfaction and certain other circumstances exist, sub-section 16(10) requires the Commissioner to issue a benefit classification certificate which for the purposes of the Act is to be deemed to have been in force immediately before that person's death or retirement on the ground of that incapacity. In Miller's Case a question arose, in addition to the question concerning paragraph 66(2)(c), as to whether that lack of satisfaction could have existed in respect of the respondent Miller. That is why reference is made, in the passages I shall cite from Miller's Case, to s.16(10)(b) as well as to s.66(2)(c). It should also be noted that by s.6 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 the words in sub-section 16(4) "the person is not likely" (to which reference is made in Miller's Case) were omitted and the words "there is a real risk that the person" were substituted.

7. These are the reasons of Davies J. in Miller's Case (8 FCR at 154-155):

"I have had the opportunity of reading the

reasons for judgment prepared by Beaumont J.

I agree in general with them but I would

express some views of my own on the operation

of the words 'connected with' in ss. 16(10)(b)

and 66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976

(Cth)(the Act).

The words 'connected with' also appear in the

basal provision, s.16(4) of the Act which

provides that a Commissioner may issue a

benefit classification certificate if he is of

the opinion that the employee:

'... is not likely, by reason of or

for a reason connected with a

physical or mental condition or

conditions referred to in the report

or reports, to continue to be an

eligible employee until the person

attains his maximum retiring age

....' (Emphasis added.)

Section 66(2)(c) of the Act deals with the

situation after retirement where

'the Commissioner is of the opinion

that the incapacity which was the

ground for his retirement was

caused, or was substantially

contributed to, by a physical or

mental condition or conditions

specified in the certificate or by a

physical or mental condition or

conditions connected with such a

condition or such

conditions,....'(Emphasis added.)

Section 16(10)(b) is similar but deals with

the situation where, for the Section 16(10(b)

is similar but deals with the situation where,

for the reasons set out in s.16(10), a benefit

classification certificate was not issued but

the retirement was caused or substantially

contributed to by a physical or mental

condition of the person that existed at the

time the person became an eligible employee or

by a physical or mental condition connected

therewith.

The words 'connected with' are not technical

words and in ordinary parlance permit of an

association which is not causative. In my

opinion, however, Parliament did not intend

the words to be given a wide interpretation

unrelated to the context in which they appear.

Sections 16(4), 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) are

all, in my opinion, concerned with questions

of causation. These provisions look primarily

to the circumstance where a medical or

physical condition existing at the time the

employee first became eligible is likely to

lead to early retirement and in fact causes or

substantially contributes to early retirement.

In the context, it appears to me that the

words 'connected with' were introduced to deal

with the circumstance where the early

retirement arises not from the medical

condition which existed at the time the

employee became an eligible employee but as a

result of another medical condition that arose

out of or developed from that condition.

Thus, in the present case, the immediate or

proximate cause of the retirement was not the

employee's condition of 'hormonal mastopathy

presenting as breast lumps', which had ceased

to exist, but from the adhesions which had

arisen as a result of the operation on the

employee's breasts.

In the context, I would read the words

'connected with' in ss.16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c)

as dealing with the circumstance where the

immediate or proximate cause of the incapacity

leading to the retirement was a medical

condition other than the condition existing at

the time the employee became an eligible

employee but where, nevertheless, there is a

chain of causation such that the medical

condition existing at the time when the

employee became an eligible employee can be

said, through a chain of events, to have

caused or substantially contributed to the

incapacity causing the retirement.

This reading gives due attention to the

causative elements prescribed by ss.16(10)(b)

and 66(2)(c) and also to the context of the

Act in which those provisions function. It

also gives due weight to the crucial

provision, s.16(4) of the Act. That provision

requires the Commissioner to prognosticate as

to whether, by reason of the medical condition

existing at the commencement of eligibility or

for a reason connected with that condition,

the employee would not be likely to continue

to be an eligible employee until maximum

retiring age. The provision requires the

Commissioner to be able to postulate a chain

of causation between a medical condition

existing at the time of the commencement of

eligibility and an incapacity leading to early

retirement. Sections 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c)

of the Act look at the matter after retirement

to see whether such a chain of causation

actually existed.

In all other aspects I agree with the views

expressed by Beaumont J. and I agree with the

orders proposed by his Honour."

In the same case Pincus J. observed (8 FCR at 163):

"The expression 'connected with' is not very

precise. It is my view that the sort of

connection which is meant is a causal one.

For the purposes of s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c)

of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth), two

conditions are connected with one another if

there is a substantial causal link between the

two. That may be so because one is a cause of

the other, or because both have a common

cause. It is my opinion that unless there is

a substantial causal link, there is no

connection. I appreciate that the word

'substantial' introduces questions of degree,

but they are neither questions which need to

be resolved for the purposes of this case, nor

questions as to which one can usefully give

any guidance. The view I have expressed on

this point accords, I think, with that set out

in the reasons of Davies J."

8. In Benham's Case the Full Court noted (transcript p 8) that the applicant Commissioner had submitted that the Administrative Appeals Tribunal had "erred in construing the words 'connected with' in s.66(2)(c) as requiring that there be a substantial causal link between the certified condition and the associated condition which caused, or substantially contributed to, the incapacity that resulted in the employee's early retirement. It was put that Miller (supra) was not authority for that proposition and that, if it was, it was wrongly decided." The Full Court then quoted so much of the reasons of Davies J. for his judgment in Miller's Case as commences with the words, "In the context, it appears ",and concludes with the words, "incapacity causing the retirement." Then the Full Court quoted the passage in the reasons of Pincus J. for his judgment which I have quoted, and made the following observations (transcript, p 9):

"Although, as Pincus J. pointed out, his

addition of the word 'substantial' was perhaps

not at odds with the views Davies J. had

expressed, and both spoke in terms of a causal

link, there is a significant difference

between their applications of this concept to

the interpretation of s.66(2)(c). For Davies

J., the associated condition must have arisen

out of or developed from the certified

condition, so that it could be said the

incapacity was caused by the certified

condition. For Pincus J., it would suffice

that each condition arose from some common

origin, and that the incapacity was caused by

the associated condition. There is a discord

between these constructions of the section."

9. It would seem that for Pincus J. it is enough that each condition - the specified and the incapacitating - is an effect of the same cause. If that be so, the words "connected with" in paragraph 66(2)(c) may be satisfied without any causal contribution to the existence of the incapacitating condition by the specified condition.

10. After characterising the judgment of Beaumont J. in Miller's Case as the majority view the Full Court in Benham's Case quoted the passage from that judgment which has been reproduced above and then observed (transcript, pp 11-12):

"There is no difficulty, on this view, with

the word 'substantial'. The context demands

nothing less. For s.66(2) is concerned with

depriving a retired employee of a benefit paid

for by a contribution proportionately equal to

that made by other eligible employees. Having

a certified condition, she is thought to be in

a situation requiring a diminution of benefit

if early retirement is caused or contributed

to by that condition. So much protection the

fund requires in return for her statutory

eligibility. It is easy, too, to comprehend

that the legislature may have considered the

protection inadequate should retirement be

produced by a condition not expressly

mentioned in the certificate, but either

arising out of it sufficiently proximately (as

in Miller), or forming part of a wider

condition sufficiently closely involving the

certified condition (as postulated by Pincus

J.). There may be other examples, but there

is nothing to suggest an insubstantial

connection was contemplated as the basis for

depriving a retired employee of benefits.

That would be unnecessary. If the connection

is insubstantial, the risk of such a condition

befalling the particular employee cannot have

been significantly greater than in the case of

employees generally. Only a necessary

protection of the fund could justify taking

away from the retired employee a portion of

her full benefit where the incapacity does not

flow from a certified condition. This is,

after all, beneficial legislation to which the

'established principle' mentioned by Fullagar

J. in Wilson v. Wilson's Tile Works

Proprietary Limited (1960) 104 CLR 328 at

335 applies, as it does in relation to a

Workers' Compensation Act, that 'where two

constructions ... are possible that which is

favourable to the (employee) should be

preferred.' (See also Thompson v. Armstrong

and Royse Proprietary Limited (1950) 81 CLR

585 at 616; Johnston v. The Commonwealth

[1982] HCA 54; (1982) 150 CLR 331 at 342, 343.) The

construction which should be adpted, in the

case of this Act, is a reading of s.66(2)(c)

as referring to cases where there is a real

and substantial connection between the

certified condition and the condition that

caused or substantially contributed to the

incapacity."

11. I am not certain whether the reference in that passage to what is there said to have been postulated by Pincus J. amounts to an expression of approval of the view Pincus J. had expressed, or merely forms part of a parenthetical concession that "the legislature may have", not that it had, intended what Pincus J. expounded concerning paragraph 66(2)(c). If the latter be the correct understanding of the reference, the final sentence of the passage last quoted from the Full Court's reasons in Benham's Case cannot be understood as going further than to insist that the connection contemplated by paragraph 66(2)(c) must be "real and substantial". If the former be the correct understanding of the reference, the Full Court's approval of the view Pincus J. had expressed is in my opinion necessarily approval of the proposition that the words "connected with" in paragraph 66(2)(c) comprehend a relationship between certified condition and incapacitating condition other than that of cause and effect. Whatever the Full Court in Benham's Case ought to be taken to indicate by the phrase "a condition not expressly mentioned in the certificate, but .... forming part of a wider condition sufficiently closely involving the certified condition", Pincus J. in plain terms says that there may be "a substantial link" between incapacitating condition and specified condition - and therefore in his opinion a connection between them satisfying paragraph 66(2)(c) - "because both have a common cause", that is to say each is an effect of the same cause.

12. Supposing myself, but doubtfully, without authoritative guidance as to whether paragraph 66(2)(c) can be satisfied only if the incapacitating condition either is or is caused by the specified condition, I turn, as Davies J. turned, to s.16. I respectfully agree with the opinion of Davies J. that sub-section 16(4) "requires the Commissioner to prognosticate as to whether, by reason of the medical condition existing at the commencement of eligibility or for a reason connected with that condition, the employee would not be likely to continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retiring age. The provision requires the Commissioner to be able to postulate a chain of causation between a medical condition existing at the time of the commencement of eligibility and an incapacity leading to early retirement". The reason for my agreement rests on what I conceive to be the limits which logic and usage impose on the nature of the connection indicated by the expression "for a reason connected with". It is in my opinion important to bear in mind that the phrase of which that expression forms part - "by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports" - qualifies "will not continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age", and not anything which precedes the phrase. To say that there is a real risk that a person will prematurely retire for a reason connected with a particular physical or mental condition is in my opinion to say that the reason, in the sense of explanation, for the premature retirement includes reference to the condition, and, since explanation for the retirement is a statement of the cause or causes of the retirement, is to say that the explanation includes reference to the condition as one of the causes of the retirement.

13. I am unable, however, to group ss. 16(4), 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) together as all three "concerned with questions of causation," in the sense intended by Davies J.. It is to be observed that ss. 16(10) and 66(2)(c) enact provisions which are to have effect after death or incapacity has occurred and therefore when it is possible to know, rather than to prognositicate, the cause of death or incapacity. The time when the command expressed by s.16(4) is to be obeyed, on the other hand, precedes incapacity and knowledge of its cause. It is to be observed also that the expression "connected with" forms part of an idiomatic phrase in s.16(4), but stands grammatically and idiomatically on its own feet in the other two statutory provisions. If the draftsman had desired to maintain a perfect consonance between the three provisions he might have omitted from s.16(4) the phrase "or for a reason connected with" and have inserted, after the word "reports" the phrase "or by reason of a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions", and after the word "conditions" where it last occurs in s.16(4) the words "first mentioned." It would seem that the drafting of s.16(4) was designed to confine specification in a benefit classification certificate issued before death or retirement to physical or mental conditions foreseen as putting the eligible employee at risk of a premature retirement of which the causes will include that condition or those conditions. It is understandable that more tenuous connections would be withheld from the reach of the Commissioner's prophetic opinion. But when death or incapacity has occurred and the prospect of ascertaining causes of the death or the incapacity has been greatly enhanced, recognition and evaluation by the Commissioner of what Beaumont J. called "something short of" causal connection between a condition recognised when a person became an eligible employee and the subsequently incapacitating condition may be more readily supposed to be a legitimate means to achieve that "protection of the fund" to which the Full Court refers in Benham's Case.

14. In my opinion the expression "connected with" in paragraph 66(2)(c) does comprehend, on the proper construction of the paragraph, something short of the relationship between cause and effect. The choice of that expression by the draftsman strongly suggests that something more comprehensive than a causal relationship was to be indicated, for there is no shortage of stock expressions for denoting a causal relationship in the phrase book of legislative drafting, and "connected with" is not one of them. I respectfully agree in the opinion Beaumont J. expressed in Miller's Case that "connected with" is intended to have in paragraph 66(2)(c) its ordinary meaning, and that that meaning comprehends "those cases where a link or an association exists between two medical conditions". In my opinion such a link or association might be constituted by a higher incidence, in a community, of a medical condition among those of the community in whom another medical condition is known to exist than among the rest of that community. Unless the one were distinctly higher than the other the requirement that the connection be "substantial", upon which the Full Court insisted in Benham's Case, would no doubt not be satisfied. If the disparity of incidence were explained by the circumstance that those in whom the other condition is known to exist are more frequently or more thoroughly subjected to procedures calculated to disclose the existence of the condition first mentioned than are other members of the community, that might lead to the conclusion that the connection was not "real", in the sense I take to have been intended by the Full Court in Benham's Case, and that the two conditions were not linked in what Beaumont J. in Miller's Case called "a relevant medical sense". What may be an association in a relevant medical sense will depend largely on facts, which it would be unwise to attempt to classify. I apprehend that the connection will be neither "real" nor "substantial" unless the disparity of incidence justifies an inference that the risk of suffering the condition which incapacitated the eligible employee is substantially greater for those who suffer the specified condition than it is for the rest of the community.

15. There was evidence before the Tribunal to suggest a disparity of incidence of the kind which might justify a finding of the required connection, for the purposes of paragraph 66(2)(c). The incidence of psoriatic arthritis among those known to be, or to have been, suffering psoriasis is, according to the evidence, higher than among those who have been free of psoriasis. The Tribunal noted the evidence, but put it aside because it did not satisfy what the Tribunal took to be the requirement of paragraph 66(2)(c) that the specified condition should have contributed to cause the incapacitating condition. Counsel for the parties were agreed that, if an order were made remitting the case to be heard and decided again, there should be an order authorising the reception of further evidence. I agree. There is no warrant for any direction by me with respect to the evidence or to facts the finding of which the evidence might justify. The applicant Commissioner challenged only the construction which the Tribunal had placed on the words "connected with" in s.66(2)(c). For the reasons I have stated, I consider that construction to have been erroneous.

16. The appeal will be allowed and the decision of the Tribunal set aside. It will be ordered that the matter of the application for a review of the applicant's decision made by his delegate on 15 December 1987 be remitted to the Tribunal and be heard and determined by the Tribunal according to law and that the Tribunal be at liberty to receive further evidence on the said hearing.
