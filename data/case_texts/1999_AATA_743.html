REASONS FOR DECISION



30 September 1999 Mr B A Barbour, Senior Member



1. The application in these proceedings is for review of a decision of the Tax Agents' Board NSW (T5) refusing to register the applicant as a tax agent under provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

2. A hearing was held before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on 5 August 1999. The applicant represented himself and gave oral evidence to the Tribunal. The respondent was represented by Mr G Elliot, of Counsel.

3. The Tribunal had before it documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as well as the following exhibits:

Exhibit 1 – Statement of Facts and Contentions of the applicant dated 26 July 1999

Exhibit 2 – Further submissions of the applicant dated 30 July 1999

Exhibit A – Statement of Facts and Contentions of the respondent dated 3 August 1999

Exhibit B – Statement of John Morony dated 4 August 1999

4. The respondent called John Morony, of the Australian Taxation Office, as a witness.

ISSUES AND LEGISLATION

5. The issue before the Tribunal was whether the applicant's work experience at the Australian Taxation Office ("ATO") satisfied the requirements of "relevant employment" under regulation 156(2) of the Income Tax Regulations ("the Regulations").

6. Paragraph 251JA(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") provides that:

(1) The Board shall register the applicant as a tax agent if the applicant satisfies the Board that:

(a)...

(i) the applicant is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters;



7. What is meant by the expression, "a fit and proper person" is dealt with in section 251BC(1). In so far as it is relevant, it provides:

(1) Without limiting the generality of an expression used in this Part, but subject to this section, a person is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters, as at a particular time, if:

(a)...

(b) both of the following conditions are satisfied:

(i) the person was not registered as a tax agent, or as a nominee, for the purposes of this Part immediately before the commencement of section 39 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No. 2) 1988;

(ii) the person does not hold such qualifications (whether academic, by way of experience or otherwise) as are prescribed;



8. Regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations prescribes the qualifications for the purposes of section 251BC(1)(b)(ii) of the Act. Various academic qualifications, together with qualifications by way of experience or otherwise are prescribed in that regulation. There was no dispute between the parties as to the academic qualifications that the applicant holds. However, the applicant must also satisfy the requirements of regulation 156(1)(d)(i) and 156(2). Regulation 156(1)(d)(i) provides:

(d) the person:

(i) shall have:

(A) been engaged in relevant employment on a full-time basis

for not less than a total of 8 years in the preceding 10 years;

(B) otherwise been engaged in relevant employment to an

extent that the Board regards as equivalent to that referred to in

sub-subparagraph (A); or

(C) been engaged in such other employment and for such time

as the Board regards as equivalent to being engaged in relevant employment as referred to in sub-subparagraph (A);



9. Regulation 156(2) provides that:

(2) In this regulation "relevant employment" means employment by a person or a partnership or as a member of a partnership in the course of which there has been substantial involvement in income tax matters including:

(a) the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax returns;

(b) the preparation or examination of objections to assessments issued in

respect of such returns; and

(c) the provision of advice in relation to income tax returns, assessments or objections.



THE APPLICANT'S WORK EXPERIENCE

10. Mr Ngo, the applicant, gave oral evidence to the Tribunal. He stated that he commenced work as an Administrative Service Officer (ASO) 1 with the Audit section of the ATO in Sydney around May 1988. His duties included checking paperwork in deceased estate returns, and omission of interest and dividend income in individual taxpayer's returns at a basic clerical level.

11. The applicant submitted that in the period 1990 through to June 1992, he was promoted to an ASO2, ASO3 and then an ASO4 in the same section. His work included tax issues in relation to individuals as well as partnership and company taxpayers. The applicant submitted that during this time he dealt with more difficult cases.

12. Mr Ngo told the Tribunal that in June 1992 he moved to the Appeals and Review section at Bankstown Tax Office as an ASO4. The applicant worked in this position until June 1994. For a brief period (from 21 April to 26 June 1994) the applicant was promoted to an ASO5 within the Appeals and Review section. Mr Ngo said that in this position he determined objections from taxpayers (mainly individuals) and had some exposure to trust and company returns. He said that he was required to obtain and analyse information from clients and third parties. The applicant submitted that these tasks differed from auditing in that they involved an assessment of the entire return.

13. The respondent put to the applicant that in this role, he only looked at the tax return in relation to a particular objection. Mr Ngo agreed but added that he would also look at the whole return for arithmetic errors. He said that he would usually receive an objection in the form of a letter which was always accompanied by the return. The applicant outlined the usual procedure he would follow as - determining what the objection was and how the Audit section came to the decision, assessing how the objection would be decided, and then going through the whole tax return. The applicant further stated that he would advise the taxpayer of rulings and when a dispute was determined.

14. The respondent also put to the applicant that he rarely had access to the return and that his assessment would be of a lower order of complexity. The applicant disagreed and stated that his work involved a broad range of income tax matters. Mr Ngo said that in order to amend a return he would have to look through the whole return. However, the applicant did add that he mainly dealt with objections and would not check receipts or records of income. The applicant said that his main task was to determine when objections and amendments were needed and this would involve going through the entire return.

15. The applicant said that he dealt with wage and salary earners and small businesses. The applicant submitted that he also had some experience in fringe benefits tax and sales tax issues.

16. The applicant approximated that during his time in the Appeals and Review section he dealt with between 500 and 600 cases. He said that in the majority of these cases he would go through the whole return. During the same period, the applicant approximated that he dealt with 50 or 60 disputes involving a company. The applicant agreed that these were basic company matters. The applicant said that during his time in Appeals and Review he may have dealt with 70 or 80 trust matters. He said that because the taxpayer was usually successful in the majority of trust disputes, he would have to go through the whole return in the majority of cases. The applicant agreed that these trust matters were usually of the lower order of complexity.

17. The applicant submitted that from June 1994 through to September 1997, he worked at the Small Business Income (SBI) Advising section at the Bankstown Tax Office. Mr Ngo said that this was not a supervisory role. He said that Mr Gormly, Mr Wood (now deceased) and Mr Khouri supervised his work at different times during this period.

18. The applicant submitted that during his period with the SBI section he was often given work by different sections of the office and his workload was heavy. The applicant submitted that the ATO had been restructured since he had worked there, and that now employees are streamed into specific areas. He further submitted that when he worked for the SBI section he would do a broad range of work involving companies, sole traders, and trusts. The applicant approximated that 50 to 60 per cent of his work involved going through the return. Mr Ngo said that requests for private rulings did not involve going through the return.

19. The applicant submitted that while he worked in this unit, officers were rotated around different specialist areas including the fringe benefits tax and trusts areas. The applicant submitted that when Mr Wood died, Mr Sims would answer more complex questions regarding trusts. However after a while, Mr Sims would consult the applicant as Mr Sims acknowledged the applicant's skills in this field.

20. The Tribunal asked the applicant why he would have to go through returns in this area of work. The applicant said that the system would reject some difficult trust returns and so these would be referred to him. The applicant said that these matters did not constitute normal SBI work. The Tribunal stated that people would tend to seek advice in order to fill out a return and asked why people would be seeking advice after they had completed a return. The Tribunal noted that this function seemed to be inconsistent with the applicant's role in the SBI section. The applicant stated that he may have misunderstood the question, he said that he would not examine the source of income in relation to these returns. However, the applicant noted that if it arose as an issue later when the return had been made, he would examine the return.

21. The respondent put it to Mr Ngo that when he was determining an issue, he would look at other matters in the return; however, if it was a private ruling that would affect another area of the return, he would advise another area of the ATO of this issue. The applicant agreed. The applicant noted that if the effect of the ruling was significant, the issue would be passed on to the Auditor, however, if it was minimal, he would complete the changes on the return.

22. The applicant submitted that one of his tasks while working in the SBI section was to give advice over the phone, by correspondence and at the counter. The applicant agreed that he was supervised by Mr Gormly when he gave advice. Mr Ngo said that during his time in the SBI unit he would have dealt with 250 to 300 queries where he was required to look at the return, and in the majority of cases he would look at the whole return. The applicant stated that he also looked at approximately 100 company returns, checked approximately 250 to 300 trust returns in detail, and did less than 20 cases involving superannuation.

23. Mr Ngo approximated that 80 per cent of the small business cases he dealt with had "turn-overs" which were much less than $10 million. The applicant further stated that very few of these cases involved complex issues such as international transfer pricing.

24. The applicant stated that he was made redundant by the ATO on 12 September 1997. He stated that he has had no experience in completing or assisting with tax returns since that time.

THE EVIDENCE OF MR MORONY

25. At the hearing, the Tribunal had before it a statement of Mr John Morony of the ATO, dated 4 August 1999. The Tribunal was concerned that the applicant was not served with this statement until the morning of the hearing and therefore had not had an opportunity to prepare any submissions in relation to the statement. The Tribunal gave the applicant an opportunity to consider whether he wished to continue with the hearing or whether he needed any more time to consider the evidence of Mr Morony. The Tribunal told the applicant that the evidence of Mr Morony may be quite detrimental to his case, and that he should carefully consider whether or not he could proceed on that day. The Tribunal also noted that the applicant was unaware that Mr Morony was going to be giving oral evidence on that day. The hearing was adjourned for 15 minutes to give the applicant an opportunity to consider his position. After the tribunal was resumed, the applicant stated that he still wished to proceed.

26. Mr Morony told the Tribunal that he was the Director of the SBI section of the ATO Office at Bankstown from mid 1994 until the section closed in approximately October 1997. The essence of Mr Morony's statement and oral evidence was that officers in the SBI section did not generally examine tax returns in their entirety. He stated that generally the officers were providing advice prospectively in relation to one particular aspect of law, or more usually, simply advising a taxpayer as to how their affairs would be regarded by the Commissioner, in the absence of a completed return. Mr Morony said that issues such as capital gains tax, public benevolent institutions and fringe benefits tax would be referred to specialist officers. He noted that he could not remember the applicant ever being considered to be an expert. However, he did state that it was possible that officers in the section may have encountered and dealt with these issues from time to time.

27. Mr Morony also stated that the work dealt with by the SBI Advising section was not particularly complex, and did not deal with even larger small businesses. He stated that the bulk of the work done in the SBI section was responding to requests for private rulings from taxpayers, and that the officers did not at any stage examine primary material in order to ascertain whether that material supported claims made by the taxpayer.

28. The Tribunal questioned Mr Morony. As a result of this, Mr Morony agreed that he did not know how work was allocated to certain officers. Further, Mr Morony agreed that he could not comment on the day to day work of officers in the SBI section. Mr Morony said that his statement and oral evidence was a general statement of the operation of the area and that he would accept Mr Gormly's statement as to the day to day work carried out by the applicant. Mr Morony did state though that it was rare for officers to have access to returns.



APPLICANT'S SUBMISSIONS

29. The applicant addressed three issues in his submissions. Firstly, whether his work at the ATO involved the "examination" of tax returns; secondly, whether that examination involved a "broad range" of returns; and thirdly, whether his involvement with those returns was "substantial".

30. The applicant stated that he could not agree with the respondent's definition of "examination" as outlined in their statement of facts and contentions. The applicant submitted that the respondent's definition was wrong because it suggested that "examination" was similar to auditing work undertaken by a tax agent. The applicant referred to the Australian Oxford Paperback Dictionary definition of "examination" which is 'to look at closely; inspect, investigate'. He stated that he did not believe that this was analogous to auditing.

31. The applicant said that if "examination" is analogous to the work undertaken by a tax agent, the definition is contradictory to the definition adopted in the case of Re Gupta and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales (1994) 94 ATC 2,113 ("Gupta"). In that case the Tribunal stated that "examination" was meant in 'the sense the Tax Office examines objections to determine them'. The applicant accepted this definition. He submitted that the regulation's use of "examination" can not have two meanings.

32. Mr Ngo referred to the statement of Mr Gormly (T4) which noted that the applicant had 'examined, answered, resolved and processed returns, for partnerships, trusts (including superannuation trusts), companies and individuals on a daily basis' (T4). The applicant also referred to a statement of Mr Khouri, a former colleague of his in the SBI Advising section, dated 15 April 1999, which noted that the daily duties of officers in that section included 'the examination and process of income tax returns of a wide range of taxpayers'. The applicant therefore submitted that he did examine tax returns.

33. The second issue the applicant addressed was whether his work at the ATO involved examination of a "broad range" of tax returns. The applicant referred to Deputy President McMahon's decision in Re Chenouda and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales (1991) 91 ATC 2,027 ("Chenouda") at p 2,031 where the Deputy President said that "broad range" contemplated a range of income tax returns 'spreading over the spectrum of the taxpayers. A large group of taxpayers consists of small businesses, individual traders and partnerships'. The applicant referred again to the statements of Mr Gormly and Mr Khouri which stated that the applicant looked at tax returns of individuals (both PAYE and business taxpayers), trusts, partnerships and companies. The applicant submitted that this satisfied a "broad range" of tax returns as defined in Chenouda and in the cases of Re Webb and the Tax Agents' Board of Queensland (1992) 92 ATC 2,101 ("Webb") and Re Starr and the Tax Agents' Board of Victoria (1997) 97 ATC 2,090 ("Starr").

34. The applicant further submitted that a "broad range" of returns does not mean every type of return. He referred to the decision in Re The Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales v Martin (1996) 96 ATC 2,043 ("Martin") where it states at 2,050 that 'the Sub-regulation does not require experience in preparing every conceivable return'.

35. The third issue the applicant outlined was whether his involvement with a broad range of tax returns was "substantial". The applicant referred to the decision of Deputy President McDonald and Member Dr Re in Starr which stated (at 2,093) that "substantial" meant '"of ample or considerable amount, quantity or dimensions" (the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary on Historical Principles)'. Mr Ngo noted the statement of Mr Gormly, which stated that the applicant's work load was 'heavy' and that the applicant 'substantially and thoroughly' examined tax returns. The applicant said that the Board had decided that his experience was not "substantial" as he did not examine the primary material of tax applicants. However, the applicant submitted that he did obtain information from clients and analysed information in relation to rulings. Mr Ngo further stated that, contrary to the Board's decision, his experience at the ATO was not at a lower level of complexity. He noted again the statements of Mr Gormly and Mr Khouri. The applicant further stated that regulation 156 did not require a high level of complexity.

36. The applicant further submitted that the Board had failed to observe the rules of procedural fairness including the "bias rule" and the "no evidence rule". The applicant said that the Board, in reaching its original decision, was clearly biased against lower ranked officers and had failed to consider all the evidence before it.

RESPONDENT'S SUBMISSIONS

37. Mr Elliot, for the respondent, submitted that the applicant's recent employment in the SBI Advising section, Bankstown Tax Office, did not provide him with experience of a sufficiently wide range of tax returns to satisfy the requirements of regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations.

38. The respondent referred to the decision in Re Tax Agents' Board of Queensland v Seymour (1990) 21 FCR 357 ("Seymour") in relation to the definition of "employment". In that case (at 362) the Federal Court said that "employment" 'excludes those simply working alone... the so-called "self-employed".' Mr Elliot concluded that regulation 156(2) requires that the person's work is supervised.

39. The respondent also referred to the decision in Re Culmer and Tax Agents' Board (1990) 90 ATC 2,018 ("Culmer"). There the Tribunal considered the experience of a person employed with the ATO. The Tribunal, at 2,021, stated that 'officers of the Australian Taxation Office are unlikely to be able to establish the necessary relevant employment... They join the ranks of the self employed as persons who are unlikely to meet the new and more stringent tests for registration as tax agents'. The respondent said that the reason why this is the case is set out in previous paragraph of the decision where it is noted that 'the applicant's work in the Australian Taxation Office was unsupervised and therefore not relevant employment or its equivalent'.

40. The respondent further submitted that advising in relation to one aspect of an income tax return does not constitute "examination" for the purposes of regulation 156(2)(a). The respondent disputed the applicant's claim that "examination" means to 'look at closely'. Mr Elliot submitted that the fundamental task of "examination" is gathering primary material and interpreting the data, and that it involves the examination of the whole return not only certain aspects of the return.

41. The respondent also submitted that the work undertaken by the applicant in the SBI Advising section was quite different to auditing work as required by regulation 156. Mr Elliot said that advising work within the ATO is essentially reactive rather than pro-active in nature. He added that the work of officers in the ATO involves advising in relation to, and interpreting, isolated sections of a tax return, usually without actually examining the return itself.

42. Mr Elliot said that unlike auditing work, which involves looking at the primary material (including receipts and other records) relevant to a tax return, advising work, such as that undertaken by the applicant, involved no such investigation or analysis of primary material. Mr Elliot therefore concluded that the applicant's work between June 1994 and September 1997 did not involve "examination" of tax returns in the sense intended by regulation 156, that is, "examination" analogous to that which would be undertaken by a tax agent in preparing a return.

43. The respondent also submitted that the applicant's work involved little of what is meant by "examination", that is a "line-by-line" examination. The respondent further submitted that it is the task of a tax agent to undertake such an examination, and so "examination", in the context of regulation 156(2), should clearly be analogous to that of a tax agent.

44. The respondent referred to the applicant's claim that he dealt with between 500 to 600 cases over two years. The respondent submitted that, compared to the number of cases a tax agent deals with over a similar time period, the applicant's experience was not "substantial". The respondent submitted that the applicant may have looked at these returns, but he did not "examine" them. Further, the respondent submitted that only 35 per cent of the applicant's work while in the SBI section involved any consideration of a return.

45. The respondent further stated that while in the SBI section, the applicant's work involved making adjustments to returns, not the "examination" of the return. The respondent concluded that only a small proportion of the work of the applicant involved returns. The respondent stated that this does not constitute "substantial involvement" in a "broad range" of returns. The respondent also noted that the applicant had little or no experience with superannuation or company returns.

46. Mr Elliot stated that the applicant's work has only related to small business and has been of the lower order of complexity. The respondent also noted that the applicant was an ASO5 - not a higher grade of Officer. The respondent qualified this statement by saying that someone of that grade in the Australian Taxation Office may be able to become a tax agent, however, because the applicant was a moderately ranked officer in a small business section, he does not satisfy the requirements of the regulation.

47. The respondent considered the reference to the statement of Mr Gormly (T4). The respondent submitted that in the light of the applicant's evidence, Mr Gormly uses "return" in a very broad sense to include any tax issue that the applicant faced.

48. The respondent then addressed the meaning of the phrase "substantial involvement in a broad range of returns". Mr Elliot referred to the decision of Deputy President Forrest in Re Civitti and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria (1990) 90 ATC 2,039 ("Civitti"). In that case, at 2,043, the court said that the qualifying adjective "broad" has 'a similar meaning to "wide"... The word "broad" is not one of similar meaning to "substantial". In the context in which they are used, both terms are used as qualifying adjectives. "Broad range" does not mean the same thing as "substantial involvement" and vice versa. There may be one without the other. The respondent has to be satisfied the applicant has acquired both in order to have been engaged in relevant employment within the meaning of reg 156...It is not a relevant consideration that reg 156 does not specify the categories of returns in defining "relevant employment". It is explicit in the language used, that wide-ranging experience is required. A lack of experience in the area of returns referred to, in my opinion, precludes the satisfaction of the test of relevant employment.'

49. The respondent also referred to the decision of Re Chenouda and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales (1991) 91 ATC 2,027. In that case, at 2,030 to 2,031, Deputy President McMahon stated that, "substantial involvement" does 'not refer to income tax returns with a broad range... A few specialised returns resulting from such a broad range of enquiries does not... constitute a broad range of returns. What is contemplated by the phrase is a range of income tax returns, spreading over the spectrum of potential taxpayers. A large group of taxpayers consists of small businesses, individual traders and partnerships. If, as the evidence showed, the applicant has not been involved in the preparation or examination of tax returns for any of these classes of taxpayers, then he cannot be said to have had a substantial involvement in the preparation or examination of a broad range of tax returns.' The respondent submitted that considering the decisions of Civitti and Chenouda, and the evidence of the applicant, it is clear that his work at the ATO did not involve a "broad range" of returns. The respondent particularly noted the applicant's lack of experience in company and superannuation returns.

50. Mr Elliot, for the respondent, also considered the meaning of "substantial involvement". He referred to the case of Re Kerr and Tax Agents' Board of Queensland (1998) 98 ATC 2,109 ("Kerr") at 2,114 where the Tribunal stated that "substantial involvement" must include 'preparation of income tax returns from the beginning and not just the actual completion of the returns. Given the present system of electronic lodgment of income tax returns it is the preparation work that is vital to the insertion of correct data into the program for lodgment of the return.' Mr Elliot submitted that "examination" is not that removed from preparation and that what is important is that the return is examined in the same way that a tax agent examines a return. My Elliot concluded that "substantial involvement" must involve not simply having a look at the return, but understanding all the issues in relation to the return.

51. The respondent referred to the case of Re Egulian and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales (1991) 91 ATC 2,060 ("Egulian"). The applicant, in that case, argued that they had examined a number of returns and had been exposed to others. The Tribunal (at 2,064 to 2,065) found that, 'A demonstration of academic capacity or a passing acquaintance with the types of problems that might be thrown up with the preparation or examination of such returns is not, in my view, sufficient to satisfy the requirements of the regulation. Substantial involvement necessarily implies involvement to a substantial extent.' Mr Elliot therefore submitted that there is a distinction to be drawn between "examination" and "exposure". The respondent noted that the applicant may have been "exposed" to a number of returns, however he may not have "examined" or been involved in them to a substantial extent.

52. The respondent also referred to the issue of the complexity of the applicant's work while at the ATO. On this point, the respondent referred to Civitti at 2,042: 'The applicant's experience in the taxation office was predominantly in the field of taxpayers who are salary or wage earners, sole traders or in partnerships... the applicant agreed that her experience was with returns at the "lower level" of complexity.' The respondent submitted that this suggested that "substantial involvement" required a level of complexity. Mr Elliot further submitted that it is common sense that the issue of complexity should be considered.

53. Finally, the respondent submitted that procedural fairness issues raised by the applicant are of minimal relevance to these proceedings given that the Tribunal is undertaking a merits review and the applicant has been given ample opportunity to put his case.

54. In summary, the respondent submitted that the applicant was clearly an enthusiastic and respected member of the ATO, however the applicant had not made out the requirements for a tax agent, and has not satisfied the requirements of regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations.

FINDINGS

55. The issue to be decided in this case is whether the applicant's work experience constitutes "relevant employment" under regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations. The relevant part of that regulation is 156(2) which provides:

(2) In this regulation "relevant employment" means employment by a person or a partnership or as a member of a partnership in the course of which there has been substantial involvement in income tax matters including:

(a) the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax returns;

(b) the preparation or examination of objections to assessments issued in

respect of such returns; and

(c) the provision of advice in relation to income tax returns, assessments

or objections.



The applicant has clearly not been involved in the "preparation" of returns. Consequently, in deciding whether or not the applicant in this case has been engaged in "relevant employment", two main issues must be considered. These issues are whether the applicant has had "substantial involvement" in income tax matters including the "examination" of tax returns; and secondly, whether that substantial involvement has included the examination of a "broad range" of income tax returns. This was the focus of both the applicant and the respondent's submissions.

56. In the Egulian case Deputy President McMahon considered the meaning of "substantial involvement". He stated, at 2,064 to 2,065:

A demonstration of academic capacity or a passing acquaintance with the types of problems that might be thrown up with the preparation or examination of such returns is not, in my view, sufficient to satisfy the requirements of the regulation. Substantial involvement necessarily implies involvement to a substantial extent.



57. "Substantial involvement" was also considered by Deputy President Forgie in the case of Webb. Deputy President Forgie, at 2,106 to 2,107, stated:

The returns prepared by the applicant may have been the result of a broad range of enquiries and research. A few specialised returns resulting from such a broad range of enquiries does not, however, constitute a broad range of returns...

I am not satisfied, however, that her work, concentrated as it is upon certain types of returns, can be described as amounting to substantial involvement in a broad range of income tax returns as required by regulation 156(2)... Mrs Webb readily agrees that she does not have a great deal of experience in the preparation of returns other than those for individuals and partnerships, which also include those for small business. She submitted that, were she to be registered as a tax agent, there is no way she would undertake the preparation of returns for public companies and corporations or companies operating in specialist areas...

The problem is that the Act and Regulations do not limit registration as a tax agent to a particular range of returns just as they do not limit the experience which is required to a particular range. As Deputy President Forrest observed in Re Civitti and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria 90 ATC 2039 at 2043:

"Once registration is granted, the agent is not limited in the taxation work he or she may undertake. With the best intention it is not an easy thing, more so when establishing a practice, to decline a potential client outside one's sphere of experience. The respondent has to ensure as best it can, that the person entrusted with any type of return or income tax business is fit to undertake the work."



58. The same must apply to the applicant in this case. After considering the evidence of the applicant at the hearing and the statements of Mr Gormly, Mr McLenahan (T4) and Mr Khouri, I am unable to find that the applicant has had "substantial involvement" in income tax matters. It is not in dispute that the applicant was well respected during his time at the ATO, indeed, his colleagues and supervisors have stated that Mr Ngo is a man of 'intellect and integrity'. However, looking at the evidence before the Tribunal, it is clear that the applicant's experience of "examination" was only in relation to specific aspects of returns brought to his attention, and as a result was too specific to constitute "substantial involvement".

59. The applicant agreed that while in the Appeals and Review section of the ATO he would only look at a return in relation to a particular objection. He did state that he would often check a return for inconsistencies and arithmetic errors, however, this would not involve checking any receipts, records of income or primary material. This cannot amount to "substantial involvement". It was clear to the Tribunal at the conclusion of the applicant's evidence, that his work had mostly been specific and task oriented, rather than a general and full examination.

60. The Tribunal finds little assistance in the evidence of Mr Morony. Although he was Director of the SBI area while the applicant was an officer there, his evidence tells us little of the day to day operation of the unit and therefore what the applicant's work actually involved.

61. The applicant stated that while in the SBI section, 50 to 60 percent of his work involved going through returns. However, he also noted that he would not examine the source of income or primary information in relation to these returns.

62. The Tribunal was referred to the statement of Mr Gormly (T4). In that statement, Mr Gormly noted that during his time in Appeals and Review and Advising, the applicant had 'examined, answered, resolved and processed returns, rulings, correspondence and objections for partnerships, trusts (including superannuation trusts), companies and individuals on a daily basis. His duties also required him to resolve legal issues for capital gains tax, fringe benefits tax and sales tax matters'. Mr Gormly's statement is a very positive reference, however, it does not indicate to what extent the applicant was involved in these tax matters and returns. The applicant may have been involved in a variety of tax matters, however, it does not necessarily follow that the applicant has had "substantial involvement" in dealing with these tax matters.

63. In another statement from Mr Gormly dated 31 March 1999, he stated that the applicant has dealt with sole traders, trusts, companies and partnerships. Mr Gormly noted that the applicant spent 30 per cent of his time dealing with trust returns and that those trusts covered investment and damages trusts, superannuation funds, trading trusts and deceased estates.

64. The applicant also referred the Tribunal to the statement of Mr Khouri dated 15 April 1999. Mr Khouri provided details in relation to the applicant's work while in the Advising area in Bankstown Taxation Office. He noted that the applicant's daily duties included providing answers to technical and procedural questions in relation to all aspects of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, the provision of private binding rulings and the examination and process of income tax returns of a wide range of taxpayers. Mr Khouri noted that when he and the applicant worked in the SBI section, the applicant dealt with individuals, partnerships, trusts and companies whose taxable income was up to $8 million. However, Mr Khouri does not specify to what extent the applicant was involved in these returns or tax matters.

65. Perhaps Mr McLenahan's statement is more indicative of the applicant's work at the ATO. He states that while in the Appeals and Review group the applicant was primarily responsible for the determination of income tax dispute cases. Mr McLenahan also notes that the cases the applicant dealt with 'primarily related to individual taxpayers however also extended to trust and company returns.'

66. Having considered all of the evidence, particularly the oral evidence of the applicant at the hearing, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the applicant's experience at the ATO involved "substantial involvement" in tax matters. Rather, the evidence suggests that while the applicant worked on a variety of tax matters, his involvement varied according to the type of tax matter. It is clear from the evidence before the Tribunal that the applicant was not always required to examine a return; and when he was, it was generally in relation to a specific issue or objection. The applicant's work was concentrated upon certain types of returns. Undoubtedly he developed considerable expertise in relation to trust returns as indicated by Mr Gormly. However, the applicant's own evidence established clearly that he had not had "substantial involvement" in a broad range of returns.

67. Further, as Deputy President Forrest stated in Civitti:

once registration is granted, the agent is not limited in the taxation work he or she may undertake... The respondent has to ensure as best it can, that the person entrusted with any type of return or income tax business is fit to undertake the work.



I am satisfied that the applicant is a very capable person and that he was obviously valued by his colleagues and employees at the ATO. However, I am not satisfied that his review based experience at the ATO involved "substantial involvement" in a broad range of income tax matters.

68. There was some debate at the hearing in relation to the meaning of "examination". The applicant preferred Senior Member Ettinger's decision of Gupta. Conversely, the respondent argued that "examination" must be given a meaning akin to that undertaken by a tax agent. The Tribunal agrees with the respondent. The purpose of these regulations must always be kept in mind, as was noted in Starr, at 2,093:

These provisions are aimed at ensuring people who are engaged in the preparation of income tax returns and advice for reward have appropriate knowledge of the law and practice.



It is common sense, then, that considering the purpose of these regulations, a person's experience should reflect appropriate knowledge of the law and practice, and that this would extend to "examination".

69. However, what is meant by "examination" is not the issue in this case. The question for the Tribunal is whether the applicant's work experience constituted "substantial involvement" in tax matters including the examination of a "broad range" of returns.

70. The most quoted case in relation to the meaning of "broad range" is the decision of Deputy President Forrest in Civitti. In that decision, at 2,049-2,050, the Deputy President said:

'Broad range' does not mean the same thing as 'substantial involvement' and vice versa. There may be one without the other. The respondent has to be satisfied the applicant has acquired both in order to have been engaged in relevant employment within the meaning of reg. 156.

...It is not the experience within a category of returns but rather the scope of returns which is the test to be applied.

It is not a relevant consideration that reg. 156 does not specify the categories of returns in defining 'relevant employment'. It is explicit in the language used, that wide-ranging experience is required. A lack of experience in the areas of returns referred to, in my opinion, precludes satisfaction of the test of relevant employment.



The applicant's experience in this case was predominantly in the field of taxpayers who were salary or wage earners, sole traders or partnerships. Her experience did not extend to any company, trust or superannuation returns.



71. The other significant case to the issue of "broad range" is the decision of Deputy President McMahon in Chenouda. In that case the Deputy President concluded that:

What is contemplated by the phrase is a range of income tax returns, spreading over the spectrum of potential taxpayers. A large group of taxpayers consists of small businesses, individual traders and partnerships. If, as the evidence showed, the applicant has not been involved in the preparation or examination of tax returns for any of these classes of taxpayers, then he cannot be said to have had a substantial involvement in the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax returns.



Deputy President McMahon in Egulian also noted, at 2,064, that '...superannuation, fringe benefits or trust returns... form part of the broad range referred to in the regulation.' Therefore, the Tribunal is required to make an assessment of the scope of returns the applicant may have had involvement with. In his evidence that applicant stated that he had some exposure to trust and company returns at the Appeals and Review section of the ATO. Unfortunately, this degree of exposure is insufficient.



72. In Gupta, Senior Member Ettinger noted:

The Tribunal is satisfied that the "broad range" referred to in Regulation 156(2), refers to both the range and the number of returns prepared or examined. It notes that the applicant prepared a substantial number of individual and partnership returns. However, the Tribunal is not satisfied that Mrs Gupta's involvement in company, trusts, superannuation, and fringe benefits returns comprised the "substantial involvement" required by Regulation 156(2) of the Regulations.



73. Similarly, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the applicant in this case has had substantial involvement with a "broad range" of returns as required by the regulation. The evidence of the applicant reveals that while in the Appeals and Review section he dealt with mainly wage and salary earners and small business and had only some limited exposure to FBT and sales tax matters. The applicant also stated that he had dealt with about 50 to 60 simple company matters and some trust matters of the lower order of complexity.

74. The applicant also stated that while at the SBI unit his work included companies, sole traders and trusts. He noted that only 50 per cent of his work at the SBI section involved going through the return. During this time the applicant stated that he looked at approximately 250 to 300 trust returns, however, he did less than 20 cases involving superannuation. The small business cases he dealt with were not very complex and always involved companies with a "turn over" well below $10 million. Having considered both the scope and quantity of the types of returns that the applicant dealt with while at the ATO, the Tribunal is not convinced that his experience included the examination of a "broad range" of returns. Certainly, the applicant's involvement with these returns varied. At times the applicant would go through the whole return, at other times he would only make simple changes in relation to objections, or pass the return on to another section of the ATO. This experience is insufficient to constitute substantial involvement in a broad range of returns.

75. For these reasons, the Tribunal must find that the applicant's work experience does not satisfy the inclusory tests for "relevant employment" in regulation 156(2). The Tribunal is not satisfied that the applicant has had "substantial involvement" in income tax matters that include examination of a broad range of income tax returns.

76. For these reasons the decision under review is affirmed.
