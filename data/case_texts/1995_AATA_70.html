NT94/147 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 70; 95 ATC 196; (1995) 30 ATR 1208 (17 March 1995)

Last Updated: 11 February 2009



Administrative Appeals Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



INCOME TAX - Applicant in receipt of payments from Leagues Club - Applicant contracted to play football for Football Club - whether payments by way of capital or income within ordinary concepts - whether payments by way of scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance - whether applicant is a student receiving full-time education at a university - whether payments received on condition that the applicant render services



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, subsections 23(z), 25(1), 26(e)



Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540

Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Ranson 89 ATC 5322

Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Hall (1975) ATC 4156



Ford and Austin’s Principles of Corporations Law 7th Edition, Butterworths, 1995



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: NT94/147

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:      

Applicant



And: Commissioner of Taxation

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Mr B A Barbour, Senior Member



Date : 17 March 1995



Place : Sydney



Decision :



The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and finds that the scholarship payments in the year of income under review are exempt income pursuant to subsection 23(z) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: NT94/147

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:      

Applicant



And: Commissioner of Taxation

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



Tribunal : Mr B A Barbour, Senior Member



Date : 17 March 1995



Place : Sydney



  1. This is an application to review an objection decision of the respondent dated 23 February 1994, disallowing an objection by the applicant in relation to the inclusion of scholarship income as assessable income in the tax year ended 30 June 1992, pursuant to either subsection 25(1) or 26(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act). The matter was heard before me on 10 February 1995.
  2. The Tribunal had before it documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T documents). The applicant was represented by Mr R F Edmonds of counsel, who tendered the applicant’s statement of enrolment in a University Course, and statements of the applicant and another (AA). The respondent Commissioner of Taxation, represented by Mr S Gibb of counsel, tendered the applicant's transcript of academic record, a page of minutes relating to the grant of a scholarship, and a glossy magazine-style company ‘profile’. The applicant and AA gave oral evidence.
  3. The issue before the Tribunal is whether amounts received by the applicant from ABC Leagues Club Limited (the Leagues Club), during the tax year ended 30 June 1992, are assessable income under subsections 25(1) or 26(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. The applicant contended that the amounts received were either by way of gift, or exempt income under subsection 23(z) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.
  4. Subsection 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 relevantly provides:
“The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include- (a) where the taxpayer is a resident- the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia; ... which is not exempt income ..."
  1. Subsection 26(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 relevantly provides:
“The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include- (e) the value to the taxpayer of all allowances, gratuities, compensations, benefits, bonuses and premiums allowed, given or granted to him in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly to, any employment of or services rendered by him, whether so allowed, given or granted in money, goods, land, meals, sustenance, the use of premises or quarters or otherwise, not being- ...”.
  1. Subsection 23(z) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 relevantly provides:
“The following income shall be exempt from income tax:-... (z) income derived by way of scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance or educational assistance ... by a student receiving full-time education at a school, college or university, but not including- (i) an amount received by the student from a person or authority upon condition that the student will (or will if required) render, or continue to render, services to that person or authority; ...”
  1. AA, the President and Chairman of the Board of the Leagues Club, gave evidence as to the nature of the scholarship scheme. He provided a written statement to the Tribunal. He stated that he had been a board member of the Leagues Club since October 1970 (excluding 3 years in the 1970s).
  2. The objects of the Leagues Club are set out at clause 3 of the Memorandum and Articles of Association of the Leagues Club, and they relevantly provide that the Leagues Club is: “... To assist generally in the promotion conduct and propagation of Rugby League Football in the Rugby league Football District of [ABC] or elsewhere and to provide or assist in the provision of training and conditioning and teaching facilities for football in accordance with the rules of the New South Wales Rugby League Limited ...”; and “... to render aid either financial or by other means to clubs or associations in the Rugby League Football District of [ABC] or elsewhere which clubs or association are playing in accordance with the rules of the New South Wales Rugby League Limited ...”.
  3. AA writes that in 1970, the Leagues Club’s Board of Directors (of which he was not a member at the time) established a scholarship scheme to “... provide financial assistance to playing members of the ABC Football Club Limited (the Football Club) who were undertaking full-time courses of education at universities and other tertiary institutions and otherwise qualified for the award of scholarship ...”. The scheme, originally established to assist local players, was “... subsequently extended to include country and inter-state players ...”. At the time of hearing, AA said 53 scholarships had been awarded under the scheme. In response to questions from the Tribunal, he disagreed with the statement that the scholarship was not offered to ensure players joined the Football Club, but stated that this was a joint purpose with the back-up and support of the player’s education.
  4. He states that the award of a scholarship is completely in the discretion of the Leagues Club’s Board of Directors, and is subject to an over-riding budgetary constraint, and that only those players who are recommended by the Football Club, and satisfy the Leagues Club directors that they qualify within the guidelines, are considered. He writes that the Chief Executive Officer of the Football Club (CEOFC) or other Football Club senior executive interviews the parents, teacher and other persons who know the scholarship ‘candidate’. AA’s evidence was that to his knowledge no scholarship applicant had been rejected by the Leagues Club’s Board of Directors.
  5. The guidelines, as recorded in AA’s statement, provide that the candidate: has to be an active playing member of the Football Club; has to be of good character and preferably from a ‘strong family background’; has to be undertaking full-time tertiary studies leading to an award or qualification; has to have reasonable prospects of success at the course; has to have above average potential at playing rugby league.
  6. AA stated in answer to questions from the Tribunal that there was no application form for the scholarship, and that the guidelines had not been written down, although they were well understood. He stated that the CEOFC was well aware of the guidelines. He stated that the Leagues Club directors did not satisfy themselves that persons had been accepted for tertiary studies prior to awarding a scholarship.
  7. Once awarded, the scholarship continues unconditionally until the player completes the course unless the player is guilty of ‘conduct ... unbecoming a scholarship holder’ or his attitude to his studies is not of a sufficient standard or he terminates his course. In his written statement, AA refers to two scholarship holders who, though they stopped playing for the Football Club, through injury or the pressure of studies, continued to receive their scholarships.
  8. In cross-examination, AA stated that the Chief Executive Officer of the Leagues Club (CEOLC) reports annually on the progress of scholarship awardees, or earlier if there are problems in relation to , for example, conduct. Such a yearly report would be in the form of ‘XX [the scholarship holder] is going fine’ or ‘XX failed’. He stated that a scholarship would not be withdrawn for failing to pass one year only. The CEOLC was in constant contact with the scholarship holders at a personal level.
  9. In cross-examination, AA stated that prior to March 1991, there was a maximum of 3 of the 7 Leagues Club directors who were nominated by the Football Club, and that since that time the Football Club could nominate 4 out of the 7 directors. He agreed that in the last six years, the bulk of the Football Club’s funds had been provided by the Leagues Club by way of grant, and that the grants were determined by having regard to the Leagues Club’s profit, and made to give effect to the Leagues Club’s objects. He stated that the Leagues Club also supports football by sponsoring junior leagues, through the schools, and via the Scholarship scheme.
  10. He stated that the reason that the Leagues Club granted scholarships, as opposed to providing funds to the Football Club for that purpose, was that this had been a major platform of the ‘Reform Team’ (with which he had a connection) when that team was elected in 1970 to the Board of Directors of the Leagues Club. In addition to the scholarships, a junior leagues initiative and a schools program were proposed, to lift the game of football in the Leagues Club’s district. He stated that at the time there was also an awareness of the taxation situation.
  11. He denied that the Leagues Club made the payments on behalf of the Football Club. He also agreed in cross-examination that there was no relation between the amounts awarded for scholarships and the amounts provided to the Football Club by way of grant, and later agreed with the Tribunal that the scholarship grants were in addition to grants made to the Football Club. He also stated that at times the full budgetary allocation for scholarships was not used.
  12. In his written statement, the applicant gave evidence that he was selected for the Australian Schoolboys’ Rugby Union Team while in his final year at high school. Towards the end of that year, he was approached by representatives of the Football Club, who wished to secure his services to play for the Football Club. Discussions were also held between the applicant’s parents and teachers and the Football Club representatives.
  13. His statement continues that through these discussions, he became aware of a scholarship scheme to assist Football Club players pursuing full-time education. As the applicant wished to study a physical education course on a full-time basis, the scholarship scheme influenced his decision to sign with the Football Club. He stated in oral evidence that he received match payments for his contracted services from the Football Club, these being paid twice a year (in July and October), and that apart from the match payments and other amounts paid pursuant to the contract, he received no other sums from the Football Club.
  14. A letter of 3 December 1990 from the Leagues Club (attached to the applicant's statement) informed the applicant that the directors of the Leagues Club had granted him a ‘Sporting Scholarship”, and that ‘there are no services to be rendered by you in return for this payment’. The applicant records in his written statement that he was paid $866.66 per calender month in the form of a cheque that he picked up from the Leagues Club. The applicant stated that he had never been required to render services to the Leagues Club. Aside from a university vacation maintenance position, which he had requested and obtained, and for which he was separately compensated, the applicant stated that he did not render services to the Leagues Club. He stated that he applied the scholarship money to his University expenses and to his living expenses.
  15. In answer to questions from the Tribunal, the applicant stated that he understood the purpose of the scholarship to be to allow him to go to University and play football at the same time, and that if he achieved poor results or committed misdemeanours, the scholarship may be terminated. He stated that there was no obligation for him to provide his academic record to anyone at the Leagues Club.
  16. The applicant, in his written statement, notes that in 1991 he enrolled as a full-time student at a University in a Bachelors degree in Education (Human Movements). In cross-examination, he stated that he had repeated his final year at school in order to gain enrolment in a physiotherapy course, but had been unsuccessful in this. He also stated that the ‘human movement’ aspect of the course did not commence until first year subjects were passed, and that this course could be an avenue for him to gain admission to a physiotherapy course.
  17. His written statement records that he attended lectures each week day during University terms; of the four subjects, he writes that he passed two and failed two. Records of enrolment identified by the applicant in oral evidence indicated that he was enrolled in the subjects Mathematics 1, Psychology 1, Chemistry 1 and Education 1 in 1991, and Chemistry 1, Education II, General Pure Mathematics 1, and General Statistical Methods in 1992. He conceded in cross-examination that he passed Education 1 in 1991, and that he passed some parts of the other subjects, but that he did not pass two full subjects. The University records tendered by the respondent and identified by the applicant indicate that he passed only Education 1 in 1991.
  18. He stated in cross-examination that he began classes in March 1991, and that in each subject he was required to attend lectures and tutorials, and in Chemistry he was required to attend ‘lab pracs’. He agreed that he was required to sit exams in the subjects at least once a semester. He agreed that he did not attend lectures Monday to Friday as suggested in his statement, and that he did not attend some days in 1991. He stated that he attended Chemistry ‘pracs’ as a rule, and could not recall if he ceased attending ‘pracs’ after September 1991. He stated that in 1992 he was not required to attend ‘lab pracs’ because he was quite sure that he had completed those sessions in 1991.
  19. He stated that he did not sit a final year examination in Psychology 1 because he felt underprepared for it and that it would have been a waste of time to sit the exam. He stated that he was required to do an assignment a week in Psychology 1, and that he completed ‘a fair number’ of the assignments required. He agreed that he did fewer assignment as the year ‘wore on’.
  20. In relation to questions asked in cross-examination concerning his football commitments, he stated that in the first part of 1991, he attended training from 3 to 5 o’clock, Mondays to Fridays, and that sessions lasted for 1 1/2 to 2 hours. He stated that his training did not conflict with his University commitments until 1992. He also stated that his team went away in 1991 during the October holidays, and that additional training was required towards the end of the football season. This training, he stated, did not interfere with his attending lectures, but did interfere with his ability to complete assignments.
  21. After 3 games in the main football season of 1992, the applicant stated that he was elevated to a position in the Football Club’s first grade team, and was sometimes required to train from 2 or 3 o’clock in the afternoon. He stated that at some time he came to the decision that football would take precedence over his studies, and that he needed to cement his position in the first grade team. He gave evidence that in the first half of 1992, after training, he would come home and watch television as he was tired, and that this led to him being underprepared for University work. He stated that he missed few lectures as he scheduled his training around his lectures, and that he could not recall whether he completed the required assignments in Pure Mathematics. He did not agree that he did not make a real attempt at his Chemistry studies in 1991, and stated that he applied himself better at the beginning of 1992, but that this effort dropped off.
  22. He records in his statement that he ceased his course due to the fact that his girlfriend became pregnant, and the applicant stated in oral evidence that their child was born in March 1993. He notified the general manager of the Leagues Club of his decision to leave the course in December 1992.
  23. The applicant stated in cross-examination that about half way through 1992 he realised he would have to defer or give up his course, and that he did not notify the University that he was proposing to discontinue his studies. The applicant stated that he ceased attending lectures late in the second half of 1992. He did not sit exams in November 1992. He stated that he did not inform the Leagues Club of his departure from University for some months.
  24. In relation to the grant of scholarship to the applicant, the respondent tendered the minute of the Leagues Club (identified by AA) of 1 December 1990 recording: “... Resolved that [the applicant] be awarded a Sporting Scholarship of $866.66 per month commencing 1st February 1991, until 31st October 1994 ...”. AA stated that he was not directly involved in negotiation with the applicant in regards to the scholarship, although he was in the restaurant where the CEOFC was dining with the applicant and his parents in relation to engaging the applicant to play football.
  25. He stated in answer to questions from the Tribunal that it was the CEOFC (who is also a director of the Leagues Club) who offered the scholarship to the applicant. He also stated that no scholarship applicants had been rejected. He stated that the applicant was an ideal and outstanding candidate for a Leagues Club scholarship.
  26. I have had regard to all the other evidence material before me. I note the Club profile, a glossy magazine, tendered by the respondent, which includes information about the scholarship scheme. In a section headed ‘Sporting Scholarships’, the magazine refers to the ‘Club’s Sporting Scholarship Scheme’, the ‘Club’ being a reference to the Football Club.
  27. I also note the applicant's two contracts with the Football Club both expired on 31 October 1994, and that this was the same date as the expiry of the scholarship as noted in the minutes of the Leagues Club identified by AA.
  28. Both parties provided written and made oral submissions. Prior to considering them (and I will make specific findings in relation to the evidence as I consider each of the submissions), I would note that I accept the majority of the evidence provided by both the applicant and AA. Although there are some inconsistencies particularly between the applicant's written statement and his oral evidence, I nevertheless found him to be honest and frank in giving his evidence. I also found AA to be a credible and honest witness.
  29. Mr Edmonds first submitted on behalf of the applicant that the payments were by way of gift, and therefore payments of capital. He submitted that as the applicant did not have to render services for the payments, they are not income in ordinary concepts. He stated that they would only be income by reason of their periodicity.
  30. Here, the evidence of the applicant is that he received the scholarship payments monthly, and the payments were applied to educational and living expenses. In these circumstances, the payments have the character of income in ordinary concepts, and both their periodicity and the applicant’s reliance upon and expectation of the payments point to this finding; see Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, and Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Ranson 89 ATC 5322. I would therefore characterise the scholarship payments as income for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.
  31. The applicant next contended that the payments were exempt pursuant to subsection 23(z) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. That provision exempts income, in his submission, where: (i) the income is derived by way of scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance; (ii) the income is derived by a student receiving full-time education at a school, college or university; (iii) the income is not an amount received by the student from a person upon condition that the student will, or will if required, render or continue to render services to that person.
  32. As regards (i), the applicant submitted that the scholarship provider could have in mind collateral advantages and not be motivated by purely educational aims in providing the scholarship, and that the educational purpose of the Leagues Club is clear from AA’s evidence. The respondent submitted on this point that the payments appeared to be remuneration for past and present services to the Leagues Club as real or apparent payer, or to the Football Club as the real payer. Additionally, the respondent submitted that, having regard to the nature of the course, “... it may be doubted whether the purpose of those who recommended the award and those who made the award was not simply to better prepare the applicant for his employment as a professional footballer with the Football Club, or for future such employment ...”.
  33. In the joint judgement of Davies and Hill JJ in Ranson’s case, their Honours note that:
“... The common thread behind the words “scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance” as used in the section is the educational purpose in the person who provides the allowance, and it matters not whether he who provides the allowance is motivated by purely educational aims (as would be the case with University and similar scholarships) or has in mind some collateral advantage, ...”.
  1. Here, the evidence of AA is that the scholarship scheme was set up by the Leagues Club in 1970 to provide for footballers who wished to pursue a full-time course of study. He agreed that there was another purpose in seeking to secure the services of players for the Football Club. I am satisfied that there was indeed this duality of purpose, and that there was an educational purpose, which was more than ancillary, in the award of the scholarship. This finding is based both on the history of the scheme as recounted by AA, and the evidence of the applicant as to his understanding of the scholarship scheme, and I accept this evidence.
  2. I reject that the course was undertaken by the applicant so as to better prepare himself to play football, and on this point I accept his evidence as to both his desire to study physiotherapy, and then his decision to undertake Human Movement studies as a way to gain entry into physiotherapy. The course subjects studied also do not appear to have a bearing on the applicant's footballing career.
  3. For reasons that I will explain later, I also reject that the payments were in the nature of remuneration for services to either the Leagues Club or the Football Club.
  4. I therefore find that the income in dispute was ‘derived by way of scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance or educational assistance’.
  5. As regards (ii), Mr Edmonds submitted that the applicant was a full-time student during the relevant year of income, and that he attended lectures each week during the University term. Mr Gibb for the respondent submitted that mere enrolment in a course is insufficient, and that education involved the two elements of imparting of knowledge and system. He stated that where a person fails to attend classes, complete assignments and sit exams, the student is not receiving a full-time education, and that the evidence suggests that the applicant spent little time attending classes or completing assignments and that he failed to sit for exams.
  6. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Hall (1975) ATC 4156, Rath J stated that “... education involves the dual concepts of imparting knowledge and system ...”. As regards the imparting of knowledge, Rath J notes that in advanced education, direct teaching may have little prominence, and that instruction comes from books that the student reads. He continues that:
“More significant perhaps is the element of system. In all educational establishments there is a planned course of learning. It is primarily for the educator to plan the course of study, and the procedures of learning. Typically these proceedings will involve reading, discussion and teaching, with the first two having the predominant role as education advances...”.
  1. That matter concerned a Medical Practitioner undertaking a doctorate of Medicine or Philosophy by research, and under the supervision of a Professor at New South Wales University. It was found that this supervised and directed research was ‘full-time education’. Although of use as a generic form of guidance in this type of matter, I do not find this aspect of that decision of particular assistance on the facts before me. There was clearly a ‘system’ in place for the applicant's undergraduate studies, and there is no dispute that the applicant was enrolled in a full-time undergraduate course.
  2. As I understand the submission of the respondent, the Commissioner says that the applicant was so deficient a student that he could not be regarded as a student at all. This was not a matter considered by Rath J, and his decision is of little assistance on this point. The applicant’s counsel, in oral submissions, stated that such a proposition was a matter of fact and degree.
  3. The applicant has given evidence that he attended the lectures, tutorials and ‘lab pracs’ that he was required to attend in the tax year (ie. the later half of 1991 and the first half of 1992) on a regular basis, and that he structured his University course so that he could attend these and his football training. He has given evidence that he completed ‘a fair number’ of the assignments required in his Psychology studies, and that he did not sit this exam as he felt that he was not prepared for it. He stated in oral evidence, and the University records indicate that he passed one of his four subjects in 1991, and failed three, but sat for exams in two of those. I accept his evidence on these points and find accordingly.
  4. While I accept that the evidence would support a finding that the applicant put a lesser effort in his studies both at the end of 1991 and then at the end of the first half of 1992, as his footballing commitments increased, and while I also accept that his application to his studies in the tax year as a whole was less than ideal, I do not accept that the applicant did not make serious attempts at his studies. He attended to most of his scheduled University commitments, and passed one subject, which suggests that he took his studies seriously, despite his less than diligent application. I also accept his evidence of renewed scholarly vigour in the beginning of 1992, which dissipated as his football career blossomed. I do not accept that the applicant was so deficient a student as not to be regarded as receiving a full-time education.
  5. I therefore find that the applicant was ‘receiving full-time education at a ... university’ during the year of income.
  6. As regards (iii), the applicant contended that the amounts were received from the Leagues Club in its own right, and not from the Football Club or on behalf of the Football Club, and that they were not received by the applicant upon condition that he would, or would if required, render services to the Leagues Club. Further, it was contended that the amounts were not paid by the Leagues Club on condition that the applicant provide services to the Football Club, and that even if this were the case it would not matter as the services would not have been rendered to ‘that person’; ie., the Leagues Club.
  7. The respondent submitted that where payments are made by a person (including a corporation) on another person’s behalf, and provided the requisite condition exists, the payments will not be exempted income. Although the respondent did not seek to ‘pierce the corporate veil’ or make out that the scholarship payments were a sham, it was contended that at all times the Leagues Club acted as the agent for the Football Club for the following reasons: the Football Club held out the scholarship as an inducement to the applicant to play with the Football Club; the Football Club interviewed the applicant's parents and teachers before recommending the applicant to the Leagues Club for a scholarship; in order to be eligible for the scholarship, the applicant had to be a professional footballer with the Football Club, and that he had to continue playing with the Football Club; the scholarship ceased at the end of the applicant's playing contract; the Football Club was the parent of the Leagues Club, controlling 4/7 of its Board; the two principal objects of the Leagues Club were to promote football, including by the provision of scholarships, and to give financial aid to the Football Club, this being done by giving substantial grants to the Football Club; in the promotional material the scholarships are treated as the Football Club’s scholarships. Alternatively, it was argued that the evidence established that the payments were made by the Leagues Club on condition that the applicant render services to it by playing for the Football Club, which benefited the Leagues Club.
  8. I am satisfied that the payments made to the applicant were received from the Leagues Club. The letter of offer is from the Leagues Club, the payments came from Leagues Club funds, and the applicant collected his monthly scholarship cheque from the Leagues Club.
  9. I am also satisfied that the amounts were received from the Leagues Club without condition that the applicant would, or would if required, render services to the Leagues Club. This finding is based particularly upon the following evidence which I accept: the applicant's evidence as to his understanding concerning the scholarship conditions and his evidence that he had never been required to render services to the Leagues Club; his separate employment and remuneration by the Leagues Club during the University vacation; the letter of offer from the Leagues Club that specifically states that the applicant is not required to render services to the Leagues Club; and the oral and written evidence of AA as regards the unconditional nature of the scholarship.
  10. I am also satisfied that the amounts were not paid by the Leagues Club on condition that the applicant render, or render if required, services to the Football Club. On this point I am persuaded particularly by the evidence of AA as regards two other scholarship holders who ceased to play for the Football Club, and yet continued to receive the scholarship. While being an active playing member of the Football Club is a criteria of eligibility for a scholarship, I am satisfied that should a scholarship holder no longer play for the Football Club, the scholarship could continue, and that it would only be in circumstances of misbehaviour or academic slothfulness that a scholarship would be discontinued.
  11. Given this finding, it is not necessary for me to express an opinion as to whether ‘that person or authority’ at the end of paragraph 23(z)(i) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 should be narrowly construed so as to refer only to the person or authority who pays the scholarship.
  12. As to the respondent's contention that the Leagues Club acted as the Football Club’s agent in making the scholarship payments to the applicant, I would make the following observations:
    • the Football Club held out the scholarship as an inducement to the applicant to play with the Football Club; The applicant submitted and I accept that the purpose of the scholarship was two-fold; one purpose being educational, and the other purpose being the advancement of football in ABC district. I would therefore agree that the scholarship acted as an inducement to the applicant to join the Football Club, and indeed this was his evidence;
    • the Football Club interviewed the applicant's parents and teachers before recommending the applicant to the Leagues Club for a scholarship; The CEOFC did interview the applicant and his teachers and parents prior to recommending to the Leagues Club that the applicant be granted a scholarship, and it was a condition of the scholarship that the applicant be so recommended;
    • in order to be eligible for the scholarship, the applicant had to be a professional footballer with the Football Club, and had to continue playing with the Football Club; I have already found that it was not a condition of the scholarship that the applicant continue to play football for the Football Club;
    • the scholarship ceased at the end of the applicant's playing contract; This, in itself, does no more than provide an inference of the existence of some agreement between the Leagues Club and Football Club, and on its own does not establish an agency agreement;
    • the Football Club was the parent of the Leagues Club, controlling 4/7 of its Board; At the time of the grant of the scholarship, AA stated that the Football Club controlled only 3 of the 7 Board positions, and it was in March 1991 that the Football Club could nominate 4 of the positions. I accept this evidence, and I would not find that at the time of the grant of the scholarship, the Football Club was the parent of the Leagues Club. The case law cited to me on this point (and collected in Ford and Austin’s Principles of Corporations Law 7th Edition, Butterworths, 1995 , paragraph [4.370]) deals with situations where one corporate entity exercises control over another, and I do not accept that at the time of granting the scholarship the Football Club was in such a position;
    • the two principal objects of the Leagues Club were to promote football, including by the provision of scholarships, and to give financial aid to the Football Club, this being done by giving substantial grants to the Football Club; AA’s evidence is that funds devoted to the scholarship were separate to and in addition to those granted to the Football Club. He stated that they were not part of those grants, and the accounts attached to his statement support this evidence (see annexures F, G and H to AA’s statement). I accept this evidence, and further, reject the further contention that the scholarship payments were made at the request of the Football Club. The minute of the Leagues Club, and the evidence of AA, persuade me that the decision to award the applicant the scholarship was an independent exercise of discretion by the Leagues Club;
    • in the promotional material the scholarships are treated as the Football Club’s scholarships; I accept this submission, and am somewhat troubled by the inclusion of the scholarship scheme as the Football Club’s in the ‘profile’. However, the other evidence before me persuades me that the scheme is not one where the Leagues Club acts on the Football Club’s behalf or as its agent.
  13. As I have already stated, I do not accept that the payments were made by the Leagues Club on condition that the applicant render services to the Football Club, and I therefore reject the respondent’s contention that he was required to render services to the Football Club, which in turn benefited the Leagues Club.
  14. It is evident that there is a close relationship between the Leagues Club and the Football Club, and their functions are in many ways complimentary to each other, as reflected in their respective articles of association, and the evidence of AA. But this close association does not lead to the conclusion that in this transaction, the Leagues Club acted as agent for the Football Club. Even the fact that all scholarship applicants were awarded scholarships by the Leagues Club does not, in this application, satisfy me that the exercise of discretion by the Leagues Club to grant the scholarship was anything less than an independent act.
  15. I therefore find that the payments were received by the applicant without ‘... condition that the [applicant] will (or will if required) render, or continue to render, services to ...” the Leagues Club, or anyone else.
  16. There are several ancillary matters I wish to address. Firstly, given the amounts of money involved in the scholarship scheme, I am surprised at the ad hoc nature of the guidelines. AA stated that these were well known by persons involved, but I think that they should be formalised in some way other than a presumption that those who administer the scheme at the Leagues Club are familiar with its parameters.
  17. I would also stress that this application turns very much on its particular facts. My decision may well have been different if, for example, the Football Club had, at the time of the grant of scholarship, directorial control of the Leagues Club, or the applicant had not been properly enrolled in his University Course, or had shown a total disregard to his studies, or the scholarship was conditional upon the applicant continuing to play football for the Football Club.
  18. I therefore set aside the objection decision under review, and find that the scholarship payments in the year of income under review were exempt income pursuant to subsection 23(z) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, and therefore not assessable income.
