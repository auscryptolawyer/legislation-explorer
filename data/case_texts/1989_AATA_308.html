Last Updated: 4 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Applicant claimed expenses incurred in maintaining his expertise as a Marine Biologist. Amount of income obtained from this activity small in relation to the totality of the Applicant's income nontheless a business activity.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Sections 6(1), 51(1)

John v The Commissioner of Taxation (unreported No- FC89/005, 8 February 1989)

Case P67 82ATC 317, and

Case N30 1981ATC 163, followed;

Case P18 82ATC 77 distinguished.

Applicant

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. NT88/998

AAT Decision No 5018

Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member)

15 February 1989

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/998

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Before : MR M.D. ALLEN (SENIOR MEMBER)

Date : 15 FEBRUARY 1989

Place : SYDNEY

The Tribunal decides that:

(1) the objection decision is set aside.

(2) the matter is remitted to the Respondent for reassessment of the Applicant's income having regard to the finding that the sum of $2175-00 was an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing assessable income.

(3) liberty to apply is reserved to both parties generally.

(Sgn) M.D. ALLEN

Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/99B

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

15 February 1989 Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member)

At the conclusion of the hearing of this application for review Mr M.D. Allen, the Senior Member who presided, stated orally the terms of the decision intended to be made and the reasons therefore. After service upon the Respondent of a copy of the decision that was in fact made, the Respondent pursuant to Sub-section 43(2)A of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 requested the Tribunal to furnish to the Respondent a statement in writing of the reasons of the Tribunal for its decision. Those reasons are now restated accordingly, and will be furnished to the Applicant and to the Respondent.

  1. The Applicant is a Marine Biologist with an international reputation regarding whales, porpoises and dolphins.
  2. In his return for the tax year ended 1985 he claimed an amount of $2175 expenses which claim was rejected by the Respondent.
  3. In the original Notice of Objection attention was focused on expenses incurred as a result of a consultancy undertaken by the Applicant for the Australian Parks & Wildlife service, being the production of a bibliography on Whaling In Australia and New Zealand.
  4. As the evidence revealed, that consultancy was not a consultancy as that term is understood commercially although there was appropriated to the Applicant in the budget drawn up pursuant to the consultancy agreement a small amount of money nominated as "consultant's fee and incidental expenses". The evidence further revealed that that nominated sum was subsumed by the expenses of the project.
  5. As stated above, the Applicant has an international reputation in his field and is frequently consulted by Governments both Federal and State together with private bodies. He is an honorary consultant to the Australian Museum and is invited to overseas seminars plus being the author of numerous scientific papers and some popular articles including chapters in a large coffee-table type work, on whales and their habitat.
  6. The Applicant has received payment for the articles he writes in the more general and popular publications, for example Australian Natural History, plus separate payment for photographs. As I understand the evidence, the larger colour plate work referred to above is his most financially rewarding task to date as payment is not received for articles in scientific journals, nor when invited to overseas seminars does he receive payment as opposed to fares and expenses.
  7. To maintain his expertise in his subject, the Applicant is forced to incur expenses. He attends whale strandings where occurring, he must subscribe to scientific journals and maintain equipment, for example camera and taperecorder. He also is required to make telephone calls overseas and to entertain visiting experts or workers in his field.
  8. The basic question is therefore whether in the tax year in question it can be said that the Applicant was engaged in a business as that term is defined in Sub-section 6(1) of the Income Assessment Act 1936(as amended), and whether the expenses claimed by the Applicant were losses or outgoings deductable under Sub-section 51(1) of the Act.
  9. A loss or outgoing is deductable under Sub-section 51(1) of the Act only if it was:
    • (a) incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or
    • (b) necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income.
  10. It is essential to the taxpayer's case to be satisfied as a preliminary point that he was in fact carrying on a business. The business could best be described as that of a Consultant Marine Biologist.
  11. The test for what constitutes a business has been dealt with by the courts in several cases. To my mind if one substitutes the concept of business for the term "trader" in the following passage occurring at page 10 of the unreported Reasons for Decision of the Chief Justice and Justices Wilson, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron in John v The Commissioner of Taxation (unreported No. FC89/005, 8 February 1989} the test is succinctly set forth, namely

"whether or not a person is a trader seems to us to be a question of fact, albeit that in some cases the determination of that fact may depend on questions of impression and degree".

  1. The Court went on to discuss the situation where there was no discernable trading pattern. In this case I can discern a pattern and indeed a continual activity in a specialised area in the field of marine biology albeit that that activity did not during the year of income, nor indeed to date, result in any great remuneration.
  2. Miss Lee for the Respondent pointed specifically to the lack of remuneration achieved by the Applicant, and submitted that the consultancy work was not his major source of income and indeed was often done in an honorary capacity.
  3. However it is not necessary to be engaged in a business to demonstrate that a profit has been received. As the High Court said in John's case supra at page 6 of the unreported Reasons for Decision:

"Although the first limb of s.51(1) speaks of a loss or outgoing 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income', a loss or outgoing may be so incurred notwithstanding that no income has been gained or produced in the period in which the loss or outgoing is claimed to be deductable. The test of deductability under that limb, as laid down in Ronpibon Tin (Ronpibon Tin N.L. and Tongkah Compound N.L. v FCT (1949) 78CLR47), is that 'it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income'".

  1. It was submitted that the Applicant's activities constituted no more than the vigorous pursuit of a pastime or hobby which did not amount to the carrying on of a business. To my mind the case of P18, 82ATC 77 distinguishable as in the present matter the whole of the Applicant's working activities are devoted to marine biology. He does have other income that is true but that income is not produced by personal exertion.
  2. If one seeks analogy this case is, to my mind, more akin to that of P67 B2ATC 317. At paragraph 16 the Board said

"Quite apart from the benefit that the taxpayer gained from his photographic and allied activities in relation to his profession as a medical practitioner, we regard those activities as having sufficient elements of continuity, organisation and repetition to warrant the description of 'a business'. The taxpayer's evidence indicated an object of profit making, even though the returns in the years under review were small, due no doubt in part to the apparently limited market for the particular products and the seeming difficulty of breaking into it. It may be said that the taxpayer's activities, at least initially, were not organised in a business-efficient manner, but this can be attributed to the fact that he was trying to enter what to him was a new and untested market. All in all, the evidence satisfies us that the taxpayer's activities in the photographic and related fields went further than the vigorous pursuit of a hobby. On this ground alone, therefore, his claim to deduct the Section 51(1) outgoings - that is, all those claimed apart from depreciation and repairs - should be allowed in full under the second limb of that section. And as an alternative, we think they are deductable under the first limb. He do not accept the submission on behalf of the Commissioner that the sums returned as income were not income, or perhaps not assessable income, but were merely contributions towards expenses. On the contrary, we regard them as assessable income, and the relevant outgoings were, in our view, incurred in or in the course of gaining that income. If it be said that the two amounts, income and outgoings, are altogether disproportionate one to the other, reference should be made to what was said in the joint judgement of members of the High Court in Ronpibon Tin N.L. v P.C. of T.(1948-49) [1949] HCA 15; 78 CLR 47 at p.60 'it is not for Court or the Commissioner (or, one might add, a Board of Review) to say how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income, but only how much he has spent;.....

The disproportion may be grounds for questioning the soundness of the taxpayer's business judgement, but it is not by itself a ground for saying that the outgoings are therefore nondeductable."

  1. See also case N30 1981 ATC163. In that case the taxpayer held as a full-time job a schoolmaster's position but as well spent long hours over many years in research for and writing poetry, and contributing articles to book reviews and to the press, as well as activities concerned with the publication and sale of his books. It would appear that the publication of the taxpayer's books was subsidised as to one third by the Government and he had to meet the other costs. He claimed for these costs as well as the costs of book launching, stationery, telephone, vehicle running costs, accounting fees and depreciation on a typewriter, reference books, library and records. No. 2 Board held his activities were considerable, organised and systematic and amounted to the carrying on of the business. His claims were therefore allowed.
  2. Rather surprisingly the Applicant's tax agent submitted that the Applicant did not in the year of income have a present business but expenditure was incurred with regard to future income. Whereas John's case supra and the passage I quoted from page 6 thereof would cover that submission for the reasons I have adverted to above, I do consider that the Applicant did in fact conduct a business.
  3. That business was small if purely remunerative aspects are taken into account, but it is clear that the Applicant is a poor businessman and, with respect, naive as to what his expertise is worth. However as was said by Walsh J. in Thomas v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4094 at 4099

"A man may carry on a business although he does so in a small way."

and added at page 4100

"I have given close consideration to the criticisms that were advanced as to the lack of business efficiency with which the appellant conducted the operations. But in the circumstances I have not found that this should lead to a conclusion that it was not a business adventure and ...."

  1. I am of a like mind in this matter.
  2. The objection decision is to be set aside and the matter remitted to the Respondent for reassessment of the Applicant's income tax having regard to the findings expressed above, namely that the sum of $2175 was an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. I give liberty to apply.
