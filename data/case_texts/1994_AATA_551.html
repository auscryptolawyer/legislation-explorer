INCOME TAX - Deductible Loss - share transactions - whether incurred in producing assessable income - intention to make profit - whether sharetrader



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : Ss.51(1), 52



FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199; 18 ATR 693; 87 ATC 4363

These are applications for review by the applicant taxpayer ("T") against two objection decisions dated 18 May, 1993. In respect of the income tax return for the year of income ended 30 June 1989, the respondent disallowed losses claimed by T under section 52 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). In the income tax return for the year of income ended 30 June 1990, T claimed a deduction for losses suffered as a sharetrader under sub-section 51(1) of the Act which was disallowed by the respondent. Separate appeals were lodged in respect of each of the objection decisions but the appeals were heard together because of the similarity of issues in each application.



2. The central issue in review of the objection decisions is whether in the relevant years T was a sharetrader or had acquired shares in a company for the purpose of selling those shares at a profit, thus allowing him to claim losses made on share transactions as being on revenue rather than capital account, either under sub-section 51(1) or section 52 of the Act.



3. Section 52 of the Act allows as a deduction for a loss incurred on property acquired before 20 September 1985 for the purpose of profit-making by sale. Section 52 reads as follows:



"52(1) Any loss incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income upon the sale of any property or from the carrying on or carrying out of any undertaking or scheme, the profit (if any) from which sale, undertaking or scheme would have been included in his assessable income, shall be an allowable deduction."



4. This provision comes with a proviso which directs that in respect of property acquired after 31 December 1941, no deduction is allowable under section 52 unless the taxpayer notifies the Commissioner that the property was being acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale or for the carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme no later than the date on which the taxpayer furnishes his first return after acquiring the property. There is a discretion vesting in the Commissioner to allow the deduction despite lack of notice if satisfied that the property was acquired by the taxpayer for the purpose of profit-making by sale or for the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme.



5. Sub-section 51(1) of the Act allows deductions for losses and outgoings in two situations. Firstly to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of the taxpayer and secondly to the extent to which they are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income. The sub-section excludes deductions for losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature.



6. The applicant was represented by his accountant, Mr Walsh, and Mr McGill of counsel appeared for the respondent. The Tribunal had before it documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as well as certain additional documents admitted by consent and marked as exhibits. The Tribunal also had the benefit of oral evidence from the applicant and an officer of the Australian Taxation Office.



7. The applicant and his family emigrated to Australia in 1982. From that time until August 1987, T was employed as a real estate consultant to C Corporation. The Tribunal heard evidence that this position involved the management of property development projects for which T was paid an annual retainer plus the occasional bonus. After August 1987, at which time T left C Corporation, the taxpayer continued with this profession, working for a number of different companies, and is currently engaged in independent real estate consultancy work.



8. In both the relevant returns, those for the 1989 and the 1990 financial years, T listed his occupation as real estate investment adviser. The Tribunal is satisfied, and so finds, that at all relevant times T's occupation was as a real estate investment adviser.

40. There are two separate sets of acquisitions to look at because of the limited operation of section 52. Those shares acquired before 20 September 1985 and those acquired after that date.



Prior 20 September 1985



41. In respect of the deduction claimed by T in his 1989 return for loss suffered on the sale of 7,500 C Corporation shares, there is no evidence before the Tribunal that this transaction ever took place. There is no mention of this acquisition in any of the later correspondence before the Tribunal, some of which purports to be exhaustive of T's share transactions. The Tribunal is satisfied, and so finds, that this transaction did not take place.



42. The Tribunal had evidence before it of only two parcels of shares being acquired by T prior to September 1985. T spent a total of $32,829 on the acquisition of 19,500 shares. 3,500 of these were sold for $21,891.80, a price per share of $6.40. Considering that these shares would have been purchased at a price of either $1.00 per share or $3.00 per share, the Tribunal is unable to determine which, it is hard to see how a loss could have been made on this transaction and the Tribunal finds that no such loss was suffered. In fact, as pointed out above, the profit on this transaction should have been $11,391.80 at the lowest, and, should the applicant's submissions be accepted by the Tribunal, that profit would be expected to be included in the return for the year ended 30 June, 1986.



43. When questioned as to why no section 52 declarations were lodged in respect of these purchases, it was explained that T believed he would derive substantial profits by selling the shares and therefore thought that it was unnecessary to notify the Commissioner. It is the Tribunal's view that this is an erroneous interpretation of the way section 52 operates and the requirement of notification to the Commissioner of Taxation contained therein.



44. Furthermore, T's entire residual holding of J Holdings shares was exchanged for C Corporation shares in March 1986. That is, the holding of 13,000 J Holdings shares acquired prior to September 1985 were effectively sold in exchange for 26,000 C Corporation shares. There can be no finding of a loss in respect to this transaction as the market price per share of C Corporation shares at the time was $8.50. As pointed out by counsel for the respondent, this equates to a substantial paper profit on those particular shares, even though that profit was never actually realised. As no loss was suffered by the taxpayer in respect of the shares purchased prior to September 1985, any question as to deductibility under section 52 is simply irrelevant.



Post 20 September 1985



45. The two limbs of sub-section 51(1) are in themselves straight forward. It is in their application to particular factual circumstances that difficulties arise. For a deduction to be allowable under the first limb of sub-section 51(1), the question to be answered is whether the losses were incurred for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. Under the second limb, the taxpayer must be carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, and must have necessarily incurred a loss in the carrying on of that business.



46. In determining whether T was carrying on business, presumably as a sharetrader, the Tribunal's attention was drawn to the decision of Deputy President Gerber in T and FCT (Decision No 9242). The principal issue in that case was whether the taxpayer was a sharetrader compared with an investor and therefore whether the shares held by the taxpayer should be regarded as trading stock. There the Tribunal made it clear, relying on John v FCT (1989) 166 CLR, that it was a question of fact whether a person was a trader or not.



47. The applicant's representative sought to rely on the reasoning of the Full Court of the High Court in FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199; 18 ATR 693; 87 ATC 4363 to establish that a taxpayer entering into an isolated transaction could be said to be carrying on a business. Further, he referred the Tribunal to Taxation Rulings ("TR") 92/3 and 92/4 in which the Commissioner for Taxation interpreted his view of the application of the Myer case to sub-section 25(1) and sub section 51(1) of the Act respectively. Taxation Rulings are not binding on this Tribunal. If, however, the applicant wishes to rely on the arguments set out by the Commissioner the Taxation Ruling may be considered by the Tribunal.



48. TR 92/3, in paragraph 6, relies on the reasoning in FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199 at 213; [1987] HCA 18; 18 ATR 693 at 699-700; 87 ATC 4363 at 4369 for the proposition that profit from an isolated transaction is generally assesssable income when both of the following elements are present:



(a) the intention or purpose of the taxpayer in entering into the transaction was to make a profit or gain; and

(b) the transaction was entered into, and the profit was made, in the course of carrying on a business or in carrying out a business operation or commercial transaction.



49. That ruling also sets out that the relevant intention or purpose of the taxpayer is the taxpayer's intention or purpose discerned from an objective consideration of the facts and circumstances of the case. Further TR 92/4 opines that an isolated transaction which results in a loss may fall within the first limb of sub-section 51(1) if, in entering that transaction, the taxpayer intended or expected to derive a profit which would have been assessable income (ie within sub-section 25(1)). It is argued that the second limb is no wider with respect to isolated transactions.



50. Therefore, it was submitted by Mr Walsh that there were two ways in which the losses suffered by the taxpayer could be brought within section 51 as a deduction. The activities of the taxpayer in relation to the C Corporation shares could either be described as a sharetrading business when looked at as part of a whole or, if looked at in isolation, they could be described in terms of a commercial transaction entered into with the intention of profit-making.



51. As pointed out by counsel for the respondent on a number of occasions, the applicant failed to realise any of the substantial paper profit which was made almost immediately, despite having several opportunities to do so. The applicant's reasoning for not selling at the time when a substantial profit could have been made was that he thought those shares which he did not sell had "potential to go better" (Transcript p27). The decision on whether to sell the shares would depend on his assessment at any given time of the future prospects of the company and he would stay in a company as long as it was a good investment.



52. In the 1986 financial year T disclosed a section 26AAA profit on C Corporation shares of $26,425. Further, during that year T's holdings in C Corporation, Jupiters, CSR and Petro Energy were all sold. The explanation given for the sale, both in the return itself and during cross-examination of T, was that the capital was required for the development of a rental property through E Pty Ltd, a company owned by T. The applicant made it clear in his evidence that just enough was sold at this time to meet the specific purpose and no more. All the shares with the exception of the C Corporation shares had been held for more than 12 months. Rights offered during the year were apparently not taken up during the year but were sold.



53. Dividend income was declared in two income tax returns before the Tribunal. In the 1987 return (Exhibit 3) two separate amounts of $9,442 and $1,319 were disclosed in respect of C Corporation shares, in particular, in a total of $11,932, and in the 1989 return (T2), an amount of $1,218 was disclosed. The submission from T's representative was that these amounts were so small when compared to the cost of the acquisition of the shares producing the income, that they went to show that the taxpayer's intention must have been to make profit by sale rather than invest in income producing shares. Although this may be a reasonable inference in some circumstances, the applicant's close association with C Corporation and its dealings leads the Tribunal to believe there was something more behind the acquisition of those shares. Speculation as to what that motivation was is irrelevant here. What is relevant is that the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant did not acquire the shares with the purpose of profit making by sale. In support of this finding is the statement in the applicant's return for the year of income ended 30 June 1987 (Exhibit 3) which claimed as an expense borrowing charges on funds borrowed to finance the purchase of shares. In the explanatory notes attached to that return it is stated that the shares where purchased "in order to provide dividend income". This statement is repeated in the 1988 return. T, in cross-examination, claimed that this explanation had been included erroneously but, as submitted by Mr McGill for the respondent, its inclusion was probably a reflection of his instructions to his accountant at the time. I think the latter is the more likely explanation and I so find.



54. The Tribunal is satisfied that at no time, until the possibility of the deduction of the loss in 1990, did the applicant consider himself to be a sharetrader in relation to the C Corporation shares. There was certainly no reflection in his attitude of a sharetrader. All his actions reflect the purpose of a long-term investor. This is made clear by the following questions and answers by the applicant in cross-examination by Mr McGill at pages 27-28 of the transcript:

55. T's representative also sought to rely on the judgment in Myer (supra) as authority for the proposition that an isolated transaction could be seen as constituting a business. The applicant's reliance on the decision in Myer is, with respect, somewhat misguided. There the transaction in question, although isolated in type, was held by the Full Court of the High Court to be in the course of the taxpayer's business, the distinction was merely that it was not in the ordinary course of the taxpayer's business. That is, the essential element there was that the taxpayer was carrying on a business. According to the evidence presented to the Tribunal, T's occupation is that of a property consultant. Any suggestion that T was carrying on a business of sharetrading has not been established. As pointed out in the hearing of this matter, if T had been carrying on the business of trading in shares, details of that business should have appeared in each annual income tax return lodged by him. Further, the circumstances as set out in these reasons, surrounding the acquisitions do no lend themselves to such a conclusion.



56. It was submitted by counsel for the respondent that the purpose of profit making by sale in itself was not sufficient to bring a transaction within section 51, there must also be some business operation or commercial transaction. Further counsel submitted that it is not enough to say that because shares have a certain commercial flavour to them, then any acquisition of shares is a commercial transaction for the purposes of the Myer case. The Tribunal accepts and adopts these submissions.



57. Expenses listed in the 1989 return included borrowing expenses of $858 on a loan established in August 1987 to finance the purchase of shares. The expenses on this loan amounted to $4,290 and were spread over a 6 year period. In the same year, a capital loss was claimed in a Schedule G attached to the income tax return. This loss of $45,682 was incurred on the disposal of particular shares. On C Corporation shares a loss of $48,930 was claimed and offset against the gain made on the disposal of IEL and Power Brewing shares. The C Corporation shares were claimed to be acquired on 18 July 1986 for $54,085 and sold 5 April 1989 for $5,155. This disposal figure corresponds with the market price of that date as set out in the price schedule (Exhibit 7). By later correspondence (T18), T claimed that the inclusion of the total loss of $45,682 in the Schedule G was an error and that the loss should properly have been included at Item 10 as part of the net income (loss) and therefore deductible under section 52. This matter was apparently raised in the letter from the respondent Commissioner dated 19 December 1990 in answer to T's request for a Section 169A ruling which was attached to the 1990 return. The respondent sought an explanation as to why the shares in question were listed in the 1989 return as capital assets and in the 1990 return they appeared as the stock of a sharetrader.



58. Counsel for the respondent invited the Tribunal to draw an inference from the funding arrangements of the taxpayer in respect of these share purchases. However, I am satisfied on the facts of this case, that source of the funds can only be neutral in determining the character of the transactions in this case.



59. The claim for the deduction of the loss claimed to be suffered by T on the sale of the shares in question is not allowable in terms of sections 51 and 52 of the Act. For these reasons, the objection decisions under review will be affirmed.
