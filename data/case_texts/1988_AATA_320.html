VT86/10266 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 320; (1988) 19 ATR 4077; 88 ATC 1051 (4 November 1988)

Last Updated: 6 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income tax – Allowable deduction - Tax avoidance - Loss on disposal of depreciable assets of partnership - Arrangement designed to generate loss - Whether arrangement void against the Commissioner pursuant to s.260 - Scope of the "choice principle" Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1978, SS. 54, 59, 59AA, 60, 61, 62, 260.

Newton v FC of T [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1

Gulland, Watson & Pincus v FC of T [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55

Case U117 87 ATC 700

Case V62 88 ATC 467

Case V33 88 ATC 288

(Sgd) P. Gerber DEPUTY PRESIDENT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

REASONS FOR DECISION

Tribunal: Dr P. Gerber (Deputy President)

Date: 4 November 1988

1． The applicants in this case, husband and wife ("H" and "W") have been farming in partnership since 1966. On July 1, 1977, a unit trust was established of which the trustee was H & W Pty Ltd, a company incorporated on 11 March, 1977 with the object of holding as trustee "real or personal property of any kind". The unit trust consisted of ten units, five units were held by H Pty Ltd and the remaining five units by W Pty Ltd, being two family trusts the specified beneficiaries of which were the children of the marriage. H and W were included in the class of general beneficiaries.

2. On 13 July 1977, the farming partnership was varied by deed, and a new partner - H & W Pty Ltd in its capacity as trustee of the H & W Unit Trust - was admitted as a partner. The deed states that H and W have agreed to admit the new partner as to a twenty-five per cent interest in the business. The arrangement was designed for the purpose of transferring the depreciable assets from the "old" to the "new" partnership for $355, an arbitrary amount which, it was conceded, bore no relation to the real or written down value of the transferred assets, valued at the hearing at $52,809. It was argued that s.59AAof the Tax Act as it then stood, enabled the parties to nominate their own value, provided it corresponded to "the amount specified in the agreement". The difference - $52,454 - was claimed as a deduction in the partnership return and resulted in H and W each returning losses in the year now under review. After the "new" partnership was in place, it continued to carry on as before.

3. It will be seen that the change in the composition of the partnership had two consequences: it enabled the "old" partnership to deduct from its return the difference between the closing written down value of its assets and the amount specified in the agreement; it also enabled H and W to "split" the farming income as to 25%, which could henceforth be distributed to the three children of the marriage via the trust. Since neither side relied on the latter consequence of the arrangement as having any bearing on the outcome of this application, I do not propose to decide whether that aspect can pass the "predication" test enunciated by Lord Denning in Newton v. FC of T [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1 at p.8, as explained by the High Court in Gulland et al [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55. The only issue argued before me is whether the so-called "choice principle" permits the deduction of the difference between the closing down value and "the amount specified in the agreement".

4. Section 59AA (as it then stood) provides:

"Where, for any reason, including -

(a) the formation or dissolution of a partnership, or

(b) variation in the constitution of a partnership, or in the interests of the partners,

a change has occurred in the ownership of, or in the interests of persons in, property in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable under this Act or the previous Act, and the person, or one or more of the persons, who owned the property before the change has or have an interest in the property after the change, the provisions of this Act relating to depreciation apply as if the person or persons who owned the property before the change had, on the day on which the change occurred, disposed of the whole of the property to the person, or all the persons, by whom the property is owned after the change for a consideration equal to the amount specified in the agreement in consequence of which the change occurred as the value of the property for the purposes of that agreement, or, if there is no such agreement or no amount is so specified, an amount determined by the Commissioner."

It will be seen from a reading of the section that, on one view, it is open to any partnership in which a change of ownership or interest occurs, to elect to dispose of the assets for an amount "equal to the amount specified in the agreement". The Commissioner's power to determine the value of the assets operates only in circumstances where the parties have themselves failed to specify a value to the assets. In the instant case, the Commissioner has, in effect, substituted his own valuation by denying the legal efficacy of what the parties have done. This is sought to be justified by recourse to s.260 of the Tax Act.

5. Before embarking on an examination of the law, I make the following finding of fact. Despite some dark mutterings about "probate", I am satisfied that the introduction of the trustee of the unit trust as a partner was done for the sole and exclusive purpose of tax minimization; a fact which not only emerges from the correspondence involving the solicitors, Messrs Herbert Geer & Rundle, but from the cross-examination of H and W. Thus H was asked by Mr Maxwell, of counsel for the respondent:

Q Mr Whiting (accountant) told you, did he not, that an arrangement could be made whereby, on the alteration of the partnership, you and your wife could obtain a substantial tax deduction, but without otherwise affecting the conduct of your business? I do not think you could describe it as 'substantial'.

Q A tax deduction? --- A tax deduction, but not 'substantial'.

Q But that is how it was put to you, that this was a way of reducing your tax for that year? --- My first concern was probate in forming the trust.

In case the matter goes on appeal, I would merely add that whilst neither H nor W were familiar with the logistics of the whole arrangement, they were keenly aware that - whatever was done it would result in substantial tax advantages in the year now under review. This much emerges both from the oral evidence, from the terms of the arrangement itself, and from the overt acts by which it was carried into effect. The fact that the reconstituted partnership was also able to "split" income was incidental to the arrangement; a kind of "bonus".

6. I now turn to the law and the effect of s.260 on this arrangement. The case was predominantly argued on the application of that section, (with "sham" and "fiscal nullity" thrown in for good measure), so that yet another analysis of this section is required, if only because there is so little unanimity in this Tribunal on just what Gulland et al decided; see for example Case U117 87 ATC 700, (Davies P, Deputy President Breen and Senior Member Beddoe) and Case V62 88 ATC 467, a decision of Senior Member Roach (on appeal), which both stand in strong contrast to the views I expressed in two decisions; Case V33 B8 ATC 288 and Case V134 88ATC 852. That the problem is not simple is evidenced by the fact that neither in Gulland et al, nor in the decisions of the Federal Court which purported to follow these landmark cases, were the courts unanimous in their approach or conclusion. In short, no clear exegesis has, as yet, emerged; of Donebus Pty Ltd v. FC of T 88 ATC 4582 and Stamp v. FC of T 88 ATC 4803. In this state of uncertainty in the law, I am thus compelled to attempt yet another reconciliation. I therefore propose to set down a number of axioms in much the same way as Euclid sought to "prove" his theorems.

(a) "Section 260 is a difficult provision, inherited from earlier legislation, and long overdue for reform by someone who will take the trouble to analyse his ideas and define his intentions with precision before putting pen to paper." (per Kitto J; FC of T v. Newton [1957] HCA 99; (1957) 96 CLR 577, 596. If Part IVA is in response to the above cri de coeur (as to which I make no comment), his Honour's message seems to have finally reached its intended destination.

(b) It is impossible to reconcile all the judicial pronouncements on this section.

(c) Pared down, the section has produced two streams of interpretation which are parallel in the sense that they never meet. Any attempt to join them up is best left to the professors of jurisprudence. In the real world, this defies the laws of geometry.

(d) Maxims of construction, translated into Latin, tend to obscure rather than clarify legislative intent, of which that well known aid to navigation generalia specialibus non derogant is a good example.

(e) Borrowing from the language of Lord Atkin, there must be, and is, some general conception of the purpose of anti-avoidance legislation of which the particular cases found in the books are but instances.

(f) When these cases are studied, what emerges is that the courts have applied a litmus test to determine an acceptable pH. What is acceptable; more specifically, what scheme or arrangement turns to an acceptable colour is one of general impression, or, as Dawson J put it in Gulland et al; "It is a matter of construing the Act as a whole to determine where the incidence of tax was intended to fall. Only by this means might it be discovered whether or not a particular transaction falls within s.260." (p. 107)

(g) The majority decisions in Gulland et al are wider than strictly necessary. The rest, as they say in Latin, is obiter dicta, albeit dicta from a source so august that one ignores it at one's peril.

(h) Applying strict rules relating to the determination of the res decisa of Gulland et al, (the various judgements have been analysed in detail by Davies, Wilcox and Pincus JJ in Donebus; it would be impertinent of me if I were to attempt to do repeat an analysis already undertaken by these distinguished judges), the decison comes to this: S.260 does not, for tax purposes, permit income derived from personal activities to be taxed as though it had been derived by a trust.

7. If the above analysis was the law immediately post-Gulland, has it been extended by later decisions of the Federal Court? In Donebus Pty Ltd v. FC of T 88 ATC

4582, the facts, taken, from the headnote, were as follows:

"The directors and major shareholders of the taxpayer company were husband and wife. Early in 1979 the husband became concerned with the potential tax liability on that year's earnings, which were in excess of $500,000. On 23 April 1979, on the advice of its accountant and for the avowed purpose of tax minimistion, the taxpayer purchased from the wife for $100,000 a domestic refrigerator worth $30. The purchase price was immediately loaned back to the taxpayer for an interest free term of 32 years. These transactions were evidenced by an invoice, a loan agreement and a bill of exchange endorsed by the wife in favour of the taxpayer. The next day, the taxpayer purported to lease or bail the refrigerator 'to another company associated with its accountant for an annual rental of $10. However, the refrigerator at no stage left the taxpayer's possession and was used by the taxpayer's employees to store their lunches. On 25 June 1979 the taxpayer sold the refrigerator to the husband for $100 and the next day received the first instalment of rental from the accountant's company."

Davies J., consistent with the view expressed in Case U117 delivered a minority view in relation to s.260. Of the majority, Wilcox J defined the problem in these terms:

"The main problem about s.260 has been to define the limits to which the choice principle is subject. Of particular importance in the present case is the question whether the choice principle is confined to transactions which have some basis in commercial reality or in family dealing, or whether it extends to any transaction, however bizarre and devoid of non-tax justification, which purports to come within the literal wording of one of the special provisions in the Act." (p.4591).

After analyzing the various judgements in Gulland et al with his Honour's customary thoroughness, Wilcox J concluded that one aspect which runs throughout the decisions of the majority, when read in historic context, is that arrangements which are contrived or "nonsensical in a business sense" are in danger of being "caught" by s.260 even where the transaction can be brought within the literal application of one or more specific provision(s) of the Act, as in Donebus, where heavy reliance was placed on ss.54 and 59. Whilst not conclusive, the fact that an arrangement is contrived or nonsensical in a business sense may constitute a relevant circumstance which must be considered. The following extract from the decision of the learned judge deserves to he set out at length:

"The critical significance of artificiality and lack of business purpose is expressed more clearly in the judgement of Dawson J (in Gulland) than it is in the judgements of Gibbs CJ and Brennan J. Nonetheless, it seems to me that each of their Honours adopted similar reasoning. I have already referred to the fact that Gibbs CJ resurrected, at least in a qualified manner, the Newton test; a test which - as Deane J showed at pp. 92-93 - might have been thought to have been buried by Mullens and Cridland. The resurrection seems to me to be significant; the whole point of the Newton test was that it required the question to be asked; whether 'the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax'. It is true, as I have said, that Gibbs CJ added a qualification relating to cases in which the taxpayer does 'no more than take advantage of an opportunity to reduce tax which the Act itself provides'. But, read in their context, these words must be taken to refer to a situation which is fairly contemplated by the Act, a transaction which makes commercial or familial sense; even though the form it takes may be dictated by a desire to take advantage of an option conferred upon taxpayers by the Act.

The test adopted by Brennan J leads to a similar result. His Honour read down s.260 only to the extent necessary 'to allow other and specific provisions of the Act to have their intended operation'. Once again one must consider whether the relevant transaction was one fairly contemplated by the specific provisions of the Act.

For the above reasons, it seems to me that the majority in the Tribunal were correct in reading Gulland as a decision 'to strike down circumstances and artificial arrangements the sole purpose of which is to avoid liability to tax, notwithstanding the choice principle'". (p.4594).

Wilcox J went on to quote with approval a Victorian Supreme Court decision -

"Mention should be made of a further case, not referred to by the Tribunal: McLean v. FC of T 86 ATC 4288. In that case a taxpayer claimed deductions pursuant to ss.36 and 36A of the Act in connection with share trading losses. Tadgell J, of the Victorian Supreme Court, held that the relevant transaction was void against the Commissioner pursuant to s.260. In so doing, his Honour referred to Gulland and went on, at p.4309:

As will now be evident, I regard the arrangements for the acquisition and disposal by Tactown of the unlisted shares as having had stamped on their face an indication that their sole purpose was to avoid tax; and a purported tax avoidance was their undoubted effect. Consistently with the cases in Gulland, Watson and Pincus, I conclude that any application of sec. 36A, 36, 28 and 51 of the Act to the dealings by the partnership in the unlisted shares is irreconcilable with the existence and purpose of sec .260. I cannot accept the submission that any of those provisions, properly construed, allows a taxpayer the opportunity to treat dealings dependent on these arrangements as though they give rise to deductible trading losses ... The requisite deeming relied on an impermissible use, amounting to an abuse, of sec. 36A and 36.' (p.4309).

It seems to me that the reference to an 'abuse' of specific provisions of the Act neatly summarizes the modern approach to the application of the choice principle to s.260, as explained in Gulland and as illustrated by both Oakey Abattoir and McLean. The word 'abuse' is an apt description of the present applicant's attempt to earn a deduction under ss.54 and 59 of $99,900 in connection with its refrigerator." (p.4595).

8. Pincus J came to much the same conclusion. Thus his Honour noted that "where business transactions make no conceivable commercial sense and have, if not caught by s.260, a considerable tax effect, they must prima facie be within that provision's scope." (p.4596). Dealing with the "choice" principle, Pincus J noted:

"It cannot be enough to attract the operation of the choice principle that, however contrived and artificial the scheme, one can point to sections in the Act which comprehensively govern the arrangements made if these arrangements are not struck down." (p.4597).

9. Davies J (diss.) observed (obiter):

"The present scheme as originally proposed was not particularly complex or circuitous and was artificial only in the sense that it was not at arms-length, was not based upon commercial reality and had no object over the achievement of a deduction under s.59. Those matters are not necessarily sufficient of themselves to attract the application of s.260 to transactions designed to achieve one of the deductions provided specifically by the Act. As Dawson J said, in Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland; Watson v. Commissioner of Taxation; and Pincus v. Commissioner of Taxation at p. 110:

'Section 260 is not explicitly directed at contrived arrangements or arrangements which are out of the ordinary, but the fact that an arrangement is of that nature is a circumstance which, when the purpose or effect of the arrangement is to do any of those things which are referred to in para. (a) to (d), may indicate that as a matter of construction the arrangement is one for the avoidance of tax to which s.260 is intended to apply.'" (pp. 4588-9).

Later, his Honour stated:

"However, if the refrigerator was not used in the company's business for the purpose of producing assessable income, then the company would not be entitled to a deduction and there would be no room for the application of s.260 to any part of the events that occurred. And if the refrigerator was used by the company in part at least in its business for the purpose of producing assessable income, and a determination was made under s.61 as to a proper deduction having regard to circumstances of that use, it may be questioned whether s.260 would apply to override the deduction for which the Act makes specific provision. If it were found that a deduction was proper having regard to the terms of s.59 and s.61 and to the use of the refrigerator in the company's business for the purpose of producing assessable income, what contract, agreement or arrangement should be avoided by s.260?" (p. 4590).

The above proposition, although consistent with the decision in Case U117 (supra), in which Davies J presided, is, with great respect to that learned judge, wholly irreconcilable with the decisions of Wilcox and Pincus JJ. In Case U117, this Tribunal concluded (obiter):

"We are therefore of the view that sec. 260 of the Act has no application to the present case. If the provisions of sec. 54, 59 and 59AA of the Act are satisfied, then those sections provide a deduction in the circumstances which have eventuated. Section 260 does not override such express and detailed provisions of the Act and no antecedent transaction or situation was altered. That the applicant may deliberately have taken advantage of the provisions of the Act is of no consequence." (p. 704).

The Tribunal's decision, quoted above, is in my view no longer consonant with the later decision of the majority of the Federal court in Donebus (supra). For this reason, I do not propose to follow it.

10. In view of the complete analysis of the present law undertaken by the majority in Donebus, I will merely cite Stamp v. FC of T 88 ATC 4803, a decision on s.260 by Pincus J, merely noting that his Honour's reasoning is consistent with the view he and Wilcox J had expressed in the aformention decision.

11. This is sufficient to dispose of this application. For the sake of completeness, I merely mention the deficiencies in the documentation, on which much emphasis was placed at the hearing. To give one example, the following purports to be minutes of the Board of Directors of H & W Pty Ltd, attended only by H on an unidentified date in July 1977:

H & W PTY LTD

MINUTES of Meeting of the Board of Directors of the Company meeting as Trustee of the H & W Unit Trust held at 81 Carnation Ave, Footscray on the day of July 1977.

PRESENT: H (Chairman)

MINUTES; RESOLVED that the Minutes of the previous Meeting of the Company now produced and read, he and are hereby approved, recorded and ratified.

DEED OF

PARTNERSHIP: RESOLVED that the Company enter into a Deed of Partnership with H and W in the form of the Deed of Partnership tabled at the Meeting and FURTHER RESOLVED to affix the common seal of the Company thereto.

SECTION 36A

ELECTION: RESOLVED to join with H and W in giving a Notice to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation pursuant to sub-section 2 of Section 36A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-77 in respect of the property identified in a Form of Election tabled at the Meeting comprising the livestock of the partnership and FURTHER RESOLVED that the Company affix its common seal to the Form of Election.

PURCHASE OF

PLANT AND

EQUIPMENT: RESOLVED that the Company acquire sundry plant and equipment at the cost of $355.00 from H and W as partners as to 25% with H and W on the terms and conditions as set out in the Agreement for the Sale of Plant and Equipment and FURTHER RESOLVED to affix the common seal of the Company thereto.

CLOSURE: There being no further business, the Meeting was closed.

12. Since a director cannot, in the circumstances of this case, hold a meeting with himself, the respondent submitted that the whole arrangement was a nullity, so that no new partnership came into existence. Having perused the whole of the documentation, I am satisfied that the above "minute" is surplusage to the arrangement and did nothing which had not already been achieved by the various partnership agreements. There are other similar deficits which, since "discovery" was not achieved before the hearing, only showed up when I waded through a tonne of documents in an attempt to trace the chain of events for purposes of my decision. I have not, therefore, had the benefit of counsels' submissions on these deficits. One example will suffice. H & W Pty Ltd was incorporated on March 11, 1977. On the same day, the directors of the company resolved to accept appointment as trustee of the H & W Unit Trust. Since that trust did not come into existence until July 1, 1977, it is difficult to see what that particular meeting actually achieved. In short, the whole documentation is sloppy and bears the classic hallmarks of what one has come to associate with "quicky" tax minimisation arrangements, cobbled together at a time when the limits of s.260 were less well defined. Nevertheless, put end to end, and "blue pencilling" the unnecessary links in a poorly constructed chain, I am not prepared to uphold the submission that there was no effective dissolution of the "old" partnership and the creation of the "new". Nor am I prepared to find "sham" merely because, in the eloquent words of Mr Maxwell, "there was not an iota of change before and after these purported transactions were put through. The assets stayed where they were. Their ownership and control for practical purposes did not change. The individual partners continued to have control of the partnership. They were the directors of the company. They continued to run the business as they had done before. They continued to use the assets in the business and, as H conceded, an observer would not have thought that anything had changed." With great respect, that is a far cry from "sham". To the question: was there a new partnership? there can only be one answer: "yes". This arrangement therefore fails, not because there was no disposal of assets from one partnership to the other, but because the arrangement by which the depreciable assets of the "old" partnership were transferred to the "new" constitutes an abuse of s59AA and is void as against the Commissioner. I can do no better than to repeat what Pincus J observed in Donebus:

"Where business transactions make no conceivable commercial sense and have, if not caught by s.260, a considerable tax effect, they must prima facie be within that provision's scope .... It cannot be enough to attract the operation of the choice principle that, however contrived and artificial the scheme, one can point to sections in the Act which comprehensively govern the arrangements made if these arrangements are not struck down." (p.4596-7).

13. It is only in outer space that refrigerators and farm equipment can be thrown up and own in defiance of the laws of gravity and tax. On earth, they are subject to the laws of physics and commercial reality.

14. I would affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection in the case of H. It is common ground that the decision in W will abide the result.
