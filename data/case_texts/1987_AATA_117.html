Re An Applicant and Secretary, Tax Agents' Board of Victoria [1987] AATA 117 (2 April 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: AN APPLICANT

And: SECRETARY, TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF VICTORIA

No. V86/684

AAT No. 3502

Tax Agent

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Tax Agent - deregistration - whether neglected the business of a principal - delays and failure to lodge returns - delays in forwarding refunds - inaccessibility to clients - whether fit and proper person - causing taxpayers to sign incomplete returns - failure to lodge own returns in time - signing declarations that returns complete when not - neglect of clients - use of Taxation Department envelopes - unauthorised use of government photocopier - balancing of public interest against applicant's own private interest

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 251J, 251K

Hughes and Vale Pty. Ltd. v. New South Wales (No. 2) [1955] HCA 28; (1955) 93 CLR 127

Maxwell v. Dixon (1965) WAR 167

Re Su and Tax Agents' Board, South Australia 4 ALD N279

Drake v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60

Re Denton and Tax Agents' Board, South Australia 5 ALN N21

Re Chapman and Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 5 ALN N552

HEARING

CANBERRA

2:4:1987

ORDER

For reasons given orally at the hearing of this application the Tribunal decides that the decision under review be affirmed.

DECISION

At the conclusion of the hearing of this application for review, I stated orally the terms of the decision intended to be made and the reasons therefor. Subsequently, pursuant to section 43(2A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the respondent requested the Tribunal to furnish a statement in writing of the reasons of the Tribunal for its decision. Those reasons are now restated accordingly and will be furnished to the applicant and the respondent.

2. This application is made to the Tribunal under section 251K(5) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"); by virtue of the provisions of section 25 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 the Tribunal has power to hear and determine it. The decision under review was made by the Tax Agents' Board early in October 1986 and notified to the applicant by a letter dated 6 October 1986. The Board decided under section 251K(2) of the Act to cancel the applicant's registration as a tax agent.

3. The applicant was registered as a tax agent early in 1979 purusant to section 251J of the Act. At all material times he has resided in Bendigo and been employed full-time as a public officer, initially with the Rivers Commission, at present with the Department of Education. He is a professionally qualified accountant and an Associate Member of the Australian Society of Accountants. After he had obtained his registration as a tax agent, he at first undertook tax agent work on a small scale. In the income tax year which ended on 30 June 1981, he prepared 26 returns. In the following two years he handled 55 and 120 respectively. However, in 1984 there was a very substantial increase to 440; and in 1985 there was a further increase to 545.

4. Because of the increase in his tax agent practice he established an office; it was situated in Bendigo. He gave evidence that he did that in order to comply with the requirements of the Australian Society of Accountants. He leased office premises, initially for one year from 1 January 1984. The lease was renewable for one year; at the end of 1984 he renewed it for 1985.

5. In 1984 and 1985 he attended the office, and opened it to the public, on a full-time basis only between the middle of July and the middle of September. At other times the office was not open, nor did he attend it, during normal working hours on weekdays; but he attended and opened it in the evening and at weekends whenever he was able to do so. The reason why he did not attend or open the office during normal working hours on weekdays, except during the very limited period from mid-July to mid-September, was that he was in full-time employment as a public officer. Further, he was often not able to attend or open it in the evening or at weekends because his work, as an audit officer in the Department of Education, frequently took him away from Bendigo. Until early 1985 he had an after-hours telephone contact number of which apparently his clients were aware. However, after that, as was established by the evidence of witnesses in this proceeding, there were lengthy periods when they could not contact him.

6. He appears to have run his tax agent practice reasonably satisfactorily until about May 1985. That is to say, there is no record of adverse comments from officers of the Taxation Department or of complaints by his clients. However, from the middle of May 1985 onwards his practice can fairly be described as a disaster.

7. Until some time early in 1985 he resided with his wife; his after-hours telephone number was that of the telephone installed at that residence. Then he separated from his wife and moved into premises which he owned and which he had previously rented; he did not install a telephone there. His wife had the telephone number at her house changed and arranged that the new number would not be listed in the directory. His clients were, therefore, no longer able to contact him by telephone.

8. The situation was further exacerbated by his being without an office for a period early in 1986. When the lease of his office expired at the end of 1985, he continued to occupy it in the mistaken belief that he was entitled to have the lease renewed. In March 1986 he was served with a notice to quit and did so. He was then without an office until May 1986. During that period there was no effective way for his clients to contact him. Arrangements which he made for others to accept messages for him did not work. Clients who left messages found that he did not respond; evidence was before the Tribunal that often for long periods he did not collect messages from the persons who had accepted them for him.

9. In the course of this proceeding he agreed that he had taken on a volume of work well beyond his capacity. That is undoubtedly correct; even if his clients had been able to contact him by telephone after hours, he could not have coped satisfactorily with the load. His employment required that he travel often and extensively, so that frequently he was not in Bendigo.

10. Before the hearing the parties agreed to certain facts. They were:-

(1) that there had been complaints made by clients of

the applicant;

(2) that one reason for the complaints was that his

office was not open on a full-time basis, save

for the period from July/August and to

mid-September each year;

(3) that some Australian Taxation Office envelopes

containing notices of assessment were

re-addressed to the applicant's clients and

posted;

(4) that a number of taxpayers' signatures on income

tax returns were removed and placed on other

returns which were then lodged.

Oral evidence was given at the hearing by five clients of the applicant, by Mr McLean, who is the Senior Inquiries Officer in the Taxation Department's Office in Bendigo, by Mr Walton, who is the Assistant Director for Returns Control in the Taxation Department in Melbourne, and by Mr Pietka, who is the Secretary to the Tax Agents' Board. The applicant himself also gave evidence. In addition the Tribunal received in evidence numerous documents, including details of 26 complaints made to the Tax Agents' Board by clients of the applicant between late May 1985 and January 1987.

11. The evidence of Mr McLean was that the complaints by the applicant's clients which were received by the Board were the tip of the iceberg of problems which the applicant's clients and the Department of Taxation were having with the applicant. Mr McLean said that during that period his office was receiving a very large number of inquiries from the applicant's clients regarding the state of their taxation business, matters which should have been dealt with by the applicant and about which they should not have needed to inquire themselves if he had been performing his work as a tax agent properly. He said that only a small proportion of the inquiries were in the form of actual complaints about the applicant; nevertheless, there were a substantial number of complaints.

12. The most common complaint disclosed by the documentary evidence and the oral evidence was that, although the applicant had been accessible to take clients' instructions to prepare and lodge their returns, thereafter he had been inaccessible to them. There were a number of other grounds of complaint. One was of delays by the applicant in lodging returns; in some cases a total failure to lodge was alleged. Another ground was delay in forwarding refunds to clients.

13. Mr Warden gave evidence that, when he visited the applicant's office on 25 November 1986, there were a considerable number of refund cheques for the applicant's clients which had been sent to the applicant and which he had not forwarded to the clients. The earliest of those cheques had been issued on 23 September 1986; some of them were much more recent. It is fair to say that eight of them had been issued a sufficient time before Mr Warden's visit there that the applicant's failure to have forwarded them by then must be regarded as neglect of the client's interests and inexcusable. The others had been issued during the week before the visit; although any delay in forwarding refund cheques to clients is unsatisfactory, in the applicant's circumstances I find in respect of those cheques that there was no neglect of his clients' interests.

14. Those were the three main grounds of complaint by clients. The applicant admitted the delays in lodging returns and in forwarding refunds. He admitted that he had been inaccessible frequently and for considerable periods except during the two months when his office was open full-time. He explained those deficiencies in the service he gave his clients by reference to having taken on too much work, to having been obliged to travel a lot on duty away from Bendigo and to not having had a telephone by means of which he might have been contacted. I am satisfied that those were the reasons why he did not provide satisfactory service. However, they afford no excuse. He should have limited the work he undertook to what he could cope with properly. He knew that he had to travel on duty; it was a matter he should have taken into account when he undertook work. He did not suggest that the reason why he had no telephone in his new home was other than his own failure to arrange for one to be installed.

15. There was one other ground of complaint, that the applicant cashed clients' refund cheques without their approval. The applicant admitted that he had cashed some cheques but denied that he had done so without authority. In respect of one client, Mr Dole, he produced a photocopy of a written authority, apparently signed by Mr Dole. Mr Dole gave evidence that he did not recollect having signed it. However, I find no reason not to accept the applicant's evidence that it was in fact signed by Mr Dole. In the other cases there was evidence that the practice had been established over a number years with, in effect, tacit consent of the clients to refund cheques being cashed, the applicant's fees being taken from the proceeds and then the balance being forwarded to them. It is unsatisfactory that the applicant did not obtain their authority in writing; but on the balance of probabilities I find that he did have tacit authority as a result of the practice that had been accepted by the clients over several years.

16. In addition to the complaints made by the applicant's clients, a number of other irregularities were established by the evidence adduced at the hearing. Those irregularities had come to light as a result of investigations made by the Tax Agents' Board and its officers into the complaints which it had received from the applicant's clients.

17. First, there was evidence that the applicant obtained the signatures of a number of his clients to declarations on incomplete return forms. He gave evidence that in some cases the returns were partially completed when the declarations were signed. But he admitted that in respect of one client who gave evidence, Mr Porter, none of the figures had been inserted before the client signed. The extent to which the returns of other clients were incomplete when they signed them was not clearly established; but there is no doubt that on a number of occasions his clients signed the declarations before being properly able to make them. The declaration on a return is required in order that the taxpayer should take responsibility for what is in the return. He cannot do that until he knows what is in it. A tax agent who has a client sign a return which is incomplete is acting with gross impropriety.

18. A somewhat similar irregularity was committed by the applicant by pasting onto a return a signature to a declaration which he had cut off another form. He admitted that he had done that on a number of occasions; but he gave what might perhaps be described as an innocent explanation for doing it, namely that, having been unable to obtain the correct return forms, he had arranged for his clients to sign other return forms which were available and then, when he had been able to obtain the correct forms, he had completed them, cut the signatures from the forms that had been signed and pasted them onto the new forms. Mr Pietka gave evidence that there was a period when the correct forms had not been available. I accept as true the applicant's explanation for what he did. The fact that it was done because of the unavailability of the correct form affords some mitigation; nevertheless it was irregular and improper. As a tax agent the applicant should have been aware of that; consequetly, although there were mitigating circumstances, he cannot be absolved from blame.

19. The second irregularity was reuse by the applicant of Taxation Department envelopes. Such envelopes have the postage charge prepaid so that they are accepted by Australia Post for transmission by post without a postage stamp being affixed to them. Mr MacGrotty, a client of the applicant, gave evidence that he had received a refund cheque from the applicant in such an envelope. Mr Warden gave evidence of finding a number of such envelopes, apparently carefully opened, in the applicant's office when he visited it on 25 November 1986. The applicant gave evidence that the envelope was posted to Mr McGrotty in error; he normally used the envelopes only for refund cheques which he intended to hand-deliver and did not post them. However, in the statement of agreed facts lodged with the Tribunal before the hearing one of the facts agreed was some envelopes were re-addressed and posted. I find the applicant's evidence on this point unsatisfactory. On a balance of probabilities, I am satisfied that he was adopting a practice of reusing the Taxation Department's envelopes for posting assessment notices and refund cheques to his clients.

20. The next irregularity alleged was in respect of the applicant's tax returns. First, he submitted them late, not just once but in several years. Normally a taxpayer is required to lodge his return by the end of August, i.e. within two months of the end of the preceding tax year. Tax agents are given a certain amount of leeway and are permitted to lodge their returns by 31 December. The applicant admitted that he was aware that as a tax agent that was the latest date by which he was required to lodge his tax return. Nevertheless, he did not lodge until 22 February 1985 his return for the tax year which ended on 30 June 1984; he has not yet lodged his return for the tax year which ended on 30 June 1986. A tax agent who is competent and desires, as he should, to comply with the law, should be able to lodge his own tax return within the time allowed. The fact that the applicant did not do so is significant; it indicates either incompetence or disregard for the law, or both.

21. Second, the tax returns which he lodged for 1984 and 1985 were incomplete. In every tax year recently, the Commissioner has sent to tax agents a circular, a copy of which for the 1986 tax year was tendered in evidence, in which he has drawn attention to the fact that tax returns must be complete and that it is not permissible to omit anything that should be included and to add it later. Quite apart from the Commissioner's circular, the return form itself contains a declaration that the contents comprise a complete statement of the taxpayer's income and expenditure in the tax year. When a tax agent completes his own return, he should know what is in the declaration on the form that in the course of his practice he completes for so many other people. It is, therefore, a matter of great concern that the applicant's returns were incomplete. Either he was grossly incompetent or he was deliberately making a false declaration, or both.

22. The final irregularity alleged is that, as the applicant stated in his evidence at the hearing, he used the Department of Education's photocopier for making photocopies of documents for use in his practice as a tax agent. He gave evidence that he supplied his own paper but admitted that he used the Department's equipment without authority and without paying for its use.

23. The five clients of the applicant who gave evidence generally expressed satisfaction with the services which he had given to them, except in respect of the matters about which they had complained to the Tax Agents' Board. They were generally happy with what occurred when he took their instructions and with the advice and assistance which they received at that stage. He gave evidence that he always obtained his instructions from clients on a face-to-face basis; he said that many tax agents did not do that. He said also that he kept up to date with the state of the law and advised his clients accordingly.

24. The registration of tax agents is provided for in section 251J of the Act. The most important provision there, for the present purposes, is sub-section (3) which provides that:-

"251J. (3) If the applicant satisfies the Board

that -

(a) in the case of an individual - the

applicant;

. . . . .

is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax

returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers

in income tax matters and . . .

is over the age of 21 years at the date on which the

application is made, and is of good fame, integrity

and character, the Board shall register the applicant

as a tax agent."

That is a mandatory provision; if the requirements are met, the applicant must be registered. It is in the light of that provision that those of section 251K(2) must be read. So far as is relevant for the purpose of these proceedings, that sub-section reads:-

"251K. (2) A board may cancel the registration of any

tax agent upon being satisfied that -

. . . . .

(b) the tax agent -

(i) has neglected the business of a

principal;

(ii) has been guilty of misconduct as a

tax agent; or

(iii) is not a fit and proper person to

remain registered;"

25. The facts established in this case fall into two categories, although to some extent they overlap one another. Those in the first category relate to neglect by the applicant of the business of his principals. That neglect is constituted by his delays and failures in respect of the lodgment of returns, his delays in forwarding refunds, and his inaccessibility. The facts in the second category relate to the question whether he is or is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters.

26. There are a number of cases in which the Courts and this Tribunal have considered what is meant, in cases of this sort, by "a fit and proper person". In Hughes and Vale Pty. Ltd. v. New South Wales (No. 2) [1955] HCA 28; (1955) 93 CLR 127 at 156 Dixon C.J. and McTiernan and Webb JJ. in a joint judgment said:-

"The expression 'fit and proper person' is of course

familiar enough as traditional words when used with

their very purpose is to give the widest scope for

judgement and indeed for rejection. 'Fit' (or

'idoneus') with respect to an office is said to

involve three things, honesty, knowledge and ability:

'honesty to execute it truly, without malice

affection or partiality; knowledge to know what he

ought duly to do; and ability as well in estate as in

body, that he may intend and execute his office, when

need is, diligently, and not for impotency or poverty

neglect it' - Coke."

27. In Maxwell v. Dixon (1965) WAR 167 at 169 Hale J. said:-

"It is true that 'fit and proper' must be interpreted

in the light of the subject-matter of the Act in

which the expression appears . . . Clearly different

qualifications are needed by e.g. lawyers, transport

operators, hotel keepers and land agents, but, as is

shown by the cases cited in (Hughes and Vale Pty.

Ltd. v. New South Wales (No. 2) (supra)), it is not

necessary to confine oneself to the special

provisions of the Act in question in deciding whether

a man is fit provided one gives weight only to

matters which can fairly be seen to be relevant to

the vocation in issue."

28. As referred to by Dr Scutt, Counsel for the respondent, Mr K. L. Milne in "The Accountant in Public Practice" summed up what made a person a fit and proper person to practise as a public accountant in terms of the following criteria:-

"The duty to the public, the profession and himself is

a moral obligation while the duty to his client and

the taxation department is both moral and legal. The

accountant owes a duty to the taxation authorities to

possess a thorough knowledge of taxation acts,

regulations and legal decisions . . . Over a period

of time reputable accountants have succeeded in

winning and retaining the confidence of taxation

officials, and it will be to the advantage of both

sides if they retain it. In the interests of all it

is desirable that accountants engaged in the

preparation of tax returns should behave in such a

manner that the officers in the departments know that

they are dealing with men of repute, whose statements

may be accepted as fact or relied upon as considered

and honest opinions. The complicated nature of

taxation law, combining as it does both statute and

common law with departmental rulings and precedents,

necessitates a close liaison between tax

practitioners and the departmental officers."

29. In Re Su and Tax Agents' Board, South Australia 4 ALN N279 Davies J., President, said that a tax agent:-

". . should be a person of such competence and

integrity that others may entrust their taxation

affairs to his care. He should be a person of such

reputation and ability that officers of the Taxation

Department may proceed upon the footing that the

taxation returns lodged by the agent have been

prepared by him honestly and competently."

I adopt, with respect, that statement of what is the requirement for a person to be a fit and proper person not only to be granted a tax agent's licence but also to continue to hold such a licence.

30. The facts established in this case which, taken together, satisfy me that the applicant is not a fit and proper person are:-

(1) his having caused taxpayers to sign their returns

while they were incomplete;

(2) his failure to lodge his own returns within

proper time;

(3) his signing the declarations that those returns

were complete when they were not;

(4) the neglect of his clients (which is why I

referred above to the two categories of facts as

overlapping);

(5) his use of the Taxation Department envelopes to

post assessments and refunds to clients;

(6) his unauthorised use of the Education

Department's photocopier.

Both (5) and (6), although possibly only minor dishonesties, disclose an unacceptable lack of probity and a failure to maintain a proper ethical standard.

31. I am satisfied - and I find that the Board had good reason to be satisfied - that the applicant neglected the business of his principals and is not a fit and proper person to remain registered as a tax agent. However, section 251K(2) gives the Board - and, by virtue of section 43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal standing in its shoes - a discretion whether or not to cancel the applicant's registration. The Tribunal has to decide what is the correct or preferable decision, not simply whether the decision of the Tax Agents' Board was reasonable (Drake v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60).

32. Cancellation of registration is the only sanction available in respect of tax agents. In Re Denton and Tax Agents' Board, South Australia 5 ALN N21 Davies J. contrasted that with the range of sanctions usually available to disciplinary bodies and:-

"The only sanction which the Tax Agents' Board may

impose is a sanction by way of cancellation of

registration. For this reason, cancellation of a tax

agent's registration may be effected for less cause

than would be the case if the alternatives of fine or

suspension were available"

However he continued:-

"Nevertheless there should be strong reasons for

cancelling a registration."

33. Essentially the question of whether or not the discretion should be exercised is one of balancing the public interest against the applicant's own private interests. The public interest is of two kinds. First, there is the interest of individual members of the public as clients of the applicant. Second, there is the interest of the public in the proper administration of the taxation system, in income tax returns being properly made and in taxpayers not being obliged by the default of their tax agent unnecessarily to seek information from the Taxation Department and thereby disrupt its work.

34. The private interest of the tax agent will, of course, vary from tax agent to tax agent. If his practice as a tax agent is the only, or a very substantial source, of his income, clearly the adverse effects on him of deregistration are likely to be severe. Even where the tax agent has other sources of income, his interests will be adversely affected by any loss of income from his practice as a tax agent; but the extent of that adverse effect will vary with the proportion of total income derived from that source.

35. The applicant is in full-time employment as a public officer and receives a salary on that basis. He gave evidence that he has operated his tax agent's practice at a loss. I find that difficult to believe; although expenses must be incurred in conducting a substantial tax agent's practice, including expenditure on renting and staffing an office, and the applicant undoubtedly had to incur some expenses, in 1984 he had 440 clients and in 1985 545, so that his income from the practice in both those years should have been considerable. However, if, as he asserts, he has operated the practice at a loss, it has not been a source of his income in recent years. He gave evidence that he had heard that consideration was being given to retrenching him from his public service employment and that that might occur by July 1987. However, he did not adduce any evidence that there was any major degree of likelihood that he would lose his employment. Further, he gave evidence that in Bendigo there was inadequate work for tax agents working on their own. Taking his evidence in that regard together with his evidence that his tax agency business has not been profitable, there is very little evidence that, even if he is retrenched from the public service, cancellation of his registration will cause him any serious adverse effect.

36. In relation to the public interest, quite clearly the main reason why the applicant has neglected his clients' interests - although it is not an acceptable excuse for his having done so - is that he is employed full-time, moreover in a job which requires him to be away from Bendigo for much of his working time. If he continues in his employment, he will continue not to be able to provide a proper service for his clients. He gave evidence that in the second half of this year he intends to take leave without pay; but that would ameliorate the situation only temporarily.

37. So, when the public interest is balanced against his own, the public interest in his registration as a tax agent being cancelled greatly outweighs the adverse effect which deregistration will have on him. As Dr Scutt pointed out, and as was referred by the Tribunal in Re Chapman and Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 5 ALN N552, cancellation of registration does not necessarily prevent future registration. Any person may apply to the Tax Agents' Board for registration as a tax agent and, if he meets the requirements of section 251J of the Act, the Board must register him. So, if at some time in the future the applicant puts his affairs properly into order, so as to be able to satisfy the Board that he meets those requirements, he may apply again, and if he meets them, the Board will have to register him.

38. For the reasons stated above the decision to cancel his registration is affirmed.
