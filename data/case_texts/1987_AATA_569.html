REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant retired from the NSW Public Service in 1981. He gave evidence that in 1973 he purchased a home unit in an inner suburb of Sydney with the intention of earning income from rental. For it, he paid $50,000 and of that amount he borrowed $30,000 from the Commonwealth Bank. His evidence was that the loan was on commercial terms and not on the basis of a home loan.

He put the unit in the hands of a real estate agent who was unsuccessful in finding a tenant after eight weeks. Thereafter, the applicant put the unit in the hands of two further agents but was still unable to find a tenant. 'He says that the price that he was asking was too high.

By the fiscal year 1978/1979, the unit had been empty for about five years. The applicant owned a terraced house in which he lived together with his aged father and a woman (who was not related) who was then working as a nurse at a large private hospital in Sydney. The nurse occupied a bedroom and bathroom, but in addition assisted the applicant's father from time to time and looked after the house when the applicant was out of the country.

The applicant conceived the idea of letting the empty unit to the nurse. He had several reasons for this proposal. Firstly, he said that he considered it the best way of looking after the property. He had known the nurse for approximately 20 years and knew her character. She was unlikely to hold parties or otherwise mistreat the unit. It happened, in addition, that she was suffering from terminal cancer.

His second reason was that it would be part of the arrangement that the nurse should visit his father and look after him for 14 hours per day. He emphasised in his final address that it would have cost him approximately $250 per week to obtain the services of a person who would cook and be a companion to his 86 year old father on that basis. Instead, he allowed the nurse to occupy the unit at a rental of $2.00 per week. A lease in standard form was drawn up by a firm of solicitors providing for occupation for 12 months and thereafter continuing from week to week. Included in the lease was an inventory of furniture.

The nurse lived in the property until the fiscal year 1983/84, a total of 4 or 5 years. In the last year of her occupancy, the rent was raised to $10.00 per week because "everything was going up". The applicant's father died in February 1982 and the nurse died in 1985. By consent, a valuation was tendered in which estimates of commercial rental value were given as follows:

After the nurse died, the applicant allowed the property to remain empty for a further three months and then moved in to live there. He has continued to live there, subject to allowing certain relatives to stay from time to time while he occupies the terraced house.

The applicant does not carry on a business of renting properties. However, he did acquire a house at an outer western suburb of Sydney in partnership with a woman 10 or 12 years ago. He acquired her interest about 6 years ago and since then has continued to let that house property at a commercial rental.

In the years of income in question, while the unit was occupied by the nurse, the applicant paid sums representing insurance, maintenance fees to the body corporate, municipal rates and water rates. Payment of these amounts was not in dispute. No amount was claimed for repairs. It was agreed between the parties that if the amount of $2.00 per week (or later $10.00 per week), which was received by the applicant, could properly be categorised as income, then the outgoings would (subject to an alternative submission to which reference will later be made), be deductible pursuant to section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

The Commissioner refused to consider the rental paid as income and consequently refused a deduction for the outgoings as it was submitted that they were not incurred in the gaining of assessable income. That position was affirmed on the hearing of this application which was brought to review that decision. It was submitted alternatively on behalf of the Commissioner that if the "rental" was to be regarded as income, then the amount of the deductions should be limited to the amount of the income.

There can be no dispute that the rental charged to the nurse was grossly less than a market rate, both having regard to the amount paid to acquire the unit and to the going rate for rental of similar units attested to by the valuer.

There can also be no dispute that the applicant considered he was obtaining a composite benefit by allowing the nurse to occupy the unit at what must be described as a nominal rental.

The evidence does not lead to "an inference of benefaction" (see case R16 84 ATC 179). No benefit was intended to the nurse. Rather, the applicant himself sought to benefit from this arrangement.

He benefited in two ways. Firstly, his capital investment was protected by having a known and suitable tenant occupying premises which might otherwise be subject to vandalisation. Secondly, and probably more importantly, the applicant benefited by having an arrangement for the care of his aged father as part of the overall arrangements. Having regard to these further purposes there is, in my view, an inescapable inference that there is not an income quality to the receipts arising from the use of the unit. As in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Groser 82 ATC 4478, the arrangements have nothing to do with transactions of the kind which result in the receipt of income as understood in ordinary usage. As in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kowal 84 ATC 4001, the predominant object in entering into the lease agreement was not to earn assessable income. This was only a relatively minor object. The evidence discloses, and indeed the submission of the applicant emphasises, that the primary object was to provide for security of the property and care of the applicant's father.

The significant disparity between the amount of rent received and the commercial rental value may be compared with the significant differences between the rates of interest charged on borrowed money and the rate of return from the investment of that borrowed money in Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4001. The same examination of the purposes for which the money was laid out and which was referred to by Brennan J. at page 4104 is appropriate in circumstances where the income is in the form of rental. As in that case, there is an air of unreality in the present application about the proposition that the unit was let wholly for the purpose of earning such a small return. Once it is acknowledged that such a purpose was minor in the overall scheme and that other purposes played a larger part in the arrangements then it becomes clear that the small amount of monies derived from the arrangement do not bear the character of income. The word rent identifies a receipt for allowing the use by another of one's property. What the applicant received here was not rent, but a minor token binding a much broader 'private and domestic arrangement. As such it cannot be regarded as income and moneys paid out in conjunction with that arrangement, although deductible in other circumstances, can not here be regarded as losses or outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income.

Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed.
