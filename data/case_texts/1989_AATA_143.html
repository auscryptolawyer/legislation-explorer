ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Unpaid Company Tax : liability of vendor shareholders for recoupment tax - service of company notice of assessment on vendor shareholders - whether vendor shareholders capable of controlling 90 per cent of voting power - whether consideration on sale of shares exceeds net assets of company - whether vendors recoupment tax payable.

Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 13 June 1989

Place: Brisbane

The Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 ("the Act") provides for collection of taxes known as recoupment taxes. The Act seeks to deal, in a retrospective manner, with the recoupment of taxes said to be owing by companies, which are euphemistically described as having been sent to "the bottom of the harbour", whereas in reality they were merely stripped of their assets.

2. The question at issue in these applications is whether:

(a) immediately before the sale of shares in the company the vendor shareholders were, by virtue of rights attaching to the shares, capable of controlling more than 90% of the voting power of the company; and

(b) the total consideration paid or given in respect of the sale or sales of the shares exceeds the net assets of the company after making provision for taxation;

thereby bringing the company within the terms of section 5 of the Act.

  1. The applicant ("T") in these proceedings is the nominated person within the terms of section 18 of the Act.
  2. A further issue before the Tribunal is whether the respondent Commissioner had ever formed the necessary state of mind, in terms of sub-section 18(1), to determine that a primary taxable amount existed prior to serving notices of assessment on the nominated person. That is, of course, a different question (from the question whether sub-section 18(1) applies to the facts of this case. For the reasons which follow it is a question which we do not need to consider further.
  3. As at 30 June 1979, shareholdings in the company were as follows (Exhibit 3):-

W - 1 "A" class share.

W Investments Pty Ltd - 1 "B" class share.

T - 1 "A" class share.

T Investments Pty Ltd - 1 "B" class share.

6. At the beginning of 1980, because of plans to establish a unit trust structure for the conduct of the business of the company, it was decided that the company was no longer required. L, the company's accountant, entered into discussions with a solicitor ("S") concerning the sale of the company to a client of S - Fifth Minrip Pty Ltd ("Fifth Minrip") (Exhibit 3).

7. On 7 March 1980 a meeting of directors of the company resolved to issue further shares as follows-

Fifth Minrip Pty Ltd - 1 "B" share

T. Investments Pty Ltd - 999 "B" shares

W. Investments Pty Ltd - 999 "B" shares

T - 2999 "B" shares

W - 2999 "B" shares

(Return of allotments lodged 14 March 1980 - Exhibit 4).

8. At an extraordinary general meeting of members of 12 March 1980 it was resolved, inter alia, that the capital of the company be $20,000 divided into 8000 $1 "A" class shares and 12,000 $1 "B" class shares, that the shares numbered 1-8000 be "A" class shares and that the "A" shares have the following rights:

(i) they shall rank in the winding up for payment of a sum equivalent to the aggregate of the amount paid up thereon;

(ii) they shall not carry the right to any further participation in profits or assets;

(ill) they shall not confer on the holders thereof the right to receive notices of or to attend or vote at general meetings of the Company; and

(iv) they shall not be entitled to any dividend.

The effect of the resolution was to transfer all other rights privileges and conditions to the "B" shares. In other words the "A" shares were stripped of value in excess of their face value. Shares issued prior to 12 March 1980 became perforce of the resolution, "A" class shares except for the 1 share held by Fifth Minrip which remained a "B" class share. As a consequence Fifth Minrip controlled all the voting power of the company and effectively owned the company subject only to $8000 of capital in the "A" shares.

  1. Not surprisingly that resolution was procured by Fifth Minrip for a consideration from the other shareholders. The terms of the agreement executed on 12 March 1980 are set out in the copy document in Exhibit 4. The consideration paid by Fifth Minrip related to the shares held by the other shareholders. The total consideration payable to procure the resolution was $215,425 whereas the net assets of the company were $245,926.
  2. Counsel for the applicant conceded that the amount of company tax which would become due and payable significantly exceeded $100,000. It is therefore apparent that the company was pregnant with profits and nothing else. In fact there was a deficiency of shareholders funds as at 30 June 1979 so that the net worth of the company as at 12 March 1980 reflected profits derived during the financial year beginning on 1 July 1979.
  3. There can be no doubt about the motive of the shareholders nor the purpose of the agreement to procure the special resolution to amend the rights of shareholders. The purpose of the agreement was to strip the company of the net assets prior to the company becoming liable to pay income tax on the current year's profits. To achieve that end, the value was first stripped from the 8,000 "A" class shares, reducing them in value to their face value of $1 and removing their voting rights. On the same day, the "A" shares were sold to Fifth Minrip for $8,000 i.e. $1 per share. The agreement in writing to sell the shares is part of Exhibit 4. The document is dated 12 March 1980, the same date as the special resolution, but it must have been effected only after passage of the special resolution. The reason we say this is that the agreement is for the sale of 8,000 $1 "A" class shares which are the shares created by the special resolution. No other possible explanation presents itself to the Tribunal.
  4. It is established that the shares sold by the vendors did not have any voting power.
  5. Application of the formula in paragraph 5(1) (d) on the facts set out above would produce the following:

A $245,923

L = NIL

T = $100,000 (approx.)

$245,923

- $100,000

S145.923

It cannot be said that the amount paid for the shares exceeds the amount so ascertained.

  1. Prima facie, therefore, there is no primary taxable amount, with the result that there is no vendor's taxable amount and therefore no liability within the terms of section 8 of the Act. If, on the evidence, this conclusion is correct, the Commissioner's view that the vendor's recoupment tax was payable (or his opinion that such tax was likely to become payable) cannot be correct.
  2. However, the respondent Commissioner does not accept this position. Counsel submitted that as the respondent, by one of his officers, apparently authorised to form the opinion, had come to the view that vendor's recoupment tax is likely to become payable, then that was the end of the matter. It was submitted that it was not for the Tribunal to decide whether the opinion was correctly held, but only to decide whether, as a fact, the respondent had formed the necessary opinion.
  3. Counsel relied upon dicta of Dixon J in Avon Downs Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 26; (1949) 78 CLR 353 at 360 where his Honour stated that it was not for the Court to be satisfied as to the state of the voting power in a company at the end of the year of income for the purpose of determining whether claims for losses were allowable. His Honour went on to say, in effect, that the proposition did not mean the Commissioner's opinion could not be reviewed by the Courts. In our opinion, it does not follow that this Tribunal is in the same position.
  4. In the Tribunal's view if the opinion had not been formed, then there would be no valid notice served on the applicant. If, however, the opinion had been formed but was in the Tribunal's view not the correct and preferred state of mind on the facts established before the Tribunal, then it is open to the Tribunal to determine the objection accordingly; (ss.43(1) Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, Cripps & Jones Holdings Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. 87 ATC 43521.
  5. The essence of the applicant's case is that the agreement to procure the special resolution altering the rights of issued shares other than the share held by Fifth Minrip and the contract for sale of the "A" shares to Fifth Minrip were separate and distinct contracts. If that is the correct position, then it cannot be said that the test in sub-section 5(1) of the Act has been satisfied. Certainly the two contracts are consistent with each other, albeit that neither expressly refers to the other. The first contract was entered into for the apparent purpose of procuring the passage of the resolutions necessary to quarantine the net worth of the company to the "B" share held by Fifth Minrip. The terns of the contract make no mention of the agreement to sell the shares.
  6. Similarly the terms of the agreement to sell the shares makes no mention, whether implied or express, of the contract to procure the resolutions of the company.
  7. There is no evidence before the Tribunal to suggest that the agreements, resolutions and transfers were not executed in the intended sequence. It therefore follows, submits Mr Byrne Q.C. who appeared with Mr Alexander of counsel for the applicant, that the tests in sub-sections 5(1)(c) and 5(1)(d) are not satisfied, whereas Mr Muir Q.C., who appeared with Mr Newton of counsel for the respondent, submits that the shares were sold as part of a scheme, and that sub-section 5(1) requires the Commissioner to look at the whole of the arrangements rather than the isolated transactions and to take into account the substance of the transactions.

21. Mr Byrne relied upon the judgment of Isaacs J in Hovts Pty Ltd v. Spencer [1919] HCA 64; (1919) 27 CLR 133 at 143 to support the proposition that the parties may decide to have separate contracts which are not collateral contracts and are separate and distinct from each other in their effect. His Honour stated:

"When two parties are entering into contractual relations with respect to a given subject matter, they may (apart from special technical requirements) elect to conclude their bargain without writing, or they may elect to record it in writing, and, if in writing, they may further decide to have it under seal. But in whatever form they determine to leave their bargain, they may further agree to have one contract only, or to have separate and distinct contracts. All that is for the parties themselves to resolve upon. If they determine to make one contract only, then the terms they decide to include are the only terms that affect them contractually. It connotes that all else is abandoned. And that is the case whatever the form of the contract. If the matter is not committed to writing, though the principle is clear, the evidence is manifestly open to great dispute. But if the parties agree to commit their agreement to writing, then what is written is the conclusive record of the terms of their agreement, and, unless it can be shown that the document was not intended as the complete record of their bargain, no oral evidence can be admitted to alter or qualify it."

22. The first contract had the effect of stripping the net worth of the company from the vendors shares, thereby effectively disposing of the company to Fifth Minrip subject only to the other shareholders having the right to receive the face value of their shares. That right was satisfied by the second contract but it was not necessary for this to happen the first contract still achieved the purpose sought to be achieved and must stand on its own.

23. It follows, says Mr Byrne, that there was no basis for the respondent to form the opinion within the terms of paragraph 18(1)(b) of the Act. There is no basis for saying that vendor's recoupment tax is payable or for forming the opinion that vendor's recoupment tax is likely to become payable if the tests in paragraphs 5(1)(c) and 5(1)(d) of the Act are not satisfied. Support for this view is to be found in the judgment of the Federal Court in F.C. of T. v. Cripps & Jones Holdings Pty Ltd 87 ATC 4977. At 4984-5 the Court referred to sub-sections 15(9) and 18(3) of the Act and went on to say:

"The true answer to the contention is that legislation of this kind, by reference to rights created in another Act for other purposes, cannot be construed in a blindly literal spirit. In cases arising under Div. 2 of Pt. V of the Income Tax Assessment Act, a taxpayer has the right 'to challenge the existence of the conditions giving rise to the exercise of a power which imposes on him a new substantive liability to tax' (F.J. Bloemen Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. 81 ATC 4280 at p.4287; [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 C.L.R. 360 at p.374, per Mason and Wilson JJ.). Sections 15 and 18 of the Act should both be construed as conferring a corresponding right to challenge the existence of the conditions giving rise to the exercise of the power, under which an assessment issued, that is served pursuant to the provisions of sec. 15 or 18 of the Act. The rights asserted are 'the same rights', but the Issues which arise, when they are asserted in respect of assessments under sec. 15, are necessarily different from the issues which arise when they are asserted in respect of assessments issued under the Income Tax Assessment Act. In any case, if there is any ambiguity in the provisions of sec. 15(9) and 18(3), a construction which would remove an Ingredient of liability from the possibility of challenge should be rejected, both upon general principles of statutory construction, and also in accordance with the approach to the constitutional invalidity of an incontestable tax taken in MacCormick v. F.C. of T. [1984] HCA 20; (1984) 158 CLR 622."

  1. There is no doubt in our view that if the respondent was incorrect in forming the view or exercising the discretion in sub-section 18(1), then the applicant must succeed in these applications.
  2. The applicant submits that the respondent was wrong because the vendors did not control and were not capable of controlling more than 90% of the voting power in the company immediately before the first sale time. Reliance was placed upon the judgments in Eeruiticorp Industries Ltd. v. A.C.I. International Ltd. VR 485 and W.P. Keighery Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. (1957) 100 CUR 66 to support the proposition that before the first sale time, the vendors were not capable of controlling the voting power of the company.
  3. In Keigherv's case a statutory provision in question was then sub-section 105(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act which provided in paragraph (f) that a company was a private company if on the last day of the year of income it was not a company in which the public was substantially interested, or a subsidiary of a public company and was capable of being controlled by any means whatsoever by persons not more than seven in number. Dixon CJ, Kitto and Taylor JJ stated in their judgment at page 70:

"It must be acknowledged, of course, that it is the capability, and not the control, which must exist on the last day of the year of income. But to describe a company as capable of being controlled by a person or group of persons is to attribute to that person or group a presently existing power of control. 'Capable of being controlled' in this context cannot be interpreted so widely as to be satisfied whenever a possibility of obtaining control over the company exists by reason of something in its constitution or its special circumstances. The natural sense of the expression is that of possessing, as a present attribute, a liability to be controlled. And a liability to be controlled by one person or not more than seven persons involves either that there is one person who holds, or has a right to command, the major portion of the existing voting power, or that there are several persons, not more than seven in number, whether they form an existing group or not, who between them hold or can command a major portion of the existing voting power."

27. It is clear from their Honour's dicta that paragraph 5(1)(c) must require that the capability of controlling more than 90% of the voting power refers to a presently existing power of control immediately before the first sale time. That conclusion is supported by the judgment of the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Victoria in Eguiticorp Industries Ltd v. A.C.I. International Ltd (supra) at p.488 where the Court (Murphy, Fullagar and Gobbo JJ) considered the decisions of the High Court in Keiqrhery (supra) and F.C. of T. v. Sidney Williams (Holdings) Ltd [1957] HCA 1; (1957), 100 CLR 95 and concluded:

"The above authorities lend strong support to the proposition that when one speaks of a company capable of being controlled in terms of voting power, or, to use the language of sec. 9(1), of being in a position to control a certain voting power, one looks for an enforceable and presently and immediately existing right enabling the voting power to be controlled. It must be more than control in certain eventualities."

  1. The applicant submits that immediately before the first sale time the vendors were not capable of controlling any voting power of the company.
  2. The respondent's counsel submitted that the Tribunal should look at the whole of the transactions entered into. There was an overall scheme which is the relevant arrangement to be considered when applying the tests in sub-section 5(1). The Tribunal's attention was directed to the fact that the Commissioner had formed the view that the tests in paragraphs 5(1)(c) and 5(1)(d) had been satisfied. It seems the officers in the Taxation Office had taken the view that the consideration payable under the two contracts should be regarded as the consideration payable for purchase of the vendors' shares and it was on this basis that the tests were thought to be satisfied. It is not established, on the evidence, that the respondent also formed the requisite opinion in sub-section 18(1) although he stated in his answers to a request for particulars that such an opinion had been formed. Neither the documentary evidence nor the oral evidence supports the contention that such an opinion was formed. Rather the officers of the Taxation Office decided as a fact that vendor's recoupment tax was payable. That conclusion is made clear by Exhibit B and folio 19 of Exhibit 2A.
    1. Whether the respondent correctly formed the view or formed the necessary opinion in terms of paragraph 18(1)(b) is an essential requirement for the operation of section 18. If one of those conditions is not met, there is no basis under the section for service of the notice of assessment in respect of the company on the vendor shareholder.
    2. As we have come to the conclusion that the contract to procure the resolutions and the contract for sale of the shares are separate and distinct contracts, that the contracts were executed in the Intended order, that the vendor shareholders were not capable of controlling more than 90% of the voting power of the company immediately before the first sale time and the consideration paid for the shares did not exceed the amount calculated in accordance with the formula in paragraph 5(1)(d), there is no proper basis for the operation of sub-section 18(1). This is because it cannot be said, on the facts established before the Tribunal, that vendor's recoupment tax is payable.

32. Nor is there any basis for forming the opinion that vendor's recoupment tax is likely to become payable. The meaning of "likely" was discussed in Tillmanns Butcheries Pty Ltd v. Australasian Meat Industry Employees' Union and others (1979) 27 ALR 367 where Bowen C.J. said (at page 375):

"The word 'likely' is one which has various shades of meaning. It may mean 'probable' in the sense of 'more probable than not' - 'more than a 50 per cent chance'. It may mean 'material risk' as seen by a reasonable nan 'such as might happen'. It may mean 'some possibility' - more than a remote or bare chance. Or, it may mean that the conduct engaged in is inherently of such a character that it would ordinarily cause the effect specified."

33. After discussing certain authorities Bowen CJ resolved the point without coming to a firm conclusion. Deane J also dealt with the meaning of likely in the following passage at pages 381-2 which is instructive for the purposes of the present case:

"Section 45D(1) proscribes conduct only if it be engaged in for the purpose of causing loss or damage to the business of the relevant corporation. Even though conduct be engaged in for such a purpose it will be outside the proscription contained in the sub-section unless it 'would have or be likely to have' that effect. Plainly the reference to 'would be likely to have' is meant to convey a lower degree of likelihood than the reference to 'would have'. In the case where conduct has not occurred, a court would be constrained to determine whether conduct 'would have' the specified effect by reference to the ordinary standard of whether it was more likely than not that it would. In such a case, if 'likely' is interpreted as meaning 'more likely than not', it would add little to the practical scope of the section. On the other hand, if conduct had run its ordinary course and had not had the specified effect, it would be but rarely that a court would feel justified in disregarding the lesson of the event and finding that while the conduct did not have the specified effect it had been more likely than not that it would have that effect (see per Dixon J in Willis v. Commonwealth [1946] HCA 22; (1946) 73 CLR 105 at 116).

The conclusion which I have reached is that, in the context of s.45D(1), the preferable view is that the word 'likely' is not synonymous with 'more likely than not' and that if relevant conduct is engaged in for the purposes of causing loss or damage to the business of the relevant corporation, it will suffice, for the purposes of the sub-section, if that conduct is, in the circumstances, such that there is a real chance or possibility that it will, if pursued, cause such loss or damage. Whether or not such conduct is likely (in that sense) to have that effect is a question to be determined by reference to well-established standards of what could reasonably be expected to be the consequence of the relevant conduct in the circumstances. In determining the answer to that question, it will be relevant that the persons engaging in the conduct did so with the purpose of causing such loss or damage."

See also Sheen v. Fields 1984 51 ALR 345 @ 348 per Gibbs C.J. and Global Sportsman Pty Ltd v. Mirror Newspapers Ltd [1984] FCA 180; (1984) 55 ALR 25 @ 30.

34. In so far as paragraph 5(1) (c) is concerned Mr Muir submitted that the shares were sold as part of an overall scheme and the subsection looks at the overall scheme, i.e. the whole of what took place, so that immediately before the first sale time means before the scheme to sell the shares. 'Immediately' in this context must refer to the situation existing at the instant in time preceding the agreement to sell the shares. If the paragraph was intended to refer to the rights attaching to the shares before the scheme was entered into, Parliament would have used language which said so. Given the nature of the legislation, the Tribunal should not go beyond the clear meaning of the words used and prefer a less obvious construction of those words. We take the view that in paragraph 5(13(c) Parliament referred to the situation as it was next preceding the first sale of the shares. It follows that the vendors were not capable of controlling more than 90% of the voting power by virtue of the rights attaching to the shares.

  1. We turn now to paragraph 5(1)(d). Mr Muir Q.C. submitted for the respondent that the Tribunal should look beyond the documents themselves and look to the substance of the matter to determine the total consideration payable. Counsel relied upon dicta of Wilberforce J. (as he then was) in Fitch Lovell Ltd v. I.R.C. [1962] 3 All E.R. 685 at 691 where his Lordship took the view that the substance of the transactions should be looked at to determine the stamp duty payable on certain incomplete share transfers. However, his Lordship's approach was not followed by the House of Lords in Wm. Cory & Son Ltd v. I.R.C. C19653 1 All E.R. 917 where the House of Lords held that liability for stamp duty on share transfers must be determined at the time the transfers were executed; an option agreement was not to be taken into account so that such transfers were not transfers on sale and therefore entitled to a lower rate of duty.
  2. In dicta which provides some assistance in the present case. Lord Re id (with whom Lords Hodson and Pearce agreed) said at page 921:

"Then counsel for the Crown said, no doubt truly, that if this appeal were allowed the door would be open for wholesale evasion of stamp duty; but that consideration has never prevailed over the rule that the words of a taxing Act must never be stretched against a taxpayer. There is a very good reason for that rule. So long as one adheres to the natural weaning of the charging words the law is certain, or at least as certain as it is possible to make it; but if courts are to give to charging words what is sometimes called a liberal construction, who can say just how far this will go. It is much better that evasion should be met by amending legislation. One is familiar with this in the realm of income tax, and if the ingenuity of taxpayers' advisers is now turning to the Stamp Act, 1891, no doubt that in time will be met in the same way. Even if I were entitled to go beyond the natural meaning of the words of s.54, I should find difficulty in bringing this case within its ambit. But taking the natural meaning of s.54 I find it impossible to say that, where there may never be a sale at all, property 'upon the sale thereof is transferred to or vested in a purchaser'."

37. A contrary view was expressed by Dixon J in Commissioner of Stamp Duties (Old) v. Hopkins [1945] HCA 14; (1945) 71 CLR 351 where the High Court was asked to decide whether a document executed in 1907 amounted to a settlement for stamp duty purposes where the property the subject of the settlement had been transferred by the settlor months prior to the settlor executing the instrument.

38. At page 378 Dixon J stated:

"When the settlor executed the instrument, he confirmed and authenticated his intention to create the trusts amounting to a settlement and, because the property had been vested in the trustee the settlement was operative.

To the objection that this view involves looking outside the instrument, it may be answered that to ascertain the operation of the instrument some facts must always be taken into account, as, for instance, the existence and identity of the parties, and of the objects and subjects referred to."

See also per La than CJ at page 362 and Pleetwood-Hesketh v. I.R.C. [1936] 1 K.B. 351.

  1. However, as we have already said each of the present documents stands by itself. The contract to procure the special resolutions does not depend upon any extrinsic facts. It states on its face the whole of the transactions agreed between the parties. The consideration does not depend upon anything other than that contained in the document itself. There is therefore no basis for saying that the agreement to sell the shares depends in any way upon the contract to procure the resolutions.
  2. In relation to paragraph 5(1)(d) counsel also submitted that consideration paid to procure the special resolution was paid or given in respect of the sale of shares. In State Government Insurance Office (0) v. Rees [1979] HCA 52; (1979) 144 CLR 549 at 553-4 Stephen J referred to the phrase "in respect of" as signifying some relationship between one thing and another and as capable of describing relationships over a very wide range of proximity. His Honour went on to find that the phrase must apply to the context in which it is found or as Mason J said at page 561 "the meaning to be ascribed to 'in respect of depends very much on the context in which it is found".
  3. In applying paragraph 5(1)(d) the total consideration paid or given in respect of the sale or sales under the scheme of the shares referred to in paragraph 5(1)(a) is related to the assets and liabilities of the company "immediately before the last sale time". Immediately before the last sale time means the instant before the last sale (ss.3(5)) and is to be contrasted with paragraph 5(1) (c) which uses the phrase "immediately before the first sale time". Paragraph (d) requires that a comparison is to be made between the consideration received (totalled where there is more than one vendor) and the company's net assets. That is straightforward enough and there is no basis for saying that the words "in respect of* can have the effect of including as consideration paid or given amounts which were paid or given in relation to another contract, not being a contract for or in respect of the sale of shares referred to in paragraph 5(1)(a).
  4. The question of the application of sub-section 5(1) would arise to determine whether there is a relevant vendor's taxable amount, thereby making the vendor shareholder liable for vendor's recoupment tax.
  5. However, it is not premature for the Tribunal to consider these questions in the present proceedings because of the operation of section 18 of the Act and in particular the need to find in terms of paragraph 18(1)(b) that vendor's recoupment tax is payable or the Tribunal is of the opinion that vendor's recoupment tax is payable.

44. Sub-section 18(3) provides that where a notice of assessment is served under sub-section 18(1) in relation to the liability of a company to pay company tax on a vendor shareholder, that person has the same rights under Division 2 of Part V of the Income Tax Assessment Act in relation to the liability of the company as the company has and, for the purpose of the exercise of those rights by that vendor shareholder, the provisions of Division 2 apply in like manner as they would apply to the company if the company were exercising the rights.

  1. A company assessed to income tax has the right, under Division 2, to object to such an assessment if it is dissatisfied with the assessment. Grounds for dissatisfaction clearly embrace the question as to whether there is a liability for tax in respect of the company served with the notice of assessment. Therefore, if a vendor shareholder wishes to contest that there is a liability for the tax assessed in respect of the company based upon the operation of section 18 of the Act, the shareholder has the same rights of objection and appeal as the company. Liability arises by the operation of section 18 in respect of the company, but the rights are to be exercised by the vendor shareholder.
  2. The Tribunal is not of the view that vendor's recoupment tax is payable, nor is the Tribunal of the opinion that vendor's recoupment tax is likely to become payable. There is therefore no basis for the service of the notice of assessment on the applicant as no liability exists under the Act. That is not to say that the company is not liable for the tax under the Income Tax Assessment Act but only to say that because the vendor shareholders are not liable under the Act they cannot be served and thereby made liable for vendor's recoupment tax.
  3. In this case it is appropriate to set the objection decisions aside.

The preceding twenty (20) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of the Tribunal in the matters of QT85/994 and QT85/1002.

Secretary

13/6/89
