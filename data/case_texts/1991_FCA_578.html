Re Robert James Burrowes Ex Parte: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 578; 789 Bankruptcy 91 Atc 5021; (1991) 22 Atr 885 (27 November 1991)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: ROBERT JAMES BURROWES

Ex parte: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. V P880 of 1991

FED No. 789

Bankruptcy

91 ATC 5021

(1991) 22 ATR 885

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Heerey J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - creditors' petition - non-compliance with bankruptcy notice - income tax - debtor's conscientious objection to payment of - whether in fact paid - freedom of conscience - whether payment a breach of debtor's legal duty under international law - illegal purpose - defence of necessity

Bankruptcy Act 1966

Income Tax Act 1936

Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission Act 1986

Universal Declaration of Human Rights: Article 18

HEARING

MELBOURNE

27:11:1991

Mr R.J. Burrowes in person.

Counsel for the Petitioning Creditor: Mr J. Mazurkiewicz

Solicitors for the Petitioning Creditor: Australian Government Solicitor

DECISION

This is a petition by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation who seeks a sequestration order against Mr Robert James Burrowes. The act of bankruptcy alleged is non-compliance with a bankruptcy notice dated 7 March 1991 which was based on a judgment in the Melbourne Magistrates Court for the sum of $7,729.55 for income tax payable by Mr Burrowes.

2. There is no dispute that tax was assessed in that amount and a judgment obtained or that there has not been compliance with the bankruptcy notice. Mr Burrowes, however, raised a number of grounds of opposition to the making of a sequestration order which can broadly be described as conscientious.

3. He gave by way of background some detail as to his personal involvement in the study of the causes of international conflict, famine and human suffering. His study has been supported by substantial and it would seem very onerous personal experiences in the drought and civil war in the Sudan in 1985. Also earlier this year he was present during the bombing of Baghdad. He spoke of his personal philosophy which sees non-violence as a way of life and places fundamental importance on the sacredness and unity of all life. In essence, his personal approach is that he objects to paying taxes used to finance war or the killing of human beings and he says that military taxes violate his conscience.

4. The sincerity of Mr Burrowes' beliefs are not challenged in any way. He presented an intelligent and courteous argument to the Court. Against that personal background he put forward five legal grounds of objection to the making of a sequestration order.

5. First, he said that the taxes in question had been paid. He said that he had tendered to the Taxation Office a part of his taxes, which he thought would be applicable to military expenditure, in kind in the form of shovels. This form of payment was rejected by the Taxation Office and since then he has paid the equivalent of what he regards as his "war tax" obligation to causes which accord with his conscience such as Community Aid Abroad and Amnesty International.

6. In this context, as counsel for the petitioning creditor pointed out, reg. 58 requires taxpayers to pay income tax by cash, bank notes or cheque, in other words in the ordinary form of legal tender for debts. More fundamentally, the objection of Mr Burrowes under this ground encounters the same problem as arises in relation to his other grounds to which I shall move in a moment.

7. In this country we elect Parliaments which may make laws which some people sincerely and honestly find distasteful or even abhorrent. Money may be spent for purposes of which some people most strongly disapprove. However, our system of democratic government assumes that an elected Parliament passes laws which have force in Australia and the rule of law requires that those laws be enforceable and does not give to individuals the choice of disobeying those laws because they happen to offend the individual conscience, however sincerely and rationally that conscience may be based.

8. While accepting the sincerity of Mr Burrowes' conscientious belief, other Australians may with equal sincerity believe the expenditure of public monies on the Armed Forces of this country is perfectly moral. Their views are also entitled to respect. In a democracy conflicts of this sort are resolved by the popular election of a legislature which makes laws binding on all citizens. The comment might also be made that there may be some Australian laws of which Mr Burrowes approves but which others strongly oppose, perhaps on grounds of individual conscientious belief.

9. The second ground of objection was that the taxes are not required to be paid because they involve a violation of Mr Burrowes' right to freedom of conscience under Australian and international law, and he says that he las the legal right to conscientiously object to paying taxes which are used by the Australian Government to finance military activities and the nuclear arms race. He referred to Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

10. Although that covenant appears as a schedule to the Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission Act 1986, it is not relevantly part of the domestic law of Australia, in the sense of creating by its own force legal rights and obligations enforceable in Australian courts; see s.11(1)(k) and (m). It certainly does not provide a legal ground for enabling Australian taxpayers at their individual option to withhold any part of the taxes which are otherwise payable.

11. Thirdly, Mr Burrowes said that the taxes are not required to be paid because they are a violation of his legal duty under international law. He says that international law as established at the Nuremberg War Trials made the individual responsible under international law, and obedience to superior orders was no defence to a breach of that law.

12. I can only say that the events of the Nuremberg War Trials do not seem to be applicable to the questions of the obligation of an individual taxpayer under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. There could be no suggestion that the act which the law requires of Mr Burrowes, that is to pay taxes assessed under Australian law against him, involves any breach of international law. Were that so, all Australian taxpayers would be in breach of international law.

13. Fourthly, he says that the taxes in question are not required to be paid because they are used for illegal purposes, and he says that there are treaties of international law which Australia has signed and ratified which make nuclear weapons illegal. This, however, is a matter of international relationships between nations and does not form part of the domestic law of Australia.

14. Fifthly, he says that the taxes are not required to be paid because this course of action will help to prevent, as he puts it, the imminent peril of a nuclear holocaust. He relies on the law of necessity. But the law to which he refers, and in particular some cases he cited from the criminal law, are clearly concerned with Australian domestic law and the defence of necessity as a defence to a charge of a criminal offence. This principle of law does not extend to enable persons assessed with tax to opt out of their taxation obligations because they disagree, however strongly and sincerely, with the use to which the Government may put their tax money or anybody else's tax money.

15. I have no option therefore but to find against the grounds which Mr Burrowes has raised in opposition to the petition. There will therefore be a sequestration order.

16. I order that a sequestration order be made against the estate of the debtor. I order that the petitioning creditor's costs of and incidental to the petition in this matter, including reserved costs, be taxed and paid in accordance with the Bankruptcy Act 1966. The date of commission of the act of Bankruptcy is 8 April 1991.
