CATCHWORDS

Taxation - Income Tax - s.79A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - rebates for residents of isolated areas - taxpayer employed on an off-shore natural gas platform on the North-West shelf - whether resident in special area of Zone A - whether platform is situated in Zone A - intent and effect of s.6AA - application of Sea Installations Act 1987 - whether the platform is part of the "mainland" or an "island" within the meaning of Part 1 of Schedule 2 - meaning of "mainland" and "island" - Commissioner's discretion in s.79A(3E) not applicable as platform is not within Zone A.

HEARING

PERTH

9:10:1992

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

The applicant seeks review of a decision of the respondent rejecting his claim for a zone rebate, pursuant to s.79A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act") in relation to the year ended 30 June 1989. His objection pursuant to s.185 of the Assessment Act was disallowed on 17 May 1990. He now appeals to this Tribunal. Broadly stated, the issue is whether by virtue of his employment on an off-shore natural gas platform on the North-West shelf, he can be considered to be a person resident in Zone A established by s.79A.

2. Mr J R Edmunds, an accountant, appeared for the applicant and Dr C J Carr, of counsel, appeared for the respondent. The applicant gave evidence before the Tribunal. Findings of material facts

3. The facts in this matter are not in dispute and are as follows. The applicant was employed as a mechanical fitter by Woodside Offshore Petroleum Pty Ltd ("Woodside") under the Hydrocarbons and Gas Maintenance Employees Award 1988. For the year in question he worked offshore on the North Rankin Platform. His shifts required him to work 12 hours per day for a two week period, followed by an off duty period of two weeks. He resided in Perth and travelled by air from Perth to Karratha and then to the North Rankin Platform by helicopter. During the years ended 30 June 1988 and 30 June 1989 the taxpayer was on the Platform for 144 days and 145 days, respectively. All meals and accommodation whilst offshore and travel to and from the rig were provided by Woodside.

4. The Platform is an offshore natural gas drilling and production platform and is located on the Continental Shelf approximately 135 km North-West of the port of Dampier. It is supported by legs which are stabilised by 32 piles embedded 120 metres into the seabed and has an operational weight of 54,000 tonnes. A pipeline of 1 metre in diameter runs the 135 Km from the Platform to storage and processing facilities on the Burrup Peninsula which are approximately 12 km by road from Dampier. The pipeline is entrenched in the seabed for most of its length. Primary and secondary production licences as well as a pipeline licence were issued to the Project's joint venturers in respect of the gas field pursuant to the Petroleum (Submerged Lands) Acts (Cth and State) 1967.

The taxpayer's submissions

10. The applicant's argument, as the Tribunal understands it, is that:

. Section 6AA of the Assessment Act "adopts" the Sea Installations

Act 1987 ("the SI Act"): see s.6AA(4)(c);

. According to s.7(2) of the SI Act, attachments to a sea

installation are themselves sea installations;

. The North Rankin Platform on which the taxpayer resided is a sea

installation that is attached by a pipeline (which is itself a

sea installation by virtue of s.6AA(5)(a)(ii) of the Assessment

Act) to the processing plant on the Burrup Peninsula, on the

mainland of Australia;

. Being so joined, the Platform is therefore, or should be treated

as if it were, "part of Australia" by virtue of s.6AA(1)(a) and

(e) of the Assessment Act;

. As the Burrup Peninsula is categorised as Zone A, the Platform,

being attached to the Peninsula, is itself within Zone A.

11. Mr Edmunds further contended that the "spirit" of s.79A was such that persons living in conditions such as the taxpayer should not be distinguished from other persons living in close proximity who are entitled to the benefits of a Zone A rebate. He submitted that in the event that the Tribunal finds the Platform to be outside the special area of Zone A, the Commissioner's discretion under s.79A(3E) to treat an area in Zone A "adjacent to or in close proximity to the special area" as itself within the special area of that zone, should be exercised.

The respondent's submissions

12. The respondent made detailed submissions in answer to the applicant's objection. In summary form they were as follows:

(1) Section 6AA of the Assessment Act is not relevant to s.79A since

it is concerned with ensuring that activities related to the

environment, or the exploration or exploitation of natural

resources off-shore is subject to taxation. It is not concerned

with the extension of the Zone A concession to off-shore areas.

(2) Should s.6AA be relevant, it merely operates to make the Platform

a "part of Australia" (s.6AA(1)(e)) which is of no consequence to

the application of s.79A.

(3) By virtue of s.79A(3D) of the Assessment Act, for an area to be

considered a special area of Zone A, it must firstly be within

Zone A. Section 79A(4) defines that Zone as "the area described

in Part I of Schedule 2" of that Act. That part refers to

"portion(s) of the mainland of Australia", "the islands forming

part of Australia" and several specified islands and territories.

(4) The North Rankin Platform could not be considered part of the

"mainland" of Australia within the meaning of Part I of Schedule

2. Moreover, even if the pipeline be a sea installation by

virtue of s.6AA(5) of the Assessment Act, were that section

relevant, and the platform part of that sea installation by

virtue of s.7(1) of the SI Act, the pipeline's attachment to the

Burrup Peninsula could not have the effect of making the Platform

or pipeline part of Zone A. An attachment to the mainland is not

of itself sufficient to enable the Platform to be described as

the "mainland".

(5) The only other means by which the Platform could fall within Zone

A is if it were considered to be an "island" under Part I of the

Schedule. The North Rankin Platform could not be said to be an

"island" within the ordinary or internationally recognised

meaning of the term.

(6) Therefore, the Commissioner's discretion under s.79A(3E) to treat

a point in Zone A or B that is not in the special area of that

Zone as being a point in that special area, does not arise as the

Platform is not within Zone A.

Discussion of issues

13. The applicant as a first step in establishing a bridge between the SI Act and the Assessment Act, relies on s.7(2) of the SI Act (see above). He contends, as the Tribunal understands his argument, that:

(a) the Rankin Platform is a sea installation, and (b) because it is attached to a pipeline which is itself a

sea installation, and

(c) is thereby joined to the Burrup Peninsula, and (d) the Burrup
Peninsula is in Zone A as defined by the

Assessment Act,

it should also be regarded as, or treated as, falling within that Zone.

14. If that were the only basis for arguing that the applicant is entitled to the benefit of the Zone A concession established by s.79A of the Assessment Act, it must be taken to fail at the threshold. Section 7(2) is prefaced by the word: "For the purposes of this Act" (ie. the SI Act). The simple truth is that, for the purposes of that Act, neither the North Rankin Platform nor the connected pipeline falls within the definition of "sea installation" in s.4(1) of that Act. This is firstly because the definition of sea installation in s.4(1)(a) only applies to man-made structures that "can be used for an environment related activity". Neither the platform nor the pipeline can be said to relate to environmental activities. Further, and more definitively, the platform and pipeline are both excluded, expressly, from the definition of "sea installation" insofar as that definition does not include "(k) a resources industry fixed structure" nor "(m) a resources industry mobile unit". Those expressions are dealt with in s.4(2) and (3) respectively. Section 4(2) (see above) quite specifically covers the case of the pipeline. The Platform, to the extent that it is fixed and not able to be moved "as an entity" from one place to another would also fall within that definition in s.4(2). Otherwise it will fall within the definition of a resources industry mobile unit in s.4(3). In neither case can the facility be regarded as a sea installation.

15. The matter however does not end there. As Dr Carr, for the respondent, quite fairly pointed out to the Tribunal, s.6AA(5) of the Assessment Act to some extent counters the effect of the SI Act by providing that, in effect, the platform and the pipeline shall be deemed, for the purposes of s.6AA of the Assessment Act, to be a sea installation installed in that part of the relevant "adjacent area" in which those installations are found (see s.6(1) of the SI Act). Accordingly, for the purposes of s.6AA(4)(c) of the Assessment Act they must be taken to be located in an "Installations Act adjacent area". An "adjacent area" for the purposes of the SI Act is determined in accordance with s.5(1) of that Act. That provision reads:

"5.(1) For the purposes of this Act, the adjacent area in

respect of a State...is so much of the area described in Schedule

2 to the Petroleum Act under the heading that refers to that

State... as comprises waters of the sea that:

(a) are outside the outer limits of the territorial sea

of Australia; and

(b) are either:

(i) within the Australian fishing zone; or

(ii) outside the Australian fishing zone but

within the outer limits of the continental shelf."

16. There is no dispute that the North Rankin Platform is outside the territorial sea and either within the Australian fishing zone (as determined under the Fisheries Act 1952) or if outside that zone within the outer limits of the continental shelf as defined in Article 1 of the Convention on the Continental Shelf signed at Geneva on 29 April 1958 (see s.5(1) and Schedule 1 of the Petroleum (Submerged Lands) Act 1967). More particularly, it is located in the Area designated as "adjacent to Western Australia" in Schedule 2 of the last mentioned Act.

17. In any event, even without the aid of s.6AA(5) it is clear that the Platform and the Pipeline are within a "Petroleum Act adjacent area" as defined by s.6AA(4) of the Act. The relevant adjacent area is, again, that adjacent to Western Australia as delineated in Schedule 2 of the latter Act. The consequence is that s.6AA(1) is then attracted to the operation of the Platform since it is within one or other or both of these "adjacent areas". It then follows from s.6AA(1) that "for all purposes of (the Assessment Act) relating directly or indirectly to... the exploitation of the natural resources of (the relevant) Petroleum Act.or Installations Act adjacent area... the provisions of (the Assessment) Act" have effect as if those adjacent areas "were a part of Australia".

18. Mr Edmunds, for the applicant, contends that that is sufficient to establish the applicant's eligibility to the Zone A rebate. Dr Carr, for the respondent, however, denies that that is the case and puts two major objections against the proposition that s.6AA(1) establishes the applicant's entitlement to rebate.

(a) Does s.6AA(1) apply to s.79A of the Assessment Act?

19. As a first and fundamental objection he submits that s.6AA was introduced into the Assessment Act for the purpose of making it clear that offshore exploitation of natural resources or environment-related activities were subject to the income tax laws of Australia. It was not intended to give the benefit of the rebate provided under s.79A to persons resident on offshore facilities. In furtherance of this proposition he argues that the introductory statement to s.79A, ie. "(f) or all purposes of this Act", which on first acquaintance appears to be very broad in its reach is limited by subsections (a), (b) and (c) of sub-section (1) to either environment related activities or the exploration for, or exploitation of natural resources. It therefore is concerned with matters such as the derivation of income by companies engaged in such offshore activities. It is not directed to any concessions that might be conferred on the employees of those companies. In aid of that submission, Dr Carr referred the Tribunal to the explanatory memorandum in respect of s.6AA. That memorandum summarised the intent of s.6AA as:

"Petroleum Activities on the Continental Shelf (clauses 3 and 4)

Measures complementary to the Petroleum (Submerged Lands) Act

1967-1968 will clarify Australia's taxing rights in respect of

petroleum exploration or mining and associated activities on the

continental shelf. In general, enterprises engaged in these

activities will be treated in the same way for income tax

purposes as if the activities had taken place on the Australian

mainland".

20. The Tribunal concedes that the respondent's argument is not without merits. From the language and context of s.6AA, it is clear that it is directed to ensuring that operators of facilities such as the North Rankin Platform are subject to Australian taxation. This view is supported by reference to the explanatory memorandum and the specific reference in s.6AA(1) to "enterprises engaged in (exploration or mining) activities" (emphasis added). This is in contrast to the more confined purpose expressed in s.79A which is to grant an income tax concession to residents, "not being a company or a taxpayer in the capacity of a trustee", of remote areas "in recognition of the disadvantages to which they are subject".

21. The Tribunal also recognises that the respondent's argument is not without its difficulties. For example, the addition of the words "related directly or indirectly" at the commencement of s.6AA(1) suggest that the provision should not be read too strictly or narrowly. Further, paragraph (c) of s.6AA(1) includes "purposes in relation to the application of this Act in respect of income derived from any such exploration... act, matter, circumstance or thing." Those words are apt to give the provision an expansive ambit though only to the extent they are directed to and qualify the activities of exploration and exploitation of natural resources, or those which relate to the environment.

22. As it happens, however, the Tribunal sees no need to come to a conclusion on this part of the respondent's argument. This is because, in its opinion, the applicant's claim clearly fails on the basis of the second objection advanced by Dr Carr (see respondent's submissions (2), (3), (4) and (5) above).

23. The point that the respondent makes in this respect is simple. It is that even if s.6AA of the Assessment Act operates so that the area of the adjacent area in which the Rankin Platform is found is to be treated as a part of Australia, that of itself is not sufficient to bring that location within the prescribed area of Zone A. It does not rewrite s.79A in any way nor does it alter the prescription in Schedule 2 by which the limits of that zone are to be determined. According to s.79A(4) Zone A "means the area described in Part 1 of Schedule 2". That description, in paragraph 1 refers to "all that portion of the mainland of Australia..." falling within the co-ordinates there mentioned, which inter alia, are delineated by the Western and Eastern coastlines, whilst paragraph 2 includes "all the islands forming part of Australia lying adjacent to the coastline of the portion of Australia" within those co-ordinates. (b) Is the North Rankin Platform part of the "mainland"?

24. So far as being part of the mainland is concerned, the respondent contends that, on the plain and ordinary English meaning of that term, the North Rankin Platform cannot be regarded as a portion of the mainland. Whether it be part of Australia or not is not the issue; unless it also forms part of the mainland, its being part of Australia is irrelevant.

25. Since the term "mainland" has not been defined in the Act, nor has it been the subject of any previous decisions of the Tribunal, Dr Carr submitted that the Tribunal should have regard to the dictionary meanings of that word, and in particular, the Macquarie (at p 1042) and Oxford English Dictionary (2 ed, at p 220) definitions, the latter being, relevantly:

"a. That continuous body of land which includes the

greater part of a country or territory, in contradistinction

to the portions outlying as islands or peninsulas.

...

26. The respondent also relied on the decision of the Taxation Board of Review in Case A47 69 ATC 278 as authority for the proposition that for the purposes of s.79A of the Act the North Rankin Platform could not be said to be part of the mainland of Australia. That case involved a claim for a Zone allowance by seamen who spent time on a vessel travelling throughout Australian coastal waters adjacent to a portion of land lying within Zone A. Mr P Dubout (Chairman) stated, at 279:

"Since the taxpayer was not stationed on any island lying

adjacent to Zone A for any period or periods sufficient to bring

him within para.(b) of the definition, it will be seen that the

real crux of the problem is what is meant by the word "mainland".

For the taxpayer to succeed, it is necessary to regard the

mainland as extending to include the Australian coastal waters

through which the vessel is moving during more than one-half of

the year of income. Whether it be sought to justify such an

extension on the basis of the existence of the continental shelf,

or the extent of territorial waters or otherwise, I do not think

that it can be supported on the ordinary acceptation of the word

"mainland", and in the absence of any definition, it is the

ordinary meaning to which the Board has to look. I think the

common understanding of the word is well expressed by the Random

House Dictionary which defines it as "the principal land of a

country, region etc., as distinguished from adjacent islands".

27. In the same case, Mr G Thompson, (Member) stated (at 280):

"...the term "mainland" is to be construed in its

ordinary sense as meaning the continent of Australia as

opposed to the islands lying off the coast. Indeed on this

aspect I note that in Part III of the Second Schedule

defining Zone B it was found desirable to make specific

mention of Tasmania - which would not ordinarily be classed

as part of the mainland".

28. He stated further:

"On this construction the mainland ceases at the

coastline and does not include either coastal waters or

islands..."

29. The Tribunal accepts the reasoning of the Board in Case A47. It agrees with the respondent's submissions and is of the view that the term "mainland" in Schedule 2 should be given its ordinary and natural meaning. The Tribunal is therefore unable to read "mainland" as extending to include a gas platform attached to the Continental Shelf, some 135 km offshore. To make such a finding would distort the meaning of the term, as ordinarily understood.

(c) Is the Platform an "island"?

30. The other possibility, namely that the Platform can be regarded as an island, is also not tenable. In fact Mr Edmunds did not develop any submissions to that end.

31. The ordinary meaning of "island" is defined by the Oxford English Dictionary (at p 110) as: "1.a. A piece of land completely surrounded by water". For the purposes of international law, Article 10 of the Convention on the Territorial Sea and the Contiguous Zone 1958, which appears in Schedule I of the Seas and Submerged Lands Act 1973, defines it as follows:

"1. An island is a naturally-formed area of land,

surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide.

2. ..."

32. The Full Supreme Court of Western Australia in Chen Yin Ten v Little (1976) 11 ALR 353 accepted that the definition in Article 10 was appropriate for application in Australian municipal law. Commenting on Article 10, C R Symmonds, The Maritime Zones of Islands in International Law, states (at p 24):

"As will be seen, in the case of installations designed to

exploit the seabed, there is no possibility of such structures

coming within the existing definition of "islands" in Article 10

of the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Territorial Sea, because

they are obviously not "naturally-formed" areas of land."

33. Similarly N Papadakis in The Legal Regime of Artificial Islands (Sijthoff, Leyden, 1977) at p 93, states:

"It is clear from this formulation that artificial islands,

however composed... do not qualify for the juridical status of an

island, as this is understood in Article 10."

34. This view is also consistent with Article 5, paragraph 4 of the Convention on the Continental Shelf 1958 which reads:

"4. Such installations and devices (necessary for exploration

of the continental shelf and the exploitation of its natural

resources - see sub-paragraph 2) though under the jurisdiction of

the coastal State do not possess the status of islands. They

have no territorial sea of their own, and their presence does not

affect the delimitation of the territorial sea of the coastal

State."

35. Similarly, Article 60 paragraph 8 of the Law of the Sea Treaty 1982 provides that artificial islands, structures, installations and structures do not possess the status of islands. Whilst the latter treaty has not been incorporated into Australian municipal law, a domestic tribunal such as the AAT should be loathe to adopt an interpretation of the word "island" in Schedule 2, Part 1, paragraph 2 of the Assessment Act that is inconsistent with current international usage (Salomon v Commissioners of Customs and Excise (1967) 2 QB 116).

36. In an oral decision of this Tribunal in application WT86/4432, 12 May 1988, Deputy President C J Bannon QC rejected the notion that an offshore gas platform situated 138 km North West of Karratha was an "island" within the meaning of Part 1 of Schedule 2 of the Act. He said:

"The other interesting point that (the taxpayer) has raised is

the argument that because the gas rig was embedded in the sea it

was in effect a fixture in the sea in an area over which

Australia claims sovereignty and the High Court has held that

this country has sovereignty over the adjacent seabed, that

because it is embedded in the sea it has become an island. Much

as I admire (the taxpayer's) ingenuity in putting this argument I

am afraid that I am unable to accept it. It seems to me that

there are some permanent structures embedded in the seabed which

could conceivably in the course of time become islands as we know

them. The typical and obvious example of course is the coral

reef extending along the North Eastern shelf of Australia.

But to be an island, in my opinion, involves more than having

some poles or legs in the seabed. It involves a permanent land

mass which is attached to the seabed and not simply a man-made

structure of the type of an oil rig."

(taken from transcript)

37. It would be straining the English language too far to include in the expression "island" a man-made structure such as the North Rankin platform which is designed for removal once its purpose has been achieved and indeed whose removal may be ordered pursuant to s.108 of the Petroleum (Submerged Lands) Act.

Applicant not entitled

38. The North Rankin Platform is neither part of the mainland nor an island. It is therefore not situated in Zone A. It follows that for the purposes of s.79A(3D) of the Act, the taxpayer did not reside in Zone A so as to be entitled to rebate. It also follows that he did not reside in a "special area" of that zone since the latter must also be "within" the zone.

39. That fact cannot be changed by recourse to the Commissioner's discretion in s.79A(3E). Section 79A(3E) quite expressly speaks of the Commissioner's discretion to treat a point "in Zone A" that is not in the special area as being in the special area of that zone. It is not open to the Tribunal to exercise the discretion in s.79A(3E) unless the applicant's point of residence is itself within the zone to start with. It cannot be used to bring into the zone something that is without the zone, albeit nearby or adjacent.

40. That being so, the applicant's contention that the Commissioner should have exercised his discretion under s.79A(3E) to treat the North Rankin Platform as being located at a point in the special area of Zone A cannot succeed.

41. Mr Edmunds for the applicant, expressed the view that it would be unfair and inequitable if this application were to fail, given that workers on the Burrup Peninsula are entitled to a zone rebate. Whether that be so or not, the Tribunal is not at liberty to misconstrue the legislation in order to overcome any perceived inequities it might create. The proper avenue to seek redress lies elsewhere.

Decision

42. For the above reasons, the decision under review is affirmed.
