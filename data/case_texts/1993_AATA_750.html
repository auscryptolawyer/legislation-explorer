Singleton and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 750 (22 December 1993)

Last Updated: 27 August 2008





ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - election to preserve benefits more than 21 days after retirement - discretion whether to recognise election 8 years after retirement - whether desirable in the circumstances - principles applicable to discretion to extend time - applicant had been given adequate information about available options - refusal to recognise late election - decision affirmed



Superannuation Act 1976 ss.137(1), 157(1)

Superannuation Act 1922

REASONS FOR DECISION



22 December 1993 Mrs J R Dwyer, Senior Member

Mr J Brassil AM, Member

Mr B H Pascoe, Member



1. This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") made on 8 November 1991 and confirmed on reconsideration on 12 May 1993. The delegate, under s157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), declined to direct that an election made by the applicant on 18 June 1990 under s137(1) of the Act be treated as if it had been made within the 21 day period specified in that subsection.



2. At the hearing of this application, Mr Singleton appeared in person and the respondent was represented by Ms Munoz. The Tribunal had before it the documents ("the T documents") lodged pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 together with the exhibits tendered by the parties during the hearing. Mr Singleton gave evidence. The respondent called Mr Cameron and Mr Herbstreit, both former employees of Telecom.



3. The relevant legislative provisions are s137(1) and s157(1). Section 137(1) reads as follows:

's137(1) A person who -

. . .

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee . . . may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.'



Subsection 157(1) of the Act contains a general power to recognise an election made after the prescribed time in certain circumstances. It provides:

's157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.'



5. The facts in this matter are not in dispute. Mr Singleton became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established by the Superannuation Act 1922 on 21 August 1958 whilst employed by the Postmaster-General's Department in Queensland as a Technician-In-Training. He was an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Act at its commencement on 1 July 1976.



6. On 7 September 1978 the applicant wrote to the Retirement Benefits Office ("RBO") requesting advice about his entitlements should he retire. The relevant parts of the letter are as follows:

'Would you please advise me the amount I would receive should I resign from the service and how this amount is made up; would it include the surplus expected to be distributed in December?



As well as the above, your advice as to Preservation Benefits entitlements should I cease employment with the Commonwealth and commence in the Christian ministry would be appreciated.



Should I make an election under Preservation Benefits can this election be changed at some future date?'



7. In reply to this request, the preservation section of the RBO sent Mr Singleton a three page letter dated 18 October 1978 (T10) providing details of the three possible forms of preserved benefit, namely a deferred benefit, a transfer value or a refund of contributions. The letter also gave some provisional estimates of the amounts of preserved benefits based on an assumed resignation date of 14 September 1978. It included the following information about a deferred benefit:

'Details of the respective benefits which may be applicable in your case, based on an assumed resignation date of 14 Sep 1978, are as follows:-



(i) A deferred benefit on attaining 60 years of age of approximately $6922.16 per annum or, on election, a government financed pension of approximately $4847.80 per annum plus a refund of accumulated contributions amounting to $20,743.64.



The exact value of the deferred benefit cannot be calculated in advance because the amount representing accumulated basic contributions, at the date on which the deferred benefit eventually becomes payable, is subject to variations in the earning rate of the Fund from the date of resignation to that date. For this reason also it is not practicable to estimate the benefit on invalidity or death.'



8. On 27 April 1982 Mr Singleton resigned from his employment with effect from 10 May 1982. On the same day, he completed a form under the Act (T11). It was headed "Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights". Mr Singleton completed that form to read:

'I . . . hereby apply for a refund of accumulated contributions . . .'



He deleted the options "apply for payment of a lump sum benefit" and "elect for preservation of superannuation rights". Mr Singleton's election was witnessed by Mr Cameron, a personnel officer at Telecom.



9. Mr Singleton gave evidence that, after a period of study, he joined the Christian ministry as foreshadowed in his letter of 7 September 1978. He said that he worked for his Church from 1982 until he resigned in October 1988. A short time later he rejoined Telecom as a Technician and again became an eligible employee.



10. On 18 June 1990 Mr Singleton, by letter to the Commissioner (T13), sought approval to make a late election to preserve his superannuation benefits relating to his prior service. In response to a letter from the Commissioner dated 17 September 1990 (T14), Mr Singleton wrote again on 8 October 1990 (T15) stating:

'To the best of my knowledge, when I resigned from Telecom, apart from completing the necessary forms, I received no advice regarding the alternatives available to me.



In fact it is only because of the necessity to compare the current C.S.S. with the new T.S.S., that I have now come to understand and appreciate something of the preservation benefits, particularly the employer funded benefit, that were available to me at the time of my resignation.'



11. Mr Singleton said in evidence that he only became aware of the true value of the preservation option, in particular the value of the employer contributions, when it was explained during staff seminars and in pamphlets which were circulated in connection with the new Telecom Superannuation Scheme ("TSS"). In support of his evidence he tendered a number of articles and extracts from books which comment on the inadequate advice given to employees about their entitlements when leaving the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme ("CSS").



12. Mr Singleton agreed in cross-examination that the letter from the RBO of 18 October 1978 advised him about the government-funded pension, should he preserve his benefit upon resignation. However, he told the Tribunal that he could not remember sending the letter of 7 September 1978 requesting advice about superannuation, nor could he remember receiving the letter of 18 October 1978 from the RBO. He said that he had only become aware of them when the RBO refused his application and referred to the letters in correspondence. He agreed that this did not mean that he had not received the letter of 18 October 1978. He said that in the period 1979-1980 he had experienced a number of upheavals which he believed had caused him to forget the correspondence. He explained that he had had a major operation in early 1979 and that in May 1980 he and his family had moved from Queensland to Melbourne. After moving to Melbourne he continued to work for Telecom although with different duties. He also took on new responsibilities with his Church.



13. Mr Singleton said at the time of completing the form on his retirement in 1982 his understanding was that preservation of benefits was not an attractive option. Asked by Ms Munoz on what he based this opinion, he said there was a "general understanding among us". He said he had not made any further enquiries at that stage about preservation, although he conceded that there would have been no difficulty in making enquiries should he have wished to do so.



14. Ms Munoz called two witnesses to give evidence about the advice available to intending retirees in 1982 when Mr Singleton made his election. Mr Cameron was the personnel officer who witnessed Mr Singleton's signature on 27 April 1982. He said that it was his responsibility to interview people resigning from Telecom. Although he could not remember Mr Singleton, he explained to the Tribunal that his procedure regarding superannuation was to go through the appropriate form with a resignee and tell him to cross out the options he did not want. The Tribunal asked Mr Cameron if he ever gave advice about the different options available. Mr Cameron said all the resignations he processed were people wanting their money back and that no-one had ever asked him about the other options. If anyone had asked, he said that he would either have given them some advice himself or would have referred them to the personnel and industrial relations unit which was situated in the same building. Mr Cameron said he kept up-to-date with superannuation matters by reading booklets and circulars. He said that a copy of every new circular was sent to each telephone exchange.



15. Ms Munoz also called Mr Herbstreit who was officer in charge of staff separations at Telecom from 1979-1988. He said that he was the "person most knowledgeable about superannuation in Telecom at the time". He had no direct role in providing information on superannuation options but said from time to time he would speak about "separation movements" and how to process application forms at staff clerk training seminars. Staff were instructed that if they received any queries about separation, they should direct those queries through to Mr Herbstreit's department. If Mr Herbstreit or his staff were unable to handle the query, then it would be directed on to the preservation department of the RBO. The Tribunal asked Mr Herbstreit whether he would advise people to preserve their benefits in certain circumstances. Mr Herbstreit said that he had only had the occasional query about preservation in response to which he had told the enquirer how preservation worked and suggested they seek further financial advice.



16. On this evidence we are satisfied that, had Mr Singleton asked about his preservation benefits, he would have been referred to Mr Herbstreit and possibly to the preservation department of the RBO. However, Mr Singleton, no doubt because he realised or was advised that the RBO staff were the experts in the field, had written to that office himself when, in 1978, he wanted information about superannuation and the possibility of preserving his benefits. As already set out, he received a detailed response to his letter of enquiry.



17. In exercising the discretion under s157(1) the Commissioner must be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised". In Re Liddle and the Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 14 AAR 456 the Tribunal considered the meaning of the word "desirable" and suggested that it connotes a "positive aspiration" and "something worthy of achievement" rather than "merely advisable". To assess what was desirable in the context of s157(1), the Tribunal considered the Act as a whole and identified the objects it seeks to achieve. It concluded that a late election should be recognised only if it would benefit the person making it while, at the same time, not adversely affecting the payment of pensions to other beneficiaries under the Act and not impeding the efficient and proper administration of the superannuation scheme. In balancing these interests, the Tribunal suggested that it can only be desirable to recognise a late election if there are "objectively good reasons" justifying non-compliance with the statutory time limit.



18. The importance of the statutory time limit was again identified in Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No 8253, delivered 26 February 1993) (at pp21-22) as the "paramount principle":

'The fact that 21 days is prescribed as an appropriate time within which to make an election indicates that discretion to extend this time is to be limited by reference to the relatively short time period prescribed. The longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and making a late election, the heavier the onus the applicant bears.'



In considering whether sufficient reasons existed for the discretion to be exercised in the applicant's favour, the Tribunal in Lander set out and relied on the principles enunciated by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd & Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 58 ALR 305, 7 ALD 315, when considering an extension of time to commence proceedings under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1975.



19. The principles set out in Hunter Valley were summarised by the Tribunal in Re CSIRO and Barbara (1987) 11 ALD 447 at p450 as follows:

'(a) It is pre-condition to the exercise of the discretion that the applicant for extension show "an acceptable explanation of the delay" and that it is "fair and equitable in the circumstances" to extend time.



(b) Action taken by the applicant which makes the decision-maker (and presumably also the other party to the decision) aware that he contests the finality of the decision is relevant. If the applicant has "rested on his rights" this may operate against the exercise of the discretion.



(c) Prejudice to the respondent is a material factor militating against the grant of an extension.



(d) The mere absence of prejudice is not enough to justify the grant of an extension. A delay which may result in the unsettling of other people or of established practices is likely to prove fatal to the application.



(e) The merits of the substantial application are properly to be taken into account in considering whether an extension of time should be granted.



(f) Considerations of fairness as between the applicant and other persons in a like position are relevant.' (emphasis added)



The Full Federal Court in Comcare v A'Hearn (unreported, 12 October 1993, No 811/93), another case concerning an extension of time in which to commence proceedings, has since stated that there is no rule that an "acceptable explanation for the delay" is an essential precondition to an application for extension of time, but that it nevertheless remains "a relevant matter to be considered".



20. What constitutes an "acceptable explanation" for delay has been considered in a number of decisions concerning s157(1) of the Act since Liddle and Lander (Re Morgan and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No 8584, delivered 12 March 1993), Re Charles and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 244; (1993) 18 AAR 61, Re Boardman and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1993) 18 AAR 70, Re Chalk and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No 8954, delivered 2 September 1993) and Re Allen and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No 9018, delivered 30 September 1993)). The following factors have been identified as relevant: whether the applicant knew of his or her right to preserve superannuation benefits, whether the applicant made enquiries, the opportunities available to make such enquiries taking into account the applicant's education level, health and language difficulty, any family or personal circumstances that might impinge upon the applicant's opportunity to make enquiries, the location of the place of employment of the applicant at the time when the election was made, and the information about preservation of benefits available at the time.



21. In both Charles and Boardman late elections have been recognised. In Charles the applicant had received a memorandum attached to the form S2A which said "enclosed is an application for refund of your accumulated contributions. Please complete and return to the Superannuation Clerk." The Tribunal found this memorandum to be misleading and directive. The Tribunal found that, at the time of his resignation, the applicant was isolated and suffering from stress and concluded "If fault exists it lay with the respondent which had not taken reasonable steps to advise the applicant adequately of his rights".



22. The Tribunal in Boardman expressed reservations about earlier Tribunal decisions on the point and in particular about the view that there should be "a duty of enquiry" about superannuation entitlements at the time of retirement. It referred to passages from the reasons for decision of Hill J in A'Hearn v Comcare (1993) 18 AAR 22 (affirmed on appeal) where his Honour said, at p24:

'Care must be taken . . . to ensure that there is not a slavish adherence to the matters which are referred to in [Hunter Valley], which are listed merely for guidance . . .'



and at p26:

'At the end of the day the discretion of a court or tribunal given power to extend time will be a wide one, to be exercised as the justice of the case may require.'



The Tribunal in Boardman said at p72:

'On the basis that the discretion under s 157 of the Act is as wide as the discretion provided for in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 (Cth), what Hill J had to say in A'Hearn (supra) is very persuasive, if not binding. The discretion contained in s 157, which provides that the discretion should be exercised if "the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized", should not be fettered by the laying down of principles such as was done in Lander (supra). . . .



This Tribunal is of the view that the nature of the superannuation legislation, and the poor level of knowledge amongst personnel staff as to superannuation rights, in particular as to preservation, which was clearly evidenced throughout these entire proceedings, is such that it is far too onerous to place any sort of "duty of inquiry" upon an applicant who has not been adequately informed or who, as in this case, has been misinformed as to their rights. . . .



We are of the view that employees who resign and are contributors to the CSS, should be given adequate information by personnel officers and that nothing less than an estimate of the benefits available at age 55 . . . placed in front of the resigning employee would satisfy the criteria that a person be properly informed as to his or her rights.



We find that Mr Boardman was not given these details. . . .



We are of the view that the nature of the discretion which we have to exercise involves determining whether it is desirable in all of the circumstances to allow the applicant to make a late election for preservation of benefits. On the authority of A'Hearn and Hunter Valley (supra), this involves a determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that extension is granted.'

23. An appeal to the Federal Court has been lodged in Boardman. It is however not necessary for us to express a preference for the approach in Charles and Boardman or for that in Liddle and Lander. On the facts we are satisfied that Mr Singleton's letter of 7 September 1978 demonstrated an awareness of the need to consider the superannuation options open to him and indicated a methodical approach to such matters. The RBO's letter of 18 October 1978 informed him in detail of the right to preserve his superannuation benefits. With this information, he completed, in 1982, the form in which he elected to take the option of a refund of his accumulated contributions. From the way in which Mr Singleton gave his evidence, the Tribunal is satisfied that he is an intelligent and well-educated man who made sensible enquiries about the possibility of preserving his superannuation benefits in 1978. He said in evidence that he had forgotten about receiving the letter when he sought late election in 1990. We cannot accept that he had forgotten about the advice in the letter when he made his election in 1982. He received appropriate and correct information in 1978, and then made his choice. He spent his money in extending his home, which provided him with a study to assist his work in the ministry. The delay in the matter is over eight years which is a substantial period. To recognise a late election in this matter simply because of the financial advantage to Mr Singleton of doing so would be to ignore the legislative time limit and would lead to the unsettling of other people and of established practices. There is nothing in the evidence that satisfies us that it would be fair and equitable in the circumstances to extend time.



24. The Tribunal is not satisfied in all the circumstances of the case that it is desirable that Mr Singleton's election should be recognised and treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by s137 of the Act. The decision under review will be affirmed.
