Re National Trust of Australia (Nsw) and Minister of Industry and Commerce [1984] AATA 146 (10 May 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: NATIONAL TRUST OF AUSTRALIA (NSW)

And: MINISTER FOR INDUSTRY AND COMMERCE

No. N83/138

Book Bounty

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Miss C.E. Backhouse (Senior Member)

I. Prowse (Member)

G.D. Grant (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Book Bounty - refusal to approve payment - statutory interpretation.

Register and Map Supplement of National Trust - whether guide or directory within Section 3A (1) (c) of the Bounty (Books) Act 1969.

Re Butterworth Pty. Ltd. and Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs 2ALD 612.

Re Potoroo Press and Minister for Industry and Commerce (No. V82/131 dated 9 May 1982)

HEARING

CANBERRA

10:5:1984

ORDER

The Tribunal decides:-

(a) the two decisions under review be set aside; and

(b) in substitution for the decisions so set aside, to approve the

payment of Book Bounty in respect of each of the two books, the subject of this review.

DECISION

This is an application by the National Trust of Australia (N.S.W.) - ("The National Trust") for a review of two decisions by the Delegate of the of the Minister for Industry and Commerce to refuse payment of a bounty under The Bounty (Books) Act, 1969 in respect of two publications of the National Trust, namely:

"Register. The National Trust of Australia (N.S.W.)" ("The Register"); and

"Register. The National Trust of Australia (N.S.W.) - Map Supplement - ("The Map Supplement").

A copy of each of these publications was received in evidence (Exhibits '1A' and '2A').

The Tribunal also had before it the documents provided by the respondent under S.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 which showed that the applications for bounty were made by Mastercraft Printing Pty. Ltd. in respect of the Register and by Pink Panther Instant Printing Pty. Ltd. in respect of the Map Supplement. In each case the decision to refuse a bounty was conveyed to the printers by letters dated 12 and 13 January, 1983 and in each the reason given was to the the effect that the book was a directory or guide and as such was not eligible to attract a bounty. (Documents T8 and T13).

Mr. Rofe, Q.C. who, with Mr. J. McLaughlin, appeared for the applicant stated, in opening, that he saw as the principal issue before the Tribunal the question of whether the subject publications were a directory, guide or similar publication. A possible second issue was whether the National Trust was a person whose interests are affected by the decisions here in question. Mr. Lysewycz, who appeared for the respondent, stated that there was a third issue and that was whether the two volumes, i.e., the Register and the Map Supplement, should be considered in fact as one work.

As the hearing proceeded, it became apparent that the second issue - the standing of the National Trust - would not be in dispute as each of the printers had appointed the National Trust as his agent to receive the bounty (Exhibit 'F' re Mastercraft). In our view the third issue did not arise because a separate application for a bounty and a separate decision had been made in respect of each publication and Counsel for the applicant was content to present his case on the footing that it involved a review of two separate decisions.

Mr. Rofe called one witness, Mr. W. Richards, who gave his formal training as Journalism and whose present position in the National Trust was Director, Publicity and Marketing, a position he has held for about 11 years. Mr. Richards gave a brief history of the formation of the National Trust and outlined its objects. These are outlined on Page 1 of the Annual Report of the Trust for 1981-82 (Exhibit 'B'). The National Trust was formed by a small group of residents in Sydney in 1945 and its membership was opened to the public in 1947. The Trust is now a body corporate pursuant to The National Trust Act, 1960 of the N.S.W. Parliament - the objects of the Trust are set out in Section 4 of the Act and are summarised in the Annual Report as follows:

. "To acquire, protect and preserve for the benefit of the public, lands and building of beauty or of national, historic, scientific, architectural or cultural interests;

. to safeguard natural features and senic landscape and conserve wildlife;

. to encourage and promote public appreciation, knowledge and enjoyment of these things".

In pursuance of these objects, the National Trust, among its other activities, -

. Surveys the natural and man-made environment, identifying and documenting those landscapes, townscapes, historic building, industrial sites, burial grounds and other items it considers part of the State's enviromental heritage.

. Maintains its Register of such places and items.

We were told by Mr. Richards that this latter reference is to the two publications which are the subject of the bounty applications under review. The National Trust first published its Register in book form in 1967 and there have been four editions since then including the current edition. The Trust has continued to publish its Register as a kind of complement to the Register of the Heritage Council of New South Wales. The Heritage Council is empowered to keep a Register only of what it considers to be the heritage of New South Wales and over which there are protective orders under its own legislation - The Heritage Act 1979 (N.S.W.). This Act established the Heritage Council on which the National Trust has a statutory position. The Trust and the Council work closely together and the Heritage Council provides a grant to assist in the publication of the National Trust's Register.

Continuing his evidence, Mr. Richards informed us that the essential purpose of the Register and the Map Supplement is to identify buildings, places and other items which the National Trust considers part of the heritage. The books are made available to each local Government Authority in New South Wales, in addition to several Government planning authorities to indicate to them the items which the National Trust considers should be conserved or placed under consideration for conservation. The Register also indicates the places in relation to which the National Trust will take action.

Asked what were the available options open to the Trust, Mr. Richards said:

"Firstly, when the Trust decides to list an item the owner is told that the Trust is taking this action and the owner is informed that the National Trust considers the item to be part of the heritage. Then the local authorities are more than likely informed of each new listing in the Register. If an item which is listed in the Register is threatened in any way the National Trust has a number of courses of action open to it. It may simply contact the owner and try to discuss the threatened action. The National Trust can report the threatened action to the Heritage Council, which might lead to other actions taking place, or in more dire situations the National Trust might introduce a public campaign to try and prevent some action occurring which would destroy or damage the listed item".

As to the places listed in the Register, Mr. Richards explained that the great majority of the listings are privately owned properties and their inclusion in the Register should not be interpreted as allowing any right of access to members of the public. This is also specifically stated in the Foreword to the Register at page (iii).

Turning to the Map Supplement, Mr. Richards said the National Trust had been asked by the Heritage Council to include in the Register maps indicating conservation areas. Mr. Richards, as the planner of the Register, decided that one volume would be too bulky if it had to contain the maps as well as the other information currently in the Register. The maps in the Map Supplement are not intended to guide a person to a particular place, but merely to outline and define conservation areas. A number of examples from the Register were cited as listed properties which had no addresses in the sense that someone could be guided to them:

. Great Western Highway - Sweet Briar (P.158).

. Lade Vale - Slab Cottage (P.216).

. Lake Bathurst - Barn (P.216).

However, in cross-examination by Mr. Lysewycz, Mr. Richards did concede that any person wishing to find the actual location of the above items could do so by enquiring at the National Trust Office.

The number of Registers produced was 2,000 whilst there were only 1,000 copies of the Map Supplement. In explaining this, Mr. Richards said that the Map Supplement was a cheaper production than the Register itself and it would be much less expensive to reproduce additional copies if any were required. The Register could be used without the Map Supplement but the converse was not the case - the Map Supplement needs to be read with the Register.

No witnesses were called by the respondent.

The relevant provisions of the Bounty (Books) Act 1969 ("The Act") are:

"3A. (1) Bounty is not payable in respect of:

(a) . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

(b) . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

(c) A book that is a directory, guide or timetable or similar

publication relating, in whole or in substantial part, to Australia or place or places in Australia;

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

9A. Where an application for bounty in respect of a book is lodged

in accordance with the Regulations, the Minister shall:

(a) if he is satisfied that bounty is payable in respect of that book - approve the payment of the bounty; or

(b) if he is not so satisfied - refuse to approve payment of the bounty".

There are other provisions in the Act relating to "manufacturer", "production process" and "registered premises" but these provisions are not in issue in this review. Nor was it disputed that the applications for bounty were made in accordance with the Regulations.

The issue before the Tribunal therefore is whether either the Register or the Map Supplement is a directory, guide or similar publication to attract the exclusionary provision of paragraph (c) in Section 3A (1) of the Bounty (Books) Act 1969.

Mr. Rofe submitted that, on a proper construction of the words of Section 3A (1) (c), neither the Register nor the Map Supplement was a directory or a guide and, by application of the "ejusdem generis" rule neither was a similar publication. He submitted that the issue is what is meant by the words "directory" and "guide". Mr. Rofe referred us to the decision of Mr. R.K. Todd (now Mr. Deputy President Todd) in re Butterworth Pty. Ltd. and Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs 2 ALD 612 for the correct approach to the construction of words of an Act which limit, restrict or take away a benefit granted. In that decision at page 619 Mr. Todd said:

" . . . . where there is a doubt as to the extent of the objects of bounty the description of those objects should not be construed narrowly and the delineation of the exceptions from the grant should not be construed widely"

However Mr. Lysewycz was to submit that before the Tribunal or a Court was to apply the approach adopted by Mr. Todd, there would have to be "a real doubt as to the proper construction of the legislation" in question. In other words that such an approach is only permissible as an aid to construction where there is ambiguity or difficulty in construing a provision. He further submitted that Paragraph 3A (1) (c) is not ambiguous, rather it is quite clear and that there is no need to depart from the ordinary English meaning of the words in the context in which they appear in the legislation. He then referred us to the decision in Pap Printers & Publishers Pty. Ltd. and the Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs [1982] FCA 148; (42 ALR 377) and in particular to a passage in the judgement of Smithers J. at 380 where he said:

"The nature of the Minister's function is to be ascertained from the terms of the Act considered by reference to the purpose of the Act as disclosed thereby. That purpose is readily discernible. It is, I think of critical significance in relation to the problem before the court. It is a single and positive purpose, namely to encourage Australian publishers, whoever and wherever they are in Australia to have books they propose to publish, manufactured by Australian printers, whoever and wherever they are in Australia, provided that pursuant to Section 11 of the Act the premises where such printers produce books have been registered by the Minister. This purpose is manifest in the bounty scheme set up by the Act. Money is to be paid to manufacturers of books so that they can charge prices which, hopefully, will enable publishers to sell books manufactured in Australia in competition with books manufactured outside Australia."

He further submitted that it is too narrow and frivolous to say simply it is a giving Act, and if in doubt, give out. He accordingly went on to submit that even if the Tribunal were faced with an ambiguity, it is not simply enough to adopt the rules set down in Re Butterworth Pty. Ltd and Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs (supra) but there is a further consideration that has to be had regard to, and that is the purpose of the legislation.

We were also invited by Mr. Rofe to consider the meaning given to the words "directory" and "guide" taken from the Oxford English Dictionary. These were as follows:

"Directory" 1. Something that serves to direct; guide especially a book of rules or directions.

3. A book containing one or more alphabetical lists of the inhabitants of any locality with their addresses and occupations.

"Guide" 4. In the titles of books;

(a) A book of instructions on or information for beginners or

novices.

(b) A book of information on places or objects of interest in a locality, city, building etc.; a Guide Book.

He also invited us to consider the meaning of the word "register" taken from the Shorter Oxford Dictionary.

"Register" 1. A book in which regular entry is made of details of any kind sufficiently important to be exactly recorded; a written record thus formed; a list or catalogue.

Mr. Rofe submitted that it is the purpose of the book or document which must be looked at to ascertain under which category it comes and that the purpose of the Register and the Map Supplement is as stated in the introduction to the Register:

"to alert responsible authorities, property owners and the public so that those concerned may adopt measures to preserve the special qualities which prompted the classification".

of properties by the National Trust and identified in the Register. He submitted that a directory or guide are published for the purpose of directing or guiding one to something or some person or some place either physically or geographically, and they are not in any sense published for a purpose which is the purpose stated in the Register. The predominant object of the Register is a listing of places and localities rather than as a guide to them. The Map Supplement is no more than an apendage to the Register. He did not rely upon the intention of the National Trust although he did say its intention was consistent with the purpose of the publication in question.

Mr. Lysewycz submitted for the respondent that the Register and Map Supplement were books of a kind described in paragraph (c) of Sections 3A (1) of the Act. By listing places under consideration for conservation the Register was, if not a directory at least a guide. He referred us to the definition of "register" in the Concise Oxford Dictionary 7th Edition which is as follows:

"A book in which entries are made of details to be recorded for reference, official or authoritative list kept, for example of births, marriages and burials or deaths, of shipping, of professionally qualified persons . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . in a constituency".

He argued that a directory lists people and places; a guide lists factual information; and a timetable lists times and traffic routes and all of these may or may not include maps and photographs, etc.

The main purpose of this publication, Mr. Lysewycz submitted is to present factual information with an absence of editorial comment or creative comment. He argued the Register and the Map Supplement both fitted this description. These books are distributed to local Councils to inform them of the places classified or to be classified by the National Trust and the best description of them is a guide. He submitted even if they are not a directory or a guide, they are "a similar publication". He further submitted the intention of the National Trust is not a matter which is relevant to a construction of the Act.

Mr. Lysewycz also submitted that the Act should be seen as part of a wider scheme interrelating with the Customs Legislation. For example in the Customs Tariff, Sub Item 49.01.1 refers to:

"Australian directories, guides and timetables which attracts an import duty at the rate of 25%".

It was conceded that the two publications could not properly be described as a timetable, thus leaving the question to be determined whether they fit the description of "directory" or "guide" or similar publication. We have come to the view that the Register (exhibit 1A) is a register as distinct from a directory and a guide and that the Map Supplement (Exhibit 2A) is an appendage to the Register.

We adopt the submission of Mr. Rofe that it is the purpose of the book or publication which must be looked at and that its purpose is as stated on Page (iv) of the Register which is to alert responsible authorities, property owners and the public so that those concerned may adopt measures to preserve the special qualities which prompted the classification of the places and items by the National Trust. Having regard to this purpose we conclude that the register is not a directory because a directory does not have that purpose, nor is it a guide because a guide does not have that purpose.

Having heard the evidence of Mr. Richards and the argument submitted on behalf of the parties, we must say that we see the words of Section 3A (1) (c) of the Act as referring to what is ordinarily understood as a directory, guide or timetable. As typical examples, we would see:

. as a directory - Sands and other Business or Professional Directories.

. as a guide - the various road and street directories and guide books which are readily available in retail outlets.

. as a timetable - "Bradshaw" (beloved of Sherlock Holmes).

In that circumstance we do not find it necessary to adopt the approach of Mr. Deputy President Todd in Re Butterworths Pty. Ltd. and the Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs (supra) and we refrain from making any comment on the application of that approach to the interpretation of the Bounty (Books) Act 1969 where there is no doubt as to the proper construction of the Section 3A (1) (c).

We have concluded that, taking into account the objects of the National Trust and the purpose of the Register and Map Supplement, these two publications are not a directory, guide, a timetable or similar publication of the kind envisaged by Section 3A (1) (c). In coming to this conclusion, we have been mindful that the Register and Map Supplement do not, and are not intended to direct or guide a person to someone or some place. Such addresses as are given are sometimes insufficient to establish the precise locality of a building or place without further enquiry of the National Trust Office. Furthermore, we have noted the warning in the Register that many of the buildings and other places included therein are privately owned and that their inclusion must not be interpreted as allowing any right of acess to the public. Such a warning note would not, we believe, fit in with what is ordinarily accepted as a guide or directory.

Having regard to the view that we have taken as to the proper construction of the words "directory" and "guide" we do not find it necessary to express any view as to the use to which extrinsic aids such as Industries Assistance Commission Reports, Media Releases and Second Reading Speeches may be used. We would only add that we see no need for reference to such aids in this case. We also add that although this matter was the subject of some debate by Counsel, Mr. Lysewycz submitted that the Tribunal could not have regard to those materials for the purpose of construing an Act of Parliament, a view with which Mr. Rofe did not disagree. We refer to this aspect in passing because of the inclusion in the "T" documents of material of this nature.

We also in the circumstances find it unnecessary to deal with Mr. Lysewycz's submission that the Bounty (Book) Act should be seen as part of a wider scheme wherein the provisions of the Customs Act interrelate with this Bounty legislation and we leave this open to be determined if and when it arises on a future occasion.

The Tribunal decides that:

(a) the two decisions under review be set aside; and

(b) in substitution for the decisions so set aside, to approve the

payment of Book Bounty in respect of each of the two books, the subject of this review.

I, having had the opportunity of reading the Reasons for Decision herein of Miss Cecily E. Backhouse (Senior Member) and Mr. I. Prowse (Member), am in agreement with their views as expressed in their paragraphs 1 to 19 inclusive. However, I do not agree with the conclusions given in their paragraph 20 and subsequent paragraphs.

My reasons for adopting a different conclusion largely stem from my acceptance of the rationale for the Bounty (Books) Act as enunciated by Smithers J at 380 in the decision in Pap Printers & Publishers Pty. Ltd. and the Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs [1982] FCA 148; (42 ALR 377), as quoted in paragraph 15 of the majority Reasons for Decision. I attach particular importance to the following sentences in this judgement of Smithers J:

"This purpose is manifest in the bounty scheme set up by the Act. Money is to be paid to manufacturers of books so that they can charge prices which, hopefully, will enable publishers to sell books manufactured in Australia in competition with books manufactured outside Australia".

It is clear from the evidence given on behalf of the applicant that the majority of the numbers printed of both the Register and the Map Supplement have been published for sale, rather than for providing as gifts (at retail selling prices of $15.00 and $10.00 respectively); and that, should bounty be payable for these two publications, this would enable the National Trust's retail selling prices for them to be lower then otherwise; and their volume of sales to be, therefore, at a higher rate per year than otherwise. In my opinion, this relative importance of the competitive selling prices for these two publications is crucial to deciding upon whether these publications do or do not fall within the exclusionary provision of sub-paragraph (c) in Section 3A(1) of this Act, having regard to the purpose of this Act generally, as outlined above.

In the case of these two publications that have already been sold, or will be sold in the future (that together account for the majority of these publications that were printed), evidence given on behalf of the applicant indicated that these Australian purchasers are not readily identifiable as a group; they may or may not be members of the National Trust; and their reasons for purchasing are far from clear. In my opinion, therefore, as to whether either or both of these publications is "a directory, guide or timetable or similar publication" should be determined according to the ordinary English meaning of these words, as understood by a member of the general public who may be considering their purchase, having regard to the overall nature of their contents and their respective selling prices. On this basis, having considered the various dictionary meanings of these words, as put forward on behalf of the applicant and the respondent, my finding is that each of these two publications is a book that is a "guide" within the meaning of Section 3A (1) (c) of this Act. Both of these publications present, in a concise and consistent fashion, basic factual information upon a specified subject matter; in my opinion, a member of the general public would regard them as a "guide".

It is, therefore, clear that I do not accept the submissions made on behalf of the applicant that the Tribunal should base its findings upon the objects of the National Trust generally and the National Trust's purpose in having these two publications printed. In my opinion, this latter purpose is only possibly relevant to that minority proportion of these two publications that are gifted to various local government and other authorities concerned with planning controls. The evidence on behalf of the applicant indicated that these donated copies are provided shortly after printing; that they account for about one-fifth of the number of copies of the Register and about two-fifths of the Map Supplement; and that the remaining (majority) of copies have been printed for sale to any interested private party or government authority. For this latter majority of potential purchasers, the National Trust's purpose in having these two publications printed would not necessarily have any bearing upon their motive in considering purchase. In any case, I believe that this intended purpose of the National Trust is subordinate to the purpose of this Act generally, as enunciated by Smithers J (see above).

In further examination of the applicant's contention that the National Trust's purpose in having these two publications printed is a major criterion in connection with this review, it is instructive to have regard to the Tribunal's decision in re Potoroo Press and the Minister for Industry and Commerce (No. V82/131 dated 9 May 1982), particularly since that decision dealt with a conceptually similar exclusionary provision of the same Section 3A of this Act (in that case, a childrens picture book). The Tribunal there held that the intentions of the author and publisher were not relevant to the question in issue (paragraph 16). In his concluding address for this current review, Counsel for the applicant sought to differentiate the National Trust's intention for these two publications from the National Trust's purpose for them. However, according to the ordinary English meaning of "intention" and "purpose", I do not accept that there is any significant distinction to be drawn between the meanings of these two words, that would warrant discarding the reasoning, in a very similar connection, as adopted by the Tribunal in re Potoroo Press.

Irrespective of the particular outcome of this review by the Tribunal, this application has drawn attention to an undesirable inconsistency in the description employed in sub-paragraph 3A (1) (c) of the Bounty (Books) Act, on the one hand, and the wording employed in the Customs Tariff to identify "Australian directories, guides and timetables" (sub-paragraph 49.01.100) and "Australian street directories, road guides and the like' (sub-paragraph 49.05.100), on the other hand - with a customs duty of 25% applying to both of these Tariff sub-paragraphs. In respect of books and other products of the Australian printing industry generally, the intentions of Parliament are made clear from a perusal of the second reading speeches of the then responsible Ministers when this Act was last amended by Parliament in 1979, following upon a Bill, that was passed without amendment by both Houses, after introduction into the House of Representatives on 10 May 1979. Those second reading speeches (Hansard - Representatives p 2094 - 2095 and Senate p 2782 - 2783) indicate that the Bill in question follows upon consideration of Industries Assistance Commission Report of 31 July 1978 on "Products of the Printing Industry" (Parliamentary Paper No. 284/1978, presented on 17 October 1978).

These extrinsic materials (when considered together) indicate that, for those nominated categories of products of the Australian printing industry generally for which assistance to compete upon the Australian market against imports was intended, such assistance was intended to be accorded by either a nominated customs duty payable upon imports or a nominated rate of bounty payable upon Australian production. In the case of whatever printed products may fall within the description of "Australian directories, guides and timetables", the intention was to confine such assistance to the alternative of a customs duty of 25% (i.e. with no bounty being intended). However, the discrepancy in the particular wording of Bounty (Books) Act sub-paragraph 3A (1) (c), Tariff sub-paragraph 49.01.100 and Tariff sub-paragraph 49.05.100 contributes towards what would appear to be some degree of unintended uncertainty as to whether either a custom duty or a bounty would become applicable to a particular directory, etc. (including the possibility of both becoming applicable). Appropriate amendment of the wording, in these three inter-related provisions, would seem desirable to minimize this uncertainty.
