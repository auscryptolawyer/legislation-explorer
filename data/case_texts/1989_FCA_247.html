Re James Andrew Baker v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1989] FCA 247; 20 Atr 798 (7 July 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: JAMES ANDREW BAKER

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. QLD G2327 of 1987

FED No. 641

Income Tax - Evidence

[1989] FCA 247; 20 ATR 798

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Pincus J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - derivation of income - tax avoidance schemes devised by accountant for clients - purported transactions entered into between client and accountant's company - money remained in accountant's company account - physical activities carried out by accountant - whether accountant derived the income.

Income Tax - splitting of income - taxpayer arranges scheme so that income is paid to entity controlled by him - who derives income?

Income Tax - appeal against assessment - onus on taxpayer - many transactions - trusts - new trustee appointed - documents sent away to hinder investigations - documents not produced in Court - trusts "stripped" - oral account given of effect of document - taxpayer not frank - whether onus satisfied.

Evidence - documents missing - some evidence given misleading - weight Court gives to oral account of transactions.

DECISION

This income tax appeal was instituted by a request to refer a decision of the respondent to the Supreme Court of Queensland under s.187 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the "Tax Act"). Since no hearing had been begun in that Court by 1 September 1987, the proceedings were, by virtue of s.4(3) of Act No. 23 of 1987, transferred to this Court. The appeal relates to an amended assessment in respect of the appellant's tax for the year ended 30 June 1981.

2. The tax for that year was initially assessed in the sum of $2,746.24, which was subsequently amended to $2,602.24. However, a further amended assessment was issued, assessing tax in the sum of $391,684.50 and adding $778,164.00 for an allegedly incorrect return.

3. The difference between the respondent's initial views as to the tax payable and the view effect to which was given by the further amended assessment is accounted for by the respondent's having taxed the appellant on income from a number of tax avoidance "transactions" entered into in the year in question. There were, it is said, forty of these, but the parties have agreed that nine only of the forty are to be considered, in the first place. The other thirty-one will, if necessary, be the subject of a further hearing. It has also been agreed that consideration of the legality of the exaction of additional tax will be deferred.

4. It should be added that the material filed used the expression "transactions" with reference to categories of dealings. The word is apt to describe commercial dealings, not mere pretences and is therefore slightly tendentious. Nevertheless, I will use it to speak of the categories of dealings, as the parties did.

5. At the relevant time (May 1981) the appellant was carrying on practice in partnership as an accountant and deriving from that source a relatively modest income; that is reflected in his return for the 1980/81 year, disclosing a taxable income of $12,173.00. However, his non-partnership activities in that year (and, I gather, in some other years) produced large income, earned in the course of certain tax avoidance arrangements made for his clients. The respondent says that this additional income was, in law, that of the appellant, whereas the appellant claims the income was earned by one of a number of companies he controlled, and that such income was earned by each such company as trustee of a discretionary trust. The relevant company which is alleged to have earned the income in question relating to the nine matters was Serula Pty Ltd as to all but one, and Larchwood Pty Ltd as to that one.

6. The dispute just mentioned appears to be essentially one of fact: it is common ground that the activities which led to the income's being earned were, in a physical sense, carried out by the appellant himself, but he says that in each of the nine matters, the income was earned by a company, in the course of carrying out contracts made between it and his clients. As to most of the nine matters, it is the appellant's case that the income was earned by a company as a profit on the execution of a contract for the sale of goods which was genuine, although entered into for the purpose of tax avoidance.

7. I am satisfied that the money in question was not earned in the way the appellant alleges and that there were no contracts for the sale of goods, or contracts of any other kind, entered into between the companies I have mentioned and the appellant's clients. All that happened was that, under agreements between the appellant himself and the clients, certain payments and (in some cases) accounting entries were made tending to show that such contracts had been made, although they never were. The payments were not made in pursuance of contracts for sale or the like and the entries in accounting records relating to them were bogus.

8. I am not satisfied of the truth of the appellant's assertions that the income was earned by the companies I have mentioned in the course of commercial transactions between those companies and the appellant's clients. Clients have given evidence, and the effect of their evidence, speaking generally, is that the appellant suggested to them that by the making of certain payments and entries, their respective taxable incomes could be reduced and that a fee or commission of 5% or 10% was to be charged. The consideration for the fee or commission was not any commercial dealing, but merely the appellant's trouble and expertise in arranging and advising on the payments and other matters he claimed would be likely to secure a tax advantage for the clients. In performing these functions, I am satisfied that the appellant acted on his own behalf and not on behalf of any of the companies which made or received payments as part of the intended tax avoidance. That is so, in my view, although in each of the nine categories matters were so arranged by the appellant that the fee or commission was deposited for a time, not in his own bank account, but in an account in the name of one of the companies he controlled.

9. It is necessary to say something of the fate of the moneys so deposited. The parties, although the transactions in question all occurred in May 1981, placed before me a considerable volume of evidence concerning other aspects of the appellant's financial dealings, before and long after the transactions in question took place. This was thought to be relevant to the question whether the appellant's actions were consistent with the profits in question being his personal moneys. For example, he bought, in his own name and with moneys derived from a bank account in his own name, paintings of considerable value and now has under his control an art collection he says is worth millions of dollars. It appears that the ultimate source of the funds used was income from tax avoidance arrangements of the kind I have to consider. But the appellant's case seems to be that the appearance of his having derived considerable personal wealth from work as a tax avoidance expert is illusory, and that, although it is common ground that very large sums were derived from that work, those sums were at all times earned by trusts he controlled, not by him. At the time the nine transactions in question took place, he controlled the following trusts:

1. Delline Trust - ceased 2 November 1981.

2. Larchwood Discretionary Trust - ceased 13 April 1981.

3. Serula Trust - ceased 2 November 1981.

4. Sopala Trust - ceased 2 November 1981.

5. Sopala Nominal Trust - ceased 2 November 1981.

10. According to the appellant's account of the matter, each of these trusts passed out of his sphere of responsibility in a similar fashion: a new trustee was appointed. Each of the new trustees, so the appellant appeared to suggest, took office on the basis that it would carry out the responsibility for filing trust returns, which he had not done. But it seems plain from other evidence, including that of the appellant, that that is untrue and that the real purpose was simply to get rid of the trusts and their records, to make investigation by the tax authorities more difficult. Most of the records, the appellant says, were sent to England. When asked about them by the Tax Department, he gave untruthful answers, referred to in more detail below.

11. The appellant says that the funds from the tax avoidance activities were invested in various ways in companies controlled by him, some of which were Pessaro Pty Ltd, Chetan Pty Ltd, JAB Investments Pty Ltd and Mercara Pty Ltd. His case appears to be that the bulk, at least, of the assets derived from his tax avoidance activities have ultimately passed into the hands of a series of three trustees, in succession to each other; all were controlled by him.

12. A fundamental difficulty in dealing with this aspect of the matter - i.e. the appellant's dealings with the income derived from his tax avoidance work - is that few of the relevant documents were produced in Court. Most of them, according to the appellant's evidence, were sent away by him to hamper investigation. I accept that he did that, but I am not prepared to rely on the appellant's oral account of the effect of documents showing the way in which the relevant income was dealt with. For example, the appellant claimed that by some transactions which he described in a general way, money the subject of trusts controlled by him was "stripped" (by means not involving a distribution to him personally) shortly before the relevant documents were sent away - mostly to England; I would not be inclined to accept his description as correct. If the matter depended upon the appellant's having given the Court a candid account of his dealings, through the various trusts and entities or personally, with the income and assets derived from the tax avoidance arrangements, the appeal must fail. In general, I am inclined to think the Court should be cautious when asked to accept oral accounts of the effect of documents which a taxpayer has disposed of for the very purpose of preventing others from ascertaining their contents for themselves, or which (as is the case with some of the trust documents here in question) he has carefully refrained from making any attempt to bring to Court. Whether or not that general proposition is correct, I could not be satisfied, in the instant case, that the appellant has given an accurate account of such trust dealings. On some matters he appeared to me to be frank, but I have no confidence in his willingness candidly to explain matters which bear upon his tax liability, nor have I any confidence in the accuracy or completeness of the explanation he gave of transactions effected by documents which he did not produce.

13. Two important examples of the appellant's attitude are as follows. The first and more general point is that he gave affidavit evidence of the nine "transactions" in question which was incomplete and misleading; its effect, to put the matter generally, was that each of the dealings constituted a genuine commercial dealing, although one designed for tax minimisation, but it became obvious in the course of the hearing that that was not so. Secondly, when the respondent asked the appellant for information, in July 1982, about the trusts and matters of that nature, he replied (exhibit 2) by a letter reading in part as follows:

"We are seeking to have copies of the trust deeds

made available by trustees which were appointed to

the trusts subsequent to the above companies and

will supply these to you as soon as and if they

become available.

We are not aware whether returns of income were

lodged in respect of the above trusts, but can

confirm that this did not occur through our office.

We have inquired of the trustees who assumed

trusteeship from the above companies whether they

have lodged or have available for lodgment any

returns, and await their reply."

14. This was all very misleading. The appellant was not in truth seeking to have copies made available. He had no intention of supplying any and was well aware that the chance that those to whose tender mercies the trusts had been subjected might lodge returns was slight indeed.

15. As I have said, the appellant has not placed before me any reliable account of his dealings with the income in question and property bought with it. But it appears to me that the appellant's subsequent dealings with the income and what it produced were entirely consistent with his having regarded the proceeds of his tax avoidance work as his own to do with as he wished, and his having regarded the various trusts as mere temporary repositories, not constituting any significant obstacle, in any practical sense, to his control of the money or property.

16. The earliest relevant trust was called the Dalhouse Trust, and the appellant said that its beneficiaries included various charities, none of which was informed that it had become a beneficiary. The trust deed was not produced, although it appeared that the appellant could have got it if he wished. He told the Tax Department that he would try to get copies of such deeds, but has not done so. According to what purport to be minutes of a meeting of directors of Dalhouse Pty Ltd (the trustee of the Dalhouse Trust), a deed of removal and appointment of trustee was executed in May 1980. About that time, the money the subject of the trust was distributed, so the appellant said, by creating artificial losses; the charities got nothing. By arrangement with one Lockyer, for a consideration, a company called First Olbap Pty Ltd took over the management of the trust and it appears to me that the appellant intended and wished that the records of the trust would disappear. This was a typical pattern and applied to subsequent trusts, which, according to the appellant's evidence, received moneys derived from his tax avoidance work. It was, as I understood the respondent's argument, contended that the setting up of these successive trusts, there being no element of genuineness about them, was a sham. The argument derives some force from the fact that it seems clear that there was no intention that the beneficiaries nominated would receive any distribution from the trust, trivial sums apart. The appellant's scheme was to have a series of trusts, none of which would distribute money, but each of which would dispose of its records, without filing a tax return, at a time when its funds were passed on to other entities. One of the purposes of all this (it is hardly contested) was to make it difficult for the respondent to recover tax from anyone, if it ever succeeded in analysing and disentangling the appellant's complex affairs.

17. But in my opinion, the whole enterprise fell down, at least as far as the nine transactions in issue were concerned, on a basic point: the appellant has not been able to show that the income, which was undoubtedly earned by his work, was in truth that of any corporate entity.

18. Two other general points require to be made, before setting out the facts relating to the nine transactions.

19. One is that, in relation to each of the nine transactions, there was a Tax Department investigation in 1985. To support the suggestion that there were genuine commercial transactions - sales and so forth - the appellant then produced what he claimed to be invoices which had been prepared in 1981. I am not satisfied that the invoices were genuine. No sensible reason was put forward for their having been shown to no-one in 1981, and I think they were fakes, like the Skene potato crop contract referred to below (Transaction 22).

20. Secondly, information obtained in the course of the investigation was placed before the Court, in the affidavit of one P.R. Massie. His analysis of the various transactions was, as to the basic facts, substantially unchallenged, but he included in it statements of legal conclusion, in particular that the appellant was paid certain sums by way of "commission" on which he was assessable. To avoid the necessity of excising these conclusions, it was agreed between counsel that I should ignore them, and I have done so. Massie's explanation of the way in which the various transactions were effected was carefully prepared and I found it helpful. Since the amounts involved are not in dispute, I shall not set them all out in discussing the various categories. The commercial effect of what was done was that moneys went, in general, from the client's funds into a bank account controlled by the appellant and came back less the agreed "commission".

Transaction 14

21. Although the heading "Transaction 14" is used, it in fact includes, as the appellant accurately said in his evidence, a number of separate dealings.

22. The theory upon which these were founded was that Serula Pty Ltd ("Serula") bought from a client of the appellant's, Magna Constructions Pty Ltd ("Magna"), chattels fabricated by Magna and resold them to entities described in the evidence as Electric Power Transmission and D & R Fabricating. The idea was that Serula would appear to have bought the chattels from Magna (the appellant's client) for a substantially lower price than Magna's customers (Electric Power Transmission and D & R Fabricating) had agreed to pay; then Serula would appear to have sold the chattels to the two companies just mentioned, making a substantial profit, which would be returned to the interests controlling Magna. That was to be done by subscription by Serula to a company, Agatab Pty Ltd, from which Magna would obtain the money by way of loan.

23. That is, Magna's revenue position would be substantially worsened by its apparently selling the chattels to Serula at a low price instead of selling them, as it had already agreed to do, to its customers at a high price. That would (or was hoped to) affect the tax position of Magna favourably and the difference between the two prices would largely be returned, via the subscription to Agatab Pty Ltd, to Magna.

24. Those in control of Magna did not give evidence, and the evidence of the appellant on the subject was, in important respects, misleading. If this group of transactions stood alone, one could not, therefore, confidently determine what the true character of the arrangement was; in determining that, I have relied on the whole pattern of dealing disclosed by the evidence. : McGovern v. Galt [1948] VicLawRp 50; (1948) VLR 285, International Paper Company v. Spicer [1906] HCA 75; (1906) 4 CLR 739.

25. The appellant gave evidence on affidavit that transactions included a "Payment by Electric Power Transmission to Serula Pty Ltd for fabrication work". It turned out that Electric Power Transmission paid Serula nothing. Although he equivocated about the point initially, the appellant eventually admitted that Serula had no contact of any kind with Electric Power Transmission; nor did he. I infer that what happened was that the appellant made an agreement with those in control of Magna, under which certain cheques paid to Magna would be endorsed to Serula, out of whose bank account most of the sum paid would be repaid, partly back to Magna, and partly to Agatab Pty Ltd. What was left was the appellant's fee or commission.

26. A reference was made during the hearing to the contracts of sale being merely "paper transactions". That is a proper description, with the qualification that one is not dealing with ostensible sales and purchases of goods to which Serula was a party. All that happened was that Magna endorsed cheques to Serula which paid cheques to Magna, with the idea that the books of Magna would be so drawn up as to represent that there had been sales of goods by Magna to Serula. There were in fact no such sales of goods.

27. One could accurately speak of sham sales of goods if (for example) Serula had made written contracts with Magna which purported to relate to goods, but which were not truly intended to have legal effect. Here, it was agreed between the appellant and those in control of Magna that the payments I have mentioned would be made, with a view to deceiving the respondent. The deception intended was, as I have explained, that the books would record sales of goods from Magna to Serula, which did not occur; there were no contracts of sale of goods, sham or otherwise, to which Serula was a party.

28. It is unnecessary to consider what the position would have been if the appellant had arranged for Magna to execute a written agreement for sale and purchase of goods, or otherwise so through the motions of making such an agreement. I think the theory upon which the appellant worked was that there was no need to go to so much trouble, since any persons interested would be likely to draw the desired inference, from the payments and entries which were made, that Magna sold goods to Serula.

29. A more difficult question is whether the agreement the appellant made with those in control of Magna was made by him on behalf of Serula. That agreement was apparently oral. In discussing like agreements - and, indeed, when speaking of other aspects of the whole complicated series of dealings - the appellant tended to accept the use of the first person and to speak of these matters as if they were transactions entered into by him personally and on his own behalf. For example, speaking of the deal with Magna, the appellant was asked:

"Anyway, you were consulted, were you, by the

directors as to whether or not you could do

something about the income tax situation?---Yes.

And then you entered into the arrangement? - -

-?---Yes.

- - - with them?---Yes.

And the arrangement was - what was the arrangement

again?---The arrangement sought to interpose Serula

between Magna and its customers."

30. This description of the matter appears to accord with reality: the appellant himself made an agreement, with Serula (and of course Agatab Pty Ltd) playing their roles in the carrying out of that agreement. But although there is no doubt that it was the appellant who personally made the agreement as a result of which the various payments were effected, it is a question whether he did so as agent for Serula or should be deemed to have done so as agent for Serula, the entity in whose bank account the "commission" was deposited.

31. I am inclined to doubt whether the appellant, or Magna's controllers, gave any thought to the agency question, when the agreement between the appellant and Magna was made. The assumption upon which the appellant proceeded was that there would be no dispute that the sum held in Serula's account, the residue of moneys paid (by way of endorsement of cheques) from Magna to Serula, was not his.

32. Of course, if the residue remaining in Serula's account after payment of the agreed sum to Agatab Pty Ltd had been a profit on contracts for sale of goods, profit would have been earned by Serula. But there were no contracts for sale of goods and the residue was therefore not a profit earned in that way. It was a profit derived from the making and carrying out of a plan to act, in some respects, as if there had been sales of goods. Putting this more shortly, the appellant made a tax avoidance agreement. The primary question in the whole case is whether, in making such agreements (which included a term that payments should be made to Serula's bank account) the appellant acted on his own behalf; if so, then the "commission" is to be regarded as the appellant's profit from his having made the tax avoidance agreement and having seen to its being, albeit imperfectly, carried out.

Transaction 16

33. According to the appellant's evidence, this transaction involved the sale by Serula of "work in progress on 200 truck bodies to Dernberg Pty Ltd". The appellant also stated that the "labour and materials for the work in progress" were bought by Serula from a company named Triple M Holdings Pty Ltd.

34. Cheques were drawn to cash on the account of Dernberg and deposited in Serula's bank account. Then, on the same day, moneys went out of the Serula bank account into the account of a company named Jalisco Pty Ltd, whence it was distributed partly back to Triple M Holdings Pty Ltd and partly ($50,000) to an entity called the Thompson Property Trust.

35. What all this was supposed to represent is obscure. Jalisco Pty Ltd, Triple M Holdings Pty Ltd, Dernberg Pty Ltd and the Thompson Property Trust were all in the same control, namely the control of the Thompson family. Jalisco Pty Ltd and Dernberg Pty Ltd were shelf companies supplied by the appellant. According to the evidence of Mr Gene Thompson, he initially approached the appellant for advice about "film schemes" to reduce the taxable income of Triple M Holdings. The appellant dissuaded him from using film schemes and suggested an alternative method, the result of which was to be that an amount of money would be paid out by Triple M Holdings and would come back to it, less 10%, being the appellant's fee or commission. Mr Thompson denied that Dernberg acquired any work in progress from Serula and denied that Triple M Holdings sold labour and material or work in progress to Serula.

36. When cross-examined about this by counsel for the appellant, Mr Thompson explained that "there was actually no management of any work done or anything sold that way". He said that the possibility of identifying particular stock attributable to an invoice was discussed, but did not occur.

37. Here, to add a touch of plausibility, the appellant provided invoices addressed to Dernberg for various amounts purporting to be for sub-contract work and the like. Two of them mentioned "Timperley Trading" and "Fischer Trading" respectively, but these were simply names made up by the appellant and had no significance in reality.

38. The appellant explained that he was unsure whether Dernberg sold goods to the public; the intention, he said, was that it would do so, but he was unable to say whether or not Triple M simply carried on selling, as it had been doing for years. He thought that Serula had subscribed for four shares in Jalisco, which "may well have been" subscribed for at a substantial premium; no share certificates were issued. According to the appellant's evidence, what happened was that Triple M sold goods to Serula for a small price, which on-sold them for a high price to Dernberg. He said that the sum of $50,000 I have mentioned was recorded as repayment of a loan.

39. I find that although there was some idea of making payments corresponding with sales of goods, there never were any sales of goods. Yet it does not appear to be a case where one is dealing with a contract which (although apparently made) should be disregarded as a sham. Here, there was not even an ostensible contract, in my opinion. The appellant is unsure precisely what the explanation for the various payments was to be, but the only true agreement was that the payments would be made, leaving a profit for the appellant's interests.

Transaction 19

40. In summary, the scheme here was supposed to be that each of two hotel companies, in common control, sold stock to the other through Serula. The transactions could not be explained by the appellant in any way which made sense; he claimed not to remember the details.

41. It was inherently unlikely, in my opinion, that this scheme could have any legal effect (other than, ultimately, the payment of money to the appellant's interests) without the taking of a great deal of care. Stock in the possession of hotel A, owned by company A, was supposed to be sold to company B, which owned hotel B, via Serula, but that stock was not to move from hotel A to hotel B. The stock sold out of hotel B would include stock owned by company A. I can see ways in which this could occur, by taking some considerable trouble, but such trouble was not taken. The appellant claims that he told the client to alter the books so that they would reflect the sales; I do not believe him, nor do I believe that he devised any system which would reflect the sales which were supposed to have taken place. No property passed or was treated as having passed. There were no contracts for sale, ostensible or otherwise.

42. The appellant swore in his main affidavit that there was a series of transactions under which Serula purchased stock cheaply from one company and sold it at normal cost price to the other. The two companies were Wilgreen Pty Ltd and North Entrance Investments Pty Ltd. One of the difficulties of the matter, from the appellant's point of view, was that the relevant cheques drawn by the appellant companies and banked to the account of Serula Pty Ltd were not drawn in favour of that company; the payee was "Serul Agencies". The appellant said that "Serul Agencies" was "a division within the company". He appeared to agree with counsel for the taxpayer that the name was used to add an appearance of authenticity, but said that it was not a "formal registered name".

43. In my opinion, the proper inference is that the appellant caused the cheques to be drawn in favour of "Serul Agencies". He did so, as I also infer, with the intention of banking those cheques to the account of Serula Pty Ltd. But that does not mean that the cheques were, when delivered, the property of Serula Pty Ltd. Serula Pty Ltd had no contract with the two companies (Wilgreen Pty Ltd and North Entrance Investments Pty Ltd) on whose accounts the cheques were drawn. The fact that, in the mind of the appellant, "Serul Agencies" was synonymous with Serula Pty Ltd did not convert the cheques into payments to Serula Pty Ltd; they were simply equivalent to cheques drawn to cash: cf. Grant v. Vaughan 97 ER 957, discussed in North and South Insurance Corporation Ltd v. National Provincial Bank Ltd (1936) 1 KB 328 at 333, 334.

44. The surplus in Serula's account was returned, less 10%, via a shelf company (Jaypore Pty Ltd) to the client.

45. Here again, when questioned about the transactions, the appellant answered on the basis that he personally made the arrangements with the client, a man called Dwyer. His discussion with Dwyer was described in the following terms:

". . . the discussion was clearly about the taxation

income situation and the arrangements proposed were

that each company would sell stock cheaply and the

other company would buy it back at the normal

price.

They would sell stock cheaply to whom? --- To

Serula.

And so one company would sell it cheaply to Serula,

the other would buy it back at the normal price?

--- Yes, then the reverse would also occur."

46. There is no suggestion that in making these arrangements the appellant expressed himself (to Dwyer) to be acting merely as agent for Serula, but I am not satisfied that the appellant has given an accurate account of the conversation with Dwyer. Although Dwyer had only a poor recollection of them, he thought that in the discussions "it was decided then to sort of make out cheques for stock". However, there was neither physical movement of stock nor any book record of stock movement.

Transaction 22

47. Under this heading come some payments made, supposedly for potatoes, and in general character the matter is much the same as the three discussed above - i.e. transactions 14, 16 and 19. However, there is a difference between this transaction and the others to which attention should be directed; here, there was a written contract, bearing the date 16 January 1981. The appellant's account of the matter in his affidavit was that Serula bought a crop of potatoes from J.A. and H.A. Skene and onsold the crop, when harvested, to several different produce agencies. The affidavit went on to say that there was, for example, a payment by Produce Centre Pty Ltd to Serula Pty Ltd for potatoes.

48. According to the appellant's evidence, the idea was that buyers of potatoes would send the cheques direct to Serula. I do not believe that account of the matter. It appears that the buyers dealt only with Mr Skene and I infer that he procured all the cheques.

49. Of the total sum received for the potatoes ($60,067.61), nearly all was returned to the Skenes. More accurately, $29,635.61 went directly to the Skenes and $27,000 was supposed to have gone to them via a company called Amank Pty Ltd.

50. The theory underlying the latter payment was that the $27,000 was a subscription of capital and the sum in question was then lent to the Skenes, less $350 for the incorporation of Amank Pty Ltd. The amount left in the account of Serula was $3,432, which was the "commission".

51. Some light is shed on the true nature of these payments by correspondence between the appellant and Mr Skene, which is in evidence. On 11 May 1981, the appellant wrote to Mr Skene accounting to him for the sums received and paid out to that date. The accounting discriminated between the position shown in Mr Skene's books and the true position. According to the former, he had received $18,112.41 from the sale of potatoes, but the true position was that $48,112.41 had been received. The summary enclosed with the letter accounted for the $48,112.41 by (inter alia) the following item:

"Costs to outside parties $3,000"

52. The expression "outside parties" is curious. According to the appellant's case, that $3,000 was unequivocally the property of Serula Pty Ltd.

53. A further letter, dated 15 May 1981, was written by the appellant to Mr Skene enclosing a cheque for $11,523.20 "which you will record in your books as proceeds for the sale of potatoes". That was made up, so the letter said, by deducting from the proceeds of sale "our costs" of $432.

54. I infer that after receipt of this letter, Mr Skene wrote to the appellant a letter dated 8 June 1981 expressing some doubt about the tax effect of these arrangements. He was reassured by a letter from the appellant dated 12 June 1981 enclosing "your copy of the contract of sale of the potatoes". It appears to me very likely that the contract came into existence about that time and not on the date (16 January 1981) which it bears. However, the price seems to have been put down incorrectly. By the time the contract was prepared (in June), Serula Pty Ltd had paid $29,635.61 to the Skenes but the contract referred to $18,000, which was approximately the amount recorded as having been paid in the letter of 11 May. It seems likely that, when the contract was prepared, the letter of 15 May recording a further payment of $11,955.20 was overlooked.

55. In short, the written contract was, I believe, a fake. The appellant gave evidence that the written contract was orally varied to raise the purchase price from the initial figure of $18,000 but I do not think that is the true explanation for the discrepancy between the price it shows and the total price.

56. The most important aspect of the correspondence, however, is that in this series of letters the appellant writes to Mr Skene in such a way as to make it hard to accept that the appellant did so on behalf of Serula. It is true that Serula is mentioned in the correspondence as an entity by which payments were made, but the letters are written by the appellant on his own behalf. This aspect of the matter is underlined by the letter of 12 June, which includes the sentence: "Our professional services should be claimed as accountancy fees for the amount of $432.00". That was part of the balance left in the Serula account and part of the sum referred to in the following answer:

"So it was necessary to get some money back to Mr

Skene. How much were you going to retain as your

commission or remuneration in the deal? --- I have

no memory of it other than the figure on p 201

which was $3,432."

57. The appellant's case was that he arranged these transactions, not on behalf of himself, but on behalf of Serula and carefully distinguished between the income earned by him in these schemes and that earned from accounting practice; the correspondence to which I have referred tends to make one gravely doubt that in dealing with clients he made any such distinction.

Transaction 28

58. According to the appellant's evidence, a sum of $1,406.20 was earned by the client's selling stock to Serula, which sold it to Sopala Pty Ltd (another company controlled by the appellant), which in turn sold it back to the client.

59. For some reason Sopala does not seem to have figured in any of the payments. All that happened, so far as Massie was able to discover, was that the client (Lepontine Pty Ltd) drew cheques totalling $152,490, which were deposited in Serula's account on 12 May 1981, and the same sum, less $1,406.20, was paid back to Lepontine Pty Ltd on the same day. Mr P.G. Butler, the managing director of Lepontine, said that the arrangement proposed by the appellant was that invoices and cheques were to be drawn reflecting sales of "allegedly damaged" stock. There was no identification of any such stock, and it seems clear that there was no contract of sale of goods involved. Serula never had possession of or control over the stock.

60. Massie's evidence suggests that transaction 28 was never carried into effect, and certainly the evidence about it seems incomplete. It appears that the appellant caused fake invoices to be prepared purporting to record sales by "Serul Agencies" to Calaba Distributors Pty Ltd, but what part these invoices were to play in the intended deception is unclear. It should also be mentioned that the appellant retained $16,000, not $1,406.20; again, that point was not elucidated.

Transaction 29

61. On 15 May 1981, cheques totally $77,436 were deposited in Serula's bank account, and on the same day, cheques totalling $67,500 were paid out by Serula to Nalver Pty Ltd. The first batch of cheques came from the account of Calitri Pty Ltd, trading as World Search; it is a vendor of chemicals.

62. The money paid out by Calitri was composed of five cheques, each drawn in favour of "Serul Agencies". Although the cheques bear various dates from January to April, I am satisfied that they were all made out at the one time, in May 1981.

63. The name "Serul Agencies" was one chosen by the appellant, in my opinion, as other such names were chosen - i.e. arbitrarily - and it was not a name under which Serula carried on business. The cheques were equivalent to cash cheques, and once the appellant had obtained them, he was entitled to put them in any bank account he liked. The theory was that these cheques would represent moneys paid for goods sold, but no goods were ever sold; there were no contracts of sale.

64. Here, again, no invoices were produced until after the Tax Department started to investigate the matter. The appellant, when that was suggested to him, said, rather oddly, "No, that is not my recollection". The invoices included an item for packaging, but no goods were in fact packaged by Serula.

65. The evidence given by the appellant that there were purchases and sales of chemicals is rejected. The appellant said that Delline Pty Ltd purchased chemicals from World Search Pty Ltd, then sold them to Serula, which resold them to World Search. There is no other evidence that Delline had anything to do with the matter. In the end, $9,936 remained in the account of Serula.

Transaction 31

66. The client was a company named Pianosa Pty Ltd, a builder of "spec" houses. It built one at Sunnybank Hills in Brisbane, which was bought by people named Bogar for $74,000. When the sale was settled, the solicitors for Pianosa accounted for the price to it it by paying, inter alia, a cheque for $57,809.65 in favour of Serula.

67. Six days later, Pianosa banked to its account two cheques, which were recorded as having been as follows:

Serula Pty Ltd $7809.65;

James Baker & Co. $45,000.00.

68. As to the second cheque, it appears that the money came, in fact, from the bank account of Serula, but the use of the name is, nevertheless, interesting. Those in control of Pianosa Pty Ltd, Mr and Mrs G.A. Jackson, regarded themselves as dealing with the appellant, not Serula, and Mr Jackson said he had not heard of Serula.

69. The theory underlying this transaction was that Serula Pty Ltd paid the $7,809.65 to Pianosa for work it had done, and the $45,000 was supposed to be a capital payment by Serula by way of subscription to a company named Liavanda Pty Ltd. It appears, however, that the $45,000 was simply banked to the account of Pianosa and did not pass through a Liavanda account. The remaining $5,000 stayed in Serula's account.

70. The suggestion made by the appellant in the course of his evidence was that some of the documents necessary to record the transaction might have gone to the United Kingdom, presumably with the other papers mentioned previously, but no comprehensible version of what was supposed to be achieved by the payment from Pianosa (through the solicitors) to Serula and the payment of $7,809.65 back to Serula was given. The appellant's evidence was that the payment was for labour and materials, and that would have made sense had Serula contracted with Pianosa to have the latter do work for it. But it is plain that it did not do so. Pianosa did not contract with anyone to build the house; it simply built the house and (at some stage) sold it to Mr and Mrs Bogar, who themselves had no contract with Serula. Baker claimed there was, in the United Kingdom, a document constituting an assignment of the proceeds of the sale.

71. The cheques I have mentioned had no legal significance except that they were a way of getting a sum of $5,000 into the control of the appellant or his interests.

72. Again, this is not a sham case in the ordinary sense; cheques passed, but there was not even an ostensible agreement behind them, and the appellant had no clear idea what sort of transaction the payments were supposed to represent; nor had Mr and Mrs Jackson.

Transaction 33

73. This involved, in theory, dealings in the stock-in-trade of a toy company owned by people called Marsh. According to the appellant's account, Marsh sold stock to Sopala, which resold it to Serula, which resold it to Marsh. That is, the appellant said, in effect, that there were two interposed companies of his. However, the documents do not support this. All that happened was that on 22 May 1981 Marsh paid cheques to the amount of $30,432 to Serula and three days later, $27,000 was paid back - the difference of $3,432 being the "commission". There was an idea that the $27,000 would be by way of subscription to the capital of a company called Mirador Pty Ltd, but in fact it was simply paid to Marsh.

74. The evidence is that, although there was a gap of three days between the banking of one lot of cheques (the $30,432) and the banking of the other lot ($27,000), in fact (at the appellant's suggestion), the cheques were simply exchanged. Book entries were made which recorded purchases of property from "Ellwood" and "Delco" and "Serul Agencies" in April and May 1981, but there were no such transactions. The entries were made simply as part of the deception, at the suggestion of the appellant.

75. When the invoices were produced by the appellant in 1985, after the Tax Department inquiry, they did not correspond with the entries in the books, which I have mentioned, either as to amount or names.

76. Mr and Mrs Marsh entered into these arrangements with the idea of reducing their tax and regarded themselves as dealing with the appellant. It is my opinion that, as far as they were concerned, Serula (although it was a company which really existed) could no more be regarded as the entity with which Mr and Mrs Marsh regarded themselves as dealing than could "Serul Agencies", "Ellwood Agencies" or "Delco".

Transaction 37

77. The distinctive aspect of this transaction is that instead of using the Serula account, the appellant used the account of Larchwood Pty Ltd. He gave evidence that payments were made for the supply of contract labour and materials by Larchwood to N.P. & R.L. Flaherty. It is not possible to fully explain what happened, but I am satisfied that there was no contract for the supply of any labour or materials by Larchwood. The appellant said that the idea was that Mr Flaherty, an electrician, was employed under a sub-contract by Larchwood, which was paid by the partnership of N.P. & R.L. Flaherty. Mr Flaherty said:

"Mr Baker advised that all I had to do was write out

cheques to some sub-contractors, they would take

10% and return the balance and I was to record the

cheques in the sub-contractor's column of the cash book."

The supposed sub-contractors were "Ellwood Industries", "Larchwood Industries" and "Sopal Agencies". None of these existed and they were simply part of the camouflage. At the direction of the appellant, the clients drew cheques in favour of Larchwood amounting to $20,139, and the appellant caused Larchwood to pay them back, less $2,000. The way in which the money was paid back involved the exercise of some ingenuity. The appellant gave evidence that $18,000 was paid for an option to purchase the Flahertys' residence, but I do not believe that. There is no reason, as to this transaction, to discuss the matter at length, for it seems clear that the cheques drawn were merely a pretence - except, of course, that the clients paid $2,000 for whatever benefit the scheme was supposed to obtain for them.

LEGAL CHARACTER OF WHAT WAS DONE

78. There is now a considerable quantity of authority dealing with the question whether attempts at diversion, or splitting, of income, by professional people and others similarly placed are effective. Insofar as these cases support any practical and simple proposition, it is perhaps that such attempts are likely to be ineffective where the income is wholly or substantially earned by a natural person; perhaps oddly, diversion or splitting appear to be more likely to succeed for those running businesses or practices of a larger sort, where the earning of the income can plausibly be attributed to the efforts of employees and the use of property as well as to the efforts of the principals.

79. Looking at the matter broadly, it must be said of the appellant that the income earned was, in a practical sense, earned by him. It is true that some efforts of staff contributed; some typing was done in the course of the enterprise and letters were sent, but the people who did that work were apparently not employees of any company, and in particular not of Serula or Larchwood. In a commercial sense, what the appellant was doing was running a very profitable business "on the side", using the resources of the partnership to a small extent.

80. A rough analogy may be postulated. Suppose a barrister accepts a brief to advise on a taxation scheme for an agreed fee, and, in the hope of helping his own tax position, advises that the scheme be arranged in such a way that his fee is paid in the course of it, and paid, not to him, but to an entity controlled by him. Suppose further that the theory is that the amount which would otherwise have been the fee emerges from the carrying out of the scheme as a profit on a purported sale of property. Then, as it seems to me, once the theory is rejected and it is ascertained that there was no sale, the barrister's fee, although so disguised and although paid to an entity nominated by him, would be part of his income. Counsel for the appellant sought to derive help from the fact that (in contrast to the case just postulated), here the "commissions" were not derived as part of the accountant's ordinary tax advising practice, that being carried on in partnership; they were earned in the course of a separate series of activities. That does not appear to me to make any difference, for if the "commissions" were earned under arrangements made and carried out by the appellant, as a product of the activities I have mentioned, it is of no consequence whether the appellant engaged also in other business activities, alone or in partnership.

81. I have not found it necessary to consider the application of s.260 of the Tax Act, since I have reached my conclusion by accepting a view of the same general sort as that alluded to by Gibbs J. in the following passage:

"There is something to be said for the view that

quite apart from s.260 the entire income derived

from the business was income derived by the

appellant and that the trust only operated on the

income after he had earned it and had become liable

to pay tax on it. In Peate v. Federal Commissioner

of Taxation (1964) 111 CLR 443, at p 446 Menzies

J. said that it is established that a family man

'cannot achieve taxation immunity by the simple

expedient of assigning his earnings to his wife and

family': Parkins v. Warwick (1943) 25 TC 419"

(Hollyock v. F.C.T. [1971] HCA 43; (1971) 125 CLR 647 at

pp 653, 654).

82. In determining whether it was the appellant who derived the income from the work the appellant did, it appears to me that a practical approach is required - although, here, it does not seem to matter whether or not one takes such an approach. In Arthur Murray (N.S.W.) Pty Ltd v. F.C.T. [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314 at 318, the High Court said:

"The ultimate inquiry in either kind of case, of

course, must be whether that which has taken place,

be it the earning or the receipt, is enough by

itself to satisfy the general understanding among

practical business people of what constitutes a

derivation of income".

It is true that the Court had in mind, in speaking of "what constitutes a derivation of income", the question of the stage at which moneys should be brought to account, rather than who derived it; but I can see no reason why the "general understanding among practical business people of what constitutes a derivation of income" should not be also of importance in a case of this sort. In F.C. of T. v. Everett [1978] FCA 39; (1978) 21 ALR 625 at p 648, the question was whether a solicitor's assignment of a share in the profits of a partnership was effective to split income. The dissenting judgment of Deane J. provides some guidance as to the possibility of a professional man's diverting or splitting his income - although his Honour's final conclusion on the facts of that case must be taken to be incorrect. His Honour said:

"The reality and the substance of the matter was

that, notwithstanding the effect of the deed of

assignment, the relevant part of the taxpayer's

share of profits was and remained income derived by

means of his own exertion, being part of his

earnings derived from his professional activities

and did not constitute the income of

income-producing property or income of a trust

estate (see Bolton v F.C. of T. (1964) 9 AITR 385 at

395-6 (particularly at 396-7); Stewart Dawson

Holdings Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 39 ALJR at 301F; Arcus v

IR Comr (NZ) (1963) NZLR 324 at 327-31; Hollyock v

F.C. of T. [1971] HCA 43; (1971) 125 CLR 647 at 658).

. . . I would note that my conclusion in that regard

does not involve necessary acceptance of the broad

statements which are found in some judgments to the

effect that it is impossible, for income tax

purposes, effectively to assign the whole or part

of what are in truth earnings from personal

activities (see, for example, Spratt v IR Comr (NZ)

(1964) NZLR 272 at 277; Johnstone v IR Comr

(NZ) (1966) 10 AITR 222 at 228; (1966) NZLR 833 at

838-9; Kelly v IR Comr (NZ) (1969) 1 ATR 380 at

384; (1970) NZLR 161 at 165; and note the comments

of Menzies J in Peate v FC of T (1962) 111 CLR 443

at 446; (1963) ALR 354 at 356."

83. It is an important aspect of the appellant's case that the income earned by the means analysed above is to be sharply distinguished from his ordinary professional income. As to the latter, it would seem clear that if he simply directed his clients to pay fees to his company rather than to him, he would nevertheless be taxable. It was argued that the company earned the income in the first place, having become entitled to it under contracts for sale and the like. There were no such contracts and the entitlement arose, in my opinion, under contracts made by the appellant himself. The sums paid to Serula were paid under that agreement, the true consideration being the work the appellant did.

84. The written argument for the appellant included extensive reference to evidence given by the clients, which was said to support the suggestion that their contract was with Serula Pty Ltd only, and not with the appellant. Most of these passages are at best neutral. Those which, read literally, might support the suggestion that the tax avoidance contract was with Serula consist in answers to questions in which the identity of the contracting party is not given emphasis. It was also submitted that the appellant "was never challenged on his evidence that Serula Pty Ltd entered into an arrangement with the client, company (or firm) in an attempt to alter the tax of the client, company (or firm)". In my opinion, reading the appellant's evidence as a whole, he should not be taken to have asserted that Serula Pty Ltd was the contracting party entitled to receive sums paid for the appellant's tax avoidance work. There is, however, room for confusion in expressions used in the evidence, since discussion of tax avoidance arrangements by the witnesses often appeared to refer to the pretended arrangements for the sale of goods and the like; I agree that, had there been sales of goods, Serula would have been a party to them, not the appellant.

85. As to quantum, the parties are agreed on the figures, except in one respect, namely as to Transaction 28. The respondent says that the income was $16,000, in accordance with the evidence of the client; the appellant would not disagree with the figure of $16,000, nor did the appellant's counsel argue against the adoption of that figure. I therefore find that the income in respect of each of the nine transactions in issue is as set out in exhibit 29, with the exception that the income in respect of Transaction 28 should be $16,000, and not $1,406.20 as there mentioned.

86. The remaining question is the order which should be made. The respondent submitted that the appeal should be dismissed and the assessment should stand. In my opinion, that would be inappropriate, since 31 transactions remain to be considered. It appears to me that the proper course is to make a declaration under s.199, which reads as follows:

"Where the Federal Court hears an appeal under this

Part, the Court may make such order in relation to

the decision to which the appeal relates as it

thinks fit, including an order confirming or

varying the decision".

A declaratory order falls, I think, within the description in that section; the advantage of making one is that the appellant can go to the Full Court at this stage, if he wishes.

87. It should be added that, taking into account reductions and an addition, the total amount of the income found in respect of the nine transactions is somewhat higher than the total income which was taken into account in respect of those nine transactions when the assessment was made. The surplus will, so to speak, be lost to the respondent unless it is found, in relation to any of the 31 transactions so far not dealt with, that tax is exigible in some lower sum than that taken into account.
