Re Commissioner of Superannuation v Heather Lynette Miller [1985] FCA 445; 8 FCR 153 (28 November 1985)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

And: HEATHER LYNETTE MILLER

No. G39 of 1985

Administrative Law - Public Service [1985] FCA 445; 8 FCR 153

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Davies(1), Beaumont(2) and Pincus(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Law - appeal from Administrative Appeals Tribunal - superannuation - retirement on ground of invalidity before maximum retiring age - pre-existing medical condition - subsequent condition - whether "connected with" earlier condition.

Whether Commissioner has power to issue benefit classification certificate under s.16(10) Superannuation Act with retrospective effect after lapse of more than reasonable time.

Appeal to Federal Court under s.44 Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act - power of Court on appeal - whether Court has power to remit matter to Administrative Appeals Tribunal.

Words & Phrases - "connected"

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 s.44

Superannuation Act, 1976 ss.16, 66.

Public Service - Superannuation - Commonwealth - Matters affecting level of benefits - Benefit classification certificate - Certificate issued three years after medical examination - Medical condition "connected with" earlier condition - Whether matter should be remitted for rehearing - Powers of Federal Court - Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth), ss 16(4), 16(10)(b), 66(2)(c) - Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth), s 44.

Administrative Law - Administrative appeals legislation (Cth) - Powers of Federal Court - Power to make appropriate orders "by reason of its decision" - Whether decision limited to question of law referred on appeal from Tribunal - Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth), s 44. Held: (1) The decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal that the respondent's medical condition at retirement was not "connected with" an earlier condition, on the ground that the latter condition was not foreseeable, involved an error of law. So held because:

(a) Per Davies and Pincus JJ - For the purposes of s 16(10)(b) and s 66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth), a physical or mental condition resulting in incapacity is "connected with" an earlier physical or mental condition if the earlier condition caused or substantially contributed to the later one. There must be a substantial causal link between the two, but it is unnecessary that the later condition should have been foreseeable.

Lindeman Ltd v. Colvin [1946] HCA 35; (1946) 74 CLR 313 per Dixon J at 321, referred to with approval.

(b) Per Beaumont J - It is sufficient if a link or association exists, in a relevant medical sense, between the two conditions. It is unnecessary to decide whether, in this case, the nexus required is one of causal connection or something short of that. The requisite link or association has been established even if the nexus required is one of causal connection.

(2) Per Davies and Beaumont JJ, (Pincus J dissenting) - The matter should be remitted to the Tribunal for rehearing.

(3) Per Beaumont J - Observations on whether the Commissioner's functions under s 16(4), dealing with the issue of a benefit classification certificate following an employee's medical examination, must be exercised within a reasonable time.

Re Homsy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALN No 18, doubted.

HEARING

Sydney, 1985, October 24, November 28. 28:11:1985

APPEAL

Appeal, pursuant to s 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth), from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on a question of law.

P Flemming, QC for the appellant.

R. Williams, for the respondent.

Cur adv vult

Solicitors for the appellant: Australian Government Solicitor.

Solicitors for the respondent: Maurice May & Co.

FPC

ORDER

The appeal be allowed.

The decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal be set aside and the matter remitted to that Tribunal for rehearing.

No order as to costs.

Upon application by the respondent, a certificate pursuant to s.6(1) of the Federal Proceedings (Costs) Act, 1981 be granted in respect of costs incurred by the respondent in relation to this appeal.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with by 0.36 of the Federal Court Rules. Appeal dismissed

DECISION

I have had the opportunity of reading the reasons for judgment prepared by Beaumont J. I agree in general with them but I would express some views of my own on the operation of the words "connected with" in ss.16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Act").

The words "connected with" also appear in the basal provision, s.16(4) of the Act which provides that a Commissioner may issue a benefit classification certificate if he is of the opinion that the employee

" . . . is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, . . . ". (emphasis added)

Section 66(2)(c) of the Act deals with the situation after retirement where

"the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions, . . . ". (emphasis added)

Section 16(10)(b) is similar but deals with the situation where, for the reasons set out in s.16(10), a benefit classification certificate was not issued but the retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition of the person that existed at the time the person became an eligible employee or by a physical or mental condition connected therewith.

The words "connected with" are not technical words and in ordinary parlance permit of an association which is not causative. In my opinion, however, Parliament did not intend the words to be given a wide interpretation unrelated to the context in which they appear.

Sections 16(4), 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) are all, in my opinion, concerned with questions of causation. These provisions look primarily to the circumstance where a medical or physical condition existing at the time the employee first became eligible is likely to lead to early retirement and in fact causes or substantially contributes to early retirement.

In the context, it appears to me that the words "connected with" were introduced to deal with the circumstance where the early retirement arises not from the medical condition which existed at the time the employee became an eligible employee but as a result of another medical condition that arose out of or developed from that condition. Thus, in the present case, the immediate or proximate cause of the retirement was not the employee's condition of "hormonal mastopathy presenting as breast lumps", which had ceased to exist, but from the adhesions which had arisen as a result of the operation on the employee's breasts.

In the context, I would read the words "connected with" in ss.16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) as dealing with the circumstance where the immediate or proximate cause of the incapacity leading to the retirement was a medical condition other than the condition existing at the time the employee became an eligible employee but where, nevertheless, there is a chain of causation such that the medical condition existing at the time when the employee became an eligible employee can be said, through a chain of events, to have caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity causing the retirement.

This reading gives due attention to the causative elements prescribed by ss.16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) and also to the context of the Act in which those provisions function. It also gives due weight to the crucial provision, s.16(4) of the Act. That provision requires the Commissioner to prognosticate as to whether, by reason of the medical condition existing at the commencement of eligibility or for a reason connected with that condition, the employee would not be likely to continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retiring age. The provision requires the Commissioner to be able to postulate a chain of causation between a medical condition existing at the time of the commencement of eligibility and an incapacity leading to early retirement. Sections 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) of the Act look at the matter after retirement to see whether such a chain of causation actually existed.

In all other respects I agree with the views expressed by Beaumont J and I agree with the orders proposed by his Honour.

This appeal, brought by the Commissioner for Superannuation pursuant to s.44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 on a question of law, involves the construction of certain provisions of the Superannuation Act, 1976 ("the Act").

The respondent, Heather Lynette Miller, was formerly employed as a switchboard operator by Telecom. She became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Act on 6 September 1977. On 27 August 1980, she was retired on the ground of invalidity before attaining her maximum retiring age. A dispute has arisen as to the level of benefits to which the respondent is entitled under the relevant provisions of the Act; the respondent claims she is entitled to invalidity benefits computed in accordance with the provisions of s.66(1) of the Act, whereas the Commissioner contends that the respondent is entitled to the reduced benefits provided by s.66(2) of the Act. In the decision under appeal, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal upheld the respondent's claim.

By s.66(1), except where s.66(2) applies, a person who ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining maximum retiring age is entitled to certain benefits. By s.66(2)(b) and (c), the exception to the operation of s.66(1) depends upon the existence of the following conditions:

(1) there being in force in respect of a person, immediately before the person a retirement, a benefit classification certificate (which, if appropriate, may be issued pursuant to s.16 - see below) (s.66(2)(b)); and

(2) the Commissioner being of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the person's retirement "was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions." (s.66(2)(c)) (Emphasis added).

Provision is made for the issue of a benefit classification certificate in certain circumstances by s.16 of the Act. By s.16(2), the Commissioner may require a person who proposes to become or who becomes an eligible employee to undergo, within such period as the Commissioner specifies, such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner determines. (The history of the legislation suggests that the Commissioner, as administrator of the statutory scheme, is not obliged to require such an examination in every case. The precursor to s.16(2) was framed in mandatory terms, requiring an examination in every case. The present significance of the change, effected by an amendment made in 1978, will be dealt with later). The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports furnished by the medical practitioner or practitioners and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that the person "is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age", the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect; the certificate is to specify the relevant condition or conditions (s.16(4)).

In the present case, the Commissioner did not issue a benefit classification certificate pursuant to s.16(4). Although the applicant was medically examined, at the Commissioner's request, in September 1977, no benefit classification certificate had issued by the time of her retirement in August 1980. Plainly enough, by that time the Commissioner's power to issue such a certificate under s.16(4) was no longer available even if its conditions were otherwise satisfied. Instead, the Commissioner purported to invoke, with retrospective effect, the provisions of s.16(10). Although a number of possible transitional situations are dealt with by s.16(10), a question has arisen whether its provisions were available to the Commissioner in the events which happened. Section 16(10) is in these terms the Commissioner seeks to rely on s.16(10)(a)(ii) and (b)):

"(10) Where -

(a) a person who is an eligible employee ceases to be an eligible

employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity -

(i) before he has undergone a medical examination or examinations as required under sub-section (2);

(ii) if he has undergone such an examination or examinations - before the report or reports of the result or results of the examination or examinations has or have been considered by the Commissioner;

(iii) if the report or reports has or have been considered by the Commissioner - before the Commissioner has reached a decision as to whether he should issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the person under sub-section (4); or

(iv) if the Commissioner has decided to issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the person under sub-section (4) - before the certificate has been issued; and

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied that. . . the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement, was not caused, and was not substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that existed at the time the person became an eligible employee, or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that existed at the time the person became an eligible employee and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his retirement." (Emphasis added).

The benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner in respect of the respondent is contentious in two respects. First, the respondent denies the existence of any relevant relationship between the condition specified in the certificate and the incapacity grounding the respondent's retirement. Secondly, although not fully canvassed in argument, a question arises as to the validity of the certificate having regard to the date of its issue: in its original form, it was not issued until 30 September 1980 - a month after the respondent's retirement. Argument on the appeal centred on the proper construction of those parts of s.66(2) and s.16(10) which have been emphasised. To place the argument in context, it is necessary to refer to the respondent's medical history.

In 1973, the respondent noticed a lump in her breast. The lump was excised in December 1973. It was reported as "benign mammary dysplasia", i.e. fibroadenosis or abnormal development of breast tissue. The surgical procedure is quite common and subsequent complications are unlikely. The disease itself, which is hormonal in origin, is also common. Usually it does not require a mastectomy.

However, in 1975, when undergoing other surgery, the respondent complained of "soreness and lumpiness" in the breasts. The discomfort persisted for some years. In February 1977, the respondent was treated with drugs for the hormonal mastopathy. In June 1977, investigations and assessments of the respondent's condition were carried cut at a major city hospital. The results disclosed no evidence of any serious disease. On 29 September 1977, shortly after becoming an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Act, the respondent, at the request of the Commissioner, was examined by a Commonwealth medical officer pursuant to s.16(2). In his report dated 29 September 1977, the officer noted the removal of the respondent's breast lump and reported the condition of "hormonal mastopathy". In December 1977, acting on the advice of her general practitioner, the respondent consulted Dr. Chong, a general and plastic surgeon. The respondent's condition had deteriorated. In Dr. Chong's opinion, she was suffering from advanced mammary dysplasia with multiple lumps in both breasts. He advised a subfascial mastectomy with prosthetic reconstructions afterwards.

(There was expert evidence before the Tribunal that women with severe mammary dysplasia and multiple lumps in the breast have a much higher than average incidence of carcinoma. In that sense, the procedure recommended by Dr. Chong, although strictly speaking elective, can be regarded as preventative. However, the operation could not, in strictness, be treated as burative: the respondent's condition was not malignant. Mammary dysplasia is caused by an hormonal disorder indicated by multiple cysts in the breast (fibrosis). Nevertheless, when the condition is at an advanced stage, it can be advisable to remove all the breast tissue in a single procedure. The alternative is for the patient to undergo a series of operations to remove the lumps as they periodically appear.)

The respondent accepted Dr. Chong's advice. In February 1978, he performed the mastectomy, removing the tissue in both breasts but leaving the skin and the nipples. In the following weeks, Dr. Chong carried out a reconstruction, using a silicone prosthesis. In a sense, it may be said that the reconstruction was done for cosmetic reasons. But it has never been suggested that the reconstruction procedure was unnecessary or an unreasonable form of medical treatment: the parties have always accepted that the mastectomy, which was performed for other than cosmetic reasons, was a proper form of medical treatment for the respondent's mammary dysplasia.

Unfortunately, although the mastectomy itself was successful, multiple complications followed the reconstruction. The respondent developed painful adhesions in the breast as a result of a fibrous reaction in the tissue around the prosthesis. Such a complication is common enough - more than half of the patients using such a prosthesis suffer some such reaction. However, in the respondent's case, the reaction was aggravated by the excessive usage of the arms required in her employment as a switchboard operator. Over a period, the complications worsened, despite the insertion of a new type of prosthesis. A number of further operations by Dr. Chong failed to alleviate the respondent's condition. Although the respondent's complications very rarely have this effect, her condition worsened to the point where she became unable to perform her duties as a switchboard operator. In January 1980, the respondent applied to Telecom for early retirement on the ground of invalidity. Medical examinations showed that, although the respondent was only 32 years old, she was permanently unfit for work involving elevation of the arms. Telecom acceded to the respondent's application to retire. By a document dated 23 June 1980, an officer of Telecom certified as follows:

"It is certified that Mrs Miller cannot be placed on restricted duties by this Commission.

In accordance with the provisions of By-Law 156 of the Telecommunications Act of 1975, approval is given for the retirement of

HEATHER LYNETTE MILLER - TEMPORARY (scl. PERMANENT) TELEPHONIST - KEMPSEY DTB.

due to the following medical conditions:-

Bilateral simple mastectomy with prosthetic replacement and adhesions

100%." (This reference to a percentage merely indicates that only one condition is involved).

On 27 August 1980, the respondent retired. On 30 September 1980, a delegate of the Commissioner issued, pursuant to a.16(10) of the Act, a benefit classification certificate in respect of the respondent specifying the condition "removal of breast lump". The delegate also decided, pursuant to s.66(2)(c) of the Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for the respondent's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the physical or mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition.

At the respondent's request, those decisions were reconsidered, pursuant to s.154(4) of the Act, by another delegate, Mr. R.C. Whithear. For the reasons he gave on 12 January 1984, Mr. Whithear varied the decision given pursuant to s.16(10) of the Act which specified the respondent's condition as "removal of breast lump" by substituting the condition as "hormonal mastopathy presenting as breast lumps". He also confirmed the decision given pursuant to s.66(2)(c) of the Act. In his reasons, Mr. Whithear said that although the respondent underwent an examination by a Commonwealth medical officer on 29 September 1977, "because of substantial arrears of work", the medical report had not been considered by a delegate by the time of her retirement some three years later. This is a controversial matter which will be dealt with later. Mr. Whithear's decision was the subject of an application to the Tribunal for review, made pursuant to s.154(6) of the Act.

In its decision, the Tribunal seems to have assumed that, provided the requisite connection between the two medical conditions were established, the issue of the certificate pursuant to s.16(10) was within the Commissioner's power, notwithstanding the lapse of time which occurred between the receipt by the Commissioner of the report of the Commonwealth medical officer dated 29 September 1977 and the respondent's retirement some three years later. But, in my opinion, for reasons to be mentioned later, the validity of the certificate on this score should not be assumed.

It is convenient to deal first with the reasoning applied by the Tribunal in finding that the relationship required by s.66(2)(c) did not exist here.

In deciding that s.66(2) had no application in the present case, the Tribunal held that the incapacity which gave rise to the respondent's retirement was pain due to adhesions which occurred as a result of stretching and lifting of the arms; that, because both breasts had been removed, the incapacitating pain was neither "caused" nor "substantially contributed to" by the breast lumps; and that no "real and demonstrable connection on a substainable medical basis" existed between the incapacity and the physical condition described in the benefit classification certificate as "hormonal mastopathy presenting as breast lumps." In support of these conclusions, the Tribunal relied upon the opinion expressed by Dr. Chong that, although adhesions frequently follow a mastectomy, it is most unusual for a patient to be unable to work as a consequence of such adhesions - this was a "wholly unexpected consequence of the operation". The Tribunal expressed its conclusions in this way:

"25. The operation which the applicant underwent was both elective and preventative. It was not essential surgery in the sense that cancer was not diagnosed.

26. The consequence of the surgery in February 1978 was quite beyond the expectation of the surgeon and could not have reasonable been foreseen. That leads, in our view, to the conclusion that the incapacity which was the ground for retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by a physical condition connected with the condition referred to in the (Benefit Classification Certificate)."

It is not clear from the reasons given by the Tribunal whether its conclusions were referable to the terms of s.16(10)(b) as well as to those of s.66(2)(c). The effect of the two provisions is similar although there may be a difference in onus of proof since s.16(10)(b), expressed in a double negative, speaks of the Commissioner being "satisfied", whereas s.66(2)(b) requires him to be of the requisite "opinion".

For the purposes of s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c), the incapacity which was the ground for the respondent's retirement was her inability to elevate her arms by reason of the adhesions. The question then is whether that incapacity was "caused" or "substantially contributed to" by her pre-existing hormonal disorder or by a physical condition "connected with" that disorder. In short, can it be said that fibrous reaction was "connected with" the hormonal mastopathy?

In its ordinary meaning, "connected with" suggests a link or an association. In the present context, in my opinion, the phrase is intended to have its ordinary meaning and thus to pick up those cases where a link or an association exists between two medical conditions. That is a question of fact, to be determined in the light of the expert evidence.

It may be accepted that, in some cases, difficult questions of fact will arise for resolution in determining whether the requisite nexus between the two conditions has been established. In this respect, since the Commissioner bears the onus of proof (see Minister for Health v. Thomson [1985] FCA 208; (1985) 60 A.L.R. 701 at p.712), if the expert evidence available does not permit of a conclusion that the two conditions are linked or associated in a relevant medical sense, the provisions of s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c) will not apply. Further, a question of law, one of construction which it is presently unnecesary to resolve, could arise in some cases as to whether the nexus required by these provisions is one of causal connection or something short of that.

In the present case, the requisite link or association has, in my view, been established, even if it were necessary for the Commissioner to establish a causal connection for this purpose. The respondent's hormonal mastopathy required surgery; as a result of the surgery, the prosthesis was inserted; the prosthesis caused the fibrous reaction. It follows, in my opinion, that the reaction was "connected with" the earlier hormonal disorder for the purposes of both s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c).

In so concluding, I have not found it necessary or even helpful to seek analogies in the general law. In particular, I have not found it useful to consider whether a tortfeasor may or may not have been liable for certain loss or damage because the consequences of medical treatment were not reasonably foreseeable (see Minister of Pensions v. Chennell (1947) K.B. 250; cf. Mahoney v. J.K. Kruschich (Demolitions) Pty. Ltd. [1985] HCA 37; 59 A.L.R. 722 at p.725; Hart and Honore, Causation in the Law, 2nd ad. at p.184).

Notwithstanding this conclusion, the dispute between the parties is not necessarily resolved. There remains the question, raised by Davies, J. in argument, whether, in any event, the Commissioner was empowered by s.16(10) to issue a benefit classification certificate as late as 30 September 1980.

The Commissioner contends that the provisions of s.16(10)(a)(ii) apply in the present case. The suggestion is that, although the report of the Commonwealth medical officer was received by the Commissioner by about 29 September 1977, the backlog was so great that the Commissioner had not considered the report until after the respondent's retirement some three years later. This raises a question of fact not previously addressed in the proceedings and it is not appropriate that we now attempt to express any view on it. The proper course is for the matter to be remitted to the Tribunal for further consideration (cf. Drake v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 24 A.L.R. 577 at p.607).

Moreover, apart from the exploration of that issue of fact, a question of law arises as to the application here of s.16(10) in any event, having regard to the lapse of time between the medical examination and the issue of the certificate. As has been mentioned, as a result of the amendment of s.16 in 1978, a medical examination is no longer mandatory. Once the Commissioner has decided to require a medical examination, there is every reason to suppose that the statutory functions provided for by s.16(4) must be exercised, if at all, within a reasonable time. That is to say, the Commissioner must consider the report of the examination within a reasonable time; and, if he forms the requisite opinion, the Commissioner must also issue an appropriate certificate within a reasonable time (cf. R. v. Fairford Justices; Ex parte Brewster (1976) Q.B. 600; see also de Smith Judicial Review of Administrative Action 4th ed. pp.145-146). It is suggested on behalf of the Commissioner that notwithstanding that s.16(4) was not complied with in the case of the respondent, the Commissioner could nonetheless avail himself of the retrospective power to issue a certificate by calling in aid s.16(10). Presumably, that power is claimed to subsist for as long as 20 years (see s.16(5)): Re Homsv and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 A.L.D. N.18). Such an arbitrary result of the use of a transitory provision such as s.16(10) is unlikely to have been the legislative intention (see Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Proprietary Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of Australia [1981] HCA 26; (1980) 147 C.L.R. 297). But, in the absence of full argument on the point, it is not appropriate to pursue it now. I would, however, indicate that, for the reasons mentioned, I am not presently persuaded of the correctness of the decision in Homsv, supra.

I would allow the appeal, set aside the decision of the Tribunal and order that the matter be remitted to the Tribunal for rehearing in the light of these reasons. I would make no order as to the costs of the appeal. If applied for, I would grant the respondent a certificate pursuant to s.6(1) of the Federal Proceedings (Costs) Act, 1981 in respect of the costs incurred by her in relation to the appeal.

I find it necessary to add little to the statement of the facts of the case given in the reasons of Beaumont J. The respondent's ultimate incapacity, following on surgical treatment in 1978 and 1979, was described in the material as "capsular contractures", "fibrous reaction", or "adhesions". I shall generally use the last term. Dr. Chong, on whose evidence the Tribunal founded its decision, said, when asked how common it is to "get an occurrence of such severity that the person's employment is then affected or prejudiced as a result?" -

"Well, in my experience, as far as I can remember is, I think, Mrs. Miller is the only one I recall. She came back and said she cannot do this work. She told me that she had difficulty managing her work."

It was that answer which the Tribunal must have had in mind when it attributed to Dr. Chong the view that ". . . it is most unusual for a patient to be unable to work as a consequence of such adhesions. Indeed, the applicant was the only such case he could recall in his experience."

The Tribunal described the consequence of the operation as "wholly unexpected" and such as "could not reasonably have been foreseen". Those expressions must be understood as references to the severity of the adhesions, not their existence. Dr. Chong said, and the Tribunal accepted, that 50% or more of operations of the nature in question lead to the complication of fibrous reaction. A conclusion that adhesions could not reasonably have been foreseen would not have been open on the accepted evidence.

It is with this understanding of the intended meaning of the word "consequence" that one has to read the ultimate conclusion of the Tribunal in paragraph 26 of its reasons:

"The consequence of the surgery in February 1978 was quite beyond the expectation of the surgeon and could not reasonably have been foreseen. That leads, in our view, to the conclusion that the incapacity which was the ground for retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by a physical condition connected with the condition referred to in the BCC."

"BCC" is an abbreviation for "benefit classification certificate".

Clearly, the adhesions were, or directly caused, the incapacity which led to retirement. For that reason, the critical question became whether the adhesions were connected with the condition referred to in the certificate, namely mastopathy. The fact that the consequence of the surgery - i.e. the severity of that consequence - could not reasonably have been foreseen led the Tribunal to the conclusion, it appears, that there was no such connection.

Where there is uncertainty, as a purely factual matter, whether one event caused another, consideration of whether the second event commonly succeeds the first may be of help. For example, if, after a surgical operation, a patient is found to suffer from a certain condition, evidence that the condition is expected to occur after the operation may tend to show that it was caused by the operation. In that sense, foreseeability may be a test of causation.

Here, however, there was no question of causation; the Tribunal expressly recognised that the adhesions were "the consequence of the surgery". It appears to me to have been an error to deny the connection between the condition for which surgery was performed and the consequence of the surgery because the magnitude of that consequence was unexpectedly great.

The next question is whether the error was a legal one; the appeal from the Tribunal is only "on a question of law". The question whether a precisely specified set of facts falls within or outside a statutory description must often, at least, be one of law, for if it is not, then each of two opposite conclusions, based on the same set of facts, must be said to be legally correct. Here, the appeal involves the correctness of the proposition that, within the meaning of the statutory provisions, what is said to be the result of medical or surgical treatment is not "connected with" the condition treated if that result was unforeseeable in degree, though not in kind. Whatever be the precise meaning of the phrase "connected with", it is my opinion that such a proposition cannot stand, for it gives the phrase an erroneously narrow scope.

The expression "connected with" is not very precise. It is my view that the sort of connection which is meant is a causal one. For the purposes of s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976, two conditions are connected with one another if there is a substantial causal link between the two. That may be so because one is a cause of the other, or because both have a common cause. It is my opinion that unless there is a substantial causal link, there is no connection. I appreciate that the word "substantial" introduces questions of degree, but they are neither questions which need to be resolved for the purposes of this case, nor questions as to which one can usefully give any guidance. The view I have expressed on this point accords, I think, with that set out in the reasons of Davies J.

It is necessarily involved in what I have said that, if there is a substantial causal link between two conditions, they are "connected with" one another whether or not the final condition was foreseeable.

In arriving at my view as to the sort of connection contemplated by the legislature, I have been influenced by notions current in other areas of the law in which this sort of problem arises. It is a familiar one, for example, in the law of workers' compensation where the question is whether a condition appearing after treatment was one "arising out of and in the course of the employment". In Lindeman Ltd. v. Colvin [1946] HCA 35; (1946) 74 C.L.R. 313 at 321, the High Court had to consider such a case. Referring to a "line of decisions dealing with further or aggravated injury ascribed to medical treatment" Dixon J. said at p.321:

"These decisions show that, if an injury resulting from accident arising out of and in the course of the employment is aggravated by medical treatment or if the surgical procedures adopted to remedy or alleviate the injury cause secondary traumatic or pathological condition or death, the total condition is to be attributed to the accident, that is so long as the workman acted reasonably. . . The surgical procedure must, however, be directed to the original injury, and death or disablement caused by additional procedures to remedy other ills cannot be attributed to the original accident or injury. . ."

In that case, of course, the boot was on the other foot; it was in the interests of the injured worker to find a causal connection between the initial condition and the final one. It may be that a more substantial link will be thought necessary under the provisions presently being considered, in order to deny a retired worker superannuation, than would have been enough for the opposite purpose under workers' compensation statutes. The significance of the dictum of Sir Owen Dixon, however, is that it illustrates that the finding of a connection, on a causal basis, between a condition consequential upon treatment and the condition treated is a familiar idea in a cognate branch of the law. It is so, further, in the law of negligence where injured plaintiffs commonly recover in respect of conditions caused by treatment designed to alleviate the injury brought about by the defendant.

My conclusion is that the Tribunal was wrong in law in denying, for the reason it gave, the existence of the requisite connection.

It is necessary to consider next what is the proper order to make. Section 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 reads, so far as relevant, as follows:

"44 (1) A party to a proceeding before the Tribunal may appeal to the Federal Court of Australia, on a question of law, from any decision of the Tribunal in that proceeding.

. . .

(4) The Federal Court of Australia shall hear and determine the appeal

and may make such order as it thinks appropriate by reason of its decision.

(5) Without limiting by implication the generality of subsection (4), the orders that may be made by the Federal Court of Australia on an appeal include an order affirming or setting aside the decision of the Tribunal, and an order remitting the case to be heard and decided again, either with or without the hearing of further evidence, by the Tribunal in accordance with the directions of the Court."

It follows from s.44(4) that the court's function is to make such order as is appropriate by reason of "its decision" - i.e. its decision on the question of law mentioned in subsection (1). Having decided the question of law on which the appeal was brought favourably to the appellant, the court must set the Tribunal's decision aside, since the reasoning held to be erroneous lay at the foundation of that decision. What is not quite so clear is whether the case should be remitted to the Tribunal "to be heard and decided again".

One consideration in favour of that course is that the respondent would then have an opportunity to urge upon the Tribunal alternative reasons for arriving at the same conclusion. It does not appear to me, however, that on the Tribunal's basic findings, a conclusion favourable to the respondent is open, with respect to the question whether there was the necessary connection. It is plain that the surgical treatment undergone was prompted by, and appropriate for, the respondent's mastopathy, and brought about the adhesions which caused or constituted the incapacity in question. On the Tribunal's findings, there was a substantial causal link between the mastopathy and the adhesions. It follows, in my view, that the matter should not be remitted for reconsideration of that aspect of the case.

A second consideration which might be urged in favour of remission is that the respondent may wish to raise a new point referred to in the reasons of Beaumont J., namely the question whether the commission was empowered by s.16(10) to issue a benefit classification certificate on 30 September, 1980; that was three years, a remarkably long time, after the medical examination conducted under s.16(2). On 12 January, 1984, in the process of reconsideration under s.154 of the Act, Mr. R.C. Whithear purported to vary the decision of September 1980 to issue a benefit classification certificate by substituting for the condition specified in it "hormonal mastopathy presenting as breast lumps". In the respondent's application to the Tribunal for review of the decision of Mr. Whithear, the solicitor for the respondent sought to challenge that variation as well as the decision pursuant to s.66(2)(c). However, counsel for the respondent disclaimed, before the Tribunal, any intention to attack the former decision. Although there is no absolute rule that a party is bound by such conduct, there is a tendency in appellate courts not to entertain points abandoned below. The appellant, of course, did not seek to debate the point either before the Tribunal, or before us.

I can see that, depending upon what facts were found, legal grounds of some cogency might well be put forward in an attack upon either the issue of the benefit classification certificate on 30 September, 1980 or its variation by Mr. Whithear. The question which troubles me, however, is whether it is correct for this court to order that the case be remitted for determination of the validity of those steps under the power to "make such order as it thinks appropriate by reason of its decision". What seems to be contemplated by s.44 is that this court shall determine the legal question brought before it, and having done so, dispose of the matter either finally or by remission to the Tribunal in such fashion as seems appropriate in consequence of the decision on the legal point. If it appears to this court that some other question, being one neither agitated before the Tribunal nor raised on appeal to this court, might, with advantage to the respondent, be determined by the Tribunal, is a remission of the case for that purpose an order which might be described as "appropriate by reason of its decision"?

I respectfully think not. In my view, "its decision" in subsection (4) means the decision on the question of law referred to in subsection (1). An order of remission for determination of another question (being, presumably, a mixed one of law and fact) does not seem to me to be an order which may be described as appropriate by reason of the decision on the question of law on which the appeal was based. It would better be described as one appropriate not by reason of any decision at all, but by reason of doubts entertained on a point other than that question of law.

I am encouraged to adopt this view by two considerations. Of these the broader one is that to remit a case to the Tribunal so that it may determine a question of mixed law and fact, not the subject of the appeal to this court, appears to me, in a practical sense, to accord ill with the scheme of s.44. I think the section's intention is that the decision of the Tribunal shall not be interfered with except on a legal question and that this court shall make no orders under s.44 except such as are consequential on its decision on that legal question. The purpose of circumscribing this court's function in such cases is presumably to limit the extent to which community resources are applied to disputation about administrative matters. This dispute has already gone from one delegate of the Commissioner to another by way of reconsideration, then to the Tribunal, and then to this court. It appears to me that the court should lean against a construction of s.44(4) which would justify an order for redetermination of the matter on a new point not the subject of the appeal - a point which, when determined by the Tribunal, can presumably come up again to this court.

A second factor inclining me against remission of the case on the basis mentioned is that such an order would be difficult to reconcile with the view of s.44 expressed by Sheppard J. in Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v. Gungor [1982] FCA 99; (1982) 42 A.L.R. 209 at pp. 220 and 221. Speaking of the relevant portions of s.44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act quoted above, his Honour said:

"It is, in my opinion, not correct to say that this court is by these provisions given wide powers to make such order as it thinks fit. Implicit in its powers are a number of restrictions. The appeal is expressly limited to error of law, which alleged error is the sole matter before this court and is the only subject matter of any order made consequent on the appeal. The order which this court can make after hearing the appeal is also similarly restricted to an order which is appropriate by reason of its decision. It follows that the only order which can be properly made is one the propriety of which is circumscribed by and necessary to reflect this court's view on the alleged or found error of law. To go further I would see as amounting to exceeding the jurisdiction of this court under this section."

The construction favoured by Sheppard J. was agreed in by Toohey J. in Director-General of Social Services v. Hangan [1982] FCA 262; (1982) 45 A.L.R. 23 at pp. 34, 35 and by Lockhart J. in Director-General of Social Services v. Hales (1983) 47 A.L.R. 231 at pp. 309-310.

In the present case, it cannot be said that an order remitting the matter for determination of the decisions with respect to the benefit classification certificate, would be "necessary to reflect this court's view on the . . . found error of law". I do not find it necessary to reach a final conclusion on the question of the court's power to determine legal questions other than that on which the appeal is brought; presumably, it is open to a respondent to seek to uphold the decision below on any ground. What is involved in the remission to determine the new point, however, is not a decision in favour of the respondent but a partial re-trial to investigate a matter which has never been before the Tribunal.

For the reasons given, I would simply allow the appeal and set aside the Tribunal's decision. I regretfully dissent from the view of Davies and Beaumont JJ. that the matter should be remitted.
