Hanlon and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 190 (7 December 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007

Superannuation - determination of retirement benefits - invalidity retirement on ground of condition specified in Benefit Classification Certificate - application for review - condition arising from work accident - Condition existing prior to entry to Fund - nature of onus of proof before Tribunal - whether incapacity caused by condition specified in Certificate. Superannuation Act 1976 ss 11, 16, 66

ARTHUR JOHN HANLON

THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. 20002 of 1979

Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

Mr W.B. Borthwick (Member)

7 December 1979

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No.20002 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: ARTHUR JOHN HANLON

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The Tribunal affirms the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined by Section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DATED this 7th day of December 1979.

Sgd. Allan N. Hall

(Allan N. Hall)

Senior Member

Sgd. M. Glick

(M. Glick)

Member

Sgd. W.B. Borthwick

(W.B. Borthwick)

Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. 20002 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: ARTHUR JOHN HANLON

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

The question which arises for our consideration in this application for review is the entitlement of Mr Arthur John Hanlon, the applicant in the matter, to benefits under the Superannuation Act 1976, following his retirement on 10 October 1978 on the grounds of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age.

Although the applicant had been a temporary employee of the Commonwealth Government for some 22 years prior to the date of his retirement, it was not until after the introduction of the Superannuation Act 1976 which came into operation on 1 July 1976 that the applicant first became entitled to contribute for superannuation benefits. He had attempted to join the former Provident Fund in 1970 but had been rejected on medical grounds. Following a direction given by the Commissioner for Superannuation pursuant to section 11 of the Act on the 25th August, 1976 directing that Mr Hanlon was, as from that date, an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act, Mr Hanlon was medically examined on the 14th September 1976 by Dr CFA Younger who is now the Director of the Commonwealth Department of Health in Tasmania. Dr Younger reported that:-

"After clinical examination, medical tests and specialist advice, as appropriate, I consider the applicant does not meet general medical standard due to the following mental/physical condition(s). Mental/Physical Condition(s) Suffered Myocardial insufficiency (Coronary 1974) Gout Osteo-arthritis"

Being of the opinion that the applicant was not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with the abovementioned conditions, to continue to be an eligible employee until he attained his maximum retirement age, the Commissioner issued a Benefit Classification Certificate dated 6 April 1977, pursuant to section 16 (4) of the Act specifying those conditions. The applicant was given a copy of the Certificate which included notification of his rights under the Act to request variation or revocation of the Certificate at any time; notification that there were rights of appeal; and notification as to the effect of the Certificate. The applicant did not before his retirement challenge in any way the correctness of the Certificate nor did he seek to have the Certificate revoked or varied pursuant to section 16(6) of the Act.

Neither of the first two grounds mentioned in the Benefit Classification Certificate were suggested to be relevant to the applicant's ultimate retirement. Acting pursuant to section 66(2)(c) of the Act however, the Commissioner formed the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition of osteo-arthritis specified in the Certificate or by a physical condition connected with osteo-arthritis. The question before us is whether we are of the same opinion.

At his medical examination by Dr Younger on the 14th September, 1976 the applicant disclosed that he had sustained an injury to his left shoulder in the course of his employment as a painter for the Department of Housing and Construction on the 10th September 1975. It appears that he had fallen through a ceiling of premises at which he was working and that, in order to save himself, had caught his left arm over one of the ceiling joists, thereby wrenching his left shoulder. The applicant said that before the accident there had been some arthritis in both shoulders, but it was "a very minor business" which had never affected his work.

Mr Michael Hodgman, M.H.R., who appeared for the applicant, endeavoured to persuade us that the reference in the Benefit Classification Certificate to "osteo-arthritis" must be taken to refer to this pre-accident arthritis or, alternatively, that the reference to "osteo-arthritis" was so unspecific as to the part of the body affected that we could not take it as referring to any injury caused to the applicant's left shoulder in the accident on 10 September 1975. However, we find ourselves quite unable to accept either of these submissions. In the first place, the applicant's evidence leaves no doubt that when he disclosed in his statement of personal medical history that he suffered from "arthritis", he was referring (to use his own words) to "arthritis brought on by the fall". This evidence is confirmed by the medical records kept by Dr Younger in relation to the applicant's medical examination. In particular, Dr Younger's report discloses that the applicant "cannot abduct L. arm beyond 90deg.. Crepitus +". "Crepitus", he said, "was a grating sound or a grating sensation which is commonly associated with osteoarthritis" . We accordingly find as a fact that at the time of his medical examination by Dr Younger, the applicant was suffering from osteo-arthritis of the left shoulder which was either caused or contributed to by the accident on 10 September 1975 and that this was the condition of osteo-arthritis referred to in the Benefit Classification Certificate.

Subsequent to the accident the applicant found himself unable to continue in his previous employment as a Leading Hand Painter and was re-deployed into gardening work. But even this work had become beyond his capabilities by April 1978. On the 19th April, 1978 Mr K Burridge, a Leading Hand Fitter employed in the Department of Housing and Construction, who was called on behalf of the applicant to give evidence, said that he wrote to the Industrial Officer, Hobart stating:-

"Following a discussion with Mr A.J. Hanlon on 18 April 1978 I am convinced that he is no longer capable of continuing his duties allocated to him as Yardman at Dowsing Point depot.

The lawn mower is too much strain on his left shoulder as is the cleaning of the incinerator and carting rubbish to the tip. His arm and shoulder has continued to deteriorate since he injured it in an accident in the course of his duty as a Leading Hand Painter with the Department."

It was following Mr Burridge's report that the applicant was referred on 28th April, 1978 to Dr HR Brigden, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, for examination. So far as relevant, Dr Brigden's report dated 28th April, 1978 was as follows:-

"After clinical examination, medical tests and specialist tests as appropriate I consider that the examinee is suffering from the following medical conditions (in order of severity) and with estimated percentage each contributes to the incapacity.

Condition Percentage

1.Frozen shoulder 80%
2.Chronic Bronchitis 10%
3.Hypertension 10%

Brief history of the condition(s) including origins and the time first noted by contributor:-

Workers Compensation injury to left shoulder in 1975. Re-deployed as Gardener, now unable to cope with use of lawn mower, due to constant pain, limitation of movement and weakness of left shoulder and left arm. Prognosis: Condition worsening and increasing loss of time. I consider that the examinee:

is unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity.

The incapacity is not due to any wilful action on the part of the examinee for the purposes of obtaining a pension.

HOBART (Sgd) H.R. Brigden

Location Commonwealth Medical

Officer

28 April 1978."

In amplification of his report of 28th April, 1978 Dr Brigden wrote to the Commissioner for Superannuation on 10th April, 1979 as follows:-

"The condition which resulted in Mr Hanlon's retirement was quite definitely the same condition which was present at the time of the medical examination on 14.9.1976 and which resulted in the benefit classification certificate for osteo-arthritis being issued by your office.

The condition was the result of a fall in 1975 which resulted in an injury to his left shoulder, commonly referred to as a rotator-cuff lesion. You will appreciate that the shoulder, with its great range of movement, is a particularly complex joint, many types of tissue are involved in an injury, and general descriptive names such as "rotator-cuff" lesion, from the muscles around the joint, or "frozen-shoulder" from the reduced movement may be used. The effect on the bony surfaces of the joint post-traumatic is osteoarthritis.

Therefore it is not unlikely that at various times such a condition may be called different names, however unfortunate this may be.

The injury, and its effects, was accepted under the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 as amended, and, in fact Mr Hanlon was paid under the provisions of the Act for time lost in September, October, November and December 1977, and January, February and March 1978.

Attached are photocopies of the referral papers from the Department of Construction, requesting Mr Hanlon's examination in April 1978. There would appear to be no reasonable doubt that Mr Hanlon was perfectly aware of the fact that he was retired because of the increasing disability due to his shoulder injury, which was causing constant pain and loss of time."

Dr Brigden was not called to give evidence, which was unfortunate having regard to the criticisms directed by Mr Hodgman on the applicant's behalf against what he described as the "ex post facto rationalisation" contained in the letter. Dr Brigden, so Mr Hodgman submitted, had nowhere referred in his original medical examination report to the "frozen shoulder" being due to osteo-arthritis. It was claimed that as the Benefit Classification Certificate would have been available to Dr Brigden at the time of the medical examination on 28 April, 1978, he should have been aware of the importance and significance of his examination. The failure to mention osteo-arthritis until twelve months later, and then only in response to specific questions raised by the Commissioner was, in the applicant's submission, very significant.

Dr Younger, however, gave evidence that the condition known as "frozen shoulder" is "a loose term given to describe a condition which is descriptive of a shoulder limited in movement ." It can frequently be caused by osteo-arthritis, although it can also be caused by other things such as "a tearing of the muscle joint which can be caused by an accident and in an older person this leads to calcification and a condition of frozen shoulder". Dr Younger considered it "quite likely" that with an osteo-arthritis condition, some two years later a "frozen shoulder" could develop.

In addition, we had before us as part of Exhibit T4 a copy of a medical report signed by Dr Douglas Parker dated 15 September 1977 relating to his examination of the applicant in connection with his workers compensation claim. That report referred to the applicant having sustained "a severe wrench injury to his left shoulder following a fall at his work in 1975." It mentioned the difficulties the applicant was then experiencing in carrying out his duties as a gardener and his complaints of pain and more restricted movement in his left arm. On examination Dr Parker noted in relation to the applicant's left shoulder, that:-

"1. Abduction is now limited to approximately 45 degrees, that is half normal range and forcing causes pain.

2.Forward flexion is limited to approximately half normal range - this has progressed since last seen. Forcing also causes pain.
3.Rotary movements are restricted to approximately one third of range and are also painful on forcing."

Dr Parker's opinions in relation to the left shoulder were:-

"1. The condition has progressed since last seen

2. His symptoms are arising from a post traumatic arthritis of the left shoulder which has been caused by his accident".

The applicant called no medical evidence as to the cause of his retirement. In his application for review, he asserted that the real cause of his enforced retirement "was the disability he suffered as the direct result of the accident of 10th September, 1975". He did not dispute that as the direct result of the accident he developed a "frozen shoulder" and that his osteo-arthritis worsened. Indeed, he affirmed repeatedly in his evidence that, in his opinion, he would still he able to work if it had not been for the injuries which he had sustained in the accident in September, 1975. The "sole cause" of his retirement, he claimed in his application for review, was the injury and disability he sustained in the accident "and the aggravation of that disability accompanied by the increased development of osteoarthritis which followed the said accident". To corroborate his evidence that it was only since the accident that he had exhibited difficulty in discharging his duties the applicant called a number of witnesses from the Department of Housing and Construction. We have no difficulty in accepting their evidence on that basis.

Having regard to the way in which the applicant presented his case and having regard to our finding with respect to the condition of osteo-arthritis referred to in the Benefit Classification Certificate we think that the only question which remains for our decision is whether we are of the opinion, on the evidence before us that the condition somewhat loosely described by Dr Brigden as a "frozen shoulder" was in fact caused by or substantially contributed to by the condition of osteo-arthritis or by a physical condition connected with the osteo-arthritis which was in evidence when the applicant became an "eligible employee" in 1977.

Mr Hodgman submitted that there was an onus on the Commissioner to establish positively the connection between the two conditions. It was not for the applicant to seek to disprove that connection. The Commissioner, he submitted, had failed to discharge this onus.

We do not see the question before us in this light. The question which arises for our consideration is not whether the Commissioner's opinion was reasonable or proper having regard to the evidence before him, but rather whether this Tribunal, standing as it does for this purpose in the shoes of the Commissioner, is itself of the opinion, having regard to the evidence and material before the Tribunal, that the necessary-causal link referred to in section 66(2)(c) is established: cf Drake and the Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60.

Where a party to proceedings for administrative review seeks to persuade the Tribunal as to the existence or non-existence of a particular fact there may, in a practical sense, be an onus on that party to call credible evidence tending to the proof or disproof of that fact. But as was said by the Tribunal in Re Ladybird Children's Wear Pty Ltd and Department of Business and Consumer Affairs (1978) 1 ALD 1 at p5 : "....such an onus does not arise from the making of a decision which is brought up to the Tribunal for review": see also Re Greenham and Minister for the Capital Territory (1979) 2 ALD 137 at p.142 and Re Eckersley and Minister for the Capital Territory (No.78/5020). This is an aspect of administrative review which is frequently misunderstood. Because the Tribunal is reviewing the decision, (see Drake's Case - supra) it is not just a matter of seeking to demonstrate that the reasons for decision given by the decision-maker may in some respects be in error. What must also be considered is whether evidence of a more positive nature to support the conclusion for which a party contends as being the correct conclusion may not need to be called.

Mr Hodgman was undoubtedly justified in pointing to the absence of any specific reference to "osteo arthritis" in Dr Brigden's report of 28 April 1978. The less specific expression "frozen shoulder", may well have left open the possibility that the "frozen shoulder" was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical condition other than osteo arthritis. But what evidence is there to give credence to any alternative possibility?

Dr Younger's evidence, supported by the evidence of the applicant himself, leaves no room for doubt that at the time of the applicant's original medical examination on 14 September 1976 he was then exhibiting severe limitation in the movement of his left shoulder due to post-traumatic arthritis. When examined by Dr Parker some twelve months later for the purposes of his workers' compensation claim, his condition had deteriorated. Dr Parker expressed the opinion that his symptoms arose from "a post traumatic arthritis of the left shoulder which has been caused by his accident". Dr Parker also gave the prognosis that "some time in the future this patient is very likely to become totally and permanently incapacitated for work". Whilst Dr Younger did not examine the applicant when he was invalided out of the service, his evidence establishes it as "quite likely" that the condition of osteoarthritis will lead to a "frozen shoulder". In the light of this evidence there do not appear to us to be any reasonable grounds for doubting the correctness of Dr Brigden's opinion in his letter dated 10 April 1979 that the condition which resulted in Mr Hanlon's retirement was "quite definitely" the osteoarthritis referred to in the Benefit Classification Certificate. We therefore find as a fact that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was either caused or, alternatively, was substantially contributed to by the condition of osteoarthritis specified in that Certificate. In the light of this finding we do not need to consider an issue which was raised by the applicant at the hearing (for the first time, so far as we are aware) that he also sustained a whiplash injury to his neck as the result of the accident and that pain and disability due to this injury contributed to his inability to continue work.

Whilst the findings of fact which we have made conclude the matter against the applicant and lead us to affirm the Commissioner's decision that the benefit entitlement of the applicant by reason of his retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976, there are two matters on which we wish to comment by way of conclusion.

Firstly, the applicant complained in his correspondence with the Commissioner that the decision to deny him invalidity benefits under the Act because of the consequences of an injury by accident sustained in the course of his employment by the Commonwealth was "unfair". Mr Hodgman, in a spirited plea for justice on behalf of his client, submitted that his client's "rights" to superannuation benefits should not be taken away without the clearest evidence to justify such a course. The evidence discloses, however, that the applicant was employed as a temporary employee throughout the 22 years of his employment by the Commonwealth. For twenty of those years he was not a contributor for any superannuation benefits. It was not until after the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976 that he first became entitled to contribute for such benefits but, unfortunately, by that time he was carrying a serious injury to his left shoulder which ultimately caused his retirement. The hardship of the case lies in the fact that the 1976 Act came too late so far as the applicant was concerned.

Secondly, we think that the evidence in this case points up the necessity for greater precision in identifying the causes for invalidity where a Benefit Classification Certificate is in existence. The present proceedings may well have been avoided if the form of the medical report which Dr Brigden completed after he had examined the applicant had required him to direct his mind specifically to the relationship which, in his opinion, existed between the grounds for retirement and the conditions specified in the Certificate. This is a problem the Commissioner may need to consider.

Our decision is that the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation is affirmed.
