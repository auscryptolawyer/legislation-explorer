Re Gunter Thiel v the Commissioner of Taxation [1988] FCA 443 (20 December 1988)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: GUNTER THIEL

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. WA G35 of 1988

FED No. 766

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

WESTERN AUSTRALIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Northrop(1), Sheppard(2) and Lee(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - taxpayer acquired units in trust which he disposed of in consideration of allotment of shares in company - shares sold at substantial profit within 12 months of allotment - taxpayer a resident of Switzerland - analysis of primary Judge's findings - rejection of challenges to them - whether profits arose from "enterprise carried on" by taxpayer within meaning of agreement between Australia and Switzerland for the avoidance of double taxation.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss. 25, 25A, 26AAA

Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953, ss. 4, 11E

Australia/Switzerland Double Taxation Agreement; Articles 3, 7 and 13

Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties; Articles 31, 32

OECD Model Convention for Avoidance of Double Taxation; Articles 3, 7, 13 and 21

Commentary thereon

HEARING

PERTH

20:12:1988

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr I. Gzell Q.C. with Mr P. Fletcher

Solicitors for the Applicant: Messrs Solomon Brothers

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr A.J. Templeman QC with

Mr M.J. Buss

Solicitor for the Respondent: The Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The appeal be dismissed.

The appellant pay to the respondent his costs of the appeal.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

At all material times the appellant was a resident of Switzerland. While in Australia in February 1984 he purchased 4 units in the Energy Research Unit Trust, an Australian trust, for $50,000. In May 1984 he purchased 2 further units for $100,000. In November 1984 he sold the 6 units for 600,000 50 cent shares in Energy Research Group Australia Ltd ("ERGA"), an Australian company established as a vehicle for the public venture of the Unit Trust. During February and March 1985, by some 40 separate transactions, he sold 252,000 of the now listed shares for $566,307.30. Part of the proceeds of the sale of these shares was frozen by action taken in March 1985 by the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") to satisfy payment of tax assessed on the profits made by the appellant from the sale of the units and the shares. As a result of the action taken by the Commissioner to assess him for tax, the appellant did not sell any of the shares still retained by him. Tax in the sum of $364,220.50 was assessed by the Commissioner as being due and payable by the appellant under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act"). This amount has been paid to the Commissioner. The taxable income was assessed by the Commissioner as follows:-

Energy Research Unit Trust

Feb. 84 acquired 4 Units for $ 50,000

June 84 acquired 2 Units for $100,000

6 Units Cost $150,000

23.11.84 Sold 6 Units for 600,000 x 50c

shares in Energy Research Group

Australia Ltd

Valued at par = $300,000

Profit caught by Section 26AAA = $150,000

Feb/Mar 85 Sold 252,000 shares for $566,307.30

Cost price (252000 x 50c) $126,000.00

Profit vide Section 26AAA = $440,307.30

Total Assessable Profit = $590,307

2. At all material times, the appellant was, for the purposes of the Assessment Act, a non-resident of Australia but under paragraph 25(1)(b) of the Assessment Act his assessable income included the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources in Australia. There is no doubt that the appellant was, under a number of the provisions of the Assessment Act, liable to be assessed for tax on the profit arising from the sale of the units and of the shares. The appellant may be liable under the laws of Switzerland to pay tax on the income derived from the sale of the same assets. The issue for determination in this appeal is whether the appellant is entitled to the benefits of the agreement between Australia and Switzerland for the avoidance of double taxation with respect to taxes on income.

3. The facts giving rise to this appeal are set out fully in the judgment of Lee J. and need not be repeated. I agree with his findings made on those facts. In particular, I find that the appellant has failed to establish that his decision to purchase the units upon the understanding that they were to be converted and made negotiable on a public market at which time the investment was to be sold and thus a profit obtained, was part of the diversification of the appellant's existing business in Switzerland or the commencement of a new business by him in Switzerland. The activities carried out by the appellant in Australia have all the hallmarks of being entered into for the purpose, or with the intention of making a profit from isolated activities. This purpose was a separate activity from his business activities in Switzerland. There is no doubt that the profit arising from the sale of the units and the shares was assessable income of the appellant under sub-section 26AAA(2) of the Assessment Act. In all probability the profit arising from the sale of the units and the shares was assessable income of the appellant under one or both of the limbs contained in sub-section 25A(1) of the Assessment Act. The profits arising from the sale of the units and the shares was derived directly from sources in Australia and unless the appellant is entitled to the benefits of the agreement between Australia and Switzerland, the appellant, being a non-resident of Australia, is liable to pay the tax assessed on that income.

4. The long title to the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953 ("the Agreements Act") is "An Act to give the force of Law to certain Conventions and Agreements with respect to Taxes on Income, and for purposes incidental thereto". The Assessment Act is incorporated in and is to be read as one with the Agreements Act; sub-section 4(1). By reason of the provisions contained in sub-section 3(1) of that Act, in that Act the word "Agreement" is defined to include the Swiss agreement, the words "Australian Tax" are defined to include income tax imposed under the Assessment Act, the words "foreign tax" are defined to include tax, other than Australian tax, which is the subject of the Swiss Agreement and the words "the Swiss Agreement" mean:-

"the Agreement between the Government of Australia

and the Swiss Federal Council for the avoidance of

double taxation with respect to taxes on income and

the protocol to that agreement, being the agreement

and protocol a copy of each of which in the English

language is set out in Schedule 15;"

5. Under sub-section 3(2) of the Agreements Act, for the purposes of that Act and the Assessment Act:-

"... a reference in an agreement to profits of an

activity or business shall, in relation to

Australian tax, be read, where the context so

permits, as a reference to taxable income derived

from that activity or business."

6. Statutory effect is given to the Swiss Agreement by s.11E of the Agreements Act and the provisions of paragraph 11E(1)(b) are set out:-

"11E. (1) Subject to this Act, on and after the date

of entry into force of the Swiss agreement, the

provisions of the agreement, so far as those

provisions affect Australian tax, have, and shall be

deemed to have had, the force of law-

(a) .......

(b) in relation to tax other than withholding

tax - in respect of income of the year of

income that commenced on 1 July 1979 and of

a subsequent year of income in relation to

which the agreement remains effective."

7. The Swiss Agreement is headed "Agreement between Australia and Switzerland for the avoidance of double taxation with respect to taxes on income", and that purpose is contained in a recital to the agreement. It was signed on 28 February 1980 and came into force on 13 February 1981. It was in force during the financial year 1984-85. The Agreement applies to persons who are residents of one or both of the Contracting States being Australia and Switzerland; Article 1. In the Agreement, the word "person" is defined to include an individual, a company and any other body of persons; Article 3. In this appeal, much turns upon the construction of the word "enterprise" used in the Agreement and for this reason sub-paragraph (1)(f) and paragraph 2 of Article 3 is set out-

"(1) In this Agreement, unless the context otherwise

requires-

(a) .....

(f) the terms "enterprise of one of the Contracting

States" and "enterprise of the other Contracting

State" mean an enterprise carried on by a resident

of Australia or an enterprise carried on by a

resident of Switzerland, as the context requires;

....

(2) In the application of this Agreement by one of the

Contracting States, any term not otherwise defined shall,

unless the context otherwise requires, have the meaning

which it has under the laws of that Contracting State

relating to the taxes to which this Agreement applies.

8. The application of Article 4 makes it clear that the appellant is a resident of Switzerland and a non resident of Australia. Article 5 defines the meaning to be given to the phrase "permanent establishment". Paragraph 5(1) is set out-

"(1) For the purposes of this Agreement, the term

"permanent establishment" means a fixed place of

business through which the business of an enterprise

is wholly or partly carried on."

9. The other paragraphs of Article 5 specify what comes or does not come within the term a "permanent establishment". On the facts of this appeal, it is clear that the appellant did not have a permanent establishment in Australia within the meaning of the Agreement at the time he engaged in the activities of acquiring and selling the units and the shares. It is interesting to note that this definition uses the expression "the business of an enterprise" and not the sole word "enterprise".

10. A consideration of the Articles of the Agreement shows that certain types of income of a non-resident of Australia may be taxed in full under the Assessment Act on income derived from a source in Australia. Thus under Article 6 income from real property situated in Australia may be taxed pursuant to the Assessment Act, while income or gains from the alienation of real property in Australia or related interests including shares in certain types of companies the assets of which consist wholly or principally of real property in Australia are liable, pursuant to Article 13, to be taxed under the Assessment Act. Special provision is made with respect to specified types of income such as income from independent personal services; Article 14, dependant personal services; Article 15, directors' fees; Article 16, entertainers; Article 17, pensions and annuities; Article 18, government service; Article 19, and students; Article 20. Other Articles contain provisions relating to the methods by which effect is to be given to the terms of the Agreement. In this respect special reference is made to Article 22.

11. In the present case, the appellant claims that he is entitled to the protection conferred by Article 7 and Article 13. Article 7 is headed "Business Profits". Paragraphs (2),(3) and (4) of Article 7 apply where an enterprise carries on business through a permanent establishment and thus have no direct application to the facts of this appeal. Paragraphs (1) and (5) of Article 7 are set out:-

"(1) The profits of an enterprise of one of the

Contracting States shall be taxable only in that

State unless the enterprise carries on business in

the other Contracting State through a permanent

establishment situated therein. If the enterprise

carries on business as aforesaid, the profits of

the enterprise may be taxed in the other State, but

only so much of them as is attributable to that

permanent establishment.

(2) .....

(5) Where profits include items of income which

are dealt with separately in other Articles of this

Agreement, then the provisions of those Articles shall

not be affected by the provisions of this Article".

12. Article 13 is headed "Alienation of Property". Paragraphs (1) and (2) of Article 13 contain provisions relating to the alienation of real property and related interests. Those paragraphs are complementary to Article 6 and provide that income or gains from the alienation of real property and related interests may be taxed pursuant to the Assessment Act. Paragraph (3) of Article 13 relates to the alienation of other types of property. That paragraph provides:-

"(3) Subject to the provisions of paragraphs (1)

and (2), income from the alienation of capital

assets of an enterprise of one of the Contracting

States shall be taxable only in that Contracting

State, but, where those assets form part of the

business property of a permanent establishment

situated in the other Contracting State, such

income may be taxed in that other State."

13. In the present appeal, the appellant has been assessed for tax on the basis of profits arising from the sale of the units and the shares. The units and the shares constituted captial assets which did not come within paragraphs (1) or (2).

14. On any view the appellant was not carrying on business in Australia through a permanent establishment situated in Australia, Article 7(1), and the first question that arises is whether the profits received by him from the sale of the units and the shares are "profits of an enterprise" carried on by the appellant being a resident of Switzerland; see Article 7(1) and the definition of the term "enterprise of one of the Contracting States" and "enterprise of the other Contracting State" contained in Article 3(1)(f). The second question is whether the profits received by the appellant from the sale of the units and the shares are "income from the alienation of capital assets of an enterprise" carried on by the appellant being a resident of Switzerland; see Article 13(3). An affirmative answer to either of these questions entitles the appellant to the benefit conferred by the Agreement.

15. The issue raised by the first question is whether the activities carried on by the appellant between February 1984 and March 1985 constituted an "enterprise" within Article 7(1) of the Agreement. This depends upon the construction to be given to the word "enterprise" in Article 7(1). Does it include isolated activities or is it to be limited to where some entity or undertaking carries on a business? The issue raised by the second question is whether the activities carried on by the appellant between February 1984 and March 1985 constituted an "enterprise" within Article 13(3) of the Agreement. This depends upon the construction to be given to the word "enterprise" in Article 13(3). Does it include isolated activities or is it to be limited to where some entity or undertaking carries on a business? Put another way, the essential question to be decided is really twofold and each relates to the meaning to be given to the word "enterprise". Does the true meaning require that the enterprise must be attached to an entity whether an undertaking or business, or does it apply to activities unattached to an entity? If, on its true meaning, the word "enterprise" applies to activities without those activities being attached to an entity, did the activities of the appellant in acquiring and selling the units and shares amount to an enterprise? In other words, did the activities have to constitute the carrying on (emphasis added) of a profit-making undertaking or scheme or is it sufficient that he carried out (emphasis added) a profit-making undertaking or scheme, namely an isolated transaction or series of transactions being the passive realisation of capital assets? These questions raise the spectrum of the differences between the two limbs of sub-section 25A(1) of the Assessment Act. Having regard to recent amendments to the Assessment Act, these questions may arise more often under the avoidance of double taxation agreements.

16. The Swiss Agreement is to be treated as part of the domestic law of Australia, see s.11E of the Agreements Act. Nevertheless it is an international agreement and may not have been drafted with the care given to the drafting of an Act of Parliament. English authorities suggest that international conventions, even though incorporated under the domestic law of England by reason of being a schedule to an Act of Parliament should be construed by English courts in a manner appropriate for the interpretation of an international convention; see for example Stag Line Ltd v Foscolo, Mango & Co Ltd. (1932) AC 328 per Lord Macmillan at p 350 and James Buchanan & Co Ltd. v Babco Forwarding and Shipping (U.K.) Ltd. (1978) AC 141 per Lord Wilberforce at p 152 and p 154 and per Viscount Dilhorne, who at p 157 said:

"In construing the terms of a convention it is

proper and indeed right, in my opinion, to have

regard to the fact that conventions are apt to be

more loosely worded than Acts of Parliament. To

construe a convention as strictly as an Act may

indeed lead to a wrong interpretation being given

to it. In construing a convention as in construing

an Act where the language used is capable of two

interpretations one must seek to give effect to the

intentions of those who made it (Coke 4 Inst 330)."

17. In Fothergill v Monarch Airlines Ltd [1980] UKHL 6; (1981) AC 251 the House of Lords applied the opinions expressed in the Stag Line Case and in the James Buchanan Case, see Lord Wilberforce commencing at p 272, Lord Diplock at pp281-2; Lord Fraser of Tullybelton at p 285 and Lord Scarman at p 291, and at p 293 and following. At p 294 Lord Scarman discussed the approach taken in the courts from which the appeal came and continued:

"They looked to the terms of the Convention as

enacted, and concluded that it was clear. I agree

with them in thinking that the court must first

look at the terms of the convention as enacted by

Parliament. But, if there be ambiguity or doubt,

or if a literal construction appears to conflict

with the purpose of the convention, the court must

then, in my judgment, have recourse to such aids as

are admissible and appear to it to be not only

relevant but helpful on the point (or points) under

consideration. Mere marginal relevance will not

suffice: the aid (or aids) must have weight as well."

His Lordship then considered the nature of the aids to be considered.

18. The method of construction adopted by the House of Lords should be adopted in the present case but at the same time the Court should keep in mind the provisions of Article 3(2) which are set out earlier in these reasons.

19. The word "enterprise" is not defined in the Agreement. In the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary it is given the following meanings "1. A design of which the execution is attempted; a piece of work taken in hand; now only, a bold, arduous, or dangerous undertaking. 2. Disposition to engage in undertakings of difficulty, risk, or danger; daring spirit 1475. 3. Management - 1803." In the first meaning given, the word "design" is to be understood as meaning a plan, scheme or purpose.

20. In the Agreement the word "enterprise" is used as part of composite terms "enterprise of one of the Contracting States" and "enterprise of the other Contracting State". Those terms have a meaning given to them by Article 3(1)(f), namely "an enterprise carried on by a resident". In this context, the word enterprise appears to include any activity as well as an activity associated with the carrying on of a business or an entity. Article 5 provides meanings to be given to the phrase "permanent establishment". This definition is based upon the concept that a fixed place of business is in existence through which "the business of the enterprise" is carried on. This seems to suggest that the word "enterprise" is used in the sense of activities which may be associated with some entity which requires a fixed place of business. The other provisions of Article 5 are consistent with that use. The need for these special provisions relating to a permanent establishment in the country of source of income is obvious. If there is a direct and obvious nexus between a country and the enterprise from which taxable income derives, that country is entitled to impose and receive tax on that income. Thus if the income derives from real estate or related interests in the country of source, that country is entitled to tax that income - Article 6 and Article 13(1) and (2). Likewise a permanent establishment in the country of source entitles that country to tax income derived through that permanent establishment. A consideration of the details of Article 5(2), (3), (4), (5) and (6) illustrates this rationale.

21. In Article 7(1) the use of the word "enterprise" appears in the terms defined in Article 3(1)(f) and by itself appears to include both isolated activities and activities connected with a business. The Article appears to be directed to businesses but there is no necessity that a business have an entity connected to it. The same observations can be made with respect to the word where it appears in Article 13.

22. The Agreement applies to individuals who are residents of one or both of Australia and Switzerland; Article 1 and the meaning given to the word "person" by Article 3(1)(d). If Article 7 and 13 were to be limited to businesses being carried on in one of the Contracting States, it would have been simple to use the word "business" instead of the word "enterprise". The word "enterprise" appears to have a meaning different to the word "business". In sub-section 3(2) of the Agreements Act the words "activity or business" are used in the context of profits and taxable income derived from that "activity or business". This terminology suggests that an activity may be different from a business being an activity having an entity or undertaking attached to it.

23. In the result, in my opinion, the correct construction to be given to the word "enterprise" in the Agreement cannot be ascertained from a reading of the Agreement itself. In these circumstances, it is necessary to look to any aids that can be used to assist to determine the true meaning of the word "enterprise" where it is used in Articles 7 and 13 of the Agreement.

24. The policy behind the Agreement is to avoid the imposition of double taxation with respect to taxes on income. That is a stated purpose of the Agreement. If possible, the proper construction of the Agreement should be consistent with that policy or purpose. The Agreement is based on the major premise that the country of residence of the taxpayer, whether an individual, a company or a group of persons, should have the right to tax the taxable income of that person, not the country of source of that income. There are a number of exceptions to that major premise. Some of these exceptions have been referred to already. They include the case where the enterprise carries on business through a permanent establishment in the country of source and where the income derives from real property or related interests in the country of source. Special provisions apply with respect to entertainers where the country of source has the power to tax. Special provisions apply with respect to dividends; Article 10. The general principle, as was said earlier in these reasons, appears to be that where there is a sufficient nexus between the income on the one hand and the country of source on the other, the country of source has the right to tax that income. The agreement specifies matters which determine sufficient nexus. In this context, it is interesting to compare the results flowing from the application of the Agreement depending upon the meaning to be given to the word enterprise. Thus, if an enterprise is construed to mean a structure or an entity attached to a business being carried on in say, Switzerland, and that enterprise receives income from activities within Australia but where it does not have a permanent establishment in Australia, it will be entitled to the benefit of the Agreement. Hence, if the appellant had established the facts to justify a finding that his activities in Australia had been connected with his business in Switzerland, he would not have been liable to Australian tax. On the other hand if a person, being an individual resident in Switzerland, purchased in Australia shares in an Australian company, dividends received would come within Article 10 and he would receive some benefit. If that person then sold the shares within 12 months of purchase and in so doing made a large profit, that person would be liable to Australian tax unless Article 13(3) applied. It is difficult to see any rationale why that person should not have the benefit of the avoidance of double taxation to the same extent as the business being an enterprise but not having a permanent establishment in Australia.

25. On 29 April 1977 the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) adopted a model convention with respect to the avoidance of double taxation. Australia and Switzerland are members of the OECD. The OECD has an official commentary on the convention to indicate the intended effect of the convention and as an aid to its interpretation. The use of the commentary for this purpose is recognised. Members of the OECD have recorded observations, derogations and reservations affecting any article in the model convention. The purpose of the model convention is referred to in CCH Australian International Tax Agreements (CCH) Vol. 1 para 1-010 as follows-

"The problems of double taxation mean that, if

revenue is to be raised equitably, it is desirable

that some agreement be made between different

taxing jurisdictions whereby the interest of the

taxpayer is served in seeing that income is taxed

only once and the interest of the nations concerned

is served by seeing that it is taxed at least once.

However, the avoidance of double taxation is

considered desirable not only from the standpoint

of fairness. Its importance has been recognized as

one feature in the promotion of international trade

and commerce and the free flow of investment

between nations. High tax rates do not permit the

same property to be taxed more than once without

virtual confiscation, so that double taxation

creates an effective barrier against foreign

investment and free trade. Accordingly, the

international tax agreements provide an important

base for the attempt by international negotiation

to bring about a freer exchange of goods and

capital between nations in an effort to contribute

towards more widespread industrial development and

a general rise in world living standards.

Serious attempts at formulating methods of

eliminating double taxation and fiscal evasion have

been carried on ever since the first world war,

particularly under the guidance of the League of

Nations and the International Chamber of Commerce.

Model conventions have been drafted to assist in

the formulation of separate treaties between

nations and to some extent the work of these

committees is reflected in the Agreements to which

Australia is a party. The latest Model is that

adopted by the Organisation for Economic

Co-operation and Development on 29 April 1977.

That followed an earlier draft adopted on 30 July

1963. Australia, as a member of the OECD, follows

the Model, subject to its expressed reservations, in

its Agreements with other members of the Organisation."

26. In their introduction to the model convention and commentary, the authors of C.C.H. say at Vol. 1, para 1-501:-

"Members of the OECD are expected, when

concluding new bilateral agreements or revising

existing bilateral agreements with other members,

to conform to the Model as interpreted by the

commentary and having regard to the reservations

and derogations to the Model.

27. The Swiss Agreement is a bilateral agreement entered into after the model convention had been adopted. Many of the provisions of the Swiss Agreement are the same as corresponding provisions in the model convention but in some cases, differences exist. Article 3(1)(f) of the Swiss Agreement (the meaning to be given to the terms "enterprise of one of the Contracting States") is in the same form as Article 3(1)(c) of the model convention. The OECD commentary on that Article, contained in CCH Vol 1, para 1-555 is:-

"The question whether an activity is performed

within the framework of an enterprise or is deemed

to constitute in itself an enterprise has always

been interpreted according to the provisions of the

domestic laws of the Contracting States. No

definition, properly speaking, of the term

"enterprise" has therefore been attempted in this

Article".

28. It is interesting to note that Article 7(1) of the model convention is in the same form as Article 7(1) of the Swiss Agreement but nothing in the OECD commentary assists in the problem presently before the Court. Article 6 and Article 13 of the model convention correspond generally with Article 6 and Article 13 of the Swiss Agreement. In the model convention, the term "immovable property" is used, not "real property". In the model convention, Article 22 relates to taxation of capital but there is no corresponding article in the Swiss Agreement. Article 13(4) of the model convention is headed 'Capital gains'. It corresponds broadly with Article 13(3) of the Swiss Agreement but applies to gains derived by a resident". The OECD commentary, C.C.H. Vol. 1, para 1-870 refers to the different methods of taxing capital gains between different countries. Australia reserved the right to propose changes to reflect the fact that it did not levy a capital gains tax and that the terms "movable property" and "immovable property" are terms not used in Australian law. Despite this reservation Australia entered into the Swiss Agreement which relates to tax under the Assessment Act. In these circumstances it is not necessary to consider whether the taxable income assessed to the appellant is a capital gain. It should be noted further that the model convention by Article 21 makes provision with respect to items of income not covered by any of the Articles in which case, subject to some exceptions, the income is to be taxed in the country of residence. There is no corresponding article in the Swiss Agreement. In any event, the existence of that Article does not relieve a Court from determining whether income comes within another Article or not.

29. In the Assessment Act the word "enterprise" is defined by s.128A(1) but only for the purposes of Division 11A of Part III of the Assessment Act. That Division is headed "Dividends and Interest paid to Non-residents and to certain other persons". In that Division, unless the contrary intention appears, the word "enterprise" is defined to mean "a business or other industrial or commercial undertaking".

30. That definition is not of direct help in construing the word "enterprise" in Articles 7(1) and 13(3) of the Swiss Agreement but it is interesting to note that in Lilydale Pastoral Co. Pty Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 87 ATC 4,235, Pincus J. gave a broad meaning to the word "enterprise", as defined, so as to include an activity where the taxpayer, for the purpose of its business, borrowed money which it on-lent to another entity to allow that other entity to acquire property.

31. In my opinion, the word "enterprise" as used in each of Article 7(1) and Article 13(3) of the Swiss Agreement, on its proper construction, is to be given a broad meaning. It does not connote the existence of an entity or structure attached to the activities entered into by a person in the execution of a plan or scheme. The word is to be understood to include a business but a person may engage in an enterprise without engaging in a business within the ordinary meaning of that word.

32. In the present appeal it is not necessary to determine whether the profits of the sale of the units and the shares become assessable income under either or both of the limbs of sub-section 25A(1) of the Assessment Act. This appeal was unusual since the taxpayer was supporting the view that the profits did come within that sub-section. The trial Judge held they did not come within either limb.

33. In all the circumstances of this appeal, in my opinion, the appellant, in entering into the isolated activities of acquiring the units and the shares in Australia for the purpose of selling them at a profit, was engaged in an enterprise within the proper meaning of the word appearing in Article 7(1) and Article 13(3) of the Swiss Agreement. He was a resident of Switzerland. He conceived the plan, scheme or purpose of making a quick profit in Australia. He engaged in activities within Australia to put that plan, scheme or purpose into effect. Those activities were not carried out through a permanent establishment in Australia. There was a risk to the appellant in entering into those activities. He borrowed money to enable him to enter into those activities. He might not have made a profit. He could have suffered a loss. Those activities, in themselves, constituted an enterprise within Article 7(1) and Article 13(3) of the Swiss Agreement. In my opinion the appellant, therefore, comes within the benefits conferred by those Articles.

34. Accordingly, I would allow the appeal with costs and make the orders sought by the appellant.

The central question in this appeal from a judgment of the Supreme Court of Western Australia (Franklyn J.) is whether the appellant's entry into a series of transactions in Western Australia in 1984 and 1985 constituted an enterprise carried on by him within the meaning of that expression in Article 3(1) of the agreement between Australia and Switzerland for the avoidance of double taxation ("the Swiss agreement"). The appellant is a resident of Switzerland. The Swiss agreement is in force by virtue of the operation of the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953; see s. 11E and also s. 4.

2. The agreement is modelled on an OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) Model Convention, but departs from the terms of that Model in certain respects. The relevant provisions of the agreement are to be found in Articles 7 and 13 and in Article 3 which defines certain expressions used therein. Articles 7 and 13 are as follows:-

"ARTICLE 7

Business Profits

(1) The profits of an enterprise of one

of the Contracting States shall be taxable

only in that State unless the enterprise

carries on business in the other Contracting

State through a permanent establishment

situated therein. If the enterprise carries

on business as aforesaid, the profits of the

enterprise may be taxed in the other State,

His relationship with Broyt was strained and he could see problems in maintaining his dealership of its products. 21. The essential submission made by counsel for the appellant concerning his Honour's findings was that, on the totality of the evidence, there was occurring, at or about the time of the acquisition of the units in the Trust, a diversification of the appellant's business activities. The acquisition of the units was but one aspect of this diversification. In support of this submission he relied upon four principal matters, namely:-

1. The state of the appellant's machinery business was the

apparently taken by his Honour that, because the profit was

thought to be available from the units, the appellant would

have invested in them regardless of the state of his

business was erroneous. That, so counsel submitted, ignored

the prospect that, if the appellant's machinery business had

remained buoyant, he would neither have wished to divert

capital from it nor take the risk that the investment might

fail.

2. The appellant's activities in relation to the ERG company

were to be likened to another proposal investigated by the

appellant which ultimately led to his importing salmon into

Switzerland from Norway. This was another instance of his

diversification of which his investment in the units was a

part. Counsel relied on detailed evidence given by the

appellant of the investigations he made into the feasibility

of this proposal in order to indicate a wide diversification

of the appellant's business activities of which, so it was

submitted, the investment in the units and shares was but one

other aspect.

3. His Honour's findings in relation to the appellant's

intentions and activities in relation to the proposed

face of the evidence and were against the probabilities. His

Honour took too much from the circumstance that the appellant

borrowed from his parents rather than through ordinary

business channels in order to finance the investment in the

units.

4. The appellant's assertion of his intention to "roll over" the

profits from the sale of the shares into similar types of

ventures was never challenged in cross-examination. Counsel

said that the appellant had never departed from his evidence

in this respect, but no challenge was made to it in the

course of his cross-examination.

22. I have considered these various matters and the evidence referred to in the course of the argument in relation to them. What the appellant needs to demonstrate is error in his Honour's ultimate findings. I think there may be some force in the contentions concerning his Honour's attaching over-much significance to the borrowing from the parents and I think that there is material from which it could be said that the appellant in a general way may have been interested in marketing, in some unspecified fashion, products which the ERG company or its subsidiaries might ultimately have available. I think all this means that if I had been the primary Judge I might have approached the facts in a slightly different way with a somewhat different emphasis. But the ultimate question is whether a combination of the matters upon which counsel for the appellant relies should lead one to the conclusion that his Honour fell into such error that his findings should be reversed and that it should be found positively by us that the investment in the units and shares was part of an over-all diversification of the appellant's business activity and thus but one component of a whole which involved the original earth moving business, the foray into the importation of salmon, the possible representation of the ERG company in relation to some of its products in Europe and other activities mentioned by the appellant in his evidence. In order to assess the strength of these various considerations, one must concentrate one's attention upon the nature of the investment which is in question here. The appellant used the colourful expression, namely, that he "jumped in" when he heard of the opportunity to acquire the units from Mr. Kristensen. The sale of the units, once the company was floated, gives to the series of transactions the hallmarks of an investment made for the purpose of taking advantage of the substantial increase in value of the shares which it was anticipated would come about once the public float took place. It may have been one thing if there were signs in the evidence that the appellant had intended to sell part of his holding and retain the balance as an investment. But his Honour found, and the evidence supports him, that the appellant had no such intention. He endeavoured to make a case to that effect, but, in my opinion, that case was not made out because the only thing which stopped him selling was the intervention of the Commissioner when he froze the amount standing to the credit of the appellant in the stockbroker's books.

23. I have considered the absence of cross-examination in relation to the appellant's evidence about investing profits made from the sale of the shares in other ventures. I do not take anything from this. It was for the appellant to establish his case. Counsel for the Commissioner made it clear, by his conduct of the case and his cross-examination generally, that that case was called into question. It was for his Honour to decide whether that case should be accepted or rejected. The appellant's state of mind and his intentions and purposes were critical matters for assessment. They were peculiarly within the knowledge of the appellant. The Commissioner could make no positive case in opposition to that made by the appellant. It was a matter of testing his evidence. His Honour did not accept the appellant's evidence about these matters and the only question is whether we find error in his Honour's conclusions in this regard.

24. Counsel for the appellant took his Honour to task for his reliance on a distinction between a positive assertion that the appellant would re-invest and what his Honour described as a tentative assertion that he would have looked closely at re-investing. I have earlier set out the passage in which his Honour discusses this matter. In some cases too much may be taken from the way in which evidence of a matter such as this is expressed. But it would be dangerous for an appellate court to take a different view of it in the absence of some clear indication that his Honour had fallen into error. It is a conclusion which must depend, at least to a degree, on the primary judge having heard the evidence as it was given and thus being in a position the better to assess it in the light, not only of the words which were used, but also of the way the words were said. I know that many witnesses use the expression "would have", when they really mean that they did something or intended to do something. Not uncommonly a witness has the difference between the two forms of expression pointed out to him so that he is given the opportunity of making his evidence clear. The task his Honour had was to assess the credibility over-all of the evidence which the appellant gave. I do not feel able to draw the conclusion that his Honour, in expressing himself in the way that is complained of, fell into error.

25. Whatever criticism one may have of the detail of the approach taken by his Honour in relation to the various matters which he had to consider (and I have very little) the fact remains that this was at bottom an isolated series of transactions entered into for the purpose of gaining a quick profit. The whole matter went almost exactly as Mr. Kristensen had forecast it would. In my opinion the only way of approaching the case is to regard the series of transactions as isolated from any other activities in which the appellant was then engaged or was contemplating being engaged in. It was not part of an over-all business activity in which he was engaged. I would therefore reject the challenges which have been made to his Honour's findings of fact. The qualification I make to that conclusion is that I remain a little doubtful whether his Honour excluded from his findings the notion that the relevant transactions, when viewed together, had the character of a business deal. At one point in his judgment his Honour said that he was not satisfied that the investment in the units and the shares was an isolated business transaction. I think there is a question whether or not the transactions viewed, though they must be in isolation, when taken together did amount to a business deal. I think that they did. But, as will be seen, that of itself does not take the appellant's case far enough.

26. The question then is whether the acquisition of the units, the substitution of the shares for them and the sale of the shares constitutes the carrying on of an enterprise for the purposes of the Swiss agreement.

force when the Convention was adopted ..."

32. In the Koowarta case Brennan J. was the only judge to refer to the Vienna Convention. In The Tasmanian Dam case it was referred to by Gibbs C.J., Mason J., Wilson J and Dawson J. Gibbs C.J. was prepared to refer to the Vienna Convention because, in his view, it did no more than confirm existing practice. He referred with approval to Monarch Airlines; see p 472. I refer also to Mason J. at p 490, Wilson J. at p 515 and Dawson J. at p 566.

33. Whether or not the Vienna Convention does directly apply to the Swiss agreement, the Koowarta and Tasmanian Dam cases establish that its provisions bear on the construction of the agreement. The relevant provisions of the Convention are Articles 31 and 32 which appear in section 3 which is entitled, "Interpretation of Treaties". Article 31, so far as it is relevant, is as follows:-

"1. A treaty shall be interpreted in good

faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning

to be given to the terms of the treaty in

their context and in the light of its object

and purpose.

2. The context for the purpose of the

interpretation of a treaty shall comprise, in

addition to the text, including its preamble

and annexes:

(a) any agreement relating to the treaty

which was made between all the parties

in connexion with the conclusion of the

treaty;

(b) any instrument which was made by one or

more parties in connexion with the

conclusion of the treaty and accepted

by the other parties as an instrument

related to the treaty.

.............................................

4. A special meaning shall be given to a

term if it is established that the parties so

intended."

Article 32 provides:-

"Recourse may be had to supplementary means

of interpretation, including the preparatory

work of the treaty and the circumstances of

its conclusion, in order to confirm the

meaning resulting from the application of

article 31, or to determine the meaning when

the interpretation according to article 31:

(a) leaves the meaning ambiguous or

obscure; or

(b) leads to a result which is

manifestly absurd or unreasonable."

34. If there be the need to resolve an ambiguity, the Court is entitled to look at the OECD Model Convention, the OECD Commentary thereon and any reservations noted when Australia acceded to the Convention. These are not travaux preparatoires because they are not working papers leading up to the adoption of the Convention. Rather they are documents which have been agreed to, subject to the observations and reservations that have been noted by countries when acceding to the Convention. Thus they ought to be accorded as much if not more weight than travaux preparatoires. That the Model Convention and the Commentary thereon are documents which the Court is entitled to consider is established by Sun Life Assurance Co. of Canada v. Pearson (1986) STC 335 at pp 345-7.

35. In general terms the purpose of the Swiss agreement is to avoid double taxation between the two countries, but only to the extent for which the agreement provides. In giving effect to the purpose, one must be careful to see that the operation of the agreement is kept within its intended bounds. This is but to give effect to the provisions of paragraph 1 of Article 31 of the Vienna Convention.

36. That one is not entitled to ignore the language which has been used even if that leads to a situation where, in a given case, double taxation is not avoided was emphasized by Windeyer J. in Emmanuel v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1968) 118 CLR 298 where he referred to the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act and continued (p 296):-

"In cases to which the latter Act applies its

provisions are dominant. Section 4 expressly

makes them so. The words of the Canadian and

New Zealand agreements and of the United

States Convention are not in all respects the

same as those of the United Kingdom

agreement. All are designed, as they

expressly state, for 'the avoidance of double

taxation and the prevention of fiscal evasion

with respect to taxes on income'. The

provisions of each are by s. 5 of the

last-mentioned Australian Act to have 'the

force of law so far as they affect Australian

tax'. In the construction of each, its

declared purpose must no doubt be borne in

mind. But each must be read as a separate

instrument."

To similar effect is the dictum of Kitto J. in English, Scottish and Australian Bank Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 24; (1969) 118 CLR 651 at p 656.

37. Of central importance to the question of construction is Article 3(2) of the Swiss agreement. It provides that, in the application of the agreement by one of the Contracting States, any term not otherwise defined shall, unless the context otherwise requires, have the meaning which it has under the laws of that Contracting State relating to the taxes to which the agreement applies. The Commentary in respect of that article, where it appears in the Model Convention, states that it provides a general rule of interpretation in respect of terms used in the Convention but not defined therein. The provisions of Article 3(2) of the agreement are significant because one will not reach a correct conclusion about the meaning of the provisions here in question unless one gives proper effect to it. The Article provides the agreement with its own internal rule of construction intended to aid in the resolution of questions concerning the meaning of words and expressions which are not otherwise defined therein.

38. There are some general matters which one must bear in mind, namely:-

1. The omission from the Swiss agreement of a provision to the

effect of Article 21 of the OECD Model Convention. Article

21(1) of the Model provides that items of income of a

resident of a contracting state, wherever arising, not dealt

with in the foregoing articles of the Convention, shall be

taxable only in that state. If that Article were included in

the agreement, no Australian tax would be payable on the

profits or gains made by the appellant as a result of the

transactions in question. The absence of the Article from

the agreement is significant because it tends to establish

that the parties did intend there to be areas of income

earning to which the agreement would not apply with the

consequence of double taxation in some circumstances.

2. That significance is heightened by a reservation made by

Australia when entering into the agreement that it reserved

its position on Article 21 and would wish to maintain the

right to tax income arising from sources in Australia; see

OECD Model Convention and Commentary in Australian

International Tax Agreements (CCH Australia Limited), Vol. 1

2-000, p 690.

3. The definitions of "Enterprise of one of the Contracting

States" and "Enterprise of the Other Contracting State" in

Article 3(1)(f) of the Swiss agreement come from Article

3(1)(c) of the OECD Model Convention - see the earlier

referred to OECD Commentary at p 540. The Commentary on the

paragraph is as follows:-

"The question whether an activity is

performed within the framework of an

enterprise or is deemed to constitute in

itself an enterprise has always been

interpreted according to the provisions of

the domestic laws of the Contracting States.

No definition, properly speaking, of the term

'enterprise' has therefore been attempted in

this Article."

39. In the Macquarie Dictionary "enterprise" is defined as a project undertaken or to be undertaken, especially one that is of some importance or that requires boldness or energy. It is also defined to mean the engagement in such projects and boldness or readiness in undertaking, adventurous spirit; or energy. It may also mean a company organized for commercial purposes. It is interesting to compare the meaning of the word "undertaking" as defined in the Macquarie Dictionary. It is defined to mean the act of one who undertakes any task or responsibility. It may also mean a task, enterprise, etc. undertaken. There are other meanings to which it is unnecessary to refer.

40. The Oxford English Dictionary defines "enterprise" to mean a design of which the execution is attempted; a piece of work taken in hand; an undertaking; "chiefly, and now exclusively, a bold, arduous or momentous undertaking." "Enterprise" also means the engagement in any such undertaking and the disposition or readiness to engage in undertakings of risk or danger. Finally it may mean the action of taking in hand; management; superintendence. This latter meaning is said to be obsolete. "Undertaking" is defined as an action, work, etc., undertaken or attempted; an enterprise.

41. References to these dictionary meanings of the word "enterprise" suggest, subject to some qualifications, that the word "enterprise" is a synonym for the word "undertaking". That view is confirmed by reference to thesauruses which give "undertaking" as one of the synonyms for "enterprise" and vice versa. Nothing particular turns on this except that it emphasizes that "enterprise", like "undertaking", is a rather broad ambiguous word of uncertain meaning. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Whitford's Beach Pty. Limited [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355 Mason J. said of different expressions (pp 378-9):-

"Unfortunately there is an element of

ambiguity in the expression 'business deal'

and 'operation of business' as there is in

the adjectives 'business', 'commercial' and

'trading' which have about them a

chameleon-like hue, readily adapting

themselves to their surroundings, different

though they may be. In some contexts

'business deal' and 'operation of business'

may signify a transaction entered into by a

person in the course of carrying on a

business; in other contexts they denote a

transaction which is business or commercial

in character."

Much the same could be said of the word "enterprise".

42. In Ostime v. Australian Mutual Provident Society (1960) AC 459, the House of Lords was concerned with the construction of Article III of the Double Taxation Relief (Taxes On Income) Australia Order 1947. Article III (2) referred to the industrial or commercial profits of an Australian enterprise. Article II defined the expressions "United Kingdom enterprise" and "Australian enterprise" to mean respectively an industrial or commercial enterprise or undertaking carried on by a United Kingdom resident and an industrial or commercial enterprise or undertaking carried on by an Australian resident. The term was not more precisely defined. Lord Radcliffe delivered the principal speech. He said (p 480):-

"Bilaterial agreements for regulating some of

the problems of double taxation began, at any

rate so far as the United Kingdom was

concerned, in 1946. The form employed,

which, for obvious reasons, employs similar

forms and similar language in all agreements,

is derived, I believe, from a set of model

clauses proposed by the financial commission

of the League of Nations. The aim is to

provide by treaty for the tax claims of two

governments both legitimately interested in

taxing a particular source of income either

by resigning to one of the two the whole

claim or else by prescribing the basis on

which the tax claim is to be shared between

them. For our purpose it is convenient to

note that the language employed in this

agreement is what may be called international

tax language and that such categories as

'enterprise,' 'commercial or industrial

profits' and 'permanent establishment' have

no exact counterpart in the taxing code of

the United Kingdom."

43. The word "enterprise" is not used in the Income Tax Assessment Act except in Division 11A of Part III which makes provision in relation to dividends and interest paid to non-residents and certain other persons. In s. 128A the word is defined, but only for the purposes of Division 11A, to mean "a business or other industrial or commercial undertaking". The definition has been the subject of consideration by this Court in Lilydale Pastoral Co. Pty. Limited v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 15 FCR 19. Although Pincus J. gave the meaning of "enterprise", in the context in which he had to construe it, a wide meaning, the case is not of assistance here because of the limited purpose of the definition of the word.

44. I have reached the conclusion that the word "enterprise" should be given a wide meaning. Its meaning is not restricted to that of a business entity or identifiable business undertaking, although both would be enterprises within the meaning of the agreement. The enterprise may consist of an activity or activities and be comprised of one or more transactions provided they were entered into for business or commercial purposes; "business deal" taken from some of the cases decided in relation to para. 26(a) of the Assesssment Act (now s. 25A) seems to me to apply equally to it.

45. A superficial reading of the provisions of Article 7(1) and 13(3) may suggest a more restricted meaning. But one needs, firstly, to read the two provisions in the light of the definitions in Article 3(1)(f) and, secondly, with a proper appreciation of the exceptions made to the two Articles. Article 7(1) does not apply where the enterprise carries on business in the other Contracting State (Australia in this case) through a permanent establishment situated therein. There is a similar exception to the operation of Article 13(3). At first sight the words of these exceptions may be thought to narrow the meaning of "enterprise" in the earlier words of each article to one that is an entity in itself. But I think that the correct view is that the earlier words were intended to have a wide ambit and the words of exception or exclusion were designed to remove from the operation of the agreement only those enterprises which are separate or identifiable entities with permanent establishments in the country where the transactions in question have occurred.

46. The fact that "enterprise" is to be given a wide meaning is not determinative of the outcome of the appeal. The difficulty, is that "enterprise" is only one of the words in the phrase, "an enterprise carried on". One cannot reach a conclusion on the meaning of "enterprise" in the context in which it appears here without taking into account the use of the words "carried on" in conjunction with it. The phrase in question is a composite one and has to be construed and understood as a whole.

47. The expressions "to carry on" and "carrying on" have been considered in a number of judgments, often as part of the expression, "carrying on a business". In s. 25A of the Assessment Act the words appear along with the expression "carrying out" in relation to a profit-making undertaking or scheme. But there are a number of authorities which look at the words "carrying on" and similar expressions in the absence of the contrast which is provided by the expression "carrying out". In Kirkwood v. Gadd (1910) AC 422 the expression in question was, "carry on the money-lending business ..." Lord Atkinson said (p 431):-

"In one sense every step, every item, in a

long or complicated financial or commercial

transaction may be said to be 'business,' but

I think it is obvious that in the phrase

'carries on his business' employed in this

section, as in the phrase 'carrying on

business' employed in the 4th section of the

Companies Act of 1862, the words 'carries on'

must be held to imply a repetition of acts,

the sum of which constitutes the 'business':

see Brett L.J., Smith v. Anderson. (1880) 15

ChD 247, at pp 277, 278."

A number of the authorities were summarized by Gibbs J. (as he then was) in Smith v. Capewell [1979] HCA 48; (1979) 142 CLR 509 where his Honour said (pp 517-18):-

"The expression 'carry on business', in its

ordinary meaning, signifies a course of

conduct involving the performance of a

succession of acts, and not simply the

effecting of one solitary transaction. In

Smith v. Anderson, where the Court of Appeal

considered the effect of s. 4 of the

Companies Act, 1862 (U.K.) which spoke of an

'association ... formed ... for the purpose

of carrying on any ... business', Brett L.J.

said: 'The expression 'carrying on' implies

a repetition of acts, and excludes the case

of an association formed for doing one

particular act which is never to be

repeated.' In Kirkwood v. Gadd, Lord

Loreburn L.C. said: 'What is carrying on

business? It imports a series or repetition

of acts.' In the same case Lord Atkinson,

referred with apparent approval to the

statement of Brett L.J. in Smith v. Anderson."

48. Gibbs J. went on to refer to Premier Automatic Ticket Issuers Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1933] HCA 51; (1933) 50 CLR 268. There, in a well known passage, Dixon J. (as he then was) said (p 298) that the alternative "carrying on" or "carrying out" appears to cover, on the one hand, the habitual pursuit of a course of conduct and, on the other, the carrying into execution of a plan or venture which does not involve repetition or system. This passage has been adopted in a number of subsequent cases; see, for example, White v. Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 41; (1968) 120 CLR 191 per Windeyer J. at p 209 and XCO Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 37; (1971) 124 CLR 343 per Gibbs J. at p 349.

49. These judicial meanings given to the expression "carry on" and also to the expression "carry out" are in accordance with ordinary dictionary meanings. For instance, the Oxford English Dictionary defines "carry on" to mean to continue or advance a proceeding from the stage already attained or to maintain, keep up, prevent from stopping. It also means to practice continuously or habitually; to conduct, manage, work at or prosecute. "Carry out", on the other hand, means to conduct duly to completion or conclusion; to carry into practice or to logical consequences or inferences.

50. One now has to bring into play the terms of Article 3(2) of the Swiss agreement, the importance of which has been earlier emphasized. As mentioned, it states that any term not otherwise defined shall, unless the context otherwise requires, have the meaning which it has under the laws of that contracting state relating to the taxes to which the agreement applies. This provision was taken from Article 3(2) of the OECD Model Convention. Reference needs also to be made to the OECD Commentary. It will be recalled that, when commenting on the definitions of the terms, "enterprise of a Contracting State" and "enterprise of the other Contracting State", the Commentary said that the question whether an activity is performed within the framework of an enterprise or is deemed to constitute in itself an enterprise has always been interpreted according to the provisions of the domestic laws of the Contracting States. That approach is in accordance with the express terms of Article 3(2).

51. In my opinion the provisions of Article 3(2) lead to the conclusion that the proper construction of the agreement requires one to determine the question whether the entry by the appellant into the various transactions in question constituted the carrying on of an enterprise within the meaning of the agreement according to conventional rules of construction applicable to Australian statutes and other instruments. Notwithstanding the considerations which guide a court in the construction of treaties which form part of the domestic law of Australia, the provisions of Article 3(2) and the statements made in the OECD Commentary to which I have referred indicate that terms falling within its purview are to be construed in accordance with the domestic law of Australia. Of course, the agreement should be accorded a purposive construction. Its purpose is to avoid double taxation of affairs which have a connection with both Switzerland and Australia. But the words used in the agreement disclose that not every area of double taxation is intended to be avoided. That conclusion is reinforced by the reservation made by Australia concerning Article 21 of the Model Convention and the absence of a provision to the effect of Article 21 of the Model in the agreement.

52. The difficulty which confronts the appellant does not stem from the use of the word "enterprise"; it arises because of the use of the expression "carried on" in conjunction with it. The question is whether the transactions entered into by the appellant, viewed in the light of the findings made by his Honour about them which I have accepted, amount to the carrying on of an enterprise within the meaning of Article 7(1) or should be viewed as leading to the alienation of the capital assets of an enterprise carried on by the appellant within the meaning of Article 13(3).

53. I have substantial difficulty in concluding that the various transactions in question here can be said to amount to the carrying on of an enterprise. I would have had little difficulty in regarding them as the carrying out of an enterprise, but the expression "carrying out" is not used. Both the dictionary and judicial meanings accorded to the expression "carrying on" compel the conclusion, in my opinion, that it refers to an activity which is in truth a continuing one - one of indefinite or indeterminate duration. I appreciate that the expression is most commonly used in connection with the word "business" which itself implies continuity and system, and that, in consequence, the cases concerned with the meaning of that expression may not be of direct assistance in determining the meaning of expressions such as "carrying on an enterprise" or "an enterprise carried on". I appreciate also that cases concerned with the application of provisions such as s. 25A of the Assessment Act may not be thought to be as helpful as at first sight they appear to be because of the use in the section of the contrasting expressions "carrying on" and "carrying out". But, notwithstanding these important distinctions, one cannot say that the cases dealing with these provisions are without relevance and one is, in any event, left to grapple with the task of giving to the expression "carried on" its proper meaning and effect. When that is done, one is driven, in my opinion, to the conclusion that the transactions in question here did not involve the appellant in the carrying on of an enterprise.

54. The original four units were acquired in February 1984. The remaining two were acquired in June 1984. In November the six remaining units were transferred to the company in return for the allotment of 600,000 shares in its capital. In January 1985 the appellant was entered in the share register of the company as the holder of 600,000 shares. Soon afterwards the company was floated and between February 1985 and March 1985 252,000 of the 600,000 shares were sold. All these transactions occurred within the space of a little over 12 months. On the findings which I have accepted all were related to a plan or scheme evolved by the appellant to take advantage of the public float of a company he knew would be formed to take over the units in the trust in exchange for the issue of shares in it. In my opinion, the enterprise in which he engaged had not that element of continuity which is required to constitute an enterprise carried on by the appellant within the meaning of the relevant articles of the agreement.

55. Counsel for the appellant asked us to take account of the fact that, apart from the effect of the Swiss agreement, the profits made by the appellant as the result of the transactions into which he entered would have been assessable pursuant to provisions of the Assessment Act other than s. 26AAA relied upon by the Commissioner in his notice of assessment. Unquestionably the profits would also have been assessable under s. 25A. On the facts as found the profits arising from the sale by the appellant of the shares arose from the sale of property, i.e. the shares, acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale and also from the carrying out (not the carrying on) of a profit-making undertaking or scheme. Counsel also submitted that the profits, apart from the operation of the Swiss agreement, were income according to ordinary concepts and usages pursuant to the provisions of s. 25 of the Assessment Act. In support of this submission he relied upon the decision of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199. It was his submission that the fact that the profits were assessable pursuant to the second limb of s. 25A and as income according to ordinary concepts pursuant to s. 25 that led to the conclusion that the transactions, viewed as a whole, constituted an enterprise. I have already said that they did. The difficulty I have with the appellant's case is not related to that matter; it is related to the requirement that the enterprise must be one carried on by the taxpayer before the relevant provisions of the agreement will apply to it.

56. I have reservations whether the profits were income according to ordinary concepts. The relevant passages from the judgment of the High Court in the Myer case are to be found at pp 209-213. I do not repeat what is there said. My reservation arises because the facts of this case differ from those of the Myer case in that the transactions here were not part of any overall business activity carried on by the appellant. They constituted a separate and isolated series of transactions not, as in the Myer case, a separate and isolated transaction different from the ordinary business activities of the Myer company but nevertheless part of its business. Whether my view in that regard be correct or not, what has to be emphasized is that one needs to construe the relevant words of the agreement which here applies. One must not be led away from the critical question whether the appellant's entry into the various transactions constituted an enterprise carried on by him within the meaning of the agreement. I have little doubt that he was carrying out a profit-making undertaking or scheme. But that does not lead to the conclusion that, within the meaning of the agreement, he was carrying on an enterprise. For these reasons I do not think that the appellant can obtain assistance from the provisions of s. 25 and s. 25A of the Assessment Act.

57. In the result I have reached the conclusion that the appeal should be dismissed with costs.

15. The respondent treated the sale of that shareholding as another transaction providing a profit to be included in the assessable income of the appellant pursuant to s.26AAA of the Assessment Act. Pursuant to para.26AAA(1)(e) the respondent regarded the shares sold in February/March 1985 as property purchased in November 1984. Under that paragraph the issue by a company to a person of shares in the company as consideration for the sale of property by that person to the company is deemed to constitute the purchase by that person of the shares so issued.

16. The appellant does not contest the respondent's calculation of a profit nor the assumptions upon which the respondent relied to calculate that profit. The appellant does contend, however, that having regard to all relevant facts and the application of the Agreements Act no liability to taxation accrued.

17. The appellant attacks the decision of the learned judge on two fronts.

18. Firstly, the appellant contends that the judge erred in his construction of the terms of the Swiss Agreement.

19. Secondly, the appellant argues that the learned judge failed to draw the only inferences of fact available on the evidence and erred in several material conclusions of fact. The appellant argues that if the appropriate findings of fact had been made the appellant would have received the benefit of the Swiss Agreement even as construed by the learned judge.

20. It was the appellant's submission that the issues raised by the appeal depended on inferences from uncontested facts and it was not an appeal from a decision which depended upon the learned trial judge's view of conflicting testimony or findings of credibility. (See Warren v. Coombes [1979] HCA 9; (1979) 142 CLR 531 and Brunskill v. Sovereign Marine & General Insurance Co. Ltd. [1985] HCA 61; (1985) 62 ALR 53.)

21. It may be accepted that in this case the appellate court is in as good a position as the trial judge to decide on the proper inferences to be drawn from undisputed facts or from other facts established by the findings of the trial judge.

22. It is appropriate to deal firstly with the appellant's arguments as to the proper inferences to be drawn from the facts established at trial.

23. The appellant argues that the trial judge erred in "disbelieving" the appellant's statement that he intended to reinvest the proceeds of sale of his shareholding in ERG when no contrary assertion was put to the appellant in cross-examination and his assertions were uncontradicted by any other evidence.

24. A close examination of his Honour's reasons shows that he considered the whole of the appellant's testimony and the relevant conduct of the appellant and concluded that the appellant's intention to reinvest the proceeds of sale was a qualified intention amounting to an intention to give due consideration to reinvestment if an opportunity to reinvest presented his entire shareholding for the purpose of taking his profit from his investment and not for the purpose of converting the shareholding to funds for reinvestment in another prospect. The intervention of the respondent prevented the appellant dealing with the proceeds of sale. The appellant was residing in Switzerland at the time the shares were sold in early 1985. At that time the appellant had a present intention to remit the proceeds of sale to Switzerland and that intention had not been fulfilled. The appellant had made no decision to apply the funds to a particular investment and the respondent's intervention, therefore, did not have the effect of stultifying such a decision.

25. In assessing the appellant's evidence the trial judge correctly identified inconsistencies and qualifications contained therein. The fact that the appellant was not cross-examined upon such inconsistencies or qualifications did not serve to increase the weight or make more certain one part of the appellant's evidence against another. It was for his Honour to determine whether he had been satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the stated facts contended for by the appellant had been established.

26. On the material before him it was clearly open for his Honour to conclude, as he did, that he was not satisfied that the appellant had invested in the Energy Research Group Unit Trust with the firm intention of applying the profits from the sale of that investment to another venture or other ventures of the same or similar type. He did not disbelieve the appellant but in material respects was not satisfied that the appellant's evidence discharged the onus of proof resting on the appellant.

The relevant evidence was as follows:

27. In May 1983 the appellant's longstanding secretary, Mrs. Kristensen and her husband, also well-known to the appellant, migrated to Australia. Mr. and Mrs. Kristensen kept in touch with the appellant. Mr. Kristensen was an engineer by profession and he obtained a senior position with the Energy Research Group Unit Trust after his arrival in Perth.

28. Kristensen, in his correspondence with the appellant, had indicated that the Energy Research Group Unit Trust was a good investment. The appellant spoke of it as an opportunity to diversify his activities at a time when he thought it would be good to get out of his "classic business" to get into something else. However, he also saw that such an investment had high profit possibilities and his later evidence showed that when the appellant came to Perth in January 1984 and spent about four or five days as a house guest of the Kristensens and received further details of the nature of the prospects for the Energy Research Group Unit Trust, he primarily saw such an investment as an opportunity for a quick profit. The appellant said it was something he had dreamt of, namely to "get involved in a company at the ground floor before it was listed publicly". As he stated it that was exactly what he decided to do. In fact he "jumped in" with an intention to sell the shares in the company as soon as the shares could be sold to the public in the market place.

29. Obviously it was open for the trial judge to conclude that first and foremost it was the appellant's intention to obtain a profit and that the manner in which that profit was to be employed was to be a matter for further decision after the profit had been secured.

30. The appellant also challenged his Honour's conclusion that the appellant had borrowed from his family for a high risk venture rather than from the usual sources of finance available to his business in order that it would leave his business lines of credit untouched. The appellant argued that these were erroneous findings of fact and as such prevented the trial judge from drawing from correct findings the proper inference as to the nature of the appellant's activity in Australia.

31. The appellant in his evidence made it clear that he did regard the investment as entailing very high risk but at the same time it represented "absolutely explosive profit possibilities". The appellant said that he was so enthusiastic about the prospects he approached his parents for additional monies. In fact approximately three quarters of the cost of the investment (approximately CHF240,000) was provided by way of interest free loan from his parents and the remainder was provided by use of the banking facilities of the appellant's business.

32. His Honour noted that the appellant had not stated that the investment could not be financed by borrowings entirely from business sources and had given no evidence of any attempt to arrange finance in that way. His Honour reached the conclusion that the purpose and source of the borrowings were more consistent with a speculative activity unrelated to current or proposed business activities or an isolated business deal or the commencement of a new business.

33. His Honour quite correctly stated that the appellant had only partially explained why family and not business sources were used for the borrowings which made up the entire amount of the investment. However, the question of the nature and purpose of such an investment is not necessarily answered by looking at the source of the borrowing. A commercial or business venture may exist notwithstanding that the venture has been funded by borrowings from private or unusual sources. Conversely, and particularly in the case of a sole trader, a private, speculative, high profit venture not bearing the stamp of a commercial business activity may be undertaken by a businessman using funds obtained from the financial channels that support his business particularly where he may activate lines of credit without providing further security or obtaining approval for the investment from the financier.

34. The trial judge did not misunderstand the relevant evidence. He concluded that it had been of no assistance to the appellant's attempt to show that the appellant had conducted a commercial or business activity or an activity related to his current business or that he had commenced a new business.

35. The next segment of his Honour's judgment challenged by the appellant deals with evidence adduced by the appellant to the effect that he was interested in several of the business activities being developed by subsidiaries of ERG and that he had sought to be appointed the European representative of ERG in respect of these developments. The appellant argues that the trial judge failed to draw available inferences from this evidence as to the nature and purpose of the appellant's activity in Australia.

The challenge cannot be sustained.

36. In a very careful analysis of the relevant evidence the trial judge correctly stated that any interest the appellant might have had in obtaining a right to represent ERG in respect of an interlocking brick product it had developed at the time of the appellant's investment in ERG was entirely equivocal when standing alone and when added to all other evidence it did not lead to a conclusion that the investment in units or shares of ERG bore the characteristics of a commercial or business venture or the conduct of a business. The appellant gave no evidence at all that his decision to invest in ERG was in any way dependent upon the prospect of a representative appointment that it presented. To the contrary the appellant was at pains to underline that he saw the opportunity to acquire units in Energy Research Group Unit Trust simply as an opportunity to make a very substantial quick profit.

37. The appellant may have intended and endeavoured to progress his business interests by seeking appointment as a representative of ERG and to gain earnings for his business by rendering such services and such endeavours and intentions may have been relevant in assessing the nature and purpose of any collateral investment in ERG but such activities may be concurrent with the unrelated separate private activity of buying and selling an investment in ERG for a quick substantial profit and would not in themselves necessarily stamp that investment with the hallmark of a business or commercial activity.

38. Finally, in the appellant's challenge to the trial judge's treatment of the evidence the appellant took issue with the trial judge's conclusion that the evidence relating to the diversification of the appellant's business into importation of salmon into Switzerland from Norway in commercial quantities was of no assistance in determining whether the investment in ERG could be categorised as a further diversification of that business.

39. His Honour accepted that in 1986 the appellant commenced importation of salmon in commercial quantities as part of his overall business activities. However the trial judge drew a distinction between the appellant's actions in extending his business to include the importation of salmon and the decision to invest in ERG. Between 1984 and 1986 the appellant made careful enquiry and assessment of the possible market for the imported product before obtaining authorization to import salmon.

40. The appellant contends that his Honour failed to reach the appropriate conclusion from the evidence relating to the appellant's investment in ERG that the appellant had made due investigation and assessed the investment in the manner to be expected for a business or commercial activity.

41. His Honour concluded that the evidence referred to did no more than show that in investing in ERG the appellant had entered into a isolated venture for the acquisition of that property for the purpose of resale at a profit, a step unrelated to the business or enterprise he carried on in Switzerland or elsewhere and not forming the first step in any business of investing or any other new business or diversification of his existing business.

42. It is difficult to see how the trial judge has erred in his conclusion. He duly considered the appellant's conduct relating to the development of a Norwegian connection for the importation of salmon into Switzerland and concluded that its careful preparation and assessment were circumstances to be contrasted with those surrounding the decision to invest in ERG. That conclusion was available. The evidence was not such that it permitted only one reasonable inference to be drawn which inference the trial judge had failed to draw.

43. Having assessed the items of relevant evidence set out above, the trial judge found that the appellant had failed to satisfy him that the decision to purchase units in the Energy Research Group Unit Trust upon the understanding that such units were to be converted and made negotiable on a public market at which time the investment was to be sold and a profit obtained was part of the diversification of the appellant's existing business or the commencement of a new business operation. His Honour found that the transaction did not carry any of the hallmarks of carrying on a business or a business activity other than that of an intention to make a profit.

44. On the material placed before him the trial judge was not bound to be satisfied that the appellant's activity bore the characteristics contended by the appellant and in the absence of demonstrated error by his Honour in the treatment of that evidence or misapprehension of its significance there is no room for this Court to interfere with his conclusions.

45. I now turn to the arguments related to the application of the Agreements Act and construction of the Swiss Agreement.

46. Pursuant to s.11E of the Agreements Act, the provisions of the Swiss Agreement so far as they affected Autralian tax were deemed to have the force of law in relation to tax in respect of income of any year of income commencing after 1 July 1979. The long title of the Agreements Act is in the following terms:

"An Act to give the force of Law to certain

Conventions and Agreements with respect to Taxes

on Income and for purposes incidental thereto."

47. The Swiss Agreement is defined in sub-s.3(1) of that Act as "the Agreement between the Government of Australia and the Swiss Federal Council for the avoidance of double taxation with respect to taxes on income and the protocol to that agreement, being the agreement and protocol a copy of each of which in the English language is set out in Schedule 15". The agreement and protocol were both executed on 28 February 1980.

48. Pursuant to s.4 of the Agreements Act, the Assessment Act is incorporated and read as one with the Agreements Act but the provisions of the Agreements Act have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent with the provisions of the Assessment Act (other than s.160AO or Pt.IVA) or an Act imposing Australian tax.

49. The Swiss Agreement applies to persons who are residents of Australia and/or Switzerland:

"This Agreement shall apply to persons who are

residents of one or both of the Contracting

States." (Art.1)

and the tax to which the Agreement applies in Australia is the Australian income tax:

"The existing taxes to which this Agreement shall

apply are -

(a) in Australia:

The Australian income tax, including the

additional tax upon the undistributed amount

of the distributable income of a private

company and also income tax upon the reduced

taxable income of a non-resident company;

(b) in Switzerland:

The Federal, cantonal and communal taxes on

income (total income, earned income, income

from capital, industrial and commerical

profits and other items of income)."

(Art.2(1))

or any identical or substantially similar taxes imposed after the execution of the Swiss Agreement whether in addition to or in place of existing taxes (Art.2(2)).

50. The Swiss Agreement does not make any general provision or statement in respect of the income tax liability of a resident of either country.

51. Instead the Agreement, in Chapter III under the heading "Taxation of Income", specifies enabling, limiting or exclusionary provisions in respect of the imposition of taxation by Australia and/or Switzerland (Arts.6-21). Those articles set out circumstances in which specified types of income, or income derived by specified persons, is taxable only in one of the two countries or the manner in which it may be taxable in both. The provisions in these articles amount to a compact by the contracting countries as to the extent to which either country may levy taxation on residents of the other in respect of the income defined in Arts.6-21.

52. The provisions of the Swiss Agreement relating to the exclusion of taxing powers principally operate to exclude taxing rights for the country which has been the source of the income derived, and reserve such taxing rights to the country of residence of the person deriving the income. The items of income in respect of which the Agreement provides for exclusion or limitation of taxing rights appear to be chosen for reasons that apply specifically to each item and no general underlying purpose or intention can be divined for the limitation of taxing powers agreed upon by the contracting parties.

53. The exclusion of the taxing powers of the country of income source apply to business profits, unless the business is carried on through a permanent establishment in that country, (Art.7), profits from the operation of ships or aircraft (Art.8), income derived by an individual from professional services or independent activities of a similar character, unless the individual is operating from a fixed base in that country (Art.14), salaries and wages derived by employees who are not residents of the country in which they work who are paid by an employer who is not a resident of that country and who are present in that country for not more than 183 days in a year (Art.15), pensions and annuities (Art.18), remuneration paid by a country to persons discharging governmental functions for that country rendered in the other country by a resident or citizen of that country (Art.19).

54. Exclusion of taxing powers apply to the country in which income is received if it is remuneration paid by the other country to persons discharging governmental functions for that country (Art.19), or payments for maintenance or education received by a student of any country temporarily resident in the other country for the purpose of education (Art.20).

55. Limitations upon the taxing rights of the country of income source are imposed in respect of dividends (Art.10), interest (Art.11) and royalties (Art.12).

56. An express acknowledgement of the source country's right to tax is made in respect of income from real property (Art.6), or from alienation of real property or rights of exploration (Art.13), directors' fees (Art.16) and the income of entertainers (Art.17).

57. Where the Swiss Agreement has not excluded or limited the taxing rights of the contracting parties, relief against the imposition of double taxation has to be found elsewhere in the Agreement.

58. In Chapter IV of the Swiss Agreement (Art.22) the contracting parties set out the methods by which double taxation is to be eliminated. The machinery of Art.22 becomes available for use in those cases in which the Swiss Agreement has not otherwise excluded the taxing powers of one of the contracting parties or limited the right to levy taxation to only one of those countries.

59. Article 22 applies different methods of eliminating double taxation for Australian and Swiss residents.

60. Pursuant to Art.22(1), an Australian resident is entitled to have any Swiss tax paid in respect of income derived by that resident from sources in Switzerland allowed as a credit against Australian tax payable in respect of the same income. The right to a credit is not limited to Swiss tax paid on income of the type referred to in the Swiss Agreement.

61. Pursuant to Art.22(2), the right of a resident of Switzerland to be freed from double taxation is expressed as an obligation upon Switzerland to exempt from Swiss tax income derived by a resident of Switzerland, being income dealt with in the Agreement and which in accordance with the provisions of the Agreement may be taxed in Australia. A discretion is reserved to Switzerland as to the form of relief from taxation it may apply in respect of dividends, interest and royalties derived by a Swiss resident and which may be taxed in Australia.

62. Although it may appear from a literal reading of Art.22(2) that relief from double taxation for a resident of Switzerland is limited to exemption from Swiss tax of such income as is referred to in the Agreement, such a construction would be unduly narrow and not consonant with the express purpose of the Agreement. Art.2(1) of the Agreement expressly states that the Agreement applies to Australian income tax. It did not limit the application of the Agreement to such Australian income tax as applied to particular forms of income referred to in the Agreement. The purpose of the Agreement was to avoid double taxation with respect to taxes on income and not taxes on certain income. Because the provisions of Art.2(1) apply the Agreement to Australian taxes levied as income tax under the Assessment Act, the income the subject of that tax, must be income dealt with in the Agreement for the purposes of Art.22(2) of the Agreement. To hold otherwise would limit the provisions for relief against double taxation available to a resident of Switzerland to taxes levied on the type of income referred to in Arts.6, 7, 13, 14, 15, 16 and 17 of the Agreement and to allow any other form of income derived by a resident of Switzerland taxable in Australia and in Switzerland to be liable to double taxation.

63. The Swiss Agreement provides machinery for a resident of Australia or Switzerland to challenge the actions of tax authorities of either country. Pursuant to Art.23(1) the appellant, if he considers that the actions of the tax authorities of Australia would result in him being taxed not in accordance with the Swiss Agreement, may present his case to the Director of the Federal Tax Administration for Switzerland notwithstanding any remedies provided by the national laws of Australia. Article 23(3) requires the Commissioner of Taxation for Australia and the Director of the Federal Tax Administration for Switzerland, or their authorized representatives, to jointly endeavour to resolve any difficulties or doubts arising as to the application of the Swiss Agreement and pursuant to Art.23(4) the two officers may bypass diplomatic channels and communicate with each other directly for the purpose of giving effect to the provisions of the Agreement.

64. That option appears to be available to the appellant at any time and the terms of Art.23 are not expressed as an alternative to the pursuit of remedies provided by national laws at the election of the appellant.

65. In construing the Swiss Agreement it is important to recognise that the Agreement is one of a large number of bilateral treaties endeavouring to effect an international purpose of regulating the problem of dual incidence of taxation upon residents of one country who derive income from sources in several countries.

66. The Swiss Agreement should be construed, therefore, by having regard to the principles that have led to its acceptance. (See Fothergill v. Monarch Airlines Ltd. [1980] UKHL 6; (1981) AC 251 per Lord Diplock pp 281-282.) Although the interpretation should not be constrained by technical rules of construction, the object of construction should be to achieve the purpose of the treaty as elucidated by any available "travaux preparatoires" in the event of any ambiguity or obscurity in the words of the treaty. That is not to say that the treaty is to be given a liberal construction without regard to ordinary canons of construction but it should receive as liberal an interpretation as will provide a true construction according to its language object and intent and will not hinder the working of international arrangements. (See Government of Belgium v. Postlethwaite & Ors. (1987) 3 WLR 365.)

67. The appellant submitted that to assist in the construction of the Swiss Agreement reference should be made to the Model Double Taxation Convention ("Model Convention") adopted by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) in 1977 and to the Official Commentary on the Articles in the Model Convention ("Official Commentary") published by the OECD.

68. Australia is a member of the OECD and by such membership signifies adherence to the intention expressed in the Commentary that the Model Convention be applied between OECD Member countries in their bilateral agreements or treaties, subject to any reservations or derogations to the Model Convention recorded by a Member country. Switzerland is one of the twenty-four Member countries of the OECD. Therefore if the contents of the Model Convention and Official Commentary can assist in identifying the principles that have led to the acceptance of the Swiss Agreement and thereby the construction of the Swiss Agreement, it would be proper to refer to the Model Convention and Official Commentary for that purpose.

69. In Art.21 of the Model Convention it is provided that items of income of a resident of a Contracting State, wherever arising, and not dealt with in the Articles of the Convention are taxable only in that State. There is no equivalent provision in the Swiss Agreement made between Australia and Switzerland three years after the adoption of the Model Convention. Indeed, in the Official Commentary provided by the Organisation it is stated that Australia reserved its position on this Article because of a desire to maintain the right to tax income arising from Australian sources.

70. The Swiss Agreement also modified the Model Convention in respect of capital gains and taxation on capital.

71. Article 13(4) of the Model Convention stipulated that gains from the alienation of any property other than that referred to in the preceding paragraphs of that article should be taxable only in the Contracting State of which the alienator was a resident. That provision was omitted from the Swiss Agreement.

72. The Official Commentary on Art.13 contained the following paragraph:

"As capital gains are not taxed by all States it

may be considered reasonable to avoid only actual

double taxation of capital gains. Therefore,

Contracting States are free to supplement their

bilateral convention in such a way that a State

has to forego its right to tax conferred on it by

the domestic laws only if the other State on which

the right to tax is conferred by the Convention

makes use thereof. In such a case para.4 of the

Article (13) should be supplemented accordingly."

73. Australia made a reservation on Art.13 by reserving the right to propose changes to reflect the fact that Australia did not levy a capital gains tax.

74. Similarly, Art.22 of the Model Convention relating to taxes on capital was omitted entirely from the Swiss Agreement.

75. The statement of reservation by Australia to Art.13 of the Model Convention was not a comprehensive statement of the effect of Australian taxation laws. Although, as at April 1977, Australia did not have a general capital gains tax, the provisions of s.26AAA of the Assessment Act had been in operation since December 1973 and had the effect of including the profits arising from the sale of property within twelve months of purchase as part of the assessable income tax of a taxpayer and to that extent operated on all such gains whether capital or revenue in nature.

76. Article 2(1)(a) of the Swiss Agreement states that the existing taxes to which the Agreement applies are the Australian income tax. Article 2(3) states that the term "Australian tax" means tax imposed by Australia being tax to which the Agreement applies.

77. Section 3 of the Agreements Act defined "Australian tax" as "income tax or income tax and social services contribution imposed as such by any Act" and s.4 of the Act incorporated the whole of the Assessment Act. Section 11E(1) of the Agreements Act provided that, subject to the Agreements Act, the provisions of the Swiss Agreement, so far as those provisions affected Australian tax, had the force of law in relation to tax in respect of income.

78. The provisions of s.26AAA of the Assessment Act which made any profits derived from the sale of property within twelve months of purchase liable for the imposition of income tax were provisions included within the expression "Australian tax" referred to in s.3(1) of the Agreements Act and Art.2(1)(a) of the Swiss Agreement.

79. The failure to include provisions such as Art.13(4) and Art.21 of the Model Convention means that the Swiss Agreement has nothing specific to say about capital profits treated as income under the provisions of the Assessment Act and the elimination of liability to double taxation is left to the general provisions of Art.22 of the Agreement and the mutual agreement procedures for the resolution of difficulties arising as to the application of the Agreement provided for in Art.23 of the Agreement.

80. Australia has concluded agreements for the avoidance of double taxation with several other countries which do include a form of Art.21 of the Model Convention. Those agreements are incorporated as schedules to the Agreements Act. However, the text of the relevant Article included in such agreements consistently reflects the reservation expressed by Australia in 1977 in respect of Art.21 of the Model Convention by expressly reserving the right for the country of source to tax income derived by a resident of the other Contracting State if such items of income are not expressly mentioned elsewhere in preceding Articles of those Agreements. (See Irish Agreement (Art. 23), Norwegian Convention (Art. 21), Korean Convention (Art. 22.)

81. Article 7(1) of the Swiss Agreement is in the following terms:

"The profits of an enterprise of one of the

Contracting States shall be taxable only in that

State unless the enterprise carries on business in

the other Contracting State through a permanent

establishment situated therein. If the enterprise

carries on business as aforesaid, the profits of

the enterprise may be taxed in the other State,

but only so much of them as is attributable to

that permanent establishment."

82. The appellant argues that the term "enterprise" should be construed as including a commercial venture or undertaking and that the "profits of an enterprise" should not be limited to the profits of a business carried on.

83. The appellant gains some support for this construction from the contents of sub-s.3(2) of the Agreements Act which provides as follows:

"For the purposes of this Act and the Assessment

Act, a reference in an agreement to profits of an

activity or business shall, in relation to

Australian tax, be read, where the context so

permits, as a reference to taxable income derived

from that activity or business."

84. That provision in the Agreements Act indicates that profits are to be read as taxable income derived. The Act does not refer to profits of an enterprise but to profits of an activity or business.

85. Article 3(1)(f) of the Swiss Agreement defines "enterprise of one of the Contracting States" as an enterprise carried on by a resident of Australia or of Switzerland as the context requires.

86. Although the term "enterprise of one of the Contracting States" is defined, the word "enterprise" is not and nor is the phrase "enterprise carried on".

87. Article 3(2) of the Swiss Agreement stipulates that any term not otherwise defined shall, unless the context otherwise requires, have the meaning which it has under the laws of that Contracting State relating to the taxes to which the Agreement applies.

88. Therefore, it is necessary to ascertain whether the meaning of enterprise is demonstrated by the context of the Swiss Agreement before resorting to the meaning that the word may have under the laws relating to Australian tax.

89. Other references in the Swiss Agreement to "enterprise" or to matters plertaining to it are as follows.

90. A definition of an expression used in association with the term enterprise in Art.7(1) is contained in Art.5(1).

"For the purposes of this Agreement, the term

'permanent establishment' means a fixed place of

business through which the business of an

enterprise is wholly or partly carried on."

91. Article 9 deals with "associated enterprise" and is in the following terms:

"Where -

(a) an enterprise of one of the Contracting States

participates directly or indirectly in the

management, control or capital of an

enterprise of the other Contracting State; or

(b) the same persons participate directly or

indirectly in the management, control or

capital of an enterprise of one of the

Contracting States and an enterprise of the

other Contracting State,

and in either case conditions operate between the

two enterprises in their commercial or financial

relations which differ from those which might be

expected to operate between independent

enterprises dealing wholly independently with one

another, then any profits which, but for those

conditions, might have been expected to accrue to

one of the enterprises, but, by reason of those

conditions, have not so accrued, may be included

in the profits of that enterprise and taxed

accordingly."

The term enterprise is also used in Art.13(3):

"Subject to the provisions of paragraphs (1) and

(2), income from the alienation of capital assets

of an enterprise of one of the Contracting States

shall be taxable only in that Contracting State,

but, where those assets form part of the business

property of a permanent establishment situated in

the other Contracting State, such income may be

taxed in that other State."

92. None of these references in the Agreement provide a context for an expansive meaning for the term "enterprise". The appellant argues, however, that where first used in Art.7(1) and where used in Art.13(3), the term is to be given its full meaning and where the term is used elsewhere in the Agreement it is to be taken to be referring to only part of the extended meaning.

93. If Art.7 of the Swiss Agreement is read with the definition of "enterprise of one of the Contracting States" contained in Art.3(1)(f), it is necessary for the appellant to show that the gains made on the sale of the units and the shares were profits of an enterprise carried on by the appellant.

94. The context provided by Arts.3(1)(f), 5(1), 7(1), 7(2) and 9 relating to the carrying-on of an enterprise or the carrying-on of a business of an enterprise indicate a clear intention in the Agreement to provide taxation exemption in respect of the profits of a business or on-going commercial activity of an enterprise. The use of the word "profits" enhances that meaning. The Agreement makes no provision for an isolated venture in the nature of trade which stands apart from the conduct or carrying-on of the business of an enterprise.

95. Because the context of the Swiss Agreement supplies a meaning for the term "enterprise carried on", it is unnecessary to resort to Australian law relating to taxes to find that meaning but such a step would only confirm the meaning supplied by the context. In cases dealing with the meaning of the carrying on or carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme (Assessment Act sub-s.26(a)), the need for an habitual course of conduct in respect of the former in distinction to the execution of a plan or a venture in respect of the latter has been long stated. (The Premier Automatic Ticket Issuers Limited v. The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1933] HCA 51; (1933) 50 CLR 268 per Dixon J. at p 298.) It may be accepted that the meaning attributed to sub-s.26(a) of the Assessment Act by the decided cases has been carried forward into its replacement s.25A. (See comments of Professor Parsons "Income Taxation in Australia" para.3.81-3.83.)

96. The intent to undertake a specific transaction with the purpose of trading in property with a view to a profit may, if the business dealing component is clear, attract liability under sub-s.25(1) or s.25A, but establishing that will not in itself answer the essential question as to what relief for such liability is provided by the Swiss Agreement.

97. The appellant argued that the profits earned by the appellant from an isolated commercial transaction entered into with a view to profit were assessable income according to the provisions of sub-s.25(1) and sub-s.25A(1) of the Assessment Act and, therefore, were profits of an enterprise within the meaning of Art.7(1) of the Swiss Agreement. Even if the appellant were able to establish that his activities amounted to an isolated business venture which attracted the operation of s.25A in either of its limbs (see The Commisioner of Taxation v. Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355) or sub-s.25(1) pursuant to the ordinary concepts of income, it will not follow that the appellant will be shown thereby to be carrying on an enterprise within the meaning of Art.7(1) of the Swiss Agreement.

98. Undoubtedly, the meaning of "an enterprise" is broad enough to encompass a commercial venture, but Art.7 is dealing with profits of an enterprise carried on and the context shows that the term enterprise means the conduct of continuing activities rather than the carrying out or execution of an isolated transaction.

99. The Swiss Agreement does not operate to provide relief from double taxation solely by exempting the taxing powers of one country. Relief is also provided by exempting the income from further tax in the country of residence and, therefore, the Agreement will not fail to provide for such a case if the exemption from taxation in the source country provided for in Art.7, does not include profits from an isolated commercial venture.

100. His Honour found that the transaction entered into by the appellant was a transaction to which s.26AAA applied and was not a transaction to which either sub-s.25(1) or s.25A applied. I express no view on the latter findings although s.25A would seem to be clearly applicable, but respectfully agree with his Honour's further findings that the profits resulting from the transaction were not exempted from taxation liability in Australia by the provisions of Art.7 of the Swiss Agreement.

101. Similarly, the appellant's alternative submission that Art.13(3) of the Swiss Agreement preserved sole taxing rights for Switzerland in this case must fail.

102. Art.13(3) provides that gains resulting from alienation of capital assets of an enterprise and treated as taxable income will be taxable only in the State in which the person carrying on the enterprise resides.

103. In the appellant's case the sale of the units and shares was the totality of the enterprise. The alienation by sale effected the enterprise and was the execution of the venture. In no sense were the units or the shares the capital assets of an enterprise carried on by the appellant.

104. Accordingly, in my opinion the appeal should be dismissed.
