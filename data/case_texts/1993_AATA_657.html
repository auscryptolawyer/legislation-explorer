Last Updated: 20 May 2009



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



INCOME TAX - Taxpayer and his father-in-law joint purchasers of property - property occupied by father-in-law until 1984 - father-in-law assigned his interest as purchaser to taxpayer in 1986 - whether rent paid by father-in-law - whether assessable income derived - whether deductions allowable - whether contributions to Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme allowable deduction - decision affirmed.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 21, 26, 51(1), 82AAS(2), 82AAT(1)

Superannuation Act 1976



Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Groser 1982 65 FLR 121

Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; 1981 50 FLR 219

REASONS FOR DECISION



30 September 1993 Deputy President B.M. Forrest

Mr D. Elsum, AM, Member

Mr B.H. Pascoe, Member



This is an application for review of a decision to disallow the taxpayer's objection against an amended income tax assessment for the year ending 30 June 1988.



The taxpayer included as assessable income in his 1988 return an amount of $250 as gross rent received from a residential property, "the property", he owned. Against that he claimed deductions totalling $6,565 made up of rates ($497), repairs ($1,000), interest ($4,738), insurance ($130) and sundry charges ($200). Following an audit an amended assessment was issued disallowing the deductions in excess of the rent. The taxpayer had also claimed in his 1988 return contributions of $1,249 to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme as an allowable deduction. This also was disallowed.



The issues before the Tribunal are:

a. whether the sum of $250 was correctly included as assessable income pursuant to the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, "the Act";



b. whether deductions totalling $6,565 claimed in respect of the property are allowable deductions under sub-section 51(1) of the Act; and



c. whether contributions of $1,249 made to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme are an allowable deduction under sub-section 82AAT(1) of the Act.



From the evidence of the taxpayer and the material before the Tribunal the following facts emerged. The property in question, originally owned by the Commonwealth, had been occupied by the taxpayer's father-in-law since 1942. In 1963 it was offered for sale to the father-in-law as part of a scheme to sell to long serving employees of the Commonwealth properties they occupied. Apparently the taxpayer's father-in-law was not particularly anxious to purchase the property, but when prevailed upon by his family agreed to do so. Eventually the property was purchased for $4,100 under a terms contract of sale dated 11 May 1964 in the names of the taxpayer and his father-in-law. The contract provided for payment of a deposit of $100 and the residue, including interest at 5½ per cent per annum on the residue of purchase money, by monthly instalments of $20.25 from 1 June 1964 until 1 June 1994 when the balance was due. The taxpayer paid the deposit of $100.



The father-in-law continued to live in the property and paid the taxpayer $20.25 per month. In turn the taxpayer paid the monthly instalments to the Commonwealth and other usual outgoings. The father-in-law made various improvements to the property including building a garage. In 1983 he agreed to increase the payments to $20 per fortnight as the taxpayer had incurred additional expenses for repairs and repainting. In 1984 the father-in-law moved to a nursing home. Thereafter the property was vacant, it being used for storage of the father-in-law's belongings until his death in November 1987.



In April 1984 the taxpayer and his wife separated. They were divorced in 1985. By an agreement in writing made on 22 October 1986 between the taxpayer and his father-in-law (the taxpayer's spouse was also a party to the agreement) the father-in-law agreed to assign his interest as purchaser in the property to the taxpayer in consideration of the taxpayer assuming liability for the payment of the balance of purchase money. The agreement also contained provision for the taxpayer and his spouse to pre-pay the funeral expenses of the father-in-law and an annual sum for his maintenance. The agreement contained the following recital:



"G. Since the purchase of the property [father-in-law] has paid to [taxpayer] the sum of $20.25 per month initially and for the last three years $20.00 per fortnight by way of reimbursement of the expenses incurred by [taxpayer]."



For the purposes of a property settlement between the taxpayer and his spouse they made an agreement dated 23 October 1986 (sanctioned by the Family Court) in which the value of the property, on the basis of an independent valuation was fixed at $50,000.



In the terms of the property settlement (which included other properties) the taxpayer was required to pay his former spouse $91,023.65. To do this the taxpayer arranged bank Bill facilities on the security of the property and the other properties he owned. He completed the terms purchase of the property from the Commonwealth of Australia in November 1986 simultaneous with the property settlement with his former spouse. The taxpayer sold the property in June 1988. It had remained vacant from the time the father-in-law ceased occupancy in 1984 until it was sold. According to the taxpayer he had intended to let the property after his father-in-law's death in 1987, but, under pressure from the bank to reduce his indebtedness, he was obliged to sell.



When questioned about the payments which the taxpayer maintained were rent, he said they reverted from $20 per fortnight back to $250 per year when his father-in-law vacated the property in 1984. He said his father-in-law gave him some money while he was in the nursing home. Questioned further he said the payment of $250 in 1988 represented the proceeds of sale of the father-in-law's unwanted furniture which was given to the taxpayer in lieu of rent by his sister-in-law who had assumed control of her father's affairs.



There can be no dispute that payments made by the father-in-law prior to assigning his interest in the property to the taxpayer were, as the parties acknowledged in the agreement dated 22 October 1986 referred to earlier, not payments of rent but reimbursement of expenses. These payments were not income according to ordinary concepts.



In the terms of the agreement with his father-in-law the taxpayer assumed liability for all expenses. The agreement also recited that, as the occupation by the father-in-law was not continuing, the parties agreed the property be placed solely in the name of the taxpayer. Any continuing occupation of the property by the father-in-law was not then contemplated and there is nothing in the agreement or other evidence to suggest otherwise. It is noted also that within the terms of the agreement, the father-in-law agreed that the consideration for transferring his interest in the property was "his intention to make a gift" to the taxpayer of his interest in the property.



Accordingly, it is difficult to conclude that whatever the taxpayer's subjective belief there was any contemplation on the part of the father-in-law that subsequent to the agreement the father-in-law would have a liability to make rental payments. There was no evidence to suggest any agreement or arrangement to pay rent subsequent to the agreement either with the father-in-law in his advancing years or anyone on his behalf. In our opinion the whole history of the property pointed to the arrangements being of a familial rather than commercial character.



Subsequent to the death of the father-in-law the taxpayer received in the year in question some unwanted furniture which the taxpayer maintained was in lieu of rent. Apparently it was a matter for the taxpayer whether he retained or sold the furniture. The transaction has on its face the characteristic of a family arrangement in which the taxpayer received not rent but a token of a loose domestic arrangement where the father-in-law's belongings were maintained in the home he formerly occupied. In our opinion the proceeds of the sale of furniture given to the taxpayer cannot be characterised as rent and therefore is not assessable income. It was not income according to ordinary concepts or a benefit of a kind that would be assessable under s. 26 or money value of a consideration given other than in cash; s. 21. It was payment of a non-commercial character.



The taxpayer did not at any stage take steps to let the property on a commercial basis. There was evidence from Mr Hendry, which we accept, that a commercial rent for the property in 1987 would be around $65 per week.



Given the view we have formed that the amount of $250 is not assessable income it follows there are no allowable deductions under s. 51. However, if we are wrong in that conclusion and the $250 is income in the taxpayer's hands then the question remains whether an amount greater than $250 on a proper basis of apportionment should be allowed as a deduction under s. 51(1).



In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Groser 1982 65 FLR 121, Jenkinson J. decided that an amount of $2 per week which the taxpayer deducted from the pension of his invalid pensioner brother who occupied the taxpayer's residence, although described as rent, was a transaction of a familial character unconnected with the receipt of income as understood in ordinary usage. His Honour went on to say that if that conclusion was incorrect and the weekly payments were assessable income then the deduction for expenses in excess of the assessable income should not be allowed; see Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; 1981 50 FLR 219; 34 ALR 237. This was so because the outgoings were incurred for mainly domestic considerations, the derivation of income by way of rent was a subordinate one and consequently the outgoings should be apportioned between what may be regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income and what could not be so regarded.



In the present case, the taxpayer was a co-purchaser of the property primarily to assist his parents-in-law. The history of the property from when it was purchased in 1963 clearly demonstrates that earning income by way of rent was not a purpose of the arrangement, certainly not during the lifetime of his father-in-law. In 1986 when the property was acquired solely by the taxpayer as part of an overall property settlement with his spouse, family considerations were paramount in the arrangements made. In our opinion even if the $250 is assessable income, on a proper apportionment for the purposes of s. 51(1) the claim for outgoings is on the authority of Ure and Groser limited to $250. Accordingly the taxpayer's claim must fail.



We turn next to the claimed deduction for superannuation contributions of $1,249 to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme during the year ended 30 June 1988.



It was not in issue that the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme was a fund to which the then s. 23FC of the Act applied. Therefore the applicant was entitled to a deduction if he satisfied s. 82AAT(1) which provided that contributions by an eligible person to a fund to obtain superannuation benefits for that person or in the event of his death for his dependants are an allowable deduction (limited to $1,500; sub-s. (2)) in the year of income in which the contributions are made.



The expressions "eligible persons" and "superannuation benefits" are referred to as follows:



"82AAS(2) Subject to sub-section (3), a person (in this sub-section referred to as the "relevant person") is an eligible person in relation to a year of income for the purposes of this Subdivision unless-



(a) during the whole or a part of the year of income circumstances existed by reason of which it was reasonable to expect that superannuation benefits would be provided for the relevant person upon retirement or for dependants of the relevant person in the event of the death of the relevant person (whether or not any condition other than the retirement or death of the relevant person would be required to be satisfied in order that those benefits be provided); and



(b) to the extent to which those benefits would be attributable to the year of income-



(i) the benefits would be wholly or partly attributable to contributions made to a superannuation fund in relation to the relevant person by a person other than the relevant person; or



(ii) the benefits would, in whole or in part, be paid out of moneys that would not represent-



(A) contributions made by the relevant person to a superannuation fund;



(B) contributions made by the relevant person under a scheme for the payment of benefits upon retirement or death, being a scheme constituted by or under a law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory; or



(C) income or accretions arising from contributions referred to in sub-sub-paragraph (A) or (B).



82AAS(2A) A reference in subsection (2) to superannuation benefits does not include a reference to superannuation benefits to the extent to which they would be attributable to, or paid out of money representing:



(a) superannuation agreement contributions made in relation to the relevant person referred to in that subsection; or



(b) income or accretions arising from such contributions."



The taxpayer is a now retired Commonwealth Public Servant with some 45 years service. He has an entitlement to retirement benefits as provided for in Part V of the Superannuation Act 1976.



The argument of the taxpayer that benefits are not paid from the unfunded consolidated revenue account but from an accumulation of contributors funds is without substance. The scheme of which the taxpayer was a member has an employer component which is unfunded and paid from consolidated revenue. In the year 1988 it was reasonable to expect that, as a contributor to the scheme superannuation benefits would be provided for the taxpayer upon retirement and that the benefits would be wholly or partly attributable to contributions made to a superannuation fund by a person other than the taxpayer. Alternatively the benefit in whole or in part would be paid out of moneys that would not represent the taxpayer's contribution to a superannuation fund or under a scheme for the payment of benefits upon retirement being a scheme constituted under a law of the Commonwealth or income or accretions arising from such contributions.



That being so the taxpayer is not an eligible person for the purposes of s. 82AAT and therefore his contributions to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme are not an allowable deduction.



For these reasons the objection decision under review will be affirmed.
