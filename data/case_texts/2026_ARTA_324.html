Statement of Reasons

  1. Adele Baaini (the Applicant) has a Bachelor of Business majoring in Financial Planning from the University of Technology, Sydney, and a Diploma in Financial Services from Pinnacle Financial Services Academy. The Applicant started working in the financial services industry in around 2010, and was a financial adviser until sometime in 2017. Between 2019 and 2022 she had a break from the industry while raising children. She returned to the industry as an employee in April 2023.
  2. From 14 April 2023 to 22 June 2023, the Applicant worked for AAN Wealth Management Pty Ltd (AAN Wealth). As part of her onboarding process with AAN Wealth, on 12 April 2023 the Applicant was asked to provide proof that she had passed the Financial Advisers Exam (Exam or FASEA Exam) administered by the Financial Adviser Standards and Ethics Authority (FASEA). The Applicant provided the Responsible Manager (RM) for AAN Wealth with a certificate on 14 April 2023, and informed them that she had passed the Exam on 31 August 2021.
  3. In the period from 14 April 2023 to 22 June 2023, the Applicant was listed on the Financial Advisers Register (Register) maintained by the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) for AAN Wealth as a ‘current’ financial adviser. During that time, the Applicant provided financial advice to at least 121 clients of AAN Wealth.
  4. The certificate the Applicant provided to AAN Wealth on 14 April 2023 (FASEA Exam Certificate) was a falsified certificate created by the Applicant. The Applicant had not, in fact, passed the Exam on 31 August 2021 or at any other time, and had not sat for the Exam. This was discovered by AAN Wealth after an enquiry made by ASIC.
  5. On 20 June 2023 the RM of AAN Wealth contacted the Applicant, requiring her to provide him with all documents relating to her Exam result. The Applicant did not initially respond and then emailed her resignation due to “personal issues” on 22 June 2023. AAN Wealth lodged breach reports with ASIC on 30 June 2023 and 3 July 2023.
  6. On 13 December 2023, ASIC sent a notice to the Applicant that it was considering making a banning order against her pursuant to s 80 of the National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009 (Cth) (NCCP Act) and/or section 920A of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) (Corporations Act). The notice set out that there would be a hearing in February 2024 at which the Applicant could appear and make submissions. The Applicant provided written submissions to ASIC on 4 March 2024 but did not appear at a hearing.
  7. On 18 April 2024, a delegate of ASIC made a decision (Reviewable Decision) to issue a banning order on the Applicant, pursuant to ss 80 and 81 of the NCCP Act, in the following terms (Banning Order):
    TAKE NOTICE that under sections 80 and 81 of the National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009 the Australian Securities and Investments Commission permanently prohibits Adele Baaini from:

    1. engaging in any credit activities;

    2. controlling, whether alone or in concert with one or more other entities (as defined by section 64A of the Corporations Act 2001), another person who engages in credit activities; and

    3. performing any function involved in the engaging in of credit activities (including as an officer (within the meaning of the Corporations Act 2001), manager, employee, contractor or in some other capacity).

  8. ASIC relied on the following grounds for the Banning Order, that it had reason to believe the Applicant was:
    (a) likely to contravene any credit legislation (s80(1)(e)(i) of the NCCP Act); or

    (b) not a fit and proper person to engage in one or more credit activities, perform one or more functions as an officer (within the meaning of the Corporations Act) of another person who engages in credit activities, or control another person who engages in credit activities (s80(1)(f) of the NCCP Act). In relation to the Applicant’s fitness and propriety, ASIC had regard to the following relevant matters, pursuant to s 37B(2)(k) of the NCCP Act:

    (i) the Applicant’s conduct as a financial adviser or relevant provider under the Corporations Act;

    (ii) the Applicant’s conduct in falsifying her FASEA Exam certificate.

  9. ASIC found that:
    Ms Baaini’s conduct was dishonest. She did not demonstrate attributes of good character, diligence, honesty, integrity and judgment. Ms Baaini not only provided a falsified Exam certificate to AAN Wealth and gave personal advice to 121 clients in circumstances where she did not meet the prescribed education and training standard, but when queried by AAN Wealth about the certificate on 20 June 2023, did not acknowledge and take responsibility for what happened.
  10. In deciding to ban the Applicant from credit activities, ASIC took into consideration:
    To permit a person who has lied about their qualifications to continue working in the financial services or credit industry has the potential to erode public confidence in licensing regimes. Such behaviour is neither acceptable nor tolerated.

    Regulatory Guide 218: Licensing: Administrative action against persons engaging in credit activities (RG 218) sets out ASIC’s views on key factors to be considered when deciding to take administrative action such as a banning order. Relevantly for this matter, I have taken into consideration the nature of the misconduct (it involved dishonesty), the benefit gained (the FASEA Certificate allowed Ms Baaini to work as a relevant provider) and conduct after the misconduct occurred. Namely, Ms Baaini has not demonstrated any contrition.

    In view of the above matters, I consider that a banning order against Ms Baaini is necessary for both specific and general deterrence, to promote investor and consumer confidence, and to promote fairness, honesty and professionalism by those who engage in credit activities, thereby improving the performance of entities within the financial system.

    I acknowledge that a ban may result in personal hardship for Ms Baaini in that it precludes her from taking up opportunities in the credit industry. However, I consider that any hardship for Ms Baaini personally is outweighed by public interest considerations.

  11. In determining the scope and duration of the ban, ASIC had regard to Table 2 in Regulatory Guide 218: Licensing: Administrative action against persons engaging in credit activities (RG 218), finding the Applicant’s conduct fell into the most serious category justifying a range of 10 years to permanent banning.
  12. On 21 July 2024, the applicant filed an application for review of the Reviewable Decision (Review Application), which included an application for an extension of time as it was filed outside the statutory time frame of 28 days. In the Review Application, the Applicant stated that she required an extension of time because “I was not aware that I can appeal the decision”, and claimed the decision was wrong because:
    I admit I made a mistake as I didn't not know the severity of my action. As I have the highest education level a university degree in my field

    I was not aware of needing the fasea exam to work in my field. When I.did find out it was too late

  13. On 14 November 2024, the Tribunal granted an extension of time for the Applicant to file the Review Application pursuant to 19(2) of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (ART Act). The Review Application proceeded to hearing on 17 November 2025.
  14. For the reasons that follow, I have decided to affirm the Respondent’s Reviewable Decision.

KEY STATUTORY PROVISIONS AND RELEVANT LEGAL PRINCIPLES

  1. Section 327 of the NCCP Act provides for review by the Administrative Review Tribunal (the Tribunal) of a decision by ASIC to make a banning order.
  2. The Tribunal’s task pursuant to ss 9, 56 and 105 of the ART Act is to review ASIC’s Reviewable Decision and make the correct or preferable decision to either affirm it, vary it, or set it aside. If it is set aside the Tribunal can either make a decision in substitution, or remit the matter back to ASIC for reconsideration in accordance with the Tribunal’s recommendations.
  3. Pursuant to s 9(1) of the ART Act the Tribunal is required to pursue the objective of providing an independent mechanism of review that is, among other things, fair and just, and pursuant to s 25 of the ART Act must ensure that each party to a proceeding is given a reasonable opportunity to present their case.
  4. The Tribunal is not limited to the evidence which was before the original decision-maker and can consider events subsequent to the Reviewable Decision: s 56 ART Act; Frugtniet v ASIC [2019] HCA 16; (2019) 266 CLR 250 at [14]- [15]. In receiving and considering that evidence, however, the Tribunal is bound by the rules of procedural fairness.
  5. Chapter 2 of the NCCP Act sets out a scheme for the licensing of persons to engage in credit activities. Within Ch 2, Div 2 of Pt 2-4 provides for the making of banning orders prohibiting persons from engaging in credit activities. Section 80(1) of the NCCP Act relevantly provides:
    Making a banning order

    (1) ASIC may, in writing, make one or more orders (banning orders) against a person:

    ...

    (e) if ASIC has reason to believe that the person is likely to:

    (i) contravene any credit legislation; or

    (ii) be involved in a contravention of a provision of any credit legislation by another person; or

    (f) if ASIC has reason to believe that the person is not a fit and proper person to:

    (i) engage in one or more credit activities; or

    (ii) perform one or more functions as an officer (within the meaning of the Corporations Act 2001) of another person who engages in credit activities; or

    (iii) control another person who engages in credit activities;

    ...

    When a person is not a fit and proper person

    (2) For the purposes of paragraph (1)(f), ASIC must have regard to the matters in section 37B.

    ...

    Person to be given an opportunity to be heard

    (4) Subject to subsection (5), if ASIC has not delegated its power to make a banning order against a person to a Financial Services and Credit Panel, ASIC may make the order only after giving the person an opportunity:

    (a) to appear, or be represented, at a hearing before ASIC that takes place in private; and

    (b) to make submissions to ASIC on the matter.

    ...

    Copy of banning order to be given to the person

    (7) ASIC must give a copy of a banning order to the person against whom it was made.

  6. Section 80 of the NCCP Act provides the Tribunal with a discretion to make a banning order if it has “reason to believe” that an individual is likely to contravene credit legislation (s 80(1)(e)(i)) or that they are not a fit and proper person to engage in credit activities (s 80(1)(f)(i)). Having “reason to believe” has been held, in other statutory contexts, to impose a test which is both subjective and objective: Frugtniet and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2022] AATA 295 at [31] to [32]. It requires:
    (a) that the Tribunal hold the relevant belief; and

    (b) that that belief be based upon objective facts that would induce that state of mind in a reasonable person: Power v Hamond [2006] VSCA 25, [105]–[106] cited in Karamian v ASIC [2024] AATA 2006 (Karamian) at [153]; WA Pines v Bannerman [1980] FCA 79; 41 FLR 175 at 186; Pilnara Pty Ltd v Cmr of Taxation [1999] FCA 945 at [18].

  7. The power to make a banning order is enlivened if any one of the prerequisites contained in s 80(1) of the NCCP Act is satisfied. If one or more of the sub-provisions in s 80(1) is enlivened, the Tribunal must then decide whether the correct or preferable decision is to ban the Applicant under s 81 of the Act, and the length and breadth of any such ban.
  8. Section 81 set outs the scope of banning orders which may be imposed under s 80(1) of the NCCP Act:
    What a banning order prohibits

    (1) A banning order made against a person may specify that the person is prohibited from doing one or more of the following:

    (a) engaging in any credit activities;

    (b) engaging in specified credit activities in specified circumstances or capacities;

    (c) controlling, whether alone or in concert with one or more other entities (as defined by section 64A of the Corporations Act 2001), another person who engages in credit activities;

    (d) performing any function involved in the engaging in of credit activities (including as an officer (within the meaning of the Corporations Act 2001), manager, employee, contractor or in some other capacity);

    (e) performing specified functions involved in the engaging in of credit activities.

    (2) The banning order may specify that a particular prohibition specified in the order applies against the person:

    (a) if the sole ground for the banning order is because paragraph 80(1)(fc) applies--for a specified period of up to 5 years; or

    (b) otherwise--either permanently or for a specified period.

    Note: This subsection applies separately to each prohibition specified in the order.

    (3) A banning order may include a provision allowing the person against whom it was made, subject to any specified conditions:

    (a) to do specified acts; or

    (b) to do specified acts in specified circumstances;

    that the order would otherwise prohibit them from doing.

    (4) A banning order is not a legislative instrument.

  9. Section 37B of the NCCP Act sets out the mandatory considerations that ASIC must have regard to when considering, amongst other things, whether ASIC has reason to believe that a person is not a fit and proper person for the purposes of s 80(1)(f) of the NCCP Act. Relevantly:
    37B Fit and proper person test—matters to which ASIC must have regard

    ...

    (i) a licence, or a registration under the Transitional Act;

    (c) if the person is an individual—whether the person has ever been disqualified under the Corporations Act 2001, or any other law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory, from managing corporations;

    (e) whether the person has ever been linked to a refusal or failure to give effect to a determination made by AFCA (within the meaning of the Corporations Act 2001);

    (i) any relevant information given to ASIC by a State or Territory, or an authority of a State or Territory, in relation to the person;

Fit and proper person

  1. Whether a person is a “fit and proper person” is a question of fact and degree, a “value judgment”, “evaluative conclusion”, or opinion based on primary facts: Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321 (Bond) at [380], [388]; [1990] HCA 33; R v Connell; Ex parte Hetton Bellbird Collieries Ltd [1944] HCA 42; (1944) 69 CLR 407 at [430].
  2. What constitutes “fitness” is derived from context: Bond at [380] per Toohey and Gaudron JJ. In Bond the High Court explained:
    “The expression “fit and proper person”, standing alone, carries no precise meaning. It takes its meaning from its context, from the activities in which the person is or will be engaged and the ends to be served by those activities. The concept of “fit and proper” cannot be entirely divorced from the conduct of the person who is or will be engaging in those activities. However, depending on the nature of the activities, the question may be whether improper conduct has occurred, whether it is likely to occur, whether it can be assumed that it will not occur, or whether the general community will have confidence that it will not occur. The list is not exhaustive but it does indicate that, in certain contexts, character (because it provides indication of likely future conduct) or reputation (because it provides indication of public perception as to likely future conduct) may be sufficient to ground a finding that a person is not fit and proper to undertake the activities in question.
  3. Mason CJ further stated at [388] that:
    “The question whether a person is fit and proper is one of value judgment. In that process the seriousness or otherwise of particular conduct is a matter for evaluation by the decision-maker. So too is the weight, if any, to be given to matters favouring the person whose fitness and propriety are under consideration.”
  4. “Fitness” encompasses honesty, knowledge and ability: Hawcroft and ASIC [2024] AATA 3596 (Hawcroft) at [148] citing Hughes and Vale Pty Ltd v New South Wales (No 2) [1955] HCA 28; (1955) 93 CLR 127 (Hughes) at [156]; [1955] HCA 28. In Hughes at [156], Dixon CJ, McTiernan and Webb JJ observed:
    "Fit" (or "idoneus") with respect to an office is said to involve three things, honesty knowledge and ability: "honesty to execute it truly, without malice affection or partiality; knowledge to know what he ought duly to do; and ability as well in estate as in body, that he may intend and execute his office, when need is, diligently, and not for impotency or poverty neglect it".
  5. Knowledge and ability encompass a person’s skill, training and competence: Hawcroft at [166]. The expression “fit and proper person” is designed to “give the widest scope for judgment and indeed rejection”: Hughes at [156]. It includes considerations of knowledge, ability, and competence, but also moral integrity, rectitude of character and reputation: Hughes at [156]-[157]; Sobey v Commercial and Private Agents Board (1979) 22 SASR 70 (Sobey) at [76]. The question is not limited to the specific functions or activities enabled by the holding of a licence: Sobey at [76]. It is, however, to be “gauged in light of the nature and purpose of the activities that the person will undertake”: Hughes at [156]-[157]; Bond at [380].
  6. An applicant’s honesty and personal integrity is relevant to whether they are a fit and proper person. As Bromberg J stated in Frugtniet v Australian Securities and Investments Commission (2016) 152 ALD 31; [2016] FCA 995 (Frugtniet 2016) at [191]:
    Honesty is a notion of broader compass than legality. The two are not coextensive. A person who baldly lies may not have acted in contravention of law. Nevertheless a person in the habit of baldly lying, especially one who sees nothing objectionable in that habit, is unlikely to be fit and proper to engage in activities involving trust and vulnerability. It follows that it is not necessarily an answer to an accusation of dishonesty to plead consistency with law, nor often will technical chicanery or linguistic artfulness improve the plea. It does not suffice for a person who has patently and deliberately misled another to point to the absence of legal obligation to excuse the misleading. I would like to think that it is an aspect of being a fit and proper repository of trust that one does the moral thing whether or not one is legally obliged to do so.
  7. A person’s character and disposition is relevant because “it provides indication of likely future conduct”, and may be sufficient to ground a finding that person is not fit and proper to undertake the activities in question: Bond at [380]; Melbourne v The Queen (1999) 198 CLR 1 at [33], [62]; [1999] HCA 32.

Likely to contravene any credit legislation

  1. Pursuant to s 5 of the NCCP Act, the term “any credit legislation” used in s 80(1)(f) of the NCCP Act includes not only the NCCP Act but also the National Consumer Credit Protection (Transitional and Consequential Provisions) Act 2009 (Transitional Act); Division 2 of Part 2 of the Australian Securities Investments Commission Act 2001 (Cth) and associated regulations (which deal with unconscionable conduct and consumer protection in financial services); and any other State or Commonwealth legislation that covers conduct relating to credit activities, in relation to credit activities.
  2. The Corporations Act has a provision in similar terms in relation to a financial services law at s 920A(1)(f). With respect to that provision, the Federal Court in ASIC v AAT (2010) 187 FCR 334; 271 ALR 593; [2010] FCA 807 at [51] held that s 920A(1)(f) does not require that ASIC be satisfied that a person would, in future, breach a financial services law, but only that the relevant decision maker has reason to believe that he or she would do so. That is applicable to the threshold in the NCCP Act provision.
  3. As stated in Callychurn and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2019] AATA 4600 at [215] to [216]:
    The issue for the Tribunal is whether there is reason to believe [the Applicant] is likely to contravene credit legislation in the future. This is not a subjective analysis and “the belief must rest on objective facts that would induce the relevant state of mind in a reasonable person”: Power v Hammond [2006] VSCA 25 (22 February 2006) at [106]. As noted in Story v National Companies and Securities Commission (1988) 13 NSWLR 661 at [674] the ordinary construction of these words is “that the person in question had the relevant belief and there were reasonable grounds to cause or cause for that belief” but, according to Callinan J in Daniels Corporation International Pty Ltd v Australian Competition And Consumer Commission [2002] 213 CLR 245 at [130], this connotes a “relatively low threshold.”

    As observed by DP Forgie in Superannuation Warehouse Australia Pty Ltd v Australian Securities And Investments Commission [2019] AATA 88 at [56] when considering whether an entity is “likely to contravene”, the issue is not whether the entity will do so, which requires a degree of certainty is not required under the legislative test. As DP Forgie noted, and the Tribunal accepts, the ordinary meaning of the word “likely” in this context is “probable”.

Banning orders

  1. In Howarth and Australian Securities and Investment Commission [2008] AATA 278, the Tribunal emphasised the importance of the protection of the public and the reinforcement of the integrity of the financial services industry by the imposition of banning orders, principles which are equally applicable to the credit industry. The banning power in the NCCP Act has been applied in the same manner as the banning power in the Corporations Act in a number of previous cases before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT): see Sullivan and Anor and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2013] AATA 592; Nguyen and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2017] AATA 920; George and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2014] AATA 167; Sahay and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2016] AATA 583; Legat and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2019] AATA 685; JSKN and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2017] AATA 818.
  2. In Sullivan and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2013] AATA 592, the AAT stated at [20] - [21]:
    ... Credit providers play an important role in the economy, and rogues can undermine confidence in the financial system and hurt individual consumers. The poor and unsophisticated are especially vulnerable. The legislative scheme, and banning orders in particular, are designed to protect consumers and promote efficiency and confidence in the integrity of credit providers.

    ... The regulatory system cannot function if individual participants are dishonest, and behaviour that suggests an individual is not committed to being open and honest with the regulator is of grave concern. I think a banning order is the only appropriate course; I do not have any confidence Mr Sullivan would rapidly and diligently address the shortcomings that have been identified if he were merely suspended. A banning order will protect the public from the risk he poses.

  3. A banning order provides deterrence not only to the Applicant (specific deterrence) but also to others who are involved in the credit industry or who may become involved (general deterrence).
  4. The primary purpose of the banning power in the Corporations Act is to protect the public from a person’s future conduct: see HIH Insurance Ltd and HIH Casualty and General Insurance Ltd; Re: ASIC v Adler [2002] NSWSC 483; (2002) 42 ACSR 80 (Adler). Notions of general and personal deterrence are also relevant, as are the personal circumstances of the person: Rich v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2004] HCA 42; (2004) 220 CLR 129 (Rich) at [52].
  5. In Rich, the High Court considered the principles relevant to the application of provisions in the nature of banning orders. At [48] to [49], McHugh J referred to the principles set out by Santow J in Adler and stated, among other things, that:
    Disqualification orders are designed to protect the public from the harmful use of the corporate structure or from use that is contrary to proper commercial standards.

    The banning order is designed to protect the public by seeking to safeguard the public interest in the transparency and accountability of companies and in the suitability of directors to hold office.

    Protection of the public also envisages protection of individuals who deal with companies, including consumers, creditors, shareholders and investors.

    The banning order is protective against present and future misuse of the corporate structure.

    The order has a motive of personal deterrence, though it is not punitive.

    General deterrence is an object of the legislation.

    In assessing the fitness of an individual to manage a company, it is necessary that the individual have an understanding of the proper role of the company director and the duty of due diligence that is owed to the company.

  6. ASIC’s Regulatory Guide RG 218 provides guidance for ASIC in applying the NCCP Act’s powers to make banning orders pursuant to s 80 of the NCCP Act, in a similar manner to ASIC’s Regulatory Guide RG 98 with respect to banning orders under the Corporations Act. In Karamian, President Kyrou determined not to apply RG 98, but had regard to it as part of the material before the Tribunal, identifying the purpose of banning orders and the factors relevant to making such orders at [149] to [152].
  7. Adopting that same reasoning in relation to the application and scope of banning orders under the NCCP Act, the Tribunal will have regard to the guidance included in RG 218, but that guidance is not determinative.

ISSUES

  1. The facts, as outlined below, are largely undisputed. At issue in these proceedings is whether the Banning Order is the correct or preferable decision on the evidence before the Tribunal. In order to determine that issue, the Tribunal must consider whether:
    (a) there is reason to believe that the applicant is not a ‘fit and proper person’ to engage in one or more credit activities, or to perform one or more functions as an officer of another person who engages in credit activities, or control another person who engages in credit activities (s 80(1)(f) and s 37B of the NCCP Act);

    (b) there is reason to believe that the applicant is likely to contravene credit legislation (s 80(1)(e)(i)); and

    (c) if the answer to (a) and/or (b) is yes, should a banning order be made against the applicant (s 81 of the NCCP Act), and if so, on what terms and for what duration?

Financial Services Ban

  1. In addition to the Banning Order, on 18 April 2024 a delegate of ASIC decided to permanently ban the Applicant from providing any financial services, controlling (whether alone or in concert with one or more other entities) an entity that carries on a financial services business, and performing any function involved in the carrying on of a financial services business (including as an officer, manager, employee, contractor or in some other capacity), under subsections 920A and 920B of the Corporations Act (Financial Services Ban). ASIC relied on the following grounds for the Financial Services Ban, that it had reason to believe the Applicant was:
    (a) Not a fit and proper person pursuant to s 920A(1)(d) of the Corporations Act;

    (b) Likely to contravene a financial services law, pursuant to s 920A(1)(f) of the Corporations Act; and

    (c) Not adequately trained or competent, pursuant to s 920A(1)(da) of the Corporations Act.

  2. The Financial Service Ban was imposed on the basis of the same conduct as the Banning Order, for the same reasons as they applied to the Corporations Act.
  3. During the hearing of the Extension Application it became apparent that the Applicant did not realise that she had not sought review of the Financial Services Ban. In the Tribunal’s reasons for decision in granting the extension of time to file the Review Application, the Tribunal noted, in Baaini and Australian Securities and Investment Commission (Practice and procedure) [2024] ARTA 296 at [52]:
    The Applicant at the interlocutory hearing indicated she was unaware that only one decision of 18 April 2024 had been provided to the Tribunal in her application for review. It may be the Applicant seeks to rectify this by challenging that second decision in the future, or she may choose not to take this path. This issue goes to the practical scope of the outcome of any decision, but in the Tribunal’s view does not change the ability to seek review or impact the reasonable prospects of success in relation to that particular decision.
  4. The Applicant did not file any application for review of the decision relating to the Financial Services Ban. The decision resulting in the Financial Services Ban is therefore not before the Tribunal for review in these proceedings.
  5. Nevertheless, the fact that the Applicant is subject to the Financial Services Ban is relevant to the Tribunal’s consideration in these proceedings, not only because the same conduct underpinned the reasons for both decisions. The Financial Services Ban is also a mandatory consideration for the Tribunal, pursuant s 37B(2)(b)(ii) of the NCCP Act, as to whether the Applicant is a ‘fit and proper person’.
  6. Additionally, in the Reviewable Decision ASIC’s delegate found that the Applicant’s conduct as a financial adviser or relevant provider was relevant to determining whether she is fit and proper under s80(1)(f) of the NCCP Act, on the basis that
    ...the provision of financial services is analogous to the provision of credit services in that both are positions of trust that involve assisting individuals to manage and deal with their money. Both regimes also contain similar obligations. For example, to not engage in misleading or deceptive conduct and licensing requirements for participants in the industry.
  7. This is relied on by ASIC as being “any other matter ASIC considers relevant” pursuant to s 37B(2)(k) of the NCCP Act.

FACTS

  1. The following relevant facts, in addition to those outlined at paragraphs 1 to 6 above, were undisputed and were supported by the documents before the Tribunal.
    (a) In addition to being a financial advisor, the applicant is the sole director and secretary of SMR Loans Pty Ltd. SMR Loans Pty Ltd ACN 657 809 642 (SMR Loans) was incorporated on 4 March 2022, and was a credit representative (representative number 540546) of BLSSA Pty Ltd (BLSSA), which is an Australian Credit Licence holder (licence number 391237), from 7 June 2022 to 4 September 2023. The applicant was a credit representative of BLSSA from 10 June 2022 to 4 September 2023.

    (b) The Applicant has stated that she never provided any credit services to clients of BLSSA and that her credit representative status was supposed to be terminated in September 2022, but was not.

    (c) AAN Wealth holds Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) 517548, which authorises it to carry on a financial services business to provide financial product advice under the Corporations Act. AAN Wealth provided financial services to clients through its corporate authorised representative, Superannuation Advice Australia Pty Ltd (SAA).

    (d) On 30 March 2023, the Applicant created an online account with ACER, the entity which administers the FASEA Exam, to book to sit the FASEA Exam being held in May 2023.

    (e) On 3 April 2023, ACER sent the Applicant an email confirming that she had successfully enrolled to sit the FASEA Exam session in May 2023. The email noted that the booking was not confirmed until she had completed payment for the exam on the ASIC website. On 24 April 2023, ACER sent the applicant a payment reminder email. The email noted that ACER’s records suggest that she had yet to make payment for the Exam. It also noted in bold that “[i]f payment for the exam is not received by 25 April 2023, your exam session will be cancelled.” The email was marked ‘urgent.’

    (f) The Applicant did not pay for the exam and was subsequently advised that her exam session had been cancelled.

    (g) The Applicant never sat or passed the FASEA Exam.

    (h) As a result of not having passed the FASEA Exam, the Applicant was not qualified to provide personal advice to retail clients. Despite this, the Applicant gave personal advice to 121 retail clients of AAN Wealth whilst knowing she was not authorised to do so.

    (i) On 20 June 2023 the RM of AAN Wealth contacted the Applicant asking that she send through all information about her FASEA Exam result. The Applicant responded to him by text message stating that she needed to obtain it from an old laptop. The RM followed up with the Applicant later that day but received no response. The Applicant did not “clock out” from work that day and failed to show up to work the next morning. The RM tried to contact her but was unable to make contact.

    (j) On 22 June 2023, the Applicant emailed her resignation due to ‘personal issues’ to AAN Wealth.

    (k) In her submissions to ASIC prior to the Banning Order, the Applicant did not make any reference to, or admit that, she had falsified the FASEA Exam certificate, despite being informed of this allegation.

    (l) In a SFIC provided to the Tribunal dated 26 May 2025, the Applicant admitted, “I acknowledge that I falsified my certificate”.

IS THE APPLICANT A FIT AND PROPER PERSON?

  1. An assessment of the Applicant’s fitness and propriety must be made in the context of the vocation to which that requirement applies, being her engagement in “credit activities” within the meaning of the NCCP Act, within its regulatory scheme: Sahai and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2021] AATA 3041 at [48]- [49], Davies v Australian Securities Commission [1995] FCA 1496; (1995) 59 FCR 221, at 232-234; Frugtniet v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2022] AATA 295.
  2. In Frugtniet 2016, the Court identified that one of the purposes of the licencing scheme contained in the NCCP Act is to ensure credit activities:
    “...are performed by persons who are fit and proper to perform them, in order that the exposure and vulnerability of consumers is lessened.”
  3. In Frugtniet v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2022] AATA 295 at [45] the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT) described the relevant context as:
    Importantly for present purposes, we are satisfied that in the context of assessing whether a person is a fit and proper person to engage in credit activities, honesty, knowledge and ability are the attributes of concern raised by the statutory test. A person who is not honest is unlikely to be fit to discharge the burdens of a regulated profession or vocation and is not fit to engage in credit activities.
  4. At [85] the AAT continued:
    Honesty is essential in the conduct of credit activities, including when acting as a mortgage broker. It is essential that financial institutions can rely on documents prepared by mortgage brokers as honestly reflecting the credit worthiness of loan applicants. It is essential that mortgage brokers can resist the temptation to inflate the credit worthiness of an applicant in order to secure a commission from a successful loan application. The dishonest inclusion of income or assets in loan documentation can endanger the broader credit system if individual borrowers are happy with the service provided.
  5. The undisputed evidence is that the Applicant falsified a FASEA Exam certificate, provided that to her employer, lied about the date on which she supposedly passed the exam, and provided advice to 121 retail clients when not authorised to do so, knowing that she was not authorised to do so. This demonstrates serious dishonesty and a complete disregard for compliance with legislation.
  6. To compound that dishonesty, the Applicant did not admit that she had falsified the FASEA exam certificate until she provided evidence and submissions to the Tribunal in these proceedings, more than 2 years after the dishonest conduct. She attempted to continue the dishonesty and deception by emailing FASEA on 20 June 2023 to request a copy of her non-existent certificate, and admitted under cross-examination to her intention to continue to deceive others, including ASIC, that she had successfully passed the FASEA Exam.
  7. Whilst acknowledging her conduct was “wrong”, she then sought to minimise her conduct by describing it as a “mistake” or an “error”, in circumstances where she actively put in effort to create a fake certificate to deceive her employer and persisted in ongoing dishonest conduct. She had many opportunities to inform her employer of her wrongdoing and remedy the situation but chose not to do so. When her employer discovered her deception, she chose to avoid responding to them and resigned. Her conduct demonstrates ongoing dishonesty, a lack of integrity, and a refusal to take accountability for her actions.
  8. The Applicant’s minimisation of her conduct and her deference to the length of time she spent obtaining her tertiary qualifications and her previous compliance, demonstrate that she does not have genuine remorse for her conduct, or insight into its seriousness. Her past qualifications and training would have made her well aware of the importance of the regulatory regimes in the financial services industry and credit industry, and her compliance with her regulatory obligations in engaging in credit activities and providing clients with financial advice. Her claimed history of having no previous compliance issues may simply demonstrate that she has not previously been caught in any non-compliance, as her conduct in relation to the FASEA Exam requirement demonstrates she is unlikely to volunteer any shortcomings, or to actively address an employer’s concerns once raised with her.
  9. The Applicant claimed her conduct was out of character. There is no supporting evidence before the Tribunal to support a claim of good character. Her conduct in relation to the falsified FASEA Exam certificate, her failure to take accountability and her ongoing dishonesty and attempts at maintaining that dishonesty demonstrate that it is more likely that her conduct was, in fact, in character, not out of character.
  10. The Applicant’s conduct took place in the context of her providing financial advice, and not in the context of her engaging in credit activities. Nevertheless, her conduct demonstrates that she is not a fit and proper person to engage in credit activities. Both roles, in the wider financial services industry, require honesty, integrity, and compliance with legal obligations, which the Applicant has demonstrated she significantly lacks. Her non-compliance with one regulatory statutory regime, under the Corporations Act, demonstrates the likelihood of her non-compliance in another regulatory statutory regime, under the NCCP Act. The fact that she has been banned under the Corporations Act is also a relevant consideration to be taken into account pursuant to s 37B(2)(b)(ii) of the NCCP Act, and I have done so.
  11. The Applicant’s circumstances divulge no other matters relevant to the considerations identified in s 37B(2) of the NCCP Act, in considering her fitness and propriety pursuant to s 80(1)(f) of the NCCP Act.
  12. The Tribunal finds that there is reason to believe the Applicant is not a fit and proper person within the meaning of s 80(1)(f) of the NCCP Act.

IS THE APPLICANT LIKELY TO CONTRAVENE ANY CREDIT LEGISLATION?

  1. In relation to whether the Applicant was likely to contravene credit legislation, ASIC submitted:
    Here what we’ve seen is that prior to taking the job, Ms Baaini took no steps to inform herself of what the statutory requirements were for her, in terms of picking back up her employment. Once she found out about the FASEA exam, it sounds like she went no further than a quick google to find out what the FASEA exam was. It doesn’t seem like there’s been any diligent investigation into the obligations on her. What it suggests is someone who doesn’t pay much regard to statutory requirements. And similarly, the willingness to mislead the regulator speaks to that, and gives ASIC reason to believe, and we say therefore the tribunal reason to believe, that she is likely to contravene credit legislation.
  2. The evidence before the Tribunal supports that submission. On that basis and the Tribunal’s findings at [63] to [67] above, particularly the findings at paragraph [65] of the Applicant’s lack of insight and lack of accountability, the Tribunal has reason to believe that the Applicant is likely to contravene any credit legislation within the meaning of s 80(1)(e)(i) of the NCCP Act.

BANNING ORDER

  1. Having found that the Tribunal’s discretion is enlivened by having reason to believe that the Applicant is not a fit and proper person pursuant to s 80(1)(f) and is likely to contravene credit legislation pursuant to s 80(1)(e)(i), the next issue for determination is whether the Tribunal’s discretion to impose a banning order should be exercised.
  2. The Applicant accepted that a banning order was an appropriate consequence for her conduct. The Respondent contended that the Applicant’s conduct, its impact on her employer and ASIC, and the need for specific and general deterrence, supported the imposition of a banning order.
  3. I agree with the Respondent’s submissions. There is a strong need for general deterrence, in part to protect the public from unauthorised financial advisers impersonating as authorised financial advisers. In circumstances where it appears relatively simple for the Applicant to have falsified her FASEA Exam results and have those accepted by the financial services industry, a strong general deterrent is necessary to promote confidence in the regulatory system. More importantly, in these proceedings a ban is justified in order to deter similar conduct by others in the credit industry. It is not acceptable for someone who has engaged in conduct similar to the Applicant, and who has also been banned from being a financial adviser, to evade a ban in the credit industry under the NCCP Act.
  4. Having regard to the relevant factors as outlined at [36] to [41] above, it is appropriate that the Tribunal exercise its discretion to impose a banning order on the Applicant under s 81 of the NCCP Act.
  5. In determining the scope of the Banning Order, there were no submissions made by the Applicant suggesting that a narrower scope of orders was more appropriate in the circumstances.
  6. The Tribunal in Karamian stated:
    In my opinion, in the circumstances of this case, it is necessary for the banning order to be comprehensive...

    The key reason for this conclusion is that the applicant’s conduct which has enlivened the power to make a banning order ... does not relate to misconduct in relation to a particular financial product, lack of familiarity with a particular regulatory process or shortcomings in his capacity to advise any particular class of clients. Rather, the misconduct involved serious and sustained dishonesty that went to the heart of the applicant’s legal authority to advise retail clients. Knowing that his Exam result meant that he would not be able to advise retail clients after 1 January 2022, the applicant engaged in a lengthy and continuous course of deliberate, serious and dishonest conduct which was predominantly motivated by self-interest. That conduct was contrary to the interests of his clients and breached the trust of those clients and his colleagues.

  7. Similarly in these proceedings, the evidence demonstrates that the broadest scope of order is appropriate, considering the fundamentally dishonest conduct the Applicant engaged in as an employee under supervision.
  8. The length of a banning order is, of course, dependent on the facts of the case. As the Tribunal noted in Norman and Tax Practitioners Board [2021] AATA 848 at [142]:
    “Each matter must be assessed objectively based on its own facts. DP McCabe cautioned in Ridden v Tax Practitioners Board [2020] AATA 422 (“Ridden”), at [40] that “[t]here is some danger in rifling through reported cases in search of comparisons”. The Tribunal agrees with this notion to the extent that other cases should not be slavishly followed. The Tribunal must exercise independent discretion based on the unique facts before it. It is also important that there is consistency in Tribunal decisions. Although the Tribunal is not bound by other decisions, it can be guided by them, with a view to ensuring that parties are treated equally and fairly and providing a form of precedence that the public can take notice of. In Drake Brennan J (as President of the AAT) noted (at 643) that consistency with comparable cases and decisions is “[o]ne of the factors to be considered in arriving at the preferable decision”.
  9. The Applicant’s submissions were focussed on the length of the Banning Order being too severe, and the impact on herself and her ability to earn an income in the financial services and credit industry. I accept that the Applicant’s exclusion from the industry in which she qualified, trained, and previously had experience significantly negatively impacts her, as stated by her:
    Just because it’s actually affected my life, to be honest. It’s, I can’t do anything, pretty much. Like, I’ve, I’ve studied to become a financial advisor. I can’t work in the industry, even in doing something else, administration, I can’t do. So a permanent banning has affected my life, yes. Yes. I can’t provide a source of income. I’m not in what I know to, how to, what to do. It’s affected my life. It’s affected my mental state. It’s affected my marriage. It’s affected my life, my whole life, yes.
  10. The Applicant’s ability to earn an income, the financial impact of the imposition of a banning order, and the effect on the Applicant’s personal life and mental health are considerations which the Tribunal must take into account. However, beyond the Applicant’s assertions (as expressed above), there was no evidence provided to the Tribunal in support of those statements. I give some limited weight, as a result, to those assertions in the context of the Tribunal’s consideration.
  11. In addition, there was no evidence provided to the Tribunal in support of the Applicant’s other assertions of being of good character, and her contention that her conduct was out of character. As discussed above, in the absence of such evidence I do not give this consideration any weight.
  12. As discussed in Karamian I am not bound by RG 218 in making the correct or preferable decision as to the appropriate length of the banning order. It is nevertheless appropriate for the Tribunal to consider a banning order in terms of increasing length commensurate with increasing seriousness of the subject conduct. RG 218 contains the following factors indicative of an appropriate period of ban being 10 years or more, including permanent banning orders:
    Dishonesty and intent to defraud;

    Continued, knowing and wilful contraventions of the law and disregard of legal obligations;

    Causing a large financial loss or making a large financial gain;

    Previous contraventions of the law;

    Serious incompetence and irresponsibility;

    A likelihood that the person will engage in similar contravening conduct in the future;

    Significant adverse impact on consumer confidence.

  13. Examples of such conduct were stated to include:
    Misappropriation of client funds or otherwise engaging in fraud or theft;

    Falsification, concealment or deliberate destruction of records required to be kept;

    Engaging in a pattern of persistent contraventions that indicates systemic failure or a general lack of understanding of and regard for compliance

  14. The Applicant’s conduct does not meet each and every one of the factors identified in RG 218 as justifying a banning order of 10 years or more. Excluding the employment income she obtained from AAN Wealth/SAA and that they conversely paid, there is no evidence that the Applicant’s conduct caused any particular individual, including any of the 121 retail clients, a large financial loss, or that she (or anyone else) gained financially from her deception, beyond her income. There is no evidence of serious incompetence in the advice she provided to the retail clients, beyond that she was unauthorised to give that advice.
  15. However, the evidence before the Tribunal establishes that the Applicant’s conduct falls within those parameters of a Banning Order of 10 years or more duration, being the most serious of conduct. This is because there was continuing dishonesty, continued and knowing contraventions of the law and disregard of her legal obligations, and because her lack of insight is indicative of a high likelihood that the Applicant will engage in contravening conduct in the future. The fact that an unlicensed unauthorised individual was able to be employed by an AFSL holder and provide personal advice to over 120 retail clients in a period of several weeks before being discovered, would have a significant adverse impact on consumer confidence.
  16. There is a significant difference between a Banning Order of 10 years’ duration, or one with a foreseeable end point, and a permanent Banning Order. In Karamian the Tribunal considered an analogous set of circumstances, in which an individual falsified his examination results in order to be registered as a financial adviser, and then worked as a financial adviser providing advice to retail clients until his deception was discovered. That individual also sought to deceive ASIC by sending through his falsified results when an inquiry was made by ASIC to his employer. During the period of deception, that individual provided advice to 11 retail clients contrary to his actual authorisation to do so, a vast number less than the Applicant in these proceedings.
  17. ASIC permanently banned the Applicant in Karamian under the Corporations Act from involvement in the financial services industry. That ban was reduced to a period of seven years by the Tribunal, in circumstances where the Applicant was able to demonstrate real remorse for his conduct (and not just the impact it had on him), had informed his supervisor prior to the imposition of the Banning Order, and had taken accountability for his actions and sought to rectify them. The Applicant in those proceedings provided the Tribunal with extensive written and oral evidence of his circumstances, and provided extensive character references and medical evidence to the Tribunal.
  18. Similarly to the factual circumstances in Karamian, the fundamental nature of the Applicant’s conduct in these proceedings, and her continued dishonesty, her failure to admit her falsification of the FASEA exam certificate, failure to take accountability for her actions, and failure to demonstrate insight into the seriousness of her conduct, puts her conduct in the most extreme or severe category of conduct warranting a banning order.
  19. In contrast to Karamian, however, the Applicant in these proceedings has not provided the Tribunal with supporting evidence of her character, medical explanation for her conduct, or evidence demonstrating insight into her conduct, real remorse, or indication of possible or likely rehabilitation with respect to her understanding of, and compliance with, her statutory obligations. The Tribunal therefore has no confidence that the Applicant would be, in the future, able or likely to rectify these shortcomings.
  20. In the circumstances, the evidence before the Tribunal supports the imposition of a permanent banning order under s 80 and 81 of the NCCP Act.
  21. The correct or preferable decision is therefore to affirm the Respondent’s Reviewable Decision.

DECISION

  1. Pursuant to s 105(a) of the ART Act, the Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
