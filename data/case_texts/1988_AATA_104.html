REASONS FOR DECISION

30 March 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. The Applicant, whom I shall refer to as Quatro, is the son of a couple who many years ago migrated to these shores from a home close to the Mediterranean Sea. Having arrived in Australia the parents came to reside and work in a regional city far removed from the sea. They prospered there. In the mid-1950's the family came to Sydney. The mother, who gave evidence and who at the time of hearing was in her eighty-first year, still resides in Sydney. Her late husband died in 1974. The couple reared four children: three boys and a girl. Of those children the first-born Primo, (born 1928), and Quatro (born 1944) play significant roles. Both gave evidence.
  2. When he left school Quatro undertook an apprenticeship as an electrician and for many years after completing his qualifications he was employed by strangers. In 1969 he married. Following his marriage he and his wife lived with his parents, as he had done prior to his marriage. At that time his wife was working. In November 1970 their first child was born. The mother ceased employment prior to the birth and has not been employed at any material time since. In due course the young couple were able to purchase a suburban property which they rented out while continuing to live with Quatro's parents. Further children were born during 1972 and 1976.
  3. By 1971 Primo, who was then over 40 years of age, was very active in business and well on the way towards a position of substantial wealth. In 1971 Primo arranged, through the vehicle of a company, to purchase a bed-and-breakfast establishment in suburban Sydney. It provided accommodation at the lower end of the market, principally for single, elderly persons. Primo's hope was that the premises could be substantially changed in character and upgraded. He offered Quatro employment managing the premises and Quatro and his wife were to have the right to occupy portion of the premises. Quatro and his wife resided there until December 1972 when they purchased the home at which they were to reside at all material times thereafter. Thereafter Quatro declared to the Commissioner that, with the exception of a partnership share of profit and small amounts of dividend income commencing in 1976, his only source of income was as an employee of one or more of several companies controlled by Primo.
  4. During 1978 the Commissioner commenced an investigation into the taxation affairs of Quatro. In October 1978 an accountant presented to the Commissioner his calculations as to the taxable income of Quatro for the years of income ended 30 June 1971 to 1977. Then, on 12 December 1979 the Commissioner issued amended assessments against Quatro.

As the amended assessments issued long before the Commissioner adopted his current practice in determining additional tax for incorrect returns. The current practice is only to remit the statutory figure of 200% of tax avoided to an assessed figure calculated as a "compensatory rate" of interest -10% of tax avoided for the period of evasion up to 13 February 1983 and 20% per annum thereafter - plus a "culpability factor" calculated as a percentage of the tax avoided (commonly 40%). That being so, it is not surprising to find that in 1979 additional tax was assessed as a constant percentage of tax avoided. The percentage chosen was 100%. Expressed in terms of current practice, and allowing that throughout the period of alleged evasion and for several years thereafter, and indeed for some years after assessment, the interest rate on unpaid income tax was only 10% per annum, it follows that, expressed in terms of present practices, a culpability factor of the order of 20% was imposed in relation to 1971, rising thereafter by approximately 10% per annum to reach a culpability rate of the order of 80% per annum in relation to the 1977 amended assessment.

  1. In the proceedings before me Quatro has not sought to persuade me that all of the amended assessments ought to be set aside. He has sought only to have the assessments of taxable income substantially reduced and to have additional tax reduced appropriately.
  2. Following the issue of the amended assessments on 5 February 1980 objection to the assessments was made on behalf of Quatro. Nothing turns on the terms of those objections. The Commissioner acted promptly and wholly disallowed the objections by notice of 18 March 1980. The Applicant responded promptly (as it was essential that he should) and requested on 15 April 1980 that the decisions of the Commissioner disallowing his objections be referred for independent review. The requests were not promptly complied with. It was only on 8 December 1986 (more than six years later) that the Commissioner complied with the request. No explanation was forthcoming as to the reason for that gross delay. The matters came on for hearing before this Tribunal in February 1988.
  3. When the amended assessments in question issued, by their terms it was claimed that the Applicant was then indebted to the Commissioner in the sum of $210,556: all due for payment on 14 January 1980. No evidence was placed before me as to any later assessments. On 29 April 1980, the Applicant's accountant caused $5,684-20 to be paid to the Commissioner, that being the amount which he considered would be due if appropriate amendments were made to the assessments in issue. In 1983, following the passage of legislation increasing the rate of interest payable in relation to unpaid tax, and after making a practical judgment as to the likely outcome of the already long dormant request for reference, a further $30,000 was paid to the Commissioner on behalf of the Applicant. The evidence was that no other amount has since been paid.
  4. When the notices of assessment were delivered to the Applicant he was only notified that "as a result of an investigation of your income tax affairs for the years of income ended 30 June 1971 to 1971 (sic) have been amended" and of the amount of each increase in assessment. The want of detail prompted a request of 15 April 1980 for information. It accompanied the formal objections. That letter was acknowledged by the Commissioner's letter of 9 May 1980 stating "Your letter dated 15 April 1980 has been received and will be attended to as soon as possible".

12. In due course the accountant was provided with a list of "unexplained withdrawals" which enabled him to give consideration to the withdrawals from accounts as to which the Commissioner had not been satisfied. The Commissioner's list identified 18 withdrawals from Building Society accounts out of a total of about 40 withdrawals. The accountant examined the records available to him as to withdrawals, and other records available to him as to the deposit and/or application of moneys, and as a result prepared and, at some time in 1983, had delivered to the Commissioner a document in which he sought to explain nearly all of the items referred to. At the same time, as a result of a different enquiry, he provided an explanation in relation to other matters.

13. The explanations then presented, and as presented in the course of the hearing before me, were substantially that the Commissioner had failed to take into account:-

(a) the significance of transactions by way of "gift" and/or "loan" between members of the family, and especially so as between Primo and Quatro;

(b) transfers of funds between known accounts, and
  • (c) funds withdrawn which were applied to known purposes.

Before embarking upon a consideration of the evidence in relation to those transactions it is appropriate to make findings in general terms as to relationships within the family.

  1. I am satisfied that the relationship between the parents and their children was a close and trusting one. I am also satisfied that the relationship between Primo and Quatro was a close and trusting one, but with Primo being dominant. After all, as at 1971, Primo was already successful as a businessman; was on the way to greater prosperity; was about 43 years of age; and was some 16 years older than Quatro. As eldest son, he was also the principal adviser to his parents in financial matters. On the other hand, in 1971, Quatro was a 27 year-old married man with one child; a tradesman employed by others on wages; and a man still residing with his wife and child in the home of his parents.
  2. The mother gave evidence. I accept it in its substance, but only so far as it went. Although at the time of the hearing she was in her eighty-first year, I find that her evidence was not significantly affected by the passage of time since these problems arose, or by her advancement in years. I am well satisfied, upon her evidence, that she understood that Primo and Quatro were working together; that she regarded them as a unit; and that she was not concerned to distinguish between them. She claimed to have no knowledge of their affairs. I accept that, but I only take it as meaning she had no detailed knowledge of their affairs or of any arrangements between them. She acknowledged, but only reluctantly, that she thought they ran "some clubs in King's Cross", but acknowledged no more than that.
  3. I am satisfied that Primo had very extensive interests in New South Wales and interstate; and that in Sydney those interests were concentrated in the King's Cross area. Quatro was able to identify a schedule of (inter alia) twenty-three companies as businesses with which Primo was related. Those business endeavours also brought the brothers into association with a third party who, assuming there is no confusion of identity, is regarded by the media as one of the colourful identities of Sydney. The identity of that person is quite irrelevant to the issues before me, and it was not suggested otherwise. Similarly, I drawn no inference from the fact that it was often said that records and documents had been with "another Commonwealth authority". I was not told why, and I will not guess. It is an undisputed characteristic of most of those businesses that they involved the turnover of substantial amounts of cash.

17. Initially at least, Primo and Quatro stood in the relationship of master and servant but it was said, and I accept it, that at all times Primo was a man of generous disposition towards his brother, and also to other members of the family. With the passage of time Primo transferred to Quatro a half interest in his major company. That was on 30 May 1974. I am not persuaded that that was occasioned wholly by altruism. I am satisfied that by that date, in practical rather than legal terms, the relationship between the brothers had changed to that of "partners", but with Primo being the senior and dominant partner. That accords with the mother's perception. I accept that that relationship continued until July 1980 when Primo and Quatro agreed that Quatro should go his own way, whereupon Primo bought out the Applicant's interest in their common affairs for $500,000. I also accept that since that date there have been no dealings between the brothers by way of gift or loan.

18. By way of additional background I also make some other introductory findings in relation to matters which will have to be more closely considered later:-

(a) In December 1972 Quatro and his wife purchased a new residence. It became their home after they vacated the lodging house. At the time of the purchase they borrowed $40,000 secured by a mortgage on the property. They did so although at the previous 30 June Quatro had had funds available with Building Societies in his own name in excess of $65,000;

(b) When the present home of the widowed mother was purchased in order to be occupied by her late husband and herself, the family was advised by the accountant that it would be to the advantage of the family if title was taken by a company in which the interests would be held by the parents and the children in equal shares. I accept that that advice was accepted and implemented. It was alleged for the Applicant that the company purchase was funded with loan moneys from the children ($9,250 each), those moneys having been made available to the children by way of gift from the parents. (I will consider that assertion later); and (c) That, in the months prior to the father's death, the mother provided $20,000 from the parents' funds to Quatro which he in time paid to Primo.

  1. Before embarking upon the detailed analysis of the many separate transactions which must be considered, it is appropriate to say that I accept that many dealings between different family members were made quite informally. To that extent and in that sense there was complete trust between the relevant family members. It is for that reason that transactions were not formally documented. Nor were they so carried out or expressed as to conveniently and easily fall into the pigeon holes such as "gift" and "loan" favoured by lawyers. Nor were such transactions as occurred expressed and cast in terms of other legal categories such as "wages", "rewards for service", "interest", and so on.
  2. Against that background it is also appropriate to recognize that, in so far as the moneys in question flowed out of the endeavours of the two brothers, in the ordinary course of events the greater the taxable income of one brother the less would be the taxable income of the other: if an amount was received by Quatro from Primo by way of "loan" rather than by way of assessable income, the taxable income to Primo would tend to be higher. On the other hand, if the same amount was received as a "reward for services", the taxable income of Primo would tend to be reduced.
  3. However, it should be emphasized that the evidence does not enable precise findings to be made as to the origins or business source of much of the money in question. (Interest and dividends which are not in dispute constitute exceptions). It follows that the business source of the moneys in question might as well be the individual efforts of Quatro as any activity in which Primo was involved.
  4. In summary, the findings I have recorded to date establish:

that Quatro had the opportunity to substantially understate income;

that he was engaged in some unidentified types of activity which could have made possible the derivation of substantial taxable income;

that he was a person who was willing to substantially understate his taxable income;

that he did not offer any explanation for the substantial omissions of taxable income which are not disputed;

that he was unwilling to acknowledge activities generating any higher taxable income than that admitted.

23. On the other hand, it does not follow that for those reasons alone his income is as has been alleged by the Commissioner, or that the explanations which have been offered for such items as he sought to explain are untrue. Further, it has to be acknowledged that the explanations so presented are quite possibly true. What has to be determined in relation to each item for which an explanation is offered is whether the possibility of the explanation being true is such that it is appropriate to say that the Tribunal is persuaded that the explanation is more probably true than not. In assessing whether that probability is established regard must be had to all of the evidence, such as it is.

24. When the Commissioner investigated the affairs of the Applicant, his officers established the existence of "assets" (including Bank and Building Society accounts) which they attributed to Quatro. The Commissioner's assessment of taxable income was based on calculations in which the view was taken that the Applicant was the beneficial owner of those assets; and that there were no other liabilities to be taken into account other than those recorded in the Commissioner's calculations. The former proposition was not disputed. However, the latter point is very much in issue in that Quatro contends that in most years he was debtor to his brother in sums greater than have been acknowledged by the Commissioner. Secondly, the Commissioner's officers have assumed that some moneys withdrawn from Building Societies have been so expended as to be either applied in the acquisition of assets not brought to account; or expended without generating any asset. (By way of illustration only I mention as a "possibility" in the former class: the purchase of jewellery the existence of which was not known to the Commissioner: and, as a "possibility" in the latter class, the loss of moneys to a bookmaker, or the gift of moneys to a friend). As to that, the Applicant contends that the moneys so withdrawn from accounts and allegedly unexplained are in fact explained and accounted for by tracing them into other assets or heads of expenditure (such as personal living expenses) which have already been taken into account in the Commissioner's calculations or in reduction of liabilities not yet taken into account in the calculations. Thirdly, the assumption on behalf of the Commissioner has been that increments in wealth are to be in large measure explained by the derivation of assessable income not previously taxed. In addition, Quatro contends that the Commissioner has failed to allow for the receipt of moneys by way of gift.

25. All of the competing contentions stand as "possibilities". The important difference between them is that the Commissioner's contentions will stand in full force and effect unless and until the Applicant persuades the Tribunal to direct the reduction of the assessments by satisfying it that, in some or all respects, his explanation is to be preferred as the more probable. In order that the matters argued before me may be seen in context I set out the relevant figures showing not only the figures on a year-by-year basis but also in aggregate. 26. The Applicant sought to persuade me that the omissions so alleged were excessive. First, he sought to do so by-providing explanations for the "unexplained withdrawals". Thereby he sought to not only indicate what had happened to the moneys withdrawn but also, in order that the explanation might be effective in explaining away the alleged omissions, to establish either that the moneys had been so applied as to be reflected in other aspects of the Commissioner's calculations or that they are to be reflected in liabilities not yet taken into account. To the extent to which he succeeds in that undertaking any reduction effected in any one year will have the consequence of reducing total omissions. 27. Thirdly, the Applicant also pointed to the state of account between his brother and himself as providing a partial explanation for the alleged omissions. The significance of the alleged transactions is quite different to the significance of such findings as there may be in relation to "unexplained withdrawals" not traced to Primo; and as to "gifts". It is common ground that neither brother was indebted to the other as at 30 June 1970. Accordingly, it is not suggested for the Applicant that there has been any error on that account in computing net opening assets. On the other hand, it has been alleged on behalf of the Applicant - and it is not disputed by the Commissioner - that at 30 June 1977 - the last day of the betterment period - Primo was indebted to the Applicant in the sum of $2,000. That it was not disputed by the Commissioner is not surprising because the calculations of the Commissioner brought to account as a closing asset a debt of $20,000 said to be due to Quatro by Primo. Accepting for the moment that the Applicant's allegation is correct, it follows that the net increase in assets during the entire betterment period was $18,000 less than that taken into account by the Commissioner in his calculations. A second important distinguishing characteristic is that, whereas in relation to the earlier items a finding reducing taxable income for one year reduces total omissions for the whole period by the same amount without affecting findings in relation to any other year, that is not so in relation to this item. If the appropriate finding be that the Commissioner erred in failing to take into account as a liability a debt due by Quatro to Primo in the sum of, say, $4,000 at 30 June 1971, it follows that, just as the net increase in assets during that year is reduced by $4,000, so too opening assets for the 1972 year are reduced by the same amount. The consequence then is that, on that account, the net increase in assets for the 1972 year is increased by $4,000, as is the amount which needs to be explained away. The same pattern is then followed in relation to each succeeding year - although the figures may vary. So long as Quatro was indebted to Primo at the close of each year, the appropriate adjustment is to reduce alleged omissions by a specified amount in one year and to increase the omissions which need to be explained by the same amount in the year following. On the other hand when it happens that Quatro came to be a creditor of Primo (as was contended for 30 June 1975) , the appropriate adjustment is to increase the figure to be explained by the amount of the debt and to reduce the amount to be explained in the following year by the same amount. 28. Against that background I now proceed to analyse the available evidence in relation to each item under the three classes of issue which have been identified.

UNEXPLAINED WITHDRAWALS

  1. The accountant gave evidence that, following assessment, the Commissioner identified to him 18 transactions in which moneys were withdrawn from Building Society accounts as to which the Commissioner was not satisfied that they should be excluded from the computation of taxable income. The accountant set out to answer the Commissioner's queries by analysing the available documentary information which might have explained the application of the moneys; and by conferring with Quatro. He proffered his explanation to the Commissioner in 1983 and stood by it in all material respects in the hearing before me.
  2. Quatro gave evidence before me but his evidence was unhelpful. He did not claim to remember why any of the questioned withdrawals was effected or what happened with the moneys so drawn. He acknowledged that he had not maintained any records which would have provided any information in that regard. I find nothing surprising about the lack of memory or records. However, there was one exception. The Applicant did recall that he had lent $2,000 to a person who had been friend from childhood; and that the $2,000 so lent had been repaid, although after the close of the betterment period. His evidence in that regard was confirmed by the evidence of the friend. I accept that there was such a loan of $2,000; that it occurred within the betterment period; and that repayment was effected after the close of the betterment period. That evidence may suffice to explain one of the unexplained withdrawals, but it will have no effect upon the overall assessment, because the effect of the finding is to eliminate from the calculations an "unexplained withdrawal $2,000" and substitute in that year and in each year thereafter an additional asset: "loan $2,000" (cf. ante).
  3. The accountant also gave evidence that, having carried out his own analyses, he conferred with Quatro, who confirmed the soundness of the accountant's analysis. On some matters he received similar confirmation from Primo and his interstate accountant. Although that evidence was plainly hearsay, when earlier giving evidence neither Quatro nor Primo had made any such assertions. Nor had Quatro offered any explanations as to why he had been able to "confirm" the analysis during 1983 but had not been able to do so in 1988. Nonetheless the evidence of the accountant was received. I find that, in confirming the analysis of the accountant, the Applicant was not professing to recall what had happened but rather was recognizing as possible, and even probable, the suggestions being made to him by his accountant based upon an analysis of available records. If the same records as were available to the accountant had been placed in evidence, the same analysis might have been made - laboriously - by the Tribunal. However, its task has been made very much easier by having available the results of the accountant's investigations.
  4. I now proceed to deal with the "unexplained withdrawals" on an item-by-item basis:-

(a) 2 May 1973 ($200):-

Counsel for the Commissioner has conceded that the withdrawal is both sufficiently and effectively explained;

(a) 3 July 1973 $453-82

Counsel for the Commissioner conceded that these items are sufficiently and effectively explained by a deposit of $1,267-81 on 4 July 1973;

(c) 20 March 1974 $564-51

Counsel for the Commissioner has conceded that these withdrawals, identical in amount but from different accounts on the same day, are sufficiently and effectively explained by a deposit of $1,129-02 on 22 March 1974;

(d) 1 February 1977 ($1,990-74):-

Counsel for the Commissioner has conceded that this withdrawal is sufficiently and effectively explained by a deposit of the same amount on 4 February 1977;

(e) 3 May 1971 ($1,000); and 13 December 1973 ($500):-

The accountant was able to demonstrate that both withdrawals could be matched with deposits of equal amount which occurred respectively on 7 May 1971 and 13 December 1973. It is possible that the moneys so deposited were identical with the moneys withdrawn. It is also possible, for example, that the moneys withdrawn were expended on non-deductible items and that the amounts subsequently deposited constituted untaxed assessable income from unknown sources. On balance, I think it more probable than not that the moneys deposited were identical with the amounts withdrawn. Accordingly, the withdrawals are sufficiently and effectively explained.

(f) 2 June 1971 ($1,300): deposit 7 June 1971 ($2,511);

21 February 1973 ($400): deposit 27 February 1973 ($406); and

10 May 1973 ($1,000): deposit 10 May 1973 ($1,012-60):-

The accountant was able to demonstrate that it was possible that the amounts so withdrawn were used to effect the deposits mentioned. Despite the small disparity in amount, I think the explanation is probably correct in relation to the $1,000. To that extent the withdrawal is explained effectively. As to the other items, I am not so persuaded.

(g) 5 June 1973 ($881);

  1. April 1973 ($600); and
  2. June 1973 ($675):-

The accountant was able to demonstrate that portion of the funds in the case of the first and third withdrawals and all of the funds in the other instance might have been used to provide the funds whereby mortgage installment payments of $651.60 were effected. He suggested that the surplus might have been used for housekeeping. In the case of the first item he was able to show that the withdrawal was effected on 5 June 1973 and the payment on 12 June 1973. In the other instances the evidence is that the withdrawals were effected on the same day, but he was not able to identify the dates upon which the payments were effected. Nor was there any evidence available as to whether the amounts were withdrawn by cash or cheque or as to whether the payments were effected by cash or cheque. Nor was there any evidence which would establish an habitual pattern of conduct as to how payment of mortgage instalments was effected. However, on balance, I think it rather more probable that the purpose in withdrawing the moneys was to enable such payments to be effected. Accordingly, to the appropriate extent ($651.60; $600; $651.60) the withdrawals are sufficiently and effectively explained. (h) 25 July 1972 ($2,000): personal loan:-

On balance, I am satisfied that these moneys were probably the moneys used to effect the loan to the friend. To that extent the withdrawal is sufficiently explained. However, as was recognized by Counsel, that finding will not lead to any reduction in the unexplained discrepancies for the simple reason that the evidence was that the loan was not repaid at any time during the betterment period. Accordingly, although it is appropriate to reduce the figure for "unexplained withdrawals" for the year of income ended 30 June 1973 by $2,000, it is also appropriate to increase the assets on hand at the close of that year by the same figure to thereby represent the indebtedness of the friend - a factor not taken into account in the Commissioner's calculations. The finding has no effect upon calculations in relation to any subsequent year. Accordingly, the withdrawal is sufficiently explained but not so as to effect any reduction in the assessment.

(i) 11 January 1971 ($1,000);

3 January 1973 ($478);

3 July 1973 ($1,500); and

20 December 1973 ($400):-

Apart from suggesting that the latter sum might have been used in order to pay some life assurance premiums, the accountant was not able to point to any particular factor taken into account in the betterment calculations which might reflect the application of the moneys so withdrawn. I also note that the $1,500 was withdrawn on the same date as two other amounts equal to the $1,267.81 already considered under sub-para. (b) above. In all the circumstances, and notwithstanding the delay factor, I am not persuaded that the withdrawals have been effectively explained.

(j) 27 June 1972 ($150):-

The accountant was able to demonstrate that deposits to other accounts included in the calculations were made on 27 June 1972 ($140) and 30 June 1972 ($400). He contended that the moneys withdrawn might have been so applied. Alternatively, he suggested that they might have been applied for general housekeeping purposes and therefore be a factor already taken into account in the betterment calculations as to cost of living. As to the latter it may also be that, although the moneys were used on living expenses, it was or had the effect of increasing those expenses beyond the allowance taken into account in the betterment. On balance, I am not persuaded that the moneys were applied as contended for.

(k) 20 December 1972 ($100):-

The accountant was only able to suggest that the moneys might have been portion of a deposit of $200 made to another account on 20 January 1973, or applied to living expenses. For the reasons mentioned in relation to the previous item, I am not persuaded that any reduction is warranted.

(1) 21 June 1973 ($3,367); and 28 June 1973 ($4,248):-

The accountant was not able to demonstrate by reference to financial records to what end these moneys were or might have been applied. His contention was that the moneys had been paid by Quatro to Primo in order to reduce Quatro's then indebtedness. That being so, I shall defer further consideration of these two items until considering the state of account between the two brothers.

Cm) 8 July 1974 ($20,000); and 8 July 1974 ($20,000);

The explanations relate to the account between the brothers. They will be considered later.

(n) 4 March 1977 ($4,716.74 or $7,381.97):-

Upon the evidence before me the figure of $4,716.74 taken into account in the Commissioner's calculations as an "unexplained withdrawal" actually represented the balance in an account at 30 June 1976. On 4 March 1977 that account was closed, but at the date of that closure the credit balance stood at the higher figure of $7,381.97. That sum was withdrawn. The accountant was not able to demonstrate on the documents how the amount so withdrawn was applied, although it is contended that the moneys were paid to Primo. In calculating the state of account between the brothers, the accountant correctly brought to account the higher figure. As with item (1) above, I shall defer further consideration of the matter until considering the state of account between the two brothers. For the moment, it suffices to say that it would appear that there was a clerical error on the part of the Commissioner's officers. As that error favoured the Applicant, I do not propose to consider that circumstance further.

(o) 1 August 1975 ($2,500); and

1 August 1975 ($2,727):-

The accountant was able to demonstrate that on or about that date the Applicant purchased a motor vehicle and it was not disputed that that vehicle was brought into account in the betterment calculations. On balance, I think it probable that the two amounts so withdrawn were applied in the purchase of that asset. Accordingly, the withdrawals are sufficiently and effectively explained.

(p) 18 July 1975 ($21,370);

The explanation relates to the account between the brothers. It will be considered later.

GIFTS FROM PARENTS

33. (a) $9,250; I accept the evidence that the home in which the Applicant's mother presently resides is owned by a company; that that company is beneficially owned by the children, possibly in association with interests formerly or presently held by the parents; that the purchase price was paid from joint funds of the parents - including the net proceeds of sale of the former parental home; that payment was made possible by gifts, or alternatively, loans from the parents to the children which resulted in the children making loan funds available to the company to enable it to effect the settlement. Although the company records as to share allotments and transfers, documents as to the purchase of the residence and funding of that purchase, were not produced in evidence, I am satisfied on balance that there was either a gift or loan to Quatro: in either case sufficient to explain pro tanto the alleged omissions. As the loan account held by the Applicant with the company has been brought into account, it follows that, to the extent of $9,250, the alleged omissions are sufficiently explained in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1972.

  1. (b) $17,000: In the accountant's betterment calculations compiled during 1978 he asserted that, during the year of income ended 30 June 1972, the Applicant had received non-taxable gifts from each of his parents in the sum of $8,500. I am not persuaded that either gift was made or that, in that year of income, the Applicant received such sums from his parents in circumstances such as to leave him liable to them in the sum of $17,000 at the close of that income year. In particular, I observe that the mother gave no evidence of any such payments and Quatro gave no evidence of receiving such sums. Nor was any explanation offered as to why such gifts should have been made at that time to Quatro; or as to the manner in which he put to use any moneys so received.
  2. (c) $20,000; On balance, I accept the evidence that, shortly prior to the death of her husband in October 1974, the mother gave $20,000 to Quatro which he immediately placed on deposit in the joint names of his mother and himself. I also accept that that sum is to be identified with an account which, when closed, had a credit balance of $21,370 (including $1,370 interest); and that the $20,000, if not the $21,370, was transferred to Primo with the full knowledge and support of the mother. I accept that the $20,000 was received by Quatro from his mother in such circumstances as to constitute it as a gift (and thereby to that extent provide a sufficient and effective explanation of alleged omissions in the year of income ended 30 June 1975); or constitute it as a sum for which Quatro was indebted to his mother. (In which case it had the same effect on alleged omissions in that the increased liabilities would reduce the increment in assets to be explained for that year).

THE LOAM ACCOUNT - GENERALLY

36. I now turn to consider the question of the state of account from time to time during the betterment period as between Primo and Quatro. I am satisfied that the brothers were both involved at least in some, and probably many, transactions of which I have no detail. No documents were presented in evidence which were directed to maintaining a record of those dealings. I make no finding as to whether such a record ever existed. I accept that it may be that there was no such record but I do not accept that the brothers were so indifferent as to the state of dealings between themselves as not to know with at least substantial accuracy the state of account between themselves. I accept that initially Primeo was the more financially substantial of the two brothers but I find that by 30 May 1974 Primo had caused a half-interest in his principal company to be transferred to Quatro. What the value of the interest transferred was at that time was not explained but the evidence was, and I accept it, that by 28 July 1980 the half-interest in the same company was of sufficient value for Primo to pay Quatro $500,000 to regain control of that interest. How the sum of $500,000 was fixed on and to what extent Quatro gave credit for previous generosity on the part of Primo was not disclosed.

  1. But that is but one indication of the extent of non-disclosure in this case. I was provided with no detailed evidence about the undertakings of Primo, either in terms of what was being done or of its financial significance. Perhaps even more significantly Quatro gave no explanation of his activities such as constituted a credible account as to how he occupied his time over several years. I do not accept that his only activities were using his technical skills in relation to his brother's projects for renovation of premises and attending to minor clerical and managerial tasks.
  2. I am not persuaded as to what the relationship was which led to a partnership (or alleged partnership) between Quatro and a third party in restaurant operations which was to occasion a claim made on 20 December 1976 that a substantial partnership loss amounting to $11,574 had been incurred in the year of income ended 30 June 1975. I have not been persuaded by any credible evidence as to how Quatro first learned of his status as partner, or of the partnership results, or of the need to amend his income tax return to 30 June 1975. (That return had been prepared by Quatro personally without any reference to any partnership, profitable or otherwise, on 28 October 1975. That resulted in him being assessed without challenge on 4 February 1976 as having derived a taxable income as returned of $8,200).
  3. Nor has there been any attempt to explain how or why it was that over so many years Quatro had substantially understated his income. That understatement needs to be considered at two levels. Firstly, the absence of any explanation as to the omission of income by way of dividends and interest throughout the betterment period. In that period Quatro returned income totalling $51,804 ($42,244 if the claimed partnership loss is taken into account). In the same period he omitted interest income of $9,325 and dividends of $5,855: a total of $15,210. But, secondly, in these proceedings, Quatro has not offered any evidence to suggest that the total omissions amount to anything less than $27,732 and he has offered no explanation as to how that could be so.
  4. It is not disputed that there has been substantial evasion of tax and that that substantial evasion is unexplained. That is a circumstance of considerable significance in evaluating evidence which purports to explain the other alleged omissions. In that regard I observe that I have no particular reason for supposing that the unexplained omissions or any explanation as to them is to be sourced within the family. Further, in so far as it might be sourced in dealings with Primo, I observe that there has been no attempt to establish the capacity of Primo to provide funds to Quatro on a non-deductible basis. Nor has there been any sufficiently persuasive explanation for some of the transactions which are sufficiently unusual to warrant some explanation. It is not necessarily a sufficient explanation to contend that particular things were done on the advice of Primo. The purchase of the family home for Quatro and his wife is an example. When that property was purchased in December 1972, $40,000 was borrowed from the bank. Yet at 30 June 1972 Quatro held over $65,000 to his credit in building society accounts. Why, in those circumstances, there was thought to be any need to borrow was not satisfactorily explained. Nor was there a persuasive explanation as to how it was that Quatro was so promptly able to pay out the mortgage. The payment to Primo was said to have been occasioned by the circumstance that, at all material times, Quatro was substantially indebted to Primo for money lent. If so, it is not unreasonable that Primo might have advised Quatro to borrow on mortgage on a long-term basis in order to ensure that Quatro's funding of his own home would not disadvantage Primo's liquidity. Similarly, it might quite as credibly have been explained on the basis that the borrowing on the security of Quatro's home was to enable Quatro to fund his own activities, whether in partnership or other association with Primo or otherwise. Further, no satisfactory explanation was offered as to why Quatro "repaid" $7,614.72 in June 1983, only to "borrow" $10,000 on 3 July 1983.

PENALTIES

49. The most obvious feature of penalties imposed against Quatro in the circumstances of this case is that they were imposed in accordance with practices which were common in 1979. By those standards penalties were commonly fixed as a percentage of tax avoided with the same percentage being debited in relation to all years despite the fact that the period of avoidance and consequent loss to the community was greater in the earlier years. In this instance the penalties were fixed at the rate of 100% of tax avoided. Accordingly, the longer the evasion was effective the less the cost to the avoider expressed in terms of a percentage per annum. As imposed, the costs in percentage per annum terms ranged from approximately 12 1/2% per annum for the 1971 year to 50% per annum for the 1977 year. Although that imbalance might have been avoided by increasing the penalty percentage as the period of effective evasion lengthened, as at 1979, that rarely occurred.

  1. The next feature to be noted is that in 1979 s.207 of the Act provided for an interest rate of 10% per annum on overdue unpaid tax. That rate changed to 20% per annum from February 1933.
  2. The third feature is that, with effect from February 1983, the Commissioner issued new directions to his staff. He set new standards for their guidance as to the exercise of discretion in remitting the additional tax of 200% of tax avoided imposed by the provisions of s.226 of the Act. Inter alia, those guidelines were directed to achieving greater consistency in the effective rate of penalties expressed as a percentage per annum. It was proposed that that would be achieved by distinguishing two factors to be taken into account. The first was a factor to be expressed in terms of a percentage per annum of tax avoided - the "compensation factor". That was to be at the rate of 10% per annum to 13 February 1983 and 20% per annum thereafter. The second factor was to reflect the seriousness and, in appropriate cases, gravity of the conduct or omissions occasioning the avoidance. To give effect to that it was proposed that in addition to the compensation factor there would be a "culpability factor" which would be expressed as a percentage of tax avoided which would operate regardless of the length of period of avoidance. In his ruling, the Commissioner mentioned a culpability rate of 40% to be increased or reduced according to circumstances. Unfortunately, in practice, the Commissioner's direction to exercise discretion was largely ignored. It seems the 40% culpability factor came to be considered as if it had the force of a "statutory minimum", to be increased in grave circumstances, but seemingly not often, and even then rarely to be increased beyond 50%. As a result, it has been commonplace to find that the 40% culpability factor has been as like to be imposed against a deliberate tax avoider who has repeatedly and frequently avoided his obligations and against persons who have made a one-off error occasioned by ignorance of the facts (the husband in Case U36 87 A.T.C. 266); or by the obscurity of the law and lack of clarity as to the obligations of the taxpayer (Case V14 post); or by simple clerical error [the colonial pensioner in Case U36 (ante)].
  3. I have had occasion to consider current practices in a number of decisions. It suffices for present purposes to say that, where and to the extent that there has been deliberate understatement of income by the simple technique of understating assessable income, I have had no hesitation in finding that the 40% culpability factor is fair and reasonable (Case V17 88 A.T.C. 191 and Case V23 88 A.T.C. 232). However, nothing said in either of those decisions indicates that a 40% culpability factor is or is not considered to be the maximum to be upheld regardless of the circumstances. On the other hand, in cases where the avoidance was not caused by such extreme and obvious conduct, I have held that the 40% culpability factor is excessive (cf. the markedly different circumstances of the seven taxpayers whose circumstances were considered in Case U36 (ante) - in all instances penalties were reduced, sometimes substantially - and the circumstances of the Applicant in Case V14 (88 A.T.C. 170) in which I directed that penalties be wholly remitted.
  4. If the assessment had related to the years of income ended 30 June 1981 to 1987 and penalties been calculated by the norms proclaimed in 1983, the review of penalties would not have been nearly so difficult as it is, relating as it does to a period a decade earlier and the imposition of penalties by reference to then quite different norms.
  5. It is appropriate to say that I take the view that the amount of time which has passed since the assessments issued is not of itself a reason for considering the penalties to be excessive. Further, each imposition of penalty should be reviewed on the basis that it is a separate assessment for a distinct fiscal year. Penalties should not be increased in one year because the penalties imposed in some other years might be thought to be inadequate. However, it is appropriate to apply to each assessment of penalties the more carefully thought out factors reflected in the Commissioner's guidelines of 1983 particularly in so far as they recognize a distinction between the compensation factor and the culpability rate and, by reason of the former, take account of the period of evasion prior to assessment. On the other hand, it is not inappropriate to accept that that compensation rate should be calculated at 10% per annum - the statutory interest rate appropriate to the period prior to assessment and for some years following. On that basis, I have no difficulty in finding that there is nothing unreasonable in the imposition of penalties prior to the 1974 year of assessment. If anything, remissions were too generous in earlier years. As to the later years of assessment I consider an imposition incorporating an interest factor - (10% per annum) and a culpability factor of 50% of tax avoided is more appropriate.

55. As I have said, the fact that remissions might have been too generous in some years does not justify heavier penalties in other years. The assessment system and the review system is based upon each year of assessment. Each needs to be separately considered even to the point where objections in relation to some years may have to be allowed even though the totality of the evidence presented shows that the aggregate assessments over the period of years were not excessive.

56. As a result, I have reached the following conclusions. Firstly, in so far as the assessments of taxable income for the years of income ended 30 June 1971 to 1974 (inclusive) have been reduced, additional tax should fall appropriately with the intent that the penalty rate of 100% of tax avoided should be affirmed. However, in addition, in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1975 and 1976 I have concluded that a penalty at the rate of 100% of tax avoided is excessive and that the imposition of penalties in accordance with the principles propounded by the Commissioner in 1983 (IT 2012 of 25 January 1983 - cf. also IT2206 of 31 October 1985) are to be preferred. In my view, in the circumstances of this case, there are what the Commissioner refers to as "aggravating" factors which need to be recognized. This is not a case in which the omissions can be attributed to simple error, or confusion or misunderstanding. Nor is it a case in which one is persuaded that the omissions occurred notwithstanding the honesty of the taxpayer. In the circumstances, I think it appropriate that additional tax should be fixed in those years by a formula which allows for an interest rate of 10% per annum - the rate current throughout the years of review and at the time of assessment and for some years thereafter - plus 50% of tax avoided.

57, Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal will be that -

(1) in relation to the years of income ended 30 June:-

(a) 1971 - taxable income be reduced by $1,000;

(b) 1972 - taxable income be reduced by $9,250;

(c) 1973 - taxable income be reduced by $3,104;

(d) 1974 - taxable income be reduced by $2,897;

(e) 1975 - taxable income be reduced by $20,000;

(f) 1976 - taxable income be reduced by $5,227.

(2) in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1971-1976 (inclusive) additional tax be reduced in accordance with these reasons for decision;

(3) the amended assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1977 be set aside; and

(4) there be liberty to apply as to the terms of the formal order of the Tribunal.

58. Having reserved liberty to apply I will defer the signing of a final determination at this time.
