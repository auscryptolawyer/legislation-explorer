Sononey Corporation Pty Ltd ATF Sononey Family Trust (Migration) [2019] AATA 6206 (19 September 2019)

Last Updated: 11 February 2020

Sononey Corporation Pty Ltd ATF Sononey Family Trust (Migration) [2019] AATA 6206 (19 September 2019)



DECISION RECORD

DIVISION: Migration & Refugee Division

APPLICANT: Sononey Corporation Pty Ltd ATF Sononey Family Trust

CASE NUMBER: 1700679

HOME AFFAIRS REFERENCE: OPF2016/1545

MEMBER: Lilly Mojsin

DATE: 19 September 2019

PLACE OF DECISION: Perth

DECISION: The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and substitutes a decision not to take one or more of the actions specified in s.140M of the Migration Act 1958.



Statement made on 19 September 2019 at 2:19pm





CATCHWORDS

MIGRATION– sponsorship bared – not notifying an employee’s cessation of employment – breach of r.2.84 – not an intentional breach by the applicant – decision under review set aside



LEGISLATION

Migration Act 1958, s 140

Migration Regulations 1994, rr.2.79, 2.84, 2.89, 2.90

STATEMENT OF DECISION AND REASONS

APPLICATION FOR REVIEW

  1. This is an application for review of a decision made by a delegate of the Minister for Immigration to take an action under s.140M of the Migration Act 1958 (the Act) in relation to the applicant’s sponsorship.
  2. The applicant, Sononey Corporation Pty Ltd ATF Sononey Family Trust, was approved as a standard business sponsor on 21 July 2014, valid until 21 July 2017.
  3. On 23 December 2016, the delegate found circumstances existed to bar the applicant for 6 months from sponsoring more people for Subclass 457 Temporary Work (skilled) visas under s.140M on the basis that it had failed to meet its sponsorship obligations in r.2.89 of the Migration Regulations 1994 (the Regulations).
  4. The applicant appealed that decision to this Tribunal, attaching a copy of the Department decision to the application for review.
  5. The applicant, represented by Chhavi Kumar Sononey, appeared before the Tribunal on 19 September 2019 to give evidence and present arguments.
  6. The Tribunal also received oral evidence from Nitin Gathani, the applicant’s accountant. The Tribunal hearing was conducted with the assistance of an interpreter in the Hindi and English languages.
  7. The applicant was represented in relation to the review by its registered migration agent.

CONSIDERATION OF CLAIMS AND EVIDENCE

  1. The nature of the applicant’s business is a restaurant business. The applicant previously operated out of three locations and registered its business names as Shikara India Restaurant in Clarkson, Mayur Indian Restaurant in Jindalee and Mayur in Yanchep. The applicant has had six nominations and six primary sponsored subclass 457 visa holders approved. The applicant now only operates two restaurants.
  2. On 14 April 2016 the Department conducted an unannounced site visit on the sponsor’s restaurant at 238 Ocean Keys Boulevard, Clarkson, and Western Australia in relation to a nomination application in the occupation of a Cook, for verification purpose.
  3. On 18 April 2016 the Department emailed the applicant’s migration agent, at the commencement of monitoring a letter requesting various records and information in accordance with r. 2.83, regarding its employees Parminder Singh, Naveneet Singh and Jagroop Singh Bhatti.
  4. On 29 April 2016 the Department received the applicant’s response through its accountant. The Department thus became aware that Jagroop Singh Bhatti, one of the applicant’s employees, had ceased employment in March 2015.
  5. A request for further information was made on 1 August 2016 by Notice of Intention to Take Action (NOITTA) under r. 2.96. On 9 September 2016 the Department received a submission and on 29 September 2016, the Department issued the NOITTA to the applicant.
  6. The NOITTA detailed the circumstances in relation to which action was being considered.
  7. The identified circumstances were:
    • Regulation 2.79 - Obligation to ensure equivalent terms and conditions of employment
    • Regulation 2.83 - Obligation to provide records and information to the Minister
    • Regulation 2.84 - Obligation to provide information to Immigration when certain events occur
  8. On 12 October 2016 the Department received the applicant’s submission in response to the NOITTA that included:
    • Two different formats of recording wage information paid to Naveneet Singh for pay period from 6 January to 12 January 2016
    • Payroll advice provided on 29 April 2016 that contained the YTD (running total) wage information of $4 146.16 as at 12 January 2016 (4 weeks of pay). This pay information confirms Naveneet Singh commenced work with the applicant on 9 December 2015.
    • Submission that there was no payroll history for Parminder Singh prior to February 2016.
    • The applicant stated that Parminder Singh commenced employment with the sponsor on 30 December 2015 and provided his payroll advices from pay period 30 December 2015 to 23 March 2016.
  9. On 15 November 2016 the Department sought clarification on whether both sponsored visa holders (Naveneet Singh and Parminder Singh) had any employment break including leave taken since commencement of employment until 31 March 2016. The applicant confirmed that Naveneet Singh did not take any leave but Parminder Singh did take leave during the pay period from 27 January 2016 to 9 February 2016.
  10. The delegate found that
    • Naveneet Singh was underpaid a weeks’ pay and the applicant has breached r. 2.79 regarding Naveneet Singh during the assessment period. The PAYG payment summary statement for year ending 30 June 2016 showed Naveneet Singh’s reportable gross wages was $29,023 (at weekly gross of $1036.54, that equated to 28 weeks). He should have been paid gross $30,059.66 for 29 weeks of pay (from 9 December 2015 to 29 June 2016) if he had not taken any leave since 9 December 2015 until 30 June 2016. The delegate found there was a breach of r.2.79.
    • The applicant did not contest nor deny its failure to notify Jagroop Singh Bhatti’s cessation of employment within the prescribed 28 days of ceasing employment and found the applicant failed to comply with r.2.84.
    • The applicant confirmed that Parminder Singh commenced employment on 30 December 2015 and was paid in arrears on 13 January 2016 for his first week of pay. The statement that there was no payroll history for Parminder Singh until February 2016 was incorrectly provided by his accountant. The delegate found the applicant had not failed the obligations set out in r.2.83 but, in confirming the December 2015 commencement date the applicant admitted to the misrepresentation of Parminder Singh’s actual commencement date. The delegate found that the applicant provided misleading information to monitoring on 9 September 2016, pertaining to Parminder Singh’s commencement of employment date and had breached r.2.90.

Applicant’s Submission to Tribunal

  1. In a submission to the Tribunal dated 11 September 2019 the applicant stated that a request was made to Department to waive the bar imposed on the applicant from sponsoring more people for 457 visas. The bar imposed by the delegate was for six months, ending on 23 June 2017 and this period has now expired.
  2. The applicant sought that the Tribunal set aside the decision by the delegate to impose a sponsorship bar on the applicant and vary it with an ‘Informal Warning-Reminder to Comply Letter’
  3. The applicant claims that significant new evidence or information has come to light which was not available at the time the Department made its decision. The applicant acknowledged that it breached r.2.84 (by not informing Immigration about a 457 employee ceasing employment with the applicant) and the applicant states that it has ensured compliance with this obligation subsequently.
  4. The delegate noted that r. 2.90 circumstances exist in relation to the employee Parminder Singh and concluded that the sponsor has provided misleading information in relation to the commencement date of employment. Relying on an email sent by Malkesh Maru (an employee of the applicant’s accountant and authorized recipient) to monitoring, dated 9th September 2016 that stated there is no payroll history for the employee for the period 30/12/2015 to 05/01/2016, the applicant stated during the monitoring that the information provided by Malkesh Maru was incorrect and had always maintained that Parminder Singh commenced employment in December 2015 and has been paid accordingly. The applicant claims that the information provided to the Department was not ‘purposefully false’ and there was no intention on part of the applicant to mislead. The applicant heavily depends on his accountant and the accountant’s employees to manage his bookkeeping, accounting and any official communication.
  5. The applicant has very limited English language ability. Therefore, he does not fully understand the written responses. The applicant operates 3 separate business units (restaurants) and has separate bookkeeping for each restaurant. Malkesh Maru is not the usual consultant who manages the applicant’s account and therefore was not familiar with the payroll for the applicant. Ms. Prajakta Kalbag is the consultant employee who usually manages the applicant’s account. She was on leave during September 2016. It was a very busy period for their accounting business. Mr. Maru provided the incorrect information because he did not check the information about Parminder Singh’s payroll correctly due to unfamiliarity with the applicant’s accounts coupled with excessive workload.
  6. The information that was sent to monitoring was not verified by the authorized recipient and was not emailed from the nominated email ID noted on the Form 956A. There was no underpayment to Parminder Singh that was identified by the monitoring. The contentious information was requested by the monitoring in order to ascertain whether Parminder Singh had been paid for the period 30/12/2015 to 05/01/2016.
  7. There was no finding made that the provision of false or misleading information had an impact on another person. The applicant has not benefitted in any way by providing the incorrect information, it was a hurried judgment under excessive workload of one of the authorized recipient’s consultants.
  8. There was no intention whatsoever to defraud or deceive DHA. Consequently, the essential element of ‘purposeful falsity’ of the information is lacking and the applicant has not breached r. 2.90.
  9. Regulation 2.79 requires that the Standard Business Sponsor must provide terms and conditions of employment that are no less favorable than those approved at nomination. A standard business sponsor must pay a primary sponsored person the ‘guaranteed annual earnings’ (GAE) approved at the nomination.
  10. The decision letter imposing the sponsorship bar notes that the applicant failed to satisfy regulation 2.79 as it had underpaid one of its 457 employees, Navneet Singh by a week at gross $1036.54 and net $846.54. The accountants investigated further into the matter to detect that Navneet Singh has in-fact been paid in excess one weeks salary of $ 846.54. The salary was paid from the bank account of Shikara restaurant rather than Mayur Yanchep where the employee worked. For the same reason, the consultant at the accountant’s office did not record the payment and hence a pay slip was not generated for that payment and consequently the payment did not reflect in the other documents such as PAYG summary, BAS and Financial statements etc. Navneet Singh was paid two weeks salary of $ 1693.08 from the Shikara bank account.
  11. The accountant Nitin Gathani was also an authorized recipient during the monitoring process. PAYG summary for the 2016 financial year and the BAS for the quarter ended Jun 2016 have been revised and all MYOB records have been rectified and the revised PAYG summary and BAS for quarter ended June 2016 have been lodged with ATO. Therefore the applicant did not breach Regulation 2.79 as Navneet Singh has been paid GAE approved at nomination.
  12. Regulation 2.84 (2) requires the sponsor to notify the Department of any notifiable event such as cessation of employment within 28 days of the primary sponsored person ceasing employment with the sponsor. Jagroop Singh Bhatti ceased employment with the sponsor in March 2015. The Department was notified of this cessation of employment on 29th April 2016. The non-compliance was due to applicant’s lack of knowledge about the sponsorship obligations coupled with difficulties with communication in English. The applicant admits to the breach of r. 2.84 and the applicant has taken the following steps to ensure that the circumstances that led to the adverse information do not recur. The applicant has consulted a registered migration agent, Mrs. Madhuri Patil Sinha (MARN 0640802) to understand its sponsorship obligations in detail. The applicant understands that it is obligated to notify DHA in writing when certain events occur within the prescribed timeframe. The applicant has notified the Department within 28 days of becoming aware that another 457 employee; Navneet Singh ceased his employment with the sponsor.
  13. The applicant claims that one count of breach of r.2.84 is a minor failure and therefore a formal action of ‘Sponsorship Bar’ is not an appropriate sanction and therefore AAT should set aside the decision of the delegate and substitute it with an ‘Informal Warning’
  14. Policy states a failure may be considered minor if all the following apply:
    • It has no detrimental effect on a visa holder or on the Department’s capacity to monitor the sponsor
    • It does not undermine the integrity or intent of the relevant visa program
    • There is no negative impact to Australia or Australian citizens and permanent residents
    • The sponsor immediately rectifies the failure
    • The failure was unintentional.

REASONS AND FINDINGS

  1. The issue in the present review is whether a circumstance for the taking of an action under s.140M (2) of the Act exists and, if so, what the appropriate action was to be taken.
  2. Sections 140K, 140L and 140M of the Act provide for the imposition of sanctions on approved sponsors in certain circumstances.
  3. Under s.140M, if prescribed circumstances exist, the Minister (and the Tribunal on review) may take one or more of the following actions:
    • cancelling the sponsorship approval in relation to a class to which the sponsor belongs;
    • cancelling the sponsorship approval for all classes to which the sponsor belongs;
    • barring the sponsor for a specified period from sponsoring more people under the terms of any existing approval; and
    • barring the sponsor for a specified period from making future applications for sponsorship approval in relation to one or more classes of sponsor.
  4. For these purposes, the circumstances are prescribed in r.2.89 - r.2.94B and include circumstances in which the Minister, or on review, is satisfied there has been: a failure to satisfy a sponsorship obligation; provision of false or misleading information; sponsorship application or variation criteria no longer met; a contravention of the law; unapproved changes to a program; a failure to pay additional security; a failure to comply with certain terms of an agreement; or a failure to pay medical and hospital expenses.
  5. Where a prescribed circumstance has been found to exist, the regulations prescribe criteria that must be taken into account when determining what action, if any, to take: r.2.89 – r.2.94B. These criteria, as they relevantly apply to the circumstances of this case are set out in the attachment to this decision.

Does a circumstance for the taking of an action exist?

  1. The delegate found that the applicant failed to satisfy r.2.89 of the Regulations, that is, the applicant failed to satisfy a sponsorship obligation because the applicant had breached r.2.79, r.2.84 and r.2.90.
  2. In regard to Parminder Singh, the Tribunal accepts the evidence of the applicant, that he heavily depends on his accountant and the accountant’s employees to manage his bookkeeping, accounting and any official communication. The applicant’s representative advised the Department the incorrect date. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant provided misleading information and therefore is satisfied the applicant breached r.2.90. But the Tribunal also accepts the evidence of the applicant’s accountant, Nitin Gatnani, that the accountant’s employee had in advertently provided this incorrect information the Department and the Tribunal therefore accepts that the information provided was not ‘purposefully false’ and there was no intention on part of the applicant to mislead.
  3. In regard to Naveneet Singh it was found by the delegate that he was underpaid a weeks’ pay and the applicant breached r.2.79. The applicant provided to the Tribunal copies of a bank account that showed Naveneet Singh’s salary was paid from the bank account of Shikara restaurant rather than Mayur Yanchep where the employee worked. The Tribunal accepts the evidence of Nitin Gatnani, the accountant, that this was only discovered when the issue was raised by Department monitoring. PAYG, BAS etc. was then rectified. The Tribunal is satisfied that r.2.79 was not breached.
  4. The applicant advised the Tribunal, in relation to Jagroop Singh Bhatti, that the applicant had omitted to advise the Department about Jagroop Singh Bhatti leaving employment, due to a lack of knowledge about his obligations. He readily admitted that he had breached r.2.84. He subsequently advised the Department when another employee left his employment and has assured the Tribunal that he has obtained advice about his obligations and has rectified his record keeping. Nevertheless the applicant has breached r.2.84.
  5. Given the findings above, the Tribunal is satisfied that a relevant circumstance for s.140L(1)(a) exists, that is, that the applicant failed to satisfy a sponsorship obligation in accordance with r.2.89. Accordingly, it is necessary to consider whether one or more of the actions mentioned in s.140M should be taken.
  6. In considering what action to take, the Tribunal has had regard to the prescribed criteria, as extracted in the attachment to this decision.
  7. The Tribunal has considered the applicant's position and determining what action to take in accordance with the requirements of r.2.90(3). The Tribunal found that the applicant representative’s conduct at the hearing was co-operative and that he provided frank and truthful evidence that was corroborated by supporting documentation. The Tribunal finds the applicant to overall be a credible witness. The applicant maintained that he was so busy opening a new restaurant he was trying to do everything right and relied heavily on the assistance given by his accountant’s staff.
  8. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant provided misleading information regarding commencement date of his employee Parminder Singh was for the purpose of the monitoring and that the information was provided in good faith. The applicant’s breach of r.2.84 by not notifying an employee’s cessation of employment was readily admitted to the Department.
  9. The Tribunal notes that with the exception of these breaches, the applicant has a good record of compliance and conduct in relation to its dealings with the Department. There is no evidence before the Tribunal to suggest that the applicant’s past or present conduct in relation to the Department or the Tribunal has been adverse. The Tribunal finds that the failure to satisfy the obligation has not had a direct or indirect impact on another person. The Tribunal finds the failure to satisfy the sponsorship obligation, in all the circumstances, to be inadvertent. There is nothing before the Tribunal to establish that there have been further breaches.
  10. The applicant has employed a migration adviser who has advised the applicant about compliance with its sponsorship obligations. There is nothing before the Tribunal to establish that the breach was repeated.
  11. The Tribunal has found there was a breach of an obligation. However, the Tribunal is not satisfied this was an intentional breach by the applicant.
  12. Considering the totality of the circumstances, and having regard to the prescribed criteria the Tribunal finds that none of the actions under s.140M should be taken.
  13. Accordingly, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the prescribed circumstance in r.2.89 exists for the purpose of s.140M of the Act.

DECISION

  1. The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and substitutes a decision not to take one or more of the actions specified in s.140M of the Migration Act 1958.

Member
