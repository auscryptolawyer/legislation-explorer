Commissioner of Superannuation v Robert David Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239, (1994) 19 Aar 563 (1994) 33 ALD 569 (17 June 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION v ROBERT DAVID BOARDMAN

No. SG87 of 1993

FED No. 385/94

Number of pages - 16

Superannuation

[1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239, (1994) 19 AAR 563

[1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 33 ALD 569

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

SOUTH AUSTRALIAN DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - appeal from the Administrative Appeals Tribunal - preservation of superannuation benefits by a resigning eligible employee - election outside of prescribed period - discretion to recognise late election - misleading information given at the time of resignation - principles guiding the exercise of discretion - proper construction of the term "desirable" in s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.137(1), 157(1)


Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344 applied

HEARING

ADELAIDE, 13 December 1993

17:6:1994

Counsel for the applicant: Dr P Buchanan QC with

Mr P Hanks

Solicitor for the applicant: Australian Government

Solicitor

Counsel for the respondent: Mr S Tilmouth QC with

Mr A Collett

Solicitor for the respondent: Johnston Withers

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The appeal be dismissed. 2. The applicant pay the respondent's costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the

Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

VON DOUSSA J This is an appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal"). Upon a review of a decision made under s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") that the Commissioner for Superannuation decline to direct that an election made by the respondent on 7 March 1990 under s.137(1) of the Act be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that section, the Tribunal set aside that decision. In substitution, the Tribunal decided that the election made by the respondent be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed.

2. The respondent, who was born on 7 November 1949, became a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1922 on 30 July 1970 by virtue of his employment with the then Department of Civil Aviation. When the Act commenced on 1 July 1976 the respondent was an "eligible employee" within the meaning of the Act. In a letter to the relevant regional director of the then Department of Transport dated 17 April 1979 the respondent resigned with effect from 28 April 1979 from the Commonwealth Public Service.

3. In relation to his resignation the applicant completed a number of forms including a Form S2A entitled "Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights". The form was completed in a way which constituted an application by the respondent for a refund of accumulated contributions. On 21 May 1979 the respondent received a payment of accumulated contributions amounting to $2,717.29. The payment was made under the provisions of s.80 of the Act.

4. The respondent again became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act when he rejoined the Department of Transport on 31 March 1980. He had been out of the Commonwealth Public Service for a period of eleven months.

5. Section 137, on 28 April 1979, provided:

"137. (1) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and,

upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or

invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73, he

may, not later than 21 days after he so ceases to be an eligible

employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that

this Division apply in relation to him.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this

section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible

employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the

day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to sub-section (4) an election under this

section is of no effect unless the person who made the election

gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or

within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after the

expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation

to him -

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of

the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address

of his employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the

prescribed period a member of the superannuation

scheme applicable in relation to that employment and,

if so, specifying the scheme concerned.

(4) Sub-section (3) does not apply if the person who made

the election dies before the expiration of the period for the

giving of a notice by him under that sub-section and without

having given such notice.

(5) Where a person who is entitled to make an election under

this section dies, without making such an election -

(a) if the person is survived by a spouse - the spouse may

make such an election within 21 days after the date of

the death of the person; or

(b) if the person is not survived by a spouse, or is

survived by a spouse but the spouse dies without

making such an election, but the person is survived by

an eligible child or eligible children - such an

election may be made within that period by such person

as the Commissioner permits,

and, in that case, this Act has effect as if the election had been

made by the first-mentioned person but sub-section (3) does not

apply."

The section has been amended since then, but the time limits for making an election remain the same.

6. An election made under s.137(1) would have the effect that Division 3 of Part IX of the Act would continue to apply to the person who ceased to be an eligible employee. That Division provides for various benefits which can be chosen by an employee who resigns. The particular benefits which the respondent now seeks to enjoy are "deferred benefits" provided for in s.136. These benefits are more commonly referred to as "preservation of superannuation rights", or simply "preservation". Had the respondent made an election under s.137(1) not later than 21 days after he ceased to be an eligible employee, he would automatically have become entitled to preservation of superannuation rights. In the particular circumstances of the respondent, upon his again becoming an eligible employee his period of contributory service for the purposes of the Superannuation Scheme would be taken to include the 8.7 years of contributory service which he had achieved prior to his resignation on 28 April 1979, along with the period of service which again commenced on 31 March 1980.

7. No election was made by the respondent within the times prescribed by s.137. However by letter dated 7 March 1990, some eleven years after his resignation, the respondent sought to make that election relying upon the provisions of s.157(1) of the Act which had shortly before come to his attention. That section provides:

"157. (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration

of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the

election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the

circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should

be recognized, the Commissioner may direct that the election be

treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the

election shall have effect accordingly."

8. In his letter of 7 March 1990 the respondent stated that at the time of his resignation he had been provided with a form (the Form S2A) and had been given incorrect advice that preservation of superannuation rights was not an option available to him. He said that his failure to made the s.137(1) election at that time was due to the incorrect advice. Before the original decision-maker, and on reconsideration of the decision by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, the respondent failed to have his late election recognised primarily because the decision-makers were not satisfied that incorrect advice had been given.

9. On the evidence led before it, the Tribunal made the following findings of fact. The respondent was first appointed to the Commonwealth Public Service in Darwin. Soon afterwards he was selected for training as an air traffic controller. He underwent training in Perth, Melbourne and Sydney, and then had a period of service in Canberra. He returned to Darwin in 1975 by which time he was married and had two children. Shortly after his return to Darwin it was discovered that one of his children was seriously afflicted with cerebral palsy. In September 1977 he sought, and was given, a transfer to Adelaide so as to have access to treatment for his child. The respondent disliked the work to which he was assigned in Adelaide as an air traffic controller. He found himself under pressure at home with stresses associated with his child's medical condition, and at work. On one occasion he misidentified an aircraft whilst performing his air traffic control duties and that led to him being downgraded. He felt "wasted", and was overcome by the feeling that "he had to get out", so he resigned.

10. The Form S2A, which the respondent received after giving his letter of resignation bears in bold letters the heading previously described, and required the following information to be filled in and completed:

I, .............. ..........

First Names Surname (BLOCK LETTERS)

of .............. ..........

Postal Address Postcode

formerly employed by .....................in the State of...

Department/Authority

as ...............

Designation

hereby

. apply for a refund of accumulated contributions) under the

provisions

. apply for payment of a lump sum benefit ) of the

Superannuation

. elect for preservation of superannuation rights) Act

. Delete whichever inapplicable

Signature of Applicant .............. ...../.../....

Signature of Witness .............. Qualification........

Place .................

The respondent gave evidence, which the Tribunal accepted, that before completing the Form S2A he sought advice from personnel staff from the Department of Transport in the Department's "headquarters" office in central Adelaide. He asked what the three items which the form required him to choose between meant. He was told that the form was also used for other superannuation schemes which did not apply to him. He was told that for a resignation he would receive a refund of accumulated contributions. He was told "well, you cross out the bottom two and you get your money back". Accordingly he placed a line through the last two options. His application became one for a refund of accumulated contributions.

11. The applicant gave evidence that had he been told about the option of preserving superannuation benefits he would have elected to do so. His evidence in chief about this was:

"My father was a Commonwealth public servant. I knew that he had

good benefits available to him on retirement so I was aware of

what I was giving up in resigning. So that's why when I was

handed that bit of paper, the S2A, I asked what three items meant

on and I was given a very clear indication that only one option

applied to resignation, that the others applied - well, the

assumption was, from what he said, that the others applied to the

other superannuation scheme that they used them for, when in fact,

only one of the others applied to the provident account, and the

preservation option, which is the third one on the firm, applied

to resignations. Had I known at that time, that preservation was

an option, I most certainly would have left my money in there,

because as I said, I was fully aware of the benefits my father

would have had and I would've known then that the Commonwealth

benefit would've been added to my own money at age 55 for improved

benefits."

Before the Tribunal counsel for the Commissioner questioned the respondent closely about the circumstances of his alleged conversation with a personnel officer concerning the Form S2A, and about the identity of that person. As the respondent had in more recent years worked in the personnel department it was suggested that he should have been able to identify the person who advised him, but he said he could not. Evidence was led on behalf of the Commissioner with a view to establishing that it was unlikely that erroneous information of the kind suggested by the respondent would have been given, and that the respondent was not misinformed. In the end the Tribunal considered the evidence did not establish those propositions but, rather, showed that preservation of superannuation benefits on resignation was virtually unknown at that time among personnel officers in the Adelaide headquarters of the Department of Transport. The Tribunal said that it had no hesitation in accepting the respondent's evidence. The Tribunal found that the applicant was given incorrect information from a person who could not now be identified.

12. For a period of approximately eleven months after the respondent resigned he was engaged in selling life assurance, including superannuation policies. At the end of that period he felt better, and applied for reappointment to the Public Service as a clerk early in 1980. He was appointed to a clerical position in the Personnel Section of the Department of Transport. In this new position the respondent developed a talent for personnel work. He received a number of promotions. He is presently engaged as a personnel officer with the Bureau of Meteorology, Adelaide.

13. As a personnel officer the respondent gained a knowledge of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. In January 1989 he prepared and published with a colleague, Evan Bomford, a booklet entitled "The Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme and You, the Facts, the Benefits and the Costs". The publication and distribution of the booklet was sponsored by CPS Financial Services Pty Ltd and was generally well received by those with an understanding of the Superannuation Scheme.

14. The booklet contains a section on Resignation Benefits, and in that section there are outlined four options available to an eligible employee on resignation. Option 3 is entitled "Leave it in the Scheme", and the booklet deals with preservation of superannuation rights. The text relating to this option concludes with the following paragraph:

"NOTE An election to preserve superannuation rights must be made

within one month before to 21 days after the effective resignation

date. A preserved benefit cannot be paid until 3 months after the

date of resignation."

15. The respondent was therefore clearly aware of the time limits provided by s.137(1) at the time of that publication. After reviewing evidence about information statements circulated to contributors to the Scheme from time to time, and other evidence about knowledge gained by the respondent and Mr Bomford whilst collaborating in the publication of the booklet, the Tribunal found that the respondent became aware of the preservation provisions in the Act in late 1987.

16. It was contended on the Commissioner's behalf before the Tribunal that the respondent should have become aware of the possibility of preserving superannuation rights earlier than he did because he had received a booklet entitled "The Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme - New Contributors" on his reappointment to the Commonwealth Public Service in March 1980. That booklet contained information that on resignation employees could either obtain a refund of accumulated contributions or alternatively elect to preserve their superannuation rights. The Tribunal rejected this submission saying that it is highly unlikely that a person starting a new career in 1980 would be seeking to know his or her resignation benefits.

17. An immediate question which arose from the finding that the respondent became aware of the preservation provisions in late 1987 was why he did not seek to make a late election to gain the benefit of preservation until his letter of 7 March 1990? The respondent's explanation was as follows. Whilst he realised from the time he learned of the provisions in the Act for preservation that he had been disadvantaged by not making an election, he was not aware of the provisions of s.157. He felt upset that he had not taken advantage of preservation and "not knowing about late election means that I just accepted what had happened but I didn't like it". He did not become aware of s.157 until the latter half of 1989. It will be noted that the language of s.137(1) does not contemplate that an election might be made beyond the 21 day time limit (in contrast with s.137(3)) and there is no reference in the text of s.137 to s.157.

18. In 1989 the respondent and Mr Bomford were again working in conjunction to prepare a new booklet on the Superannuation Act 1990. In the course of his research into the Act the respondent noted s.157 but did not know what it meant. He asked other personnel officers in the Commonwealth Public Service in Adelaide, but none of them could inform him.

19. In February 1990 the respondent and Mr Bomford travelled together to Canberra to be interviewed for a particular position that had become available. Whilst there they discussed a number of superannuation matters with a senior officer at the Retirement Benefits Office. In the course of discussion that officer advised them that s.157 made it possible to make a late election for preservation. Following the interview the respondent and Mr Bomford discussed that advice. They concluded that it could apply to the respondent's situation. The respondent then wrote his letter of 7 March 1990. The Tribunal accepted the evidence of both the respondent and Mr Bomford, and found that the respondent did not become aware of the full import of s.157 until February 1990, and that his letter seeking to have a late election recognised was lodged as soon as could have been reasonably expected.

20. The Tribunal found that if the respondent's late election was not recognised he would lose the benefit of 8.7 years of service. The Tribunal noted that the delegate who reviewed the original decision not to recognise the election had described the effect of losing this period of service as follows:

"The benefit to Mr Boardman should his request for late election

be granted is not insignificant. For example, should his 8.7

years contributory service from July 1970 to April 1979 be added

to his period of current contributory service, the following

enhancement of standard age retirement benefits under the CSS

would result:

Pension % of salary Age 55 Age 60 Age 65

With preservation 38.18 46.93 52.50

Without preservation 33.45 44.64 51.40

A similar increase in entitlements would result if Mr Boardman

were to elect to transfer to the Public Sector Superannuation

Scheme."

21. In its reasons for decision, the Tribunal referred to the decisions of Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344 and Hill J in A'Hearn v Comcare (unreported) 16 April 1993, judgment 254/1993. Since the delivery of the Tribunal's reasons for decision a Full Court has upheld the first instance decision in A'Hearn: see Comcare v A'Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85. These decisions consider the principles to be applied in applications to extend time under s.11 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 ("the ADJR Act") and s.29(7) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") respectively. Those sections do not set out any criteria by reference to which the discretion to extend time is to be exercised. Similarly there are no criteria given for the exercise of the discretion vested in the Commissioner under s.157(1). The Tribunal noted that the discretion in s.157(1) is a wide one that should not be fettered by statements made in other cases arising under s.157(1) where particular matters are emphasised as important in the circumstances of the case. The Tribunal said that it was unable to agree with views expressed by the Tribunal in two other decisions under s.157(1), Re Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 94; (1993) 29 ALD 879, and Re Morgan and Commissioner for Superannuation (Administrative Appeals Tribunal, unreported 12 March 1993). In those two decisions differently constituted Tribunals found that late elections should not be recognised by the Commissioner as the applicant in each case should have made enquiries as to the entitlements to superannuation and should have sought to have a late election recognised sooner than happened. However a consideration of the reasons of the Tribunal in the present case indicates that rather than disagree with the other decisions, the Tribunal was distinguishing them on the facts. In the present case the Tribunal commented that there was a poor level of knowledge amongst personnel staff as to superannuation rights and in particular as to preservation. The Tribunal found that even when the respondent became aware of the preservation provisions of the Act in 1987, on the evidence before the Tribunal "it is very likely that any efforts to obtain further information would have been thwarted by the severe lack of knowledge, even by the 'experts' on this issue." This observation is one peculiar to the evidence led as to the state of knowledge of the personnel staff with whom the respondent had dealings in South Australia, and could not have been intended to have any wider meaning.

22. The Tribunal summarised its conclusions in paragraphs 50 and 51 of its reasons for decision:

"50. We are of the view that the nature of the discretion which we

have to exercise involves determining whether it is desirable in

all of the circumstances to allow the applicant to make a late

election for preservation of benefits. On the authority of

A'Hearn (supra) and Hunter Valley (supra), this involves a

determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case

when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be

granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that

extension is granted. On the facts of this case, the applicant

suffers a prejudice in the form of losing the benefit of his 8.7

years of prior service if the extension is not given. The

desirability of exercising the discretion in the applicant's

favour is the alleviation of this loss, especially in these

circumstances where the loss is unfair in that it was brought

about through no fault on the part of the applicant, and was

indeed caused by the misinformation provided by the respondent.

The prejudice suffered by the respondent is in the form of the

fund not having had the use of the monies for the relevant period.

51. We find that the circumstances of this matter are such that it

is desirable that the discretion to grant an extension of time

should be exercised in favour of the applicant because the

prejudice to the respondent is far outweighed by the unfairness of

the loss which would be suffered by the applicant, which loss was

caused by the actions of the respondent, should the extension not

be granted. The correct and preferable decision is that the

applicant's late election be treated as if it had been made within

the period allowed by s.137(1) of the Act."

23. An appeal lies to this Court under s.44 of the AAT Act on a question of law. The grounds of appeal contend that the Tribunal erred in law by misconceiving the meaning of the term "desirable" in s.157(1) of the Act; by applying, as relevant to the exercise of the discretion conferred by that section, principles developed in the context of the discretions conferred by s.11 of the ADJR Act and s.29(7) of the AAT Act without regard to distinctions between those provisions and s.157(1); by failing to take into account relevant considerations and taking into account irrelevant considerations; and by holding that in the circumstances of the case there was no duty of enquiry on the respondent as to his right to apply for preservation.

24. Submissions by Senior Counsel for the Commissioner recognise that s.157(1) confers a wide discretion upon the Commissioner. The discretion in its terms is unconfined. As Gummow J observed in Bond Corporation Holdings Ltd and Others v Australian Broadcasting Tribunal [1988] FCA 433; (1988) 84 ALR 669 at 680 "Where a statute confers a discretion which is in its terms unconfined, the factors that may be taken into account in the exercise of the discretion are similarly unconfined, except insofar as there may be found in the subject matter, scope and purpose of the statute some implied limitation on the factors to which the decision- maker may legitimately have regard: R v Australian Broadcasting Tribunal and Others; ex parte 2 HD Pty Ltd [1979] HCA 62; (1979) 144 CLR 45 at 49-50".

25. The purpose of the Act, as its long title indicates, is to make provision for and in relation to an Occupational Superannuation Scheme for eligible persons. The benefits payable under that Scheme reflect, among many considerations, the length of service of, and contributions by, the eligible employee. On the one hand an election recognised under s.137(1) will extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits which fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible employee. That would be in accordance with the general policy of the Act. On the other hand the orderly administration of the Superannuation Scheme requires that time limits be applied, generally speaking, so that decisions which impinge on investment and accounting procedures are made soon after an event such as resignation occurs. Matters of this kind set the context within which the discretion under s.157(1) is conferred, and the context in which the discretion must be exercised, but in the abstract they do not elucidate the meaning of "desirable" or dictate criteria by which the Commissioner is to be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised".

26. In the course of argument Senior Counsel for the Commissioner accepted that the connotation of "desirable" in the context of s.157(1) is that of being fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election, and the requirements of the administration of the Fund. In my opinion this is correct, and the Court should not seek to further define or expand the meaning of "desirable". Although the meaning of "desirable" was not the question agitated before the Full Court in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation (as yet unreported decision of the Full Court, 4 May 1994) this view accords with the following observation of Davies J, with which Black CJ and Cooper J agreed, at p.12:

"The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth

employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory

rights. But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to

receive the benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory

base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a

decision-

maker exercising the s.157 discretion could think it fair to the

employer, to the employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit

should be calculated by reference to the totality of the

employee's service and to the totality of his contributions,

including any sums which must be refunded."

27. In saying that the issue before the Tribunal involved a "determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that extension is granted" the Tribunal did not apply a wrong test.

28. In written submissions in support of the appeal it is contended on behalf of the Commissioner that the Tribunal should have asked itself whether recognition of the late election was a "positive aspiration" or "something worthy of achievement". This submission is based on a discussion about the meaning of "desirable" in s.157(1) by the Tribunal in Re Liddle and The Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307 at 318-319. Whereas a "desire" may in ordinary language mean a "positive aspiration", that meaning implies a subjective state of mind on the part of the person having the desire. In s.157(1) "desirable" is not referable to a state of mind - whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late election is "desirable" is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case. The suggested meaning "something worthy of achievement" does not imply other than an objective evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in the end, whether the recognition of a late election is "something worthy of achievement" must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.

29. The considerations which guide the exercise of discretion to extend time in procedural rules that govern the taking of steps in litigation or other dispute resolving procedures between citizens, or between citizens and government, have at their core similar objects. It is understandable, indeed inevitable, that a decision-maker considering an application under s.157(1) will have close regard to the assistance which decisions such as Hunter Valley Developments and A'Hearn offer.

30. Counsel for the Commissioner however submits that there are sharply different consequences arising from the favourable exercise of a discretion to extend a procedural time limit in litigation, and the exercise of the discretion to recognise a belated election under s.157(1). In the former case the extension merely gives the party obtaining it a chance to establish other rights in subsequent litigation. In the latter case the extension immediately bestows the right of preservation. In my opinion these distinctions do not provide any reason for applying different considerations in the exercise of each discretion. Obviously the immediate consequences in the two situations identified are different, but this is only to be expected as the discretions to extend time arise in wholly different settings. But in each case the question is whether it is fair and just that the party who is out of time should by the exercise of the discretion, be extended the rights which would have been enjoyed had the act in question been done within time. This conclusion also has the support of dicta in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation, per Davies J at pp.10-11.

31. Where a time limit is one arising under procedural rules governing dispute resolution there is the overshadowing object of all litigation, to bring disputation between citizens to an end for the public good, and to do so sooner rather than later. There is a public interest in enforcing procedural time limits which extends beyond the immediate parties. Under s.157(1), however, the parties are not in dispute and the same wide public interest question is not present. But another consideration arises, whether the granting or withholding of late recognition of an election will affect the administration of the Scheme. Whilst these are matters of difference between procedural time limits and the limitation in s.137(1), in the end they are unlikely to make any practical difference in the application of the guiding principles which have been identified in Hunter Valley Developments. In both situations all the circumstances of the case will include wider interests beyond those of the immediate parties.

32. I do not consider the Tribunal fell into error in failing to distinguish Hunter Valley Developments, or to modify the principles identified in that decision, on the ground that a favourable exercise of s.157(1) power would confer an immediate substantive benefit on the respondent rather than simply giving him the chance for contending for that benefit in later proceedings.

33. It is contended that the Tribunal fell into error by failing to take into account as relevant considerations the fact that s.137(1) prescribed a time limit for election, namely 21 days, and that the period was a brief one, whereas the respondent was seeking to have the period extended by some 10 years and 10 months. The Tribunal expressly recognised the time limits, and it is plain from a reading of the reasons for decision that the Tribunal was well aware of the short time limit in s.137(1), and the period by which the respondent was seeking to have that time extended. But it is contended that whilst the Tribunal expressly recognised the time limits, and a number of other matters in the course of its reasons for decision, it put these matters aside when it came to exercise the discretion because no express mention was made of them in paragraphs 50 and 51 of the reasons for decision which are set out above. I do not accept this submission. Paragraphs 50 and 51 are a short expression of conclusions. It is clear from the reasons for decision that the Tribunal at all stages in its decision-making took into account both the time limit and the length of the delay in making an election which would be remedied by a favourable exercise of the discretion.

34. It is further submitted that the Tribunal fell into error by failing to take into account as a relevant consideration the difficulty, given the lapse of time, in establishing the circumstances surrounding the respondent's omission to elect for preservation when he resigned. The detailed discussion of the evidence by the Tribunal leaves no doubt that the Tribunal was fully alive to the difficulties that the lapse of time created in establishing the relevant circumstances. I do not think it can be said that the Tribunal overlooked this consideration, insofar as it may have been relevant to the exercise of the discretion, even though it was not distinctly identified and discussed as a separate topic.

35. Wherever it is necessary to have regard to factual circumstances as they existed at a date in the past, delay in making the application which initiates that consideration is likely to add to the difficulties of ascertaining those circumstances. This difficulty is likely to be a consideration in almost every application to extend time no matter what the context. Section 157(1), like s.11 of the ADJR Act discussed in Hunter Valley Developments does not, in terms, place any onus of proof upon an applicant for extension. As Wilcox J pointed out at p.348 of that decision, an application nevertheless has to be made. The time limit specified in the statute gives a prima facie rule as to when the application should have been made. Before an exercise of discretion in the applicant's favour can occur it must be made to appear that it is fair and just in all the circumstances to extend time. One of the relevant considerations will be whether there was a reasonable explanation for the delay, although the absence of a reasonable explanation is not necessarily fatal: Comcare v A'Hearn at 88. There is therefore an evidentiary burden on the applicant to place information before the decision-maker sufficient to satisfy the decision-maker that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the late election should be recognised. The lapse of time, and the difficulties created by it, are most likely to assume importance when the decision- maker considers the quality of the information that has been adduced. After a long lapse of time memories are likely to be deficient. In some cases the quality of the evidence could be so unsatisfactory that, understandably, the decision-maker will not be satisfied either about particular facts which are important in the case, or, more generally, that there is a sufficiently established factual base to justify a favourable exercise of the discretion. In the present case however, notwithstanding the lapse of time, and notwithstanding the fact that the person who was alleged to have given misinformation to the respondent about the Form S2A could not be identified, the Tribunal (the decision- maker) was satisfied that misinformation had been given. A factual base for the exercise of the discretion was established; in the circumstances of this case the difficulty in establishing background facts ceased to be a reason against a favourable exercise of the discretion.

36. It is submitted that the Tribunal fell into error by failing to take into account, or to consider, whether the respondent would have made an election for preservation within the time prescribed by s.137(1) if he had been given correct advice about preservation. It is submitted that the Tribunal failed to address this question in its reasons for decision. I reject this submission. I have earlier set out a passage from the evidence of the respondent where he asserted unequivocally that he would have elected for preservation had he been told about it. The Tribunal expressly accepted the evidence of the respondent. Moreover in paragraph 50 of the reasons for decision the Tribunal held that the loss of preservation benefits "was indeed caused by" the misinformation provided to the respondent.

37. This is not a case where the accumulated contributions were immediately invested in a business enterprise, the pursuit of which was a reason for the resignation. In such a case the use of the money would in itself often indicate that a deliberate decision had been made to reject the option of preservation in favour of receiving the immediate benefit of the accumulated contributions. In this case the respondent placed the accumulated contributions in a savings bank account.

38. In the circumstances of the present case, it was necessary to consider whether the alleged misinformation was the cause of the respondent not making an election under s.137(1) when he resigned, as his whole case was based on the assertion that the "election" recorded in the Form S2A was not an election at all, but the product of misinformation that directed that he fill out the form in one way only. It was necessary for the respondent to establish that the Form S2A did not record a deliberate and informed decision by him at the time to take accumulated contributions, not preservation. However in other cases whether there was a deliberate and informed decision to take accumulated contributions may not be critical in the exercise of the discretion under s.157(1). This was recognised in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation where Davies J said at pp.11-12:

"In the exercise of this discretion, it may be more important to

consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time

than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time.

Section 157 merely requires that the Commissioner 'be satisfied

that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the

election should be recognised'. 'All the circumstances' include

all relevant circumstances existing at the time the discretion is

to be exercised.

In the application of s.157, it may be of little consequence that

the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under

s.137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect.

Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such

case. Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because

the facts which the employee took into account, when he or she

decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently.

Oftentimes, the discretion is favourably exercised when the

employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was

unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s.157 is

to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances.

The circumstances need not be of a medical character. In Mr

Chalk's case, the change in circumstance was that he resigned from

Telecom intending to establish himself in an entirely new career

as a farmer but was unsuccessful in doing so. And so he again

took up employment with Telecom, after a lapse of two years."

39. Next it is contended that the Tribunal failed to take into account the opportunities which the respondent had to enquire about the existence and scope of the late election option. The findings of the Tribunal on this topic have already been set out. In my opinion this submission is not made good. In 1979 when the applicant resigned he was informed that preservation of superannuation rights was not an option available to him. That positive advice would have continued to affect his consideration of his possible rights when he again became an eligible employee. The Tribunal expressly considered the argument that he should have become aware of his rights when he was handed the booklet for new contributors on his reappointment in March 1980. The Tribunal considered the effect of circulars published to contributors to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme in 1977, 1978 and 1979. The Tribunal found that the respondent became aware of preservation rights in 1987 but that he did not become aware of his right to make a late election until 1990.

40. More importantly, the Tribunal found that it is very likely that any efforts by him to obtain further information would have been thwarted by the severe lack of knowledge on the part of people with whom those enquiries would have been made. This finding distinguishes the case from decisions such as those in Secourable and Morgan. It is understandable that a decision-maker might expect an applicant to follow up lines of enquiry that are suggested by facts or circumstances that are known to, or come to the attention of, the person seeking an extension of time. Depending on the circumstances, the decision-maker might proceed on the footing that there is no reasonable explanation for delay beyond the point when lines of enquiry so suggested would have revealed to the applicant the right to apply for an extension. But where the evidence establishes that the suggested lines of enquiry would have produced no useful information, so as to leave the applicant in the same state of ignorance or misinformation, the absence of enquiry becomes irrelevant. That was the case here.

41. The last matter which it is contended the Tribunal failed to take into account was the effect of a recognition of late election on the Fund, Consolidated Revenue, and the administration of the Act. These matters were not discussed in detail by the Tribunal in its reasons for decision, although the Tribunal in its conclusions had regard to the prejudice suffered by the Commissioner because the Fund did not have the use of the money for a period. The issue was therefore not entirely overlooked. The reason why these factors were not discussed in detail would follow from the fact that no evidence was led before the Tribunal on the Commissioner's behalf touching these issues. The only information on the topic is to be found in a statement in the reasons of the Commissioner's delegate who rejected the respondent's application for a review of the original decision. The delegate said:

"If a late election is allowed, Mr Boardman will have had the use

of his refunded contributions (or, at least, the nett amount if

any tax was paid on them) from 1979 until the time he repays them,

and the Superannuation Fund will have been deprived of their use;

and to that extent there is an element of advantage to him and of

disadvantage to the fund in such a course. On the other hand, he

will not have the advantage of receiving interest on his

contributions over the period during which they were not in the

fund. Obviously, the cost to the Commonwealth in funding his

preservation benefits will be no greater if he elects to preserve

his rights now than if he had elected to preserve them in 1979.

Disadvantage to the Commonwealth lies rather in the risk of

encouragement being given to other members both to enjoy the

advantage of having their contributions refunded and, later, to

enjoy the advantages of preserved benefit, where the intention of

Parliament is clear that a final choice of one option or the other

is to be made at the outset. This would produce an unanticipated

charge on public revenue because those members initially chose a

different form of benefit."

It is difficult to detect from this statement any significant financial prejudice to the Fund, at least sufficient prejudice to amount to a substantial reason against allowing an extension of time. See also Chalk v Commissioner of Superannuation, per Davies J, at 13-14. As the statement indicates, a favourable exercise of the discretion would extend to the respondent the benefit of additional years of service, but this liability would have arisen in any event had the election been exercised in time. To grant the respondent the benefit of years of service actually performed is consistent with the policy and objects of the Act.

42. The prejudice to the respondent and the public interest which the delegate seeks to identify is the risk of encouraging other people to make applications to have late elections recognised. Great care must be exercised in treating this argument as one against the exercise of the discretion. The object of the 21 day time limit in s.137(1) is to avoid risks of this kind. There is in the argument a risk of "double counting" the administrative disruption to the orderly running of the Fund caused by late elections - first in requiring reasons why the prima facie time limit should be extended, and then in adding as a separate hurdle the risk of administrative disruption from too many applications. The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that there will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant requires that the time limit be relaxed. In such cases it is the policy of the Act, and the legal right of the applicant, to a favourable exercise of discretion under s.157(1). If the proper application of s.157(1) to individual cases results in many people obtaining recognition of late elections, so be it, that is the law.

43. The irrelevant consideration which it is contended that the Tribunal took into account in reaching its decision is that the Commissioner, by one of its officers, unidentified, had misinformed the respondent thereby causing him to suffer loss. Assuming, for the moment, that the information was supplied by an officer acting in the scope of his employment on the Commissioner's behalf, the information was highly relevant. It provided the explanation for the delay, and provided a very strong reason why it was desirable to recognise belatedly the election. In written and oral submissions, this ground of appeal was modified to contend that the evidence did not establish the identity of the person who misinformed the respondent, and, absent identification, it could not be held that the person was an officer of the Commissioner acting in the scope of his employment. It does not appear that this question of authority was raised as an issue before the Tribunal. This would explain why it was not expressly dealt with by the Tribunal. But a finding of authority is implicit in the reasoning of the Tribunal. The evidence of the applicant, which was expressly accepted, was that he went into the headquarters of the then Department of Transport in Adelaide to obtain advice about the Form S2A and was given that advice from "a person from the personnel area". The implicit finding of authority was not only open on the evidence but was the only reasonable conclusion.

44. Finally it was contended that the Tribunal fell into error of law in making the following observation in paragraph 49 of its reasons for decision:

"We are of the view that employees who resign and are contributors

to the CSS, should be given adequate information by personnel

officers and that nothing less than an estimate of the benefits

available at age 55 calculated in accordance with the example in

Attachment A to exhibit R2, and placed in front of the resigning

employee would satisfy the criteria that a person be properly

informed as to his or her rights."

45. The attachment A to exhibit R2 contained a table which gave examples of deferred benefits for a contributor who resigned at age 45 and preserved the benefit and applied for a deferred benefit at age 55. The table was as follows:

Accumulated basic

contributions at age 45 $10,000 $30,000 $50,000

Lump sum Indexed Lump sum Indexed Lump sum Indexed

in cash pension in cash pension in cash pension

per annum per annum per annum

Benefit at age 55:

Assuming 16%

interest $44,114 $10,204 $132,343 $30,611 $220,572 $51,018

Assuming 10%

interest $25,937 $5,999 $77,812 $17,998 $129,687 $29,997

Assuming 4%

interest $14,802 $3,424 $44,407 $10,271 $74,012 $17,119

No evidence was led before the Tribunal as to the practical difficulties that would confront the Commissioner in endeavouring to provide such a table to each and every person who resigned. Forecasts about interest rates are in themselves likely to be unhelpful, at least over a period of many years. To the extent that it may be material in other cases to consider whether "adequate information" has been given about the options available to someone retiring from the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme, the adequacy of information provided would need to be judged according to the circumstances of the particular case. In some cases the information required to give an adequate explanation may not require such a table. With respect to the Tribunal, it does not seem feasible to judge in advance what would constitute "adequate information".

46. Whilst criticism may be levelled at paragraph 49, I do not consider that the observation about "adequate information" formed an essential step in the Tribunal's reasoning. Rather the observation was an aside added to the finding that the respondent did not at any stage sleep on his rights. It had been the Commissioner's case before the Tribunal that the respondent was informed of his rights, and in any event had become aware of them in 1987 or 1988. The exhibit R2 was a leaflet published by the Superannuation Fund Investment Trust in June 1988. This was one of the documents which had informed the respondent about his preservation rights, but one of the Commissioner's witnesses had said in evidence that he had never used a table like that contained in the exhibit to calculate an estimated deferred benefit for a resigning employee. Having completed its discussion about the items of evidence relied on by the Commissioner, the Tribunal has added its observation in paragraph 49, presumably as an encouragement to bring about a review of office practice. As the general submission of the Commissioner that the respondent had slept on his rights was rejected, the observations were immaterial to the process of reasoning which led to the decision to exercise the s.157(1) decision in favour of the respondent. It is to be noted that in paragraph 51 of the reasons for decision it is said that the loss which would be suffered by the applicant if his election were not recognised was a loss "caused by the actions of the (Commissioner)". The Tribunal did not decide the matter on the basis that the respondent's late election was caused by any omission on the part of the Commissioner.

47. It has not been contended that the conclusion of the Tribunal is so unreasonable that it should be set aside under the principle in Wednesbury Corporation and Others v Ministry of Housing and Local Government (1965) 1 WLR 261.

48. In my opinion as no error of law by the Tribunal has been demonstrated, this appeal should be dismissed.
