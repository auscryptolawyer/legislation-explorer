DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 404

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No ST98/47

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re BLAKE QUENTIN DUNNE

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)



Date 10 June 1999

Place Adelaide

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



(Signed)

D.J. TROWSE

(Member)

CATCHWORDS – income tax – whether workers' compensation receipt constitutes fringe benefit and thus exempt income

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.23L, 221A

Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 s.30, 136

Atwood Oceanics Australia Pty Ltd 89 ATC 4808

Mutual Acceptance Company Ltd v F.C. of T [1944] HCA 34; (1944) 69 CLR 389

Scott and C of T [1966] HCA 48; [1966] 117 CLR 514



REASONS FOR DECISION



10 June 1999 Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)



1. The issue to be determined in this reference is whether a lump sum workers' compensation payment received by the applicant during the year ended 30 June 1996 constitutes exempt income in terms of s.23L of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. It is that section that provides exemption to employees for fringe benefits which come within the meaning of that term as stated in the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (the FBT Act).

2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr A. Yates of counsel and the respondent by one of his officers.

3. For some years prior to 28 September 1989, the applicant worked as a rigger on the natural gas fields at Moomba which is located in the far north of South Australia. He worked and lived at Moomba for 15 days out of every 28 days and when not so employed he returned to his home in Adelaide. While living at Moomba, the applicant's employer provided at no cost to the applicant accommodation, meals and all necessary facilities. Additionally, all expenses associated with travel to and from Moomba were met by the employer. The applicant accepts that there was little opportunity or need for him to spend money while at Moomba.

4. In addition to a base salary, the applicant was paid, as a condition contained in the Oil Drilling Rig Workers' Award, a location allowance while at Moomba for the disadvantages of living away from home. The allowance, which prior to January 1989 was at the daily rate of $40, began on the day of departure from Adelaide and was paid through to the day of return to Adelaide. The applicant agrees that allowances received to that point in time represented assessable income in his hands. From January 1989, the allowance was reduced to $30 per day and which, according to the statement of facts agreed between the parties, became subject to fringe benefits tax. An explanation of that outcome is offered in the following excerpt taken from a memorandum issued by the employer to employees on 15 January 1989:

"There are no other changes in the benefits except for the Living Away from Home Allowance which with effect from 1 January 1989 will be paid at A$30 per day.

This replaces the previous cash allowance given.

This allowance is aimed to specifically compensate you for additional expenses and disadvantages which you incur living away from home and accordingly will constitute a fringe benefit under the law. In accordance with relevant tax regulations, the company will effective from 1 January 1989 pay the relevant fringe benefit tax liability. This will mean that you will receive the allowance free from personal income tax."



5. The applicant was injured at work and as a result of the injury became entitled to weekly payments of compensation from Workcover for the period 28 September 1989 to 30 June 1995. Needless to say, the applicant did not work or travel to Moomba during that period.

6. In calculating the quantum of compensation payable to the applicant, Workcover did not bring to account the allowance previously referred to and it was that exclusion that gave cause to an appeal to the Supreme Court of South Australia. The decision of the Court was that the allowance should be included in ascertaining the worker's average weekly earnings and which, in turn, resulted in an increase in the applicant's entitlement to income maintenance payments. In reaching that decision, the Court was required to consider whether the allowance was received by the worker by way of an allowance to cover special expenses incurred by the worker in the course of employment. If such was the case, the allowance was to be regarded as a prescribed allowance which consequently was to be disregarded for the purpose of arriving at the amount of average weekly earnings. On the evidence before the Court, it was concluded that the applicant when at Moomba incurred no expenses in the course of his employment and that his ordinary expenditure, meaning private expenditure and expenditure relating to employment, was no different from what it would have been if he had been somewhere else. Ultimately, the Court decided that the allowance was not a prescribed allowance and that it should be included in the determination of the applicant's average weekly earnings. Subsequent to that decision, Workcover made a lump sum payment of $31,128 to the applicant for arrears of income maintenance which had accrued at the rate of $30 per day during the period 28 September 1989 to 30 June 1995. The respondent's decision to treat this amount as part of the applicant's assessable income is the reason for the current dispute.

7. To succeed in this matter, the applicant must establish that the amount in question complies with the requirements of sub-s.30(1) of the FBT Act which reads as follows:

"Living-Away-From-Home Allowance Benefits

30(1) Where:

(a) at a particular time, in respect of the employment of an employee of an employer, the employer pays an allowance to the employee; and

(b) it would be concluded that the whole or a part of the allowance is in the nature of compensation to the employee for:

(i) additional expenses (not being deductible expenses ) incurred by the employee during a period; or

(ii) additional expenses (not being deductible expenses) incurred by the employee, and other additional disadvantages to which the employee is subject, during a period;

by reason that the employee is required to live away from his or her usual place of residence in order to perform the duties of that employment;

the payment of the whole, or of the part, as the case may be, of the allowance constitutes a benefit provided by the employer to the employee at that time."



A benefit of this kind is by way of definition, see sub-s.136(1) of the FBT Act, deemed to be a "living-away-from-home allowance fringe benefit".

8. The provisions of sub-s.30(1)were considered by Lee J in Atwood Oceanics Australia Pty Ltd 89 ATC 4808 and the following summary of comments made by the learned Judge are of assistance:

  • It is a requirement of the paragraph [sub-s.30(1)(b)] that the circumstances surrounding the payment of the allowance be such that a reasonable person would conclude, applying an objective view thereto, that the allowance bore the character described in the paragraph.
  • The required character of the allowance was that it be a payment to an employee in the nature of compensation for additional expenses incurred by the employee during a period of employment, or for additional expenses so incurred and any other additional disadvantages to which the employee was subject during that period by reasons of the fact that the employee was required to live away from his usual place of residence.
  • It may be accepted that the words 'other additional disadvantages' were not intended to be restricted to disadvantageous pecuniary results.
  • The section applies to an allowance paid in respect of a particular period and states that an allowance would be a benefit at the time of payment if the allowance contained the characteristics described in the section.
  • The Act requires demonstration of some causal connection between the likelihood of additional expenses and disadvantages and the requirement to live away from home for a period.
  • The lack of likelihood of additional expenses being incurred by employees may tend to confirm a conclusion that the purpose of the allowance negotiated by representatives of the employers and employees was to provide a pecuniary recognition of, and compensation for, the unattractive and unnatural aspect of that employment.

9. The Tribunal turns to a consideration of the circumstances of the payment as they existed at time of payment i.e. sometime soon after 2 November 1998 and which related to the period 28 September 1989 to 30 June 1995. The Tribunal finds that the relevant circumstances then existing were as follows:

  • Neither at that time or during the above-mentioned period did the applicant live away from his usual place of residence in order to perform the duties of his employment.
  • No additional expenditure was incurred by the applicant by reasons of him being required to live away from his or her usual place of residence in order to perform the duties of his employment nor was it likely that expenses of this kind would be incurred.

In those circumstances, the Tribunal is satisfied that a reasonable person would conclude that the allowance did not bear the characteristics required of the sub-section. On that basis the Tribunal decides that the amount in question is not a Living-Away-From-Home Allowance Benefit.

10. The Tribunal feels obliged to remark on the comment made on behalf of the applicant that "it was difficult to comprehend why an allowance which was exempt from income tax when paid while he was working, when paid in compensation for the same purposes, is suddenly assessable to income tax. (see page 5 of transcript) Bearing in mind the decision in Atwood Oceanics (supra) and the close resemblance to the factual situation to those in this matter, it seems that the applicant may have been somewhat fortunate in having the allowance received in that earlier period treated as exempt income. The decision in Atwood Oceanics was that allowances paid to workers required to perform their duties on an offshore oil rig and who were employed under a similar industrial award as the applicant did not constitute a living away from home benefit for the reason that the employment did not raise the prospect of the employees incurring additional expenses. Rather it was found that the nature of the allowance was to compensate the employees for the particular disadvantages of living in a remote location and for the social disruption occasioned by the need to live away from home.

11. On the information before the Tribunal it is reasonable to assume that the purpose of stating the aim of the allowance in the memorandum dated 15 January 1989 was to shift the liability for tax from the employee to the employer. It seems that in all other respects nothing changed and that, more specifically, the employer continued to provide all of the same facilities as before. In those circumstances and notwithstanding the stated aim, it seems probable that a reasonable person would still conclude that it was unlikely that the employee would incur additional expenditure.

12. There is no doubt in the Tribunal's view that the amount under review comes within the definition of "benefit" as contained in sub-s.136(1) of the FBT Act and thus, for the sake of completeness, the possibility of it being a fringe benefit under some other category needs to be addressed. The term "fringe benefit" is defined in the same sub-section and is expressed, as far as it relates to this matter, in the following manner:

"'fringe benefit' in relation to an employee, in relation to the employer of the employee, in relation to a year of tax, means a benefit:

(a) provided at any time during the year of tax; or

(b) provided in respect of the year of tax;

being a benefit provided to the employee or to an associate of the employee by:

(c) the employer;

(d) an associate of the employer; or

(e) a person (in this paragraph referred to as the 'arranger') other than the employer or an associate of the employer under an arrangement between:

(i) the employer or an associate of the employer; and

(ii) the arranger or another person;

in respect of the employment of the employee, but does not include:

(f) a payment of salary or wages or a payment that would be salary or wages if salary or wages included exempt income for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936;

(g) ..."



Other definitions within the sub-section that are relevant are:

"'employer' means:

(a) a current employer;

(b) a future employer; or

(c) a former employer;

but does not include:

(d) ...

(e) ...

'salary or wages' means assessable income, being salary or wages within the meaning of section 221A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936"



For present purposes, the definition of salary or wages in terms of s.221A includes salary, wages, commission, bonuses or allowances paid by way of compensation or of sickness or accident in respect of incapacity for work, being payments calculated at a weekly or other periodical rate, but not including living-away-from-home allowance benefits within the meaning of the FBT Act.



13. The Tribunal is of the opinion that the receipt of $31,123 represents a substitute for that part of the average amount that the applicant could reasonably be expected to have earned had he not been disabled which relates to the location allowance and that, as such, it assumed the character of that allowance. Furthermore, the Tribunal concludes that the amount in question is an allowance of the type mentioned in the definition of "salary or wages" and by way of authority refers to the decision of Latham CJ in Mutual Acceptance Company Ltd v F.C. of T [1944] HCA 34; (1944) 69 CLR 389 and more particularly to pages 396-397 where he said that the word "allowances" when used in connection with the relationship between employer and employee meant "a grant or something additional to ordinary wages for the purpose of meeting some particular requirement connected with the service rendered by the employee or as compensation for unusual conditions of that service". It follows that the amount received is an allowance for purposes of the definition of "salary or wages" and, accordingly is excluded from being a fringe benefit.

14. The Tribunal finds that the amount of $31,123 was a product or incident of the applicant's employment and as such forms part of his assessable income. Support for this conclusion is gained from the following statement made by Windeyer J in Scott and C of T [1966] HCA 48; [1966] 117 CLR 514 at pages 525 and 526:

"In other words the enactment makes it clear that the income of a taxpayer who is engaged in any employment or in the rendering of any services for remuneration includes the value to him of everything that he in fact gets, whether in money or in kind and however it be described, which is a product or incident of his employment or a reward for his services."



15. For the reasons stated, the Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
