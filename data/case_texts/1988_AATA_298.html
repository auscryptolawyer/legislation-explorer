ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Commander Australian Military Forces - Claim for Entertainment Expenditure - Special nature of position held - Whether such expenditure was of a private nature -Whether such expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income -

Section 51 Income Tax Assessment Act

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation Appeals Division No. NT 86/3547

Re: Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

7 October 1988 The Hon. Mr Justice Rodney N. PURVIS

REASONS FOR DECISION

GENERALLY

This is an application for review of a decision made by the Respondent Commissioner referable to an objection lodged by the Applicant to an assessment issued in respect of the subject year.

The applicant was, at the relevant time, a Colonel in the Australian Military Forces posted as Commandant to the Sixth Military District Tasmania. He spent three years at this post, the subject year being the second of such years.

The State of Tasmania is a small one. The obligations resting upon the Applicant to receive distinguished persons, entertain and respond to invitations, was alleged to be such that amounts expended by him, and beyond his allowance, were said to have been incurred in gaining or producing his income, or necessarily incurred in carrying on his occupation for the purpose of gaining or producing his income within the meaning of section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

The amount of the claim was initially at issue. During the currency of the hearing agreement was reached as to this sum and the same now stands at $2542.

It was said on behalf of the taxpayer that he was employed in. a unique situation different from other Commanders positions in Australia, in that the taxpayer was expected to entertain on a continuous basis over the period of his three year command. The headquarters of the Sixth Military District were at Anglesea Barracks an historical building in Hobart. The Commander's residence was at the Barracks. It was alleged that the taxpayer entertained in order to maintain good relations with civic as well as military personnel and that he worked closely with the Governor of the State, the Premier, the judiciary and other officials. He was required to be present at numerous official functions, averaging six to seven per week.

It was contended that on taking up the appointment the taxpayer was led to believe that he would be heavily involved, with his wife, in social activities, this as part of his duties. He was to receive an annual entertain allowance of $450, this being the same amount as was alleged to Commanders of other Military Districts. This sum was rot sufficient to its end. The situation pertaining in Tasmania so far as the obligation to entertain was concerned, vas said to be different to that existing in the other states.

The Commissioner submitted that no part of the expenses incurred by the taxpayer were so incurred in gaining or producing his assessable income.

THE RETURN AND THE OBJECTION

In the return of income for the year the subject of this application it was stated that the taxpayer

“ ..... is the Commandant of Sixth Military District covering Tasmania and is stationed at Anglesea Barracks, Hobart. This position represents the senior military post in Tasmania and as such he is engaged in many social functions in an official capacity.

He is required to entertain all sections of the civil, governmental, vice-regal and military community."

The taxpayer detailed in a schedule to his return the dates of functions, the nature of such functions, and their cost.

The Respondent Commissioner adjusted the declared taxable income and sc far as is relevant to this application, disallowed the whole of the expenses claimed to be attributable to entertainment.

In the Notice of Objection to the Assessment the taxpayer's then agent stated in addition to that which had been set forth in the return that

“ His position requires him to entertain many people during the year, Heads of State, Ministers of the Government, Governors of the States of Australia as well as important guests to Australia. Also many civilian organizations, charities etcetera where the military is expected to participate as well as government officials and military personnel.

Many of these official entertaining engagements were held at Anglesea Barras due to its historic nature and appeal.

Because of the necessity of this entertaining, the taxpayer maintained a record of the occasion and the expenses incurred. These expenses are recorded at cost and do not include expenses of a private nature.

All of the functions are official and would not have occurred if the taxpayer was not in this position.

The position of Commandant requires him to be so involved, although the amount of the allowance paid in recognition of the position is quite inadequate in the circumstances."

The view of the Commissioner as expressed in his reasons for disallowing the claim was that mo part of the amount claimed in the year of income in respect of entertainment expenses is allowable as a deduction under sub-section 51(1) of the Act.

THE EVIDENCE

In summary form, the duties of the applicant as stated by him in evidence given before the Tribunal were:-

- To command the Army Forces in Tasmania.

- To chair the Joint-Services Co-ordinating Committee (Senior Naval, Air-force, and Defence Public Servant in Tasmania) and through it to guide the overall defence activities in the State.

- To liaise with other Commonwealth Departments in Tasmania.

- By direct access to the Premier of the State to effect efficient liaison between State Authorities and the Defence Department.

- To assist the Governor of the State and his staff with military aspects of ceremonial activities, and

- To interface with State Officials, the Judiciary, the Clergy, local business persons and officers of charitable and philanthropic organizations and to provide assistance where this was within the guidelines of Commonwealth Government Policy.

The taxpayer elaborated on the above summary of his duties by saying that although commanding the Army Forces in Tasmania, he was also responsible for guiding the activities of the Navy and the Air-Force. He was in constant communication with the protocol staff of the Governor and likewise with the Premier. He maintained liaison with the Judiciary, including the Chief Justice of the State and politicians. He was in frequent contact with business people and Chairmen of Boards of State instrumentalities. He had liaison with Mayors of localities across the state and outside of Tasmania.

The taxpayer said that his appointment to the Command was made by the Chief of General Staff with the assistance of an advisory committee. The duties including the social responsibilities, entrusted to him were he said, not spelt out in any written form but had been advised to him before he accepted the appointment to Tasmania.

The taxpayer estimated that approximately sixty per cent of his time whilst Commandant was spent on social activities, he considering this as being "right" and an integral part of his duties in the State. The time so spent on social activities was not restricted to working hours but was also of evenings and weekends.

He travelled in his military capacity throughout the State, accompanied on many occasions by his wife and as expected, attended military and civil functions in his capacity as Commandant and the senior military officer in the State. He gave addresses at the Royal Commonwealth Society and at many schools, again in his capacity as Senior Military Officer. He accompanied Officers senior to himself when they visited Tasmania on inspection tours. He said that so far as the entertaining afforded by himself was concerned, that in many instances it was responsive to invitations that he had accepted and occasions that he had attended and at other times it was in recognition of the position held by the person who was visiting Tasmania, or are the request of the Governor of the State. There were in addition occasions when he entertained his personal staff, together with their wives or ladies, this on about two occasions a year.

The taxpayer recorded the functions and the persons who attended. He also made notation of the cost. He said that his visitor's book for the subject year showed that over one thousand people had been received and that he had held forty eight functions of an official nature in the subject year. It was substantially in respect of the latter functions that the claim was made. The taxpayer said that the people who came to such functions were well aware of the official nature of the occasion. The fact of the various occasions taking place was not at issue in the proceedings.

The taxpayer was employed on a fixed salary according to his rank. As has been earlier mentioned, there was not given to the taxpayer at any time a formal listing of his duties or a duty statement. They were, as he put it, "functional". The responsibilities that he assumed were consistent with his being the most senior Military Officer in the State. He doubted that if he had not entertained as he had done, he would have been removed from his post, but was of the view that he would not have "lasted the three years" if he had "not done the right thing through the community".

When asked as to why he did not seek reimbursement for the additional expenses incurred by him, he made mention of a previous experience he had had whilst posted to the United Kingdom where a claim had been made and refused. He said that he had discussed the matter with senior officers whilst in Tasmania and was told that it was "not worthwhile", and that the Department expected him to cover the expense.

After leaving Tasmania the taxpayer was appointed to an administrative position in Sydney for a period of fifteen months and then was moved to Canberra. He was promoted at this time to the rank of Brigadier. When asked for the basis upon which promotion tock place, he said that it was ninety per cent dependent upon confidential reports, such reports relating to not only military aspects of the work that he had done, bat also his social involvement with the military and the community, such involvement being consistent with his standing in the military forces.

The records maintained by the taxpayer of social occasions when he entertained were in evidence. They were corroborative of the oral evidence given by him, not only as to the appointment or position of the guest attending, but also as to the reason for inclusion of the person in the guest list. They were clearly not occasions on which the taxpayer entertained friends. This is not to say that such persons as to some of them might have been, and as to others might have become, friends, but rather to say that they were not invited in this capacity. Accepting, as I do, the reasons given for inviting each person, it is apparent that the persons would in all probability not have been so invited other than for the positions they occupied and the taxpayer's involvement in the civic as well as the direct military affairs of the State. In many instances the invitations were responsive, as would be expected of a person in the position of the taxpayer, to hospitality extended to him and his wife in their military capacity. In other instances tie invitations were extended by the taxpayer in his capacity as Commandant to persons of standing in a profession, the community, or a senior government official or minister.

RELEVANT PRINCIPLES

The taxpayer seeks a deduction pursuant to section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. So far as it is relevant to these reasons, the section provides:-

"51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are Incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred In carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

By section 6 of the Act "business" includes any profession, trade, employment, vocation, or calling, but does not include occupation as an employee. The taxpayer as a military personnel was an employee. He right, according to some authorities, have been carrying on a business as an army officer, but it is not necessary for me to deal in these reasons with that question. I am satisfied on the evidence, and it was not in issue, as to his employee status. Hence, the right, if any, of the taxpayer to claim the deduction is dependent upon the expenditure being "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" and not being of a "private or domestic nature".

The words "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income", as used in s51, mean incurred "in the course of gaining or producing such income" (Amalgamated Zinc De Bavay's Limited v. FC of T(1935) 54 CLR at 303) and require the outgoing to be "incidental and relevant to that end". (Ronpibon Tin N/L v. FC of T [1949] HCA 15; 78 CLR 47 at 56). The tests propounded by the two limbs of section 51 have the same effect. An employed person has to face no greater barrier to the deductibility of a claim falling under this section than does a person conducting a business as defined in section 6 of the Act. To bring the expenditure within the provisions of the section, a taxpayer must show affirmatively that in each instance the entertainment was both an incident of his office and relevant to the discharge of his duties.

I do not consider on the evidence that the relevant expenses were of a private or domestic nature. They were incurred by the taxpayer by reason of his being the Commandant of the Military District. They were incurred by him either in response to invitations that had been extended to him in that capacity and accepted or as seen by him as being apposite to a proper discharge of his duties.

In D32 72 ATC 1986 the taxpayer a senior, experienced member of the public service claimed, and in due course was by a Taxation Board of Review allowed to deduct expenses incurred in entertaining a high government official, persons in his department, and others associated with his involvement in the duties of his office. The Board was of the view that the evidence as to the expenditure showed "that it was undertaken by the taxpayer only by reason of his official position and furthermore that it did not result from any social or moral obligation arising from his position. In each case the initiative to entertain came from the taxpayer and it was in no sense a return of hospitality previously extended to him..... It lay within the capacity of the persons entertained to assist in bringing about a state of affairs which it was the taxpayer's duty to achieve. As far as the entertainment of subordinates was concerned, it was his duty to have them carry out whatever innovations he directed to be introduced and it was obviously within the scope of his office that this should be done with the maximum of goodwill and co-operation. His entertainment of the persons concerned was intended to achieve this very end..... In our view, the entertainment on each of the occasions with which we are concerned was not of a private nature but was an incident of the discharge of the duties of the taxpayer's official position. In the accepted sense the cost was "incidental and relevant" to the derivation of his official salary. It satisfies the requirement for deductibility prescribed by the first limb of section 51.... The test to be applied is, in our view, quite clear so that the outcome of this reference turns upon the factual nexus between the entertainment and the discharge of the taxpayer's duty..... We do not believe that the taxpayer sought or received any personal benefit from the occasions, but we are convinced that he sought in each case some benefit for the department in which he is employed...." With respect I would adopt the principles as here enunciated and applied by the Board.

D44 72 ATC 252 was also a case that concerned a senior member of the public service who had expended moneys in this instance in respect of entertaining persons engaged in private commerce and industry. The expenditure largely related to his chairmanship of a committee concerned with the letting of contracts and supervision of their performance. At 254 the majority of the Board said "as in Case D32 the position is that the failure of the taxpayer to carry out the entertainment in question would have meant that he was not doing his job as well as possible although he could have fulfilled the bare obligations of his appointment. As in that case the entertainment was an essential element in the proper discharge of his duties....

On the evidence before us, none of the entertainment in question was given for private purposes. It was given by the taxpayer because he felt obliged to. He in fact regarded most of it as burdensome..... In some cases the obligation was one involving a return of hospitality. In these instances it was of importance as a matter of proprietary that it be returned having regard to the sensitivities involved in a government servant receiving hospitality from commercial interests. The original receipt by him of such hospitality was justified because of the necessity for him in his department's interests to keep in touch with the person with whom he was constantly dealing....."

The Commissioner relied upon the decisions in Case M89 80 ATC 642 and Case K28 78 ATC 276 in support of his rejection of the claim. In Case M89 (supra)the taxpayer was a Major in the Army and in his return for the particular year claimed expenses incurred by him in entertaining other Army personnel. At page 646 Mr C.F. Fairleigh QC said:-

"11. On the ordinary civil standard of probabilities the taxpayer has not shown that the meals and beverages which he provided for other officers in restaurants and in hotel dining rooms and which he and his wife provided for other officers and their wives in his home, were other than private or domestic events. It matters not that some army matters particularly relevant to his position and to his hoped for (and later achieved) rank were discussed on such occasions; or that he set out to create in the minds of others an impression favourable to his promotion."

The Commissioner submitted that the entertainment in the subject case was the like of that referred to in M89 (supra), was not relevant to the taxpayer's Army position and was of a private nature. It was said that in the subject case there was not any evidence as to the view of the Department of Defence as to the dunes of the taxpayer, nor any evidence as to such duties being perceived or viewed as different to the position of any other officer of comparable rank in the Australian Armed Forces. True though it be that an officer of the Department was not called and evidence not put as to a perception or view, nevertheless the factual material before the Tribunal clearly enables the drawing of a relevant difference finding is so made.

K28 (supra) was concerned with a taxpayer who was at the relevant time an anaesthetics registrar employed in a public hospital. He claimed a deduction for expenses incurred in entertaining fellow academics and professional men in his field of medicine. Reliance was placed upon what was said, again by Mr C.F. Fairleigh QC, at page 273 namely,

"8. As the taxpayer could net identify any occasion when he set out to achieve or did achieve, or when he had the purpose in mind (to adopt the words from his Notice of Objection) at social functions "to maintain academic contacts... in the field of medicine" (whether or not "purely for the sake of his professional relationship") or "to keep abreast of current developments in the field of anaesthetics....".

It was said on behalf of the Respondent that in the subject case there was not any evidence that at times of entertainment consideration was given to matters pertaining to the Sixth Military District in Tasmania. It is clear on the evidence that it was in the context of the taxpayer's military posting that the entertaining took place and not otherwise.

As has already been stated, the issue in question in this matter is whether the outlays incurred by the taxpayer come within the opening words of section 51(1) that is, "outlays to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income" without being designated as outgoings of a private or domestic nature. The expression "incurred in" is to be understood as has already been mentioned, as "incurred in the course of" and so looks rather to the scope of the operations or activities and the relevance thereto of the expenditure than to purpose in itself (Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Limited v. FC of T (supra).

The use to which the expression "incidental and relevant" is to be put is to state an attribute without which an item of expenditure cannot be regarded as deductible under section 51(1) (Lunney and Haley v. FC of T (1958) 100 CLR 478 at 497). (See also Lodge V. FC of T 72 ATC 4174 at 4175: [1972] HCA 49; (1972) 128 CLR 171 at 174.)

In Frankcom v. FC of T 82 ATC 4599, the taxpayer, a stipendiary magistrate, claimed as a deduction expenditure said to have been incurred in entertaining visiting Judges, magistrates and lawyers. At 4605 in his reasons for judgment Kaye J. said with reference to the entertainment claim, "the hospitality extended by the appellant on those occasions was a gracious act of hospitality extended by him to his and his fellow magistrates' guests. However, the evidence did not provide a proper basis for implying that his duties as a magistrate necessitated him performing the functions of a host by providing drinks and refreshments in these circumstances. It follows that those expenses were of a private nature". I am: satisfied in the present case that the duties of the taxpayer did necessitate him performing the functions of a host as herein before detailed.

I do not consider that the decisions in M89 (supra) referable to the army major, K28 (supra) the hospital registrar or Frankcom dealing with a magistrate, are for the reasons that I have given of further assistance in a determination of this matter.

The taxpayer did not entertain his fellow officers, or at least his claim does not relate to such entertainment. He entertained persons present in Tasmania because of their official standing and by reason of his position as head of the Military Forces in that State. The entertaining was not in any sense for his own benefit. If he did feel obliged it was not a moral obligation, but one arising out of the position that he occupied.

Hence, and for the reasons herein set forth, I am of the opinion that the taxpayer has discharged the onus resting upon him and has established that the expenditure incurred in relation to entertaining was incurred in tie gaining or producing of his assessable income within the meaning of the Act and as such should be allowable to him as a deduction in the amount of $2542.

DECISION

Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal is that the decision of the Respondent Commissioner upon the objection under review be varied and that the objection be, as to $2542, allowed.
