29 June 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



  1. These applications are to review objection decisions against the inclusion in the taxable incomes of the applicants of 2 sums of money. The sums in question resulted from a chain of events in which the applicants were caught up, when they tried to sell their principal place of residence in order to use the proceeds to buy a smaller house in the same area. There is little dispute as to the facts. The questions to be decided turn upon the way in which the 2 sums in question ought to be treated for capital gains tax purposes.

  2. On 11 March 1977, the applicants (whom I will call Mr and Mrs White) purchased for $103,000 a house in a suburb of the Warringah Peninsula in Sydney, which I will refer to as White House. From the time of the purchase until the events to which I will shortly turn, they lived in their house as their principal place of residence.
  3. In 1982 Mr White retired. By February 1989 the applicants decided that the house was larger than their requirements, both having retired and their children having left home. They therefore placed the property on the market for sale and decided to buy a smaller house.
  4. Having done this, they then inspected other properties in the area and found a suitable house nearby, owned by a lady whom I will call Mrs Black. They arranged with Mrs Black for an informal right of first refusal if they found a purchaser for White House.
  5. In April 1989 2 people, whom I will call Mr and Mrs Bird, inspected and negotiated to purchase White House for $906,000. The Birds asked, and it was agreed, that the contract have an extended settlement date of 3 months. As a result of this, Mr and Mrs White agreed with Mrs Black that the contract for the purchase of Black House should allow 90 days for settlement.
  6. On 11 May 1989 Mr and Mrs White exchanged contracts to sell White House to the Birds. The relevant clauses of that contract are as follows —

  2. Simultaneously, Mr and Mrs White exchanged a contract with Mrs Black to purchase Black House. The contract was for a purchase price of $432,500 and provided for a 10 per cent deposit, namely $43,250. This was to be paid to the vendor’s solicitor, the contract having been negotiated without the intervention of an agent. Special condition 35 provided : “Notwithstanding anything to the contrary herein contained, the purchasers agree to release to the vendors half of the deposit and the vendors agree that the balance of the deposit shall be invested in accordance with special condition 31 and that all interest earned thereon shall belong to the purchasers”. It was further provided in the contract that completion was to take place within 90 days and in that respect time was to be of the essence of the agreement.
  3. Notwithstanding the terms of the contract, the whole of that deposit was released to Mrs Black. On the same day she entered into a third transaction and used the deposit received from Mr & Mrs White to pay a deposit on a contract to purchase a property in the Southern Highlands for $160,000
  4. On 10 August 1989, the Birds failed to complete the contract for White House in accordance with its terms. On 14 August 1989, the solicitors for the Whites wrote to the solicitors for the Birds enclosing a notice to complete and demanding payment of the balance of the deposit. The notice to complete expired on 29 August 1989. On that day, the applicants’ solicitors again wrote to the solicitors for the Birds enclosing a notice of termination of the contract in these terms (relevantly) —

  1. At the same time a further demand was made for the balance of the deposit. White House was relisted for sale with a local agent.
  2. On 30 August 1989 solicitors for the Birds wrote to the applicants’ solicitors denying the validity of the termination. This was subsequently to lead to litigation between those parties. In the meantime, the Whites were under an obligation to complete the purchase of Black House from Mrs Black. They negotiated extensions of time for completion of this contract but eventually Mrs Black, who had her own obligations under the third contract, was obliged to take action against the Whites.. Meanwhile, both the Whites and the Birds endeavoured to find a purchaser for or otherwise resolve the sale of White House.
  3. On 20 November 1989, an agent wrote to Mr White stating, inter alia : “In our opinion the present market value of [White House] is in the vicinity of $820,000 and $850,000 ... the local real estate market is rather fluctuating due to day to day change in economic circumstances ... we believe that local real estate values have retreated by approximately 10 per cent from their January 1989 position”.
  4. This advice turned out to accord with subsequent events. Numerous potential purchasers inspected White House but only 2 firm offers were received, each for $830,000. It was not reasonably possible for the applicants to complete the purchase of Black House without selling White House or obtaining bridging finance. In order to resolve the situation, legal proceedings were commenced.

On 17 October 1989, a Summons was filed in the Equity Division of the Supreme Court by the Whites against the Birds seeking —

  4. Interest.
  5. Such further or other relief as the court considered appropriate including (if thought fit) an injunction to restrain the Defendants from lodging a caveat against the title of the subject property.
  6. Costs.

On 19 October 1989, Mrs Black commenced proceedings by Summons against the Whites claiming —

(ii) A declaration that the time for completion of the said contract by agreed extensions between the parties was effective to make time of the essence for completion of the said contract such time for completion being 19th October, 1989.

  1. These summonses were followed by more formal pleadings.

On 12 December 1989 the applicants filed a Statement of Claim stating, inter alia —

  1. Mrs Black was at the same time involved in her own litigation for failing to complete the contract to buy the Southern Highlands property. It is not necessary to follow that litigation to its conclusion.
  2. The litigation between the applicants and the Birds was finally compromised, as is evidenced by a letter from their solicitors to the Birds’ solicitors dated 23 February 1990 (T-66A) in these terms —

No order was made as to costs. The Court then went on to make a declaration that the agreement between the applicants and the Birds was validly terminated and that the defendants had no right, title or interest in the property described in the Court order.

  1. If any further evidence was needed of the correctness of the agent’s reading of the market, it was provided subsequently on 20 November 1992 when the then owners of White House sold it to other unrelated parties for $715,000.
  2. Arising out of this series of transactions, 2 sums of money are said to be taxable in the hands of the applicants for capital gains tax purposes. The first sum is $45,300 which was treated as a capital gain in accordance with s 160ZZC(12) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The respondent included it in the applicants’ income in the year that contracts were exchanged with the Birds, namely the financial year ended 30 June 1989. One half of it was attributed to each of the applicants.
  3. The second sum relates to the $65,000 which was the gross sum ordered to be paid by the Court in the litigation brought against the Birds. From that sum, the respondent deducted the amount of $30,000 which the court ordered the applicants to pay to Mrs Black. The net sum was included in the incomes of the applicants as to one half each in the year that the litigation was finalised, namely the financial year ended 30 June 1990. In relation to both of these amounts, therefore, the relevant legislation is to be considered in its terms prior to the amendments of 25 June 1992.
  4. The first sum to be considered is the forfeited deposit. Section 160ZZC(12) is in the following terms —

“s160ZZC(12)

Where a deposit of money or other consideration, being a deposit that was made in respect of a prospective purchase or other transaction that is cancelled or otherwise abandoned, is forfeited —

  1. the deposit shall be deemed to have been paid or given as consideration in respect of the grant by the person who received the benefit of the forfeiture of an option that bound the grantor to dispose of an asset and was not exercised; and
  2. any costs that the person who received the benefit of the forfeiture incurred in connection with the prospective purchase or other transaction shall be deemed to be amounts of expenditure incurred by that person in respect of the grant of the option.”

  1. It was contended on behalf of the applicants that the sum of $45,300 was not a deposit forfeited as envisaged by this sub-section, since the money was immediately applied by the Whites to their own use. It was not held by a stakeholder to be paid to the Whites on failure to complete. It simply represented (it was argued) the first instalment of the purchase price and those moneys as such were not able to be paid as such or forfeited as such.
  2. It is not necessary, in my view, that a deposit be held by a stakeholder in order to be properly characterised as such. If it is an amount paid to secure a bargain then it seems to me immaterial whether the amount is held directly by the vendor or by a stakeholder. Furthermore, such a sum of money can not be regarded as a part payment of the purchase price and therefore not be subject to forfeiture, unless it be in the circumstances an excessive percentage of the total consideration. There is ample authority, which need not be examined here, that 10 per cent of the consideration may be regarded as susceptible to forfeiture without risk of penalty. In my view, however, the amount in question is not caught by the terms of sub-section (12) on a plain reading of the language of the statute.
  3. Division 13 deals with options generally. The whole of s 160ZZC is devoted to a description of circumstances of various types of options and the taxation consequences of payments made under or in respect of those options, together with provisions for adjustments in the form, for example, of the cost base of an option. It seems to me that sub-section (12) is merely a catch all provision of a type often found at the end of a succession of sub-sections designed to cover specific situations under a general heading. Sub-section (12) is designed to catch any other types of transactions resembling options and any moneys paid in respect of those transactions.
  4. This reading is reinforced by the use of the words “prospective purchase”. The contract entered into between the Whites and the Birds did not contemplate a purchase in the future. It was in itself a contract of purchase. It was not in the nature of an option. It was not preparatory to a binding contract to be entered into in the future. The mere execution of the contract of sale had the effect of selling the property and vesting the equitable title in the purchasers, subject to their discharge of their obligations under that contract on completion.
  5. It seems to me that the respondent has not correctly construed the effect of sub-section (12). The reference to a deposit is not, in my view, a reference to a deposit payable under an immediately operative purchase contract. It is more appropriate to a sum which could be referred to as a “holding deposit”, being an amount paid by the purchaser to the vendor pending entry by the parties into a legally enforceable written contract. In such a case, the payer could reasonably and appropriately be described as a prospective purchaser. In the present case, the payment was made not in respect of a prospective purchase of that or any other nature, but in respect of an actual and immediately effective purchase contract.
  6. It seems to have been assumed in Case 32/94, 94 ATC 298, that a deposit of the nature here under consideration would be caught by sub-section (12). No reference, however, is made in the reasons for decision of the Tribunal to the significance of the use of the word “prospective”. The report indicates that the applicant appeared in person and that an officer of the respondent appeared for the respondent. It may well be that the application of the sub-section was not raised and was not argued. Although I would depart from any other decision of the Tribunal only with great reluctance, it appears to me that with the benefit of full argument from counsel, I should adopt what appears to me to be the preferable view in construing the sub-section.
  7. It follows that the deposit that was forfeited under the contract for sale is not a deposit contemplated by the sub-section and ought not to be included in the income of the applicants for capital gains tax purposes. This will be reflected in my formal decision. For reasons which I will discuss in relation to the second sum, the forfeited deposit should be seen as part of the realisation of the value of an exempt asset, namely a principal place of residence.
  8. The second sum of money, namely, the $65,000 paid by the Birds, less the $30,000 which the Whites paid to Mrs Black, was said to be part of the applicants’ assessable income as a result of a net capital gain, resulting from the disposal of a “right to sue” acquired after 20 September 1985. Section 160A defines “asset” to include ... “an option, a debt, a chose in action, any other right, goodwill and any other form of incorporeal property ...”. It was submitted that the rights accruing to the Whites as a consequence of the default of the Birds constituted a chose in action, which was subsequently disposed of. In support of the submission that the right to sue was disposed of, counsel for the respondent turned to s 160M(3). That provides that a change is to be taken to have occurred in the ownership of an asset by ... “in the case of an asset being a debt, a chose in action or any other right, or an interest or right in or over property — the cancellation, release, discharge, satisfaction, surrender, forfeiture, expiry or abandonment, at law or in equity, of the asset”.
  9. A right to sue is almost by definition a chose in action. The first question to be resolved, therefore, is whether the right to sue in this case was disposed of within the terms of s 160M(3). In my opinion, it was not. The right to sue was not disposed of in the sense that it was surrendered or assigned or parted with in any of the other ways contemplated by the paragraph. It was in fact exercised. Upon commencement of the proceedings in the Supreme Court, the right to sue became a former right and was subsumed into the court proceedings.
  10. It is true that the parties ultimately resolved their differences. It was, however, a condition of their agreement that a court order be made. It was the court’s order, rather than the agreement between the parties, which was the operative force in ultimately disposing of the subject matter of the dispute. It is conceivable that an order of the court need not have been made. The plaintiffs could, for example, have discontinued and relied upon a deed of agreement. They decided however to seek an order and if such order was made, to conclude the dispute upon the basis of that order. There was always the possibility that an order would not be made. If there were any problems, for example, as to jurisdiction or as to approval of an agreement where a minor was concerned, it could not be assumed that the court would simply adopt whatever was agreed among the parties. In any event, the parties considered it essential to the ultimate resolution of their dispute that a court order be made. It was not until this order was made that there was a legal obligation to pay a sum of money in consideration of foregoing the right to sue.
  11. Counsel for the respondent suggested that estoppel may have had a part to play in the disposal of the right to sue. It was suggested that once a binding agreement as to a compromise was reached and such agreement was relied on by a party acting on it, the other party would be estopped from continuing to pursue the proceedings. That may well be the case in another factual situation. Here, where the parties made an agreement conditional upon a court making an order, it could not be said to have legal effect until such time as that order was made. In any event, a situation of estoppel which merely denies to one party the right to assert something contrary to the terms of the estoppel, does not fit easily into the wording adopted in paragraph 160M(3)(b) to indicate a change of ownership in an asset, nor would one expect such a concept to constitute such a change. I consider therefore that there was no disposal of the right to sue and that consequently the pre-requisites to the inclusion of the damages in the taxable income of the applicants does not exist.
  12. If I am wrong in this approach, then the next question to ask is what was the relevant asset. The respondent’s approach to the whole assessment has been to see if any individual part of the matrix of facts could be fitted into any of the headings of assessability. Quite complex submissions were made in the respondent’s statement of facts and contentions, analysing each step and attributing a head of liability to this or that element of an isolated transaction, rather than to the series of transactions as a whole. In doing so, it adopted a certain approach to the interpretation of statutory provisions which were described by Chief Justice Mason in Hepples v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4808 at 4810 as “extraordinarily complex”. At page 4819 Deane J observed that : “Complicated and even obscure taxation provisions may be necessary ... to deal with technical situations ...”. It hardly seems necessary to state that the present situation is not technical. It deals principally with the legal misfortunes of 3 families endeavouring to change their principal place of residence in an unstable economic climate. Neither the contractual relations into which they entered, nor the litigation in which they found themselves, was particularly technical. In their minds, payments in and out had more to do with their housing problems than with the extraordinarily complex provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act.
  13. In Zim Properties Pty Limited v Proctor (1984) 58 TC 371 at 392 Warner J, a judge of the Chancery Division of the English High Court of Justice observed —

“I have no difficulty in accepting that not every right to a payment is an “asset” within the meaning of that term in the capital gains tax legislation. Perhaps the most obvious example of one that is not is the right of a seller of property to payment of its price. The relevant asset, then, is the property itself. What that shows, however to my mind, is no more than that the interpretation of the capital gains tax legislation requires, as does the interpretation of any legislation, the exercise of common sense, rather than just the brute application of verbal formulae. In saying that I am, I think, expressing much the same thought as was expressed by Lord Wilberforce in Aberdeen Construction Group Limited v Commissioners of Inland Revenue 52 TC 281.”

  1. Zim is not directly binding upon this Tribunal. It has however been considered in a number of Australian cases (although significantly not by any judge of the High Court in Hepples) and does give some assistance in isolating the issues to be determined in cases of this nature. I am persuaded by the above reasoning, in particular, that the reality of the transaction ought to be determinative of the relevant asset. In this case, I consider it to be the underlying asset, namely, White House. Happily, I can comfortably arrive at the same common sense conclusion through a similar process of analysis adopted by the respondent.
  2. Counsel for the respondent conceded that where compensation is received for permanent damage to an underlying asset, then the relevant asset would be the underlying asset. In the present case, the court ordered damages to be paid to the applicants. Damages are intended to put the plaintiff in the same position that he would have been in had the wrong, or breach of contract, not being committed. The Whites did suffer a loss, in the sense that when White House was ultimately sold it was for a sum less than that agreed to be paid by the Birds. They suffered a loss of part of their bargain. The evidence indicates that there was a falling property market at the time. It is true that the default of the Birds did not cause this drop in value. It is also true, however, that had they completed in accordance with their contractual obligations, then the applicants would not have lost the benefit of the contract and would have been better off to the extent of the difference between the 2 purchase prices.
  3. It is true that after termination of the contract the applicants were still left with the house which they subsequently sold. Nevertheless their economic situation was different from that in which they found themselves when the first contract was exchanged and when they had a reasonable expectation of being paid the agreed sum of money. It was the default of the Birds that caused this change in their economic circumstances. This change was integral to their attempt to realise a particular asset. The damages ordered by the court were intended to put the Whites back in the same position as if their situation had not been so changed. The default of the Birds therefore is organically related, not so much to the change in value of the underlying asset, but to the change in economic circumstances of the applicants. That being so, I consider that the relevant asset was the underlying asset rather than a right to sue.
  4. There have been 4 cases decided in Australia dealing with the question whether damages in certain factual situations, could be the subject of capital tax. They are Provan v HCL Real Estate Limited and Others 92 ATC 4644, Tuite and another v Exelby and Others 93 ATC 4293, Carborundum Realty Pty Limited v RAIA Archicentre Pty Limited and Another (NO 2) 93 ATC 4418 and Namol Pty Limited and Another v AW Baulderstone Pty Limited and Others (NO 2) 93 ATC 5101. In none of these cases has the Commissioner been a party. The arguments put forward, with varying degrees of success, were directed to increasing the amount of damages payable by a defendant to a plaintiff by way of indemnification against possible capital gains tax liability. The interests of the parties may have ensured that matters not relevant to the issues before me may have been the subject of submissions and decisions. Certainly the absence of the Commissioner meant that the view of the revenue was not put before any of the courts. I do not consider that any of the cases can be regarded as offering any assistance in the resolution of the present situation.
  5. The reasoning in Zim must also be regarded as of limited assistance. The litigation in that case was not between the vendor and purchaser of the property, but between the vendor and its solicitors in an action for negligence based on the conduct of the solicitors in handling a particular property transaction. The damages paid in that case are not so close to the real estate as the present damages are to the asset which I regard as the underlying asset in the present case. Furthermore the plaintiff’s case was based upon a claim for compensation for the fall in property values. As I have pointed out, my view is not that the default of the Birds in the present case caused the drop in property values (an unsustainable proposition) but that their default caused the capital loss of the applicants which was compensated for to some extent by damages. If there was no relationship between the default of the Birds and that capital loss, then there would have been no basis for the order by the court to pay damages.
  6. As I have reached the conclusion that the relevant asset (if indeed there was a disposal) was the underlying asset and as there is no dispute that White House, the underlying asset, was the applicants’ principal place of residence and that any or all of the proceeds of sale, would have been free of capital gains tax (s 160ZZQ), it follows that the damages ordered by the court ought not to be included in the income of the applicants for capital gains tax purposes. It is therefore not necessary to consider the question whether an overall loss occurred or whether there is any place for the operation of s 160M(7) in the present circumstances. The argument that s 160M(7) has application as a catch all section can not be sustained. It was submitted that if the underlying asset were held to be White House, then it would be necessary to find that it was not disposed of in terms of any of “the other provisions of this part” and that therefore s 160M(7) would have a fall back operation. I do not see this as necessarily following. If, contrary to my first conclusion under this point, it is shown that there has been a disposal, then it would have been pursuant to s 160M(3) in which case there would be no room for the operation of the fall back provision.
  7. The objection decisions are therefore set aside and the matters are remitted to the respondent with the directions that the sums of $45,300 should not be included in the applicants’ taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1989 and the net sum of $35,000 should not be included in their taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1990 and that amended assessments be issued in accordance with these directions.
