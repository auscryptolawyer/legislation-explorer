CATCHWORDS

Taxation - deductions - legal expenses, investigation expenses, interest, property expenses, borrowing expenses, depreciation - alleged gifts - penalties.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s.51(1)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 - s.14ZZK     

REASONS FOR DECISION

For NT96/3-9

14 February 1997 Deputy President A M Blow OAM, QC.,



1. Applications to the Tribunal have been made by five members of the same family. Applications NT96/3 to 5 relate to Mr. A. Applications NT96/6 to 9 relate to Mrs. A. Applications NT96/14 to 17 relate to their son, Mr. A. Junior. Applications NT96/10 to 13 relate to his wife. Applications NT96/18 to 21 relate to the daughter of Mr. and Mrs. A. All are beneficiaries under the trust deed of a family trust. The trustee is a company which I will refer to as B Pty Limited. During all relevant financial years B Pty Limited resolved to distribute the trust’s profits equally amongst the five applicants.

2. The matters in dispute between the applicants and the respondent are as follows:

(a) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of legal expenses in relation to the 1987, 1988 and 1989 tax years. In relation to 1989, the amount claimed includes a sum of $7,000 charged by a private inquiry agent.

(b) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of interest and bank charges for the 1989, 1990 and 1991 tax years in respect of a property.

(c) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of rates, taxes and insurance premiums for the same property for the same tax years.

(d) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, a deduction in in respect of borrowing expenses in the 1991 tax year.

(e) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of depreciation in respect of certain machines in relation to the tax years from 1989 to 1993 inclusive.

(f) The respondent asserts, and Mr. and Mrs. A. deny, that two payments totalling $55,358 made to the credit of a joint bank account held by them in March and May 1991 represented income received by them.

Legal Expenses

3. For some years the A family has carried on or at least controlled a business involving a quantity of coin-operated machines. Machines are placed in public places, such as bars and coffee lounges. The proprietors of the places where they are placed are given an agreed percentage of their takings. The applicants contend that at all material times B Pty Limited was the proprietor of the business and the owner of all relevant machines.

4. Mr. A gave evidence that in or about 1987 a quantity of machines were imported from overseas. Customs duties were paid on the machines. Mr. A then learned from a notice in a newspaper that the machines were or might be prohibited under State law. He said at about that time the police confiscated some other machines, that there was a court case, and that the police were unsuccessful in it. He said that in about late 1988 or early 1989 the same machines were confiscated a second time and another prosecution commenced. The police were successful in the second prosecution and “the matter went to the Court of Appeal to try and get the machines back”. It appears that the matter did not go to an appellate court, but to a single judge who refused to grant a declaration that the seizure of the machines was illegal. It appears that solicitors’ costs and barristers’ fees were incurred in defending the two prosecutions, and in prosecuting the proceedings for declaratory relief.

5. It appears that the defendant in both prosecutions was Mr. A. Junior. The plaintiff in the proceedings for a declaration was Mr. A. B Pty Limited appears not to have been a party to any of the proceedings.

6. The claims made in respect of legal expenses are for $521 in the 1987 tax year, $6,000 in the 1988 tax year, and $35,500 (apart from investigative expenses and fines) in the 1989 tax year. There is evidence that a cheque for $521 was written in favour of a solicitor on 24 November 1986, but no evidence as to what work that cheque related to. There is no evidence before the Tribunal of the payment of $6,000 in the 1988 year. The evidence before the Tribunal includes bills which Mr. A received from his solicitor during the following year - one dated 15 July 1988 for $15,000, and one dated 22 May 1989 for $30,200. Although the sum of these two bills exceeds the deduction claimed for 1989, no explanation of that discrepancy has been forthcoming. Both bills appear to relate to the proceedings for a declaration.

7. The machines to which those proceedings related were identified by the brand name of a well known Australian company. It would appear from paragraphs 14 and 15 of a statement made by Mr. A for the purpose of these proceedings (Exhibit A1) that these were the machines confiscated on both occasions. There is no evidence that they were owned by B Pty Limited. Although B Pty Limited listed a number of machines in its depreciation schedules, there was no evidence that any of the confiscated machines were identical with any of the machines listed in those schedules. There is nothing in Mr. A’s statement or oral evidence, or any other evidence before the Tribunal, that would enable the Tribunal to determine the identity of the owner of the machines.

8. The deductability of legal expenses, like other business expenses, falls to be considered under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). That sub-section reads as follows:-

“All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”


9. The applicants bear an onus of proof pursuant to s.14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. They have failed to establish that B Pty Limited was the owner of the machines to which the legal proceedings related. Even if B Pty Limited was the proprietor of a coin-operated machine business, they have failed to establish that the machines to which the proceedings related were used in that business, or were ever intended to be used in that business. They have failed to establish that the legal expenses were incurred by B Pty Limited at all, or had any connection with that company gaining or producing assessable income. For these reasons I am not satisfied that there is a sufficient connection between the legal expenses and any business or income producing activities of B Pty Limited sufficient to satisfy s.51 of the Act.

Investigation Expenses

10. The applicants contend that, in connection with the legal proceedings I have referred to, they paid $7,000 to an investigation service in early 1989. For the reasons that I have stated in relation to the legal expenses, I am not satisfied that there is a sufficient link between this expenditure, if it was really incurred at all, and any business or income producing activities of B Pty Limited. The applicants’ case appears to be that a private investigator was hired to locate and observe similar machines belonging to companies unconnected with the A family and B Pty Limited, apparently with a view to establishing that the police were not taking any action in relation to such other machines. I do not see how the incurring of such expenditure could in any way have helped B Pty Limited. What is more, a letter from the private investigator (Document T45) shows that the sum of $7,000 was paid not by B Pty Limited, but by either Mr. A or Mr. A Junior. The claim in respect of this amount cannot succeed.

Interest and Bank Charges

11. The deductions claimed relate to a period from 15 March 1989 to 30 June 1991. They relate to mortgage interest and bank charges paid in respect of a mortgage over a property from which it is contended that B Pty Limited carried on business.

12. The property was purchased in an arm’s length transaction in 1984 by Mr. and Mrs. A and another couple, whom I will call Mr. and Mrs. C. There is evidence, which I have no reason to disbelieve, that there was a falling out between Mr. A and Mr. C, but that Mr. A continued to use the property. According to Mr. A, he got into financial difficulty and arranged for a group of people to take over the property. By a transfer dated 15 March 1989, the property was transferred to a collection of companies, couples and individuals. Mr. A became the registered proprietor of a 1/7th share as a tenant in common. When the transfer to the new team of registered proprietors was registered, the next dealing registered was a mortgage to the Commonwealth Bank of Australia, which was no doubt given by the new registered proprietors. The interest and bank charges for which deductions are claimed appear to have been paid pursuant to that mortgage.

13. There was no formal arrangement for B Pty Limited to pay the interest or the bank charges. It did not have a lease of the property. It had no contractual obligation to make any payments of interest or bank charges. Mr. A gave evidence, which I have no reason to doubt, that he paid the mortgage payments on the property using the cheque book of B Pty Limited, and did so on his own initiative. I am left with the impression that the mortgage payments, including payments of interest and bank charges, were thus made by B Pty Limited, but that that occurred only because its account was a convenient source of funds which could be used to satisfy the registered proprietors’ obligations to the bank. Mr. A, like his fellow registered proprietors, was no doubt personally liable pursuant to the mortgage to pay the interest and bank charges. There is no evidence that the company had any contractual liability to make those payments. It seems likely that the payments made by it ought to have been recorded in its books as payments made by it at the request of Mr. A to satisfy a liability personal to him, and ought therefore to have been debited to his loan account with the company.

14. In accordance with general principles, interest is deductable as a business expense under s.51(1) of the Act only if it is incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or carrying on a business for the purposes of gaining or producing assessable income. The evidence certainly falls short of establishing that the relevant payments were made by B Pty Limited for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, or in the course of any business carried on by it. I do not think that I need make any finding as to whether B Pty Limited used the premises for business purposes. Even if it did, there is no evidence to suggest that the use of its cheque account, rather than some other source of funds, had any connection with its use or occupation of the premises. The claims for these deductions must fail.

Rates, Taxes and Insurance Premiums

15. It follows from what I have concluded in relation to mortgage interest and bank charges that these claims must also fail. They relate to the same property for the same period. Further, s.72 of the Act permits deductions in relation to rates and taxes only if the taxpayer is “personally liable”, and there is no evidence that B Pty Limited was personally liable for them, either on a statutory basis or on a contractual basis.

Borrowing Expenses

16. A deduction of $1,900 was claimed for borrowing expenses in the 1991 tax year. As I understand it, the deduction that was claimed relates to expenses incurred by the registered proprietors of the property that I have referred to. Even if B Pty Limited paid those expenses, the claim for this deduction must still fail, since there is no evidence that it did so for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, or in the course of any business carried on by it. There is no evidence to suggest that the situation in relation to these expenses is any different from the situation in relation to mortgage interest payments.

Depreciation

17. In the tax years 1989 to 1993 inclusive, B Pty Limited claimed a deduction for depreciation in respect of certain coin-operated machines whose original cost was shown to be $90,000. They were not described as coin-operated machines in any return before the Tribunal. In the 1989 return they were described only by reference to the address of the property that I have referred to. In later returns they were described as “fixtures” (which they were not), and as “plant and equipment” and “second hand equipment”.

18. Mr. A and his accountant gave different versions of the background to these claims. I prefer the evidence of the accountant. His evidence was detailed, whereas that of Mr. A. was vague and left too much unexplained.

19. The accountant explained that the A family and the C family set up a company, which I will call D Pty Limited. That company imported a quantity of coin-operated machines. After the falling out between Mr. A and Mr. C, the company ceased trading in late 1986 or early 1987. As at 30 June 1986, the books of D Pty Limited showed a loan account balance owing by that company to Mr. A of $109,106.82. In March 1989 the accountant proposed to Mr. C that the machines belonging to D Pty Limited be transferred to Mr. A “to take up part of his loan account”. The machines are treated in the tax returns of B Pty Limited as having been acquired by it on 15 March 1989 for $90,000. No evidence was given as to any arrangement for their sale by Mr. A to B Pty Limited then at that price.

20. Under s.54(1) of the Act, a deduction for depreciation may be allowed only when the taxpayer has used the plant or articles in question during the year in question for the purpose of producing assessable income, or held the relevant plant or articles in reserve for that purpose. That sub-section reads as follows:-

“Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction.”


  1. Even if the machines were owned by B Pty Limited, the evidence does not establish that during any of the relevant years they, or any of them, were used for the purpose of producing assessable income, nor that they, or any of them, were ready for use for that purpose and held in reserve during any relevant year.
  2. The accountant gave evidence that the machines in respect of which the deduction was claimed could not be used because their use was unlawful. They were imported whereas the proceedings for declaratory relief concerned machines with an Australian brand name. Mr. A gave evidence that machines of the type in question become obsolete fairly quickly, because the industry is very fast-moving. He also gave evidence that there were still about 97 or 98 of the illegal machines in storage, that there was absolutely no intention of them being used, and that he had tried to sell them in other countries, including Indonesia, the USA and Japan, but that no one was interested in purchasing them because they were “older type machines”. The evidence strongly suggests that the machines were obsolete, not in use, and not intended to be used, throughout the years in respect of which deductions for their depreciation were claimed. The claims for such deductions therefore cannot succeed.

Alleged Gifts

  1. At the beginning of March 1991, Mr. and Mrs. A owed their bank $54,242.16 in respect of a loan. During that month, a sum of $20,000 was deposited to the credit of the account relating to that loan. In May 1991, a further deposit of $35,358.69 was made, by way of full payment of the outstanding principal, interest and bank charges. The respondent contends that these two payments represent assessable income received by Mr. and Mrs. A. Mr. and Mrs. A contend that both payments were proceeds of gifts by Mrs. A’s mother, who has since died.
  2. Mrs. A was not available to attend the hearing because of illness in the family, but gave evidence by telephone. Normally I would not have permitted such a course to be taken, given that her credibility was in issue, but I took telephone evidence on this occasion because there seemed no other practical way of getting her evidence before the Tribunal. Her evidence was given on affirmation.
  3. She had signed a statement relating to these two payments for the purpose of these proceedings. She gave evidence that the contents of the statement were true. That statement included the following assertions:-
“3 ... When my father died ... in 1988 he had very few assets. He had a share in a home unit at ... and certain furniture and furnishings of no commercial value and about $20,000 in cash. My mother had found this money after my father’s death, hidden in a drawer of his desk.

4. After my father died I looked after my mother. She was getting very old and frail. In or about May 1991 it was decided that my mother should go into a nursing home at .... Because she did not want to take her money with her, and also, because I had cared for her and my father, she gave me the $20,000 that she had inherited from my father....

5. A few months later my mother gave me a further $35,000 in cash, which together with the money from my father, made up the whole of her life savings...”.


  1. In her evidence in chief, Mrs. A said that there came a time when her mother had to go into a nursing home, and that her mother just gave her all the money she had because she did not want to take it with her. She spoke as if there had been one payment, not two. During her cross-examination, she said that her mother gave her the $20,000 at the time that she entered the nursing home. She said that her mother kept it in a drawer in her bathroom. In response to questions from me, she said that her mother had made two payments, the first from money in her father’s desk, and the second when her mother went to the nursing home. If her oral evidence were entirely correct, the payment of $20,000 would have been the second payment, but there is a bank statement before the Tribunal that reveals it to have been the first. She went on to say that her mother found the money in her father’s desk drawer and gave it to her on the same day that she found it. That is inconsistent with her written statement, in which she said that her mother “had found” the $20,000 after her father’s death, hidden a drawer of his desk.
  2. Mrs. A said that no one else was present when the two amounts were given to her by her mother. However Mr. A also gave some evidence relevant to this issue. In the written statement I have referred to (Exhibit A1), he said that his parents-in-law distrusted banks and such institutions, and that it was customary in their village in their country of origin to keep their money at home. He said their distrust arose from their experiences during and after World War II, and that they had had a very bad experience after migrating to Australia at the hands of an unscrupulous person. However the respondent adduced evidence of both Mrs. A’s parents having had bank accounts with substantial credit balances.
  3. Mrs. A’s mother was the executrix of her late husband. She obtained a grant of probate of his will some 18 months after his death. Documents relating to her application for probate, which are before the Tribunal, reveal that she deposed to her late husband having had only $20 by way of “money in house or hand” at the time of his death.
  4. Some of the business activities with which Mr. and Mrs. A were associated in and around 1991 were of such a nature that they are likely to have received substantial amounts of income in cash. The version of events given by Mrs. A - that her late mother gave her two large cash payments - is of such a nature that it is easy to invent and almost impossible to disprove. Apart from the inconsistencies that I have referred to above, there was nothing in the way Mrs. A gave her oral evidence to suggest that she was lying. However it is extremely improbable that her widowed mother would have retained cash of the order of $55,000 in her home years after the death of her father. Such a contention is contradicted by the probate inventory which was sworn to by her mother. Further, if Mrs. A lied about the source of the two payments, there is no real chance that Mr. A innocently believed her version of events to be the truth. Any evaluation of the credibility of that version of events must therefore involve an evaluation of Mr. A’s credibility. I found him to be a most unimpressive witness. I have already referred to the inconsistences between his evidence and that of his accountant in relation to the depreciation claims. My impression of him is such that I cannot accept with any confidence any evidence given by him favourable to his case without corroborration from an independent source. That applies particularly to his evidence that his wife told him these monies came from her mother.
  5. For these reasons, I am not satisfied that the deposits of $20,000 and $35,358 represent the proceeds of gifts by Mrs. A’s mother. The taxpayers have failed to discharge the onus of proof imposed by s.14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. Their objection in relation to these amounts being treated as assessable income must fail.

Penalties

  1. In their applications for review, the applicants contended that the respondent was wrong in concluding that false and misleading statements had been made contrary to the former s.223(8) of the Act, and that therefore the respondent erred in imposing penalties for incorrect returns pursuant to the former s.223(1). For the reasons stated I above, I believe that each return in question contained false and misleading statements, and that the imposition of penalties for incorrect returns was therefore appropriate. No submissions were made as to the sizes of the penalties imposed. I believe that each of the penalties imposed was appropriate, if not lenient. The challenges to the penalities must fail.
  2. For these reasons, I have decided to affirm all the decisions under review.

REASONS FOR DECISION

For NT9610 -21

14 February 1997 Deputy President A M Blow OAM, QC.,



1. Applications to the Tribunal have been made by five members of the same family. Applications NT96/3 to 5 relate to Mr. A. Applications NT96/6 to 9 relate to Mrs. A. Applications NT96/14 to 17 relate to their son, Mr. A. Junior. Applications NT96/10 to 13 relate to his wife. Applications NT96/18 to 21 relate to the daughter of Mr. and Mrs. A. All are beneficiaries under the trust deed of a family trust. The trustee is a company which I will refer to as B Pty Limited. During all relevant financial years B Pty Limited resolved to distribute the trust’s profits equally amongst the five applicants.

2. The matters in dispute between all five applicants and the respondent are as follows:

(a) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of legal expenses in relation to the 1987, 1988 and 1989 tax years. In relation to 1989, the amount claimed includes a sum of $7,000 charged by a private inquiry agent.

(b) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of interest and bank charges for the 1989, 1990 and 1991 tax years in respect of a property.

(c) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of rates, taxes and insurance premiums for the same property for the same tax years.

(d) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, a deduction in in respect of borrowing expenses in the 1991 tax year.

(e) B Pty Limited has claimed, and the respondent has disallowed, deductions in respect of depreciation in respect of certain machines in relation to the tax years from 1989 to 1993 inclusive.

Legal Expenses

3. For some years the A family has carried on or at least controlled a business involving a quantity of coin-operated machines. Machines are placed in public places, such as bars and coffee lounges. The proprietors of the places where they are placed are given an agreed percentage of their takings. The applicants contend that at all material times B Pty Limited was the proprietor of the business and the owner of all relevant machines.

4. Mr. A gave evidence that in or about 1987 a quantity of machines were imported from overseas. Customs duties were paid on the machines. Mr. A then learned from a notice in a newspaper that the machines were or might be prohibited under State law. He said at about that time the police confiscated some other machines, that there was a court case, and that the police were unsuccessful in it. He said that in about late 1988 or early 1989 the same machines were confiscated a second time and another prosecution commenced. The police were successful in the second prosecution and “the matter went to the Court of Appeal to try and get the machines back”. It appears that the matter did not go to an appellate court, but to a single judge who refused to grant a declaration that the seizure of the machines was illegal. It appears that solicitors’ costs and barristers’ fees were incurred in defending the two prosecutions, and in prosecuting the proceedings for declaratory relief.

5. It appears that the defendant in both prosecutions was Mr. A. Junior. The plaintiff in the proceedings for a declaration was Mr. A. B Pty Limited appears not to have been a party to any of the proceedings.

6. The claims made in respect of legal expenses are for $521 in the 1987 tax year, $6,000 in the 1988 tax year, and $35,500 (apart from investigative expenses and fines) in the 1989 tax year. There is evidence that a cheque for $521 was written in favour of a solicitor on 24 November 1986, but no evidence as to what work that cheque related to. There is no evidence before the Tribunal of the payment of $6,000 in the 1988 year. The evidence before the Tribunal includes bills which Mr. A received from his solicitor during the following year - one dated 15 July 1988 for $15,000, and one dated 22 May 1989 for $30,200. Although the sum of these two bills exceeds the deduction claimed for 1989, no explanation of that discrepancy has been forthcoming. Both bills appear to relate to the proceedings for a declaration.

7. The machines to which those proceedings related were identified by the brand name of a well known Australian company. It would appear from paragraphs 14 and 15 of a statement made by Mr. A for the purpose of these proceedings (Exhibit A1) that these were the machines confiscated on both occasions. There is no evidence that they were owned by B Pty Limited. Although B Pty Limited listed a number of machines in its depreciation schedules, there was no evidence that any of the confiscated machines were identical with any of the machines listed in those schedules. There is nothing in Mr. A’s statement or oral evidence, or any other evidence before the Tribunal, that would enable the Tribunal to determine the identity of the owner of the machines.

8. The deductability of legal expenses, like other business expenses, falls to be considered under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). That sub-section reads as follows:-

“All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”


9. The applicants bear an onus of proof pursuant to s.14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. They have failed to establish that B Pty Limited was the owner of the machines to which the legal proceedings related. Even if B Pty Limited was the proprietor of a coin-operated machine business, they have failed to establish that the machines to which the proceedings related were used in that business, or were ever intended to be used in that business. They have failed to establish that the legal expenses were incurred by B Pty Limited at all, or had any connection with that company gaining or producing assessable income. For these reasons I am not satisfied that there is a sufficient connection between the legal expenses and any business or income producing activities of B Pty Limited sufficient to satisfy s.51 of the Act.

Investigation Expenses

10. The applicants contend that, in connection with the legal proceedings I have referred to, they paid $7,000 to an investigation service in early 1989. For the reasons that I have stated in relation to the legal expenses, I am not satisfied that there is a sufficient link between this expenditure, if it was really incurred at all, and any business or income producing activities of B Pty Limited. The applicants’ case appears to be that a private investigator was hired to locate and observe similar machines belonging to companies unconnected with the A family and B Pty Limited, apparently with a view to establishing that the police were not taking any action in relation to such other machines. I do not see how the incurring of such expenditure could in any way have helped B Pty Limited. What is more, a letter from the private investigator (Document T45) shows that the sum of $7,000 was paid not by B Pty Limited, but by either Mr. A or Mr. A Junior. The claim in respect of this amount cannot succeed.

Interest and Bank Charges

11. The deductions claimed relate to a period from 15 March 1989 to 30 June 1991. They relate to mortgage interest and bank charges paid in respect of a mortgage over a property from which it is contended that B Pty Limited carried on business.

12. The property was purchased in an arm’s length transaction in 1984 by Mr. and Mrs. A and another couple, whom I will call Mr. and Mrs. C. There is evidence, which I have no reason to disbelieve, that there was a falling out between Mr. A and Mr. C, but that Mr. A continued to use the property. According to Mr. A, he got into financial difficulty and arranged for a group of people to take over the property. By a transfer dated 15 March 1989, the property was transferred to a collection of companies, couples and individuals. Mr. A became the registered proprietor of a 1/7th share as a tenant in common. When the transfer to the new team of registered proprietors was registered, the next dealing registered was a mortgage to the Commonwealth Bank of Australia, which was no doubt given by the new registered proprietors. The interest and bank charges for which deductions are claimed appear to have been paid pursuant to that mortgage.

13. There was no formal arrangement for B Pty Limited to pay the interest or the bank charges. It did not have a lease of the property. It had no contractual obligation to make any payments of interest or bank charges. Mr. A gave evidence, which I have no reason to doubt, that he paid the mortgage payments on the property using the cheque book of B Pty Limited, and did so on his own initiative. I am left with the impression that the mortgage payments, including payments of interest and bank charges, were thus made by B Pty Limited, but that that occurred only because its account was a convenient source of funds which could be used to satisfy the registered proprietors’ obligations to the bank. Mr. A, like his fellow registered proprietors, was no doubt personally liable pursuant to the mortgage to pay the interest and bank charges. There is no evidence that the company had any contractual liability to make those payments. It seems likely that the payments made by it ought to have been recorded in its books as payments made by it at the request of Mr. A to satisfy a liability personal to him, and ought therefore to have been debited to his loan account with the company.

14. In accordance with general principles, interest is deductable as a business expense under s.51(1) of the Act only if it is incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or carrying on a business for the purposes of gaining or producing assessable income. The evidence certainly falls short of establishing that the relevant payments were made by B Pty Limited for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, or in the course of any business carried on by it. I do not think that I need make any finding as to whether B Pty Limited used the premises for business purposes. Even if it did, there is no evidence to suggest that the use of its cheque account, rather than some other source of funds, had any connection with its use or occupation of the premises. The claims for these deductions must fail.

Rates, Taxes and Insurance Premiums

15. It follows from what I have concluded in relation to mortgage interest and bank charges that these claims must also fail. They relate to the same property for the same period. Further, s.72 of the Act permits deductions in relation to rates and taxes only if the taxpayer is “personally liable”, and there is no evidence that B Pty Limited was personally liable for them, either on a statutory basis or on a contractual basis.

Borrowing Expenses

16. A deduction of $1,900 was claimed for borrowing expenses in the 1991 tax year. As I understand it, the deduction that was claimed relates to expenses incurred by the registered proprietors of the property that I have referred to. Even if B Pty Limited paid those expenses, the claim for this deduction must still fail, since there is no evidence that it did so for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, or in the course of any business carried on by it. There is no evidence to suggest that the situation in relation to these expenses is any different from the situation in relation to mortgage interest payments.

Depreciation

17. In the tax years 1989 to 1993 inclusive, B Pty Limited claimed a deduction for depreciation in respect of certain coin-operated machines whose original cost was shown to be $90,000. They were not described as coin-operated machines in any return before the Tribunal. In the 1989 return they were described only by reference to the address of the property that I have referred to. In later returns they were described as “fixtures” (which they were not), and as “plant and equipment” and “second hand equipment”.

18. Mr. A and his accountant gave different versions of the background to these claims. I prefer the evidence of the accountant. His evidence was detailed, whereas that of Mr. A. was vague and left too much unexplained.

19. The accountant explained that the A family and the C family set up a company, which I will call D Pty Limited. That company imported a quantity of coin-operated machines. After the falling out between Mr. A and Mr. C, the company ceased trading in late 1986 or early 1987. As at 30 June 1986, the books of D Pty Limited showed a loan account balance owing by that company to Mr. A of $109,106.82. In March 1989 the accountant proposed to Mr. C that the machines belonging to D Pty Limited be transferred to Mr. A “to take up part of his loan account”. The machines are treated in the tax returns of B Pty Limited as having been acquired by it on 15 March 1989 for $90,000. No evidence was given as to any arrangement for their sale by Mr. A to B Pty Limited then at that price.

20. Under s.54(1) of the Act, a deduction for depreciation may be allowed only when the taxpayer has used the plant or articles in question during the year in question for the purpose of producing assessable income, or held the relevant plant or articles in reserve for that purpose. That sub-section reads as follows:-

“Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction.”


  1. Even if the machines were owned by B Pty Limited, the evidence does not establish that during any of the relevant years they, or any of them, were used for the purpose of producing assessable income, nor that they, or any of them, were ready for use for that purpose and held in reserve during any relevant year.
  2. The accountant gave evidence that the machines in respect of which the deduction was claimed could not be used because their use was unlawful. They were imported whereas the proceedings for declaratory relief concerned machines with an Australian brand name. Mr. A gave evidence that machines of the type in question become obsolete fairly quickly, because the industry is very fast-moving. He also gave evidence that there were still about 97 or 98 of the illegal machines in storage, that there was absolutely no intention of them being used, and that he had tried to sell them in other countries, including Indonesia, the USA and Japan, but that no one was interested in purchasing them because they were “older type machines”. The evidence strongly suggests that the machines were obsolete, not in use, and not intended to be used, throughout the years in respect of which deductions for their depreciation were claimed. The claims for such deductions therefore cannot succeed.

Penalties

  1. In their applications for review, the applicants contended that the respondent was wrong in concluding that false and misleading statements had been made contrary to the former s.223(8) of the Act, and that therefore the respondent erred in imposing penalties for incorrect returns pursuant to the former s.223(1). For the reasons stated I above, I believe that each return in question contained false and misleading statements, and that the imposition of penalties for incorrect returns was therefore appropriate. No submissions were made as to the sizes of the penalties imposed. I believe that each of the penalties imposed was appropriate, if not lenient. The challenges to the penalities must fail.
  2. For these reasons, I have decided to affirm all the decisions under review.
