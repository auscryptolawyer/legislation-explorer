Alexander Pty Ltd and Tax Agents Board of Victoria [1999] AATA 821; (1999) 43 ATR 1163; 99 ATC 2338 (3 November 1999)

Last Updated: 8 October 2009





DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 821

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No V1999/789

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re RUSSELL J ALEXANDER PTY LTD

Applicant



And TAX AGENTS BOARD OF VICTORIA

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



Date 3 November 1999

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and, in its stead, suspends the registration of the applicant as a tax agent for three months commencing 1 January 2000.



.......(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe..........

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

TAX AGENT REGISTRATION – cancellation of registration – whether nominee not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns – whether executive officer not of good fame, integrity and character – failure to respond to Board – carrying on a business after cessation of registration – whether penalty appropriate

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 251K(2)

Statos v Tax Agents Board of NSW 90 ATC 4950

Re Su and the Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 82 ATC 4284



REASONS FOR DECISION



3 November 1999 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



1. This is an application to review a decision of the Tax Agents Board Victoria ("the Board") to cancel the registration of the applicant company as a tax agent. The decision was made pursuant to section 251K(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr T. Hurley of counsel and the respondent by Mr E. Evagorou of the Australian Government Solicitor's Office. Evidence was given by Mr Alexander, a director and nominee of the applicant company, Russell J Alexander Pty Ltd, and by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office ("the ATO"), Ms N.

3. At the commencement of the hearing the Tribunal drew to the attention of the parties that the application had been lodged out of time but no extension of time had been sought and that the application had been made in the name of Mr Alexander not the company whose registration had been cancelled. By consent, the Tribunal granted the requisite extension of time in which to lodge the application and amended the name of the applicant to Russell J Alexander Pty Ltd.

4. This matter has a long history with the Board. The applicant was registered as a tax agent in July 1992 with Mr Alexander as nominee. In August 1993 the Board wrote to the applicant requesting an explanation of issues raised by a taxpayer client of the applicant. In September 1993 a further letter was sent relating to a second taxpayer complaint and requesting a response to the first letter. After many failed attempts to contact the applicant by telephone during September and October further letters were sent in October and November 1993. This culminated in a letter from the Board dated 7 December 1993 giving the applicant 14 days in which to show cause why the registration should not be cancelled or suspended. A reply from the applicant dated 7 December 1993 was considered by the Board which decided at its meeting in February 1994 that, although the matters raised were considered serious, the registration would not be cancelled or suspended.

5. In April 1995 a letter was sent from the Board to the applicant requesting an explanation relating to a complaint lodged by another taxpayer client. Subsequent reminders and telephone calls failed to elicit a response. At its September 1995 meeting the Board noted the failure to reply and that the applicant had been convicted for failure to remit Group Tax and, again, sent a "show cause" letter. The applicant responded by letter of 5 October 1995 and at its November meeting the Board again accepted the response but warned that future convictions would bring continuing registration into question.

6. In August 1997, another letter seeking an explanation of matters complained of by another taxpayer client was sent by the Board. Reminder letters seeking a response were sent monthly until January 1998. Finally, in February 1998 the applicant provided a response. At its February 1998 meeting the Board again accepted the explanations but warned the applicant of consequences of continual failure to respond to letters from the Board.

7. In June 1998 another complaint was lodged by a taxpayer client. The usual request for a response was sent in July 1998 with reminder letters in August, September and November. In the meantime two telephone complaints about the applicant were noted although the complainants did not lodge formal complaints. In January 1999 the Institute of Chartered Accountants advised the Board of disciplinary action against Mr Alexander for failure to attend to a client's taxation affairs in a timely and professional manner and failure to reply to letters in September, October, December 1997 and February, June and August 1998 from officers of the Institute. Mr Alexander had been reprimanded, required to attend a Public Practice Program and required to pay $400 costs. At the Board meeting in February 1999 a "show cause" letter was sent to the applicant referring to the failure to respond to its letters and the disciplinary action of the Institute. A reminder letter was sent in March 1999 but no response was received. At its meeting in April 1999, the Board resolved to cancel the registration effective from 30 May 1999. On 11 June 1999 a staff member of the Board Secretariat telephoned the applicant's office expressing concern at the apparent continuation of its tax practice notwithstanding cancellation of the registration. The response was that Mr Alexander was away on holidays until late the following week. Mr Alexander then telephoned the Board from Queensland and said that he was not aware of the cancellation and "thought he had attended to the 'show cause' letter". On 16 July 1999 he sent a facsimile to the Board requesting that his clients not be transferred as "we have currently applied for re-registration as tax agents". After several unsuccessful attempts to contact Mr Alexander by telephone, the Board Secretary spoke to him on 19 July 1999. He advised that re-registration was unlikely and discussed the option of applying to this Tribunal. This option had been mentioned to Mr Alexander in the letter advising of the decision to cancel registration and in the 11 June phone conversation but no application had been lodged. The application was lodged by the applicant together with an application for a stay of the decision on 20 July 1999. At the time no application was made for an extension of time in which to lodge the application for review notwithstanding that the time in which an application should be made had expired on 28 May 1999. A stay of the implementation and operation of the cancellation decision was granted by the Tribunal on 25 August 1999.

8. In the meantime the ATO wrote to the Board by letter of 16 June 1999 expressing concerns in regard to the manner of conduct of the applicant's practice. The letter resulted from the involvement of the applicant in the Work Related Expense ("WRE") program for the last four years. The letter maintained that many returns prepared by the applicant were incorrect in relation to WRE claims due to the applicant's ignorance of the relevant tax laws and being overzealous in pursuit of WRE claims. Comment was made on the failure of Mr Alexander to return phone calls or respond to letters. As the Board had cancelled the registration prior to receipt of this letter it was simply noted at the subsequent meeting of the Board.

9. Mr Alexander gave evidence that he completed a Bachelor of Business Studies in 1978 and worked with Coopers Lybrand until 1983. After some four years with two smaller accounting firms he had commenced practice on his own account in 1987. In 1992 he incorporated Russell J Alexander Pty Ltd to conduct the practice. Currently the practice has approximately 1,500 clients, primarily individual salary and wage returns. He said that his sole income is derived from the practice and he estimated this at approximately $50,000 from gross fees of approximately $190,000. Apart from Mr Alexander, the practice employs two full-time and one part-time female employees.

10. It is unnecessary to go into detail of each of the complaints made to the Board by some seven clients between August 1993 and November 1998. It is sufficient to say that each case involved the failure of the applicant to forward assessments to the client, to ensure refund cheques were received promptly, to advise of tax payable, advising of audits or lodging returns. Mr Alexander accepted that it was deliberate on his part not to reply to correspondence from the Board as, on each occasion, he was hurt and wounded by the accusation of these clients, most of whom he had regarded as friends. He said that he chose to ignore the complaints and to concentrate on work for other clients. He maintained that he now regretted ignoring the correspondence and has realised that he should have responded at the appropriate time. He said that the actions of the Institute of Chartered Accountants resulted from a complaint to that Institute by the same client who complained to the Board in June 1998. He agreed with a suggestion by the Tribunal that he is one who is inclined to "put his head in the sand" and hope trouble will go away. In relation to the conviction for failure to remit group tax deductions for the two quarters ended 30 September and 31 December 1994, Mr Alexander said that he separated from his wife in April 1992 and moved to new offices in June 1992. He maintained that the costs of maintenance, legal fees and dealing with a demanding and difficult wife had caused him significant cash shortages leading to the group tax getting into arrears. He admitted compounding the problem by failing to note the conviction on the tax agent renewal application in May 1995 but had not recognised that such a conviction was relevant.

11. In relation to the complaint of 16 June 1999 from the ATO, Mr Alexander disagreed that he was ignorant of the relevant tax laws or had prepared false returns. He was not clear what had been meant by being "overzealous" in making WRE claims as he believed that all claims were based on clients' records and legitimate. He believed that any disputes arose from difference of opinion on deductibility. Some examples were given where the taxpayer would have an arguable case. Mr Alexander maintained that he had never hidden from the Tax Office auditors and assumed that the failure to return phone calls arose from the fact that he is frequently interviewing clients and does not always receive messages or respond to them.

12. Mr Alexander said that he attended the Public Practice Program in April 1999 as directed by the Institute of Chartered Accountants which he believed was very helpful. Subsequently he has instituted new policies and procedures including a correspondence action register, a work in progress register and a lodgement register. He considered that he was now delegating to staff more efficiently with his secretary monitoring unanswered correspondence. He had advertised recently with a view to investigating merging the practice with another firm and has been actively seeking to employ a competent assistant. He said that the current position of his registration had inhibited discussions on either prospect.

13. Ms N prepared a witness statement which was filed with the Tribunal. She did not provide further oral evidence and was not required by Mr Hurley for cross-examination. Her evidence was that she is the manager of the Tax Agent Investigation Unit at a regional office of the ATO. She said that, in the absence of any application to the Tribunal for a review of the cancellation decision and a stay of the implementation of the decision, the applicant's electronic lodgement service registration was withdrawn on 2 July 1999. Subsequently, in the month of July, the applicant lodged with the respondent objections to assessments and schedules of WRE. On 14 July 1999 and by telephone on 21 July 1999, the applicant was warned of the consequences of acting as an unregistered tax agent. Ms N confirmed that the applicant remained on the WRE program for four years because the ATO had not been satisfied with the results in each relevant year of income. This program was intended to cover tax agents with over 100 clients claiming more than $300 in WRE for the 1995 lodgement year.

14. The decision of the Board as set out in its minutes and as communicated to the applicant by letter of 30 April 1999 was to cancel the registration as a tax agent under the provisions of section 251K(2)(c) and (f) of the Act. Subsection (2) of section 251K provides:

"251K(2) A Board may suspend or cancel the registration of any tax agent upon being satisfied that:

(a) any return which has been prepared by or on behalf of the tax agent is false in any material particular; unless the tax agent establishes to the satisfaction of the Board that he had no knowledge of the falsity or that the falsity was due to his inadvertence;

(b) the tax agent:

(i) has neglected the business of a principal; or

(ii) has been guilty of misconduct as a tax agent; or

(c) a registered nominee of the tax agent is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters;

(d) if the tax agent is a natural person — the tax agent is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters;

(e) if the tax agent is a partnership:

(i) in the case of a partner who is a natural person — the partner:

(A) has not attained the age of 18 years; or

(B) is not of good fame, integrity and character; or

(ii) in the case of a person who is an executive officer of a partner that is a company — the person:

(A) has not attained the age of 18 years; or

(B) is not of good fame, integrity and character; or

(f) if the tax agent is a company — an executive officer of the company:

(i) has not attained the age of 18 years; or

(ii) is not of good fame, integrity and character."

Effectively, the decision of the Board was that Mr Alexander, as an executive officer and nominee of the applicant, was not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters and was not of good fame, integrity and character.

15. It was submitted for the applicant that the evidence did not show that Mr Alexander was not a fit and proper person nor a person not of good fame, integrity and character. It was said that his primary offence was a failure to communicate and a failure to respond to correspondence from the Board. It was argued that each of the complaints lodged by clients was relatively minor in nature and, in the main, the explanations had been considered by the Board and accepted. It was suggested that, in the type of practice conducted by the applicant, complaints at an average of one per year were not exceptional. Mr Hurley argued that there was no pattern in the complains and that no permanent harm had come to the complainants. He believed that the complaints were not a criticism of Mr Alexander's technical competence and all of the criticisms from clients, the ATO and the Board were attributable to a person who was tardy in dealing with problems and, at times, confused in how to deal with them. It was submitted that the penalty imposed by the Board was excessive in the circumstances as cancellation of the tax agent registration would impose significant hardship by destroying his sole means of livelihood. He was said to be a hard working suburban sole practitioner with three children and an ex-wife to support, an overdraft at its limit and responsibility for the employment of three other people. It was said that Mr Alexander had embarked on remedial action in a conscious attempt to remedy defaults. It was suggested that he had incurred a significant penalty in legal costs, time and anxiety as a result of the Board's decision.

16. For the respondent it was submitted that the Board properly exercised its discretion under section 251K(2) of the Act. It was said that the lack of cooperation and failure to respond to the Board, the ATO, the Institute of Chartered Accountants and his clients and the continual procrastination continuing beyond the cancellation of the registration justified the cancellation. It was further argued that the complaint from the ATO since cancellation indicated that the applicant had prepared returns which were false in material particulars. The fact that the continued practice beyond the date of effective cancellation of the registration was said to be indicative of continuing disregard for the law and a likelihood of repetition of the conduct which had led to the cancellation. It was submitted that the attitude of Mr Alexander that the client complaints were minor, the deliberate refusal to respond to letters from the Board and that the problems with the ATO in the WRE program were simply the usual technical disputes was indicative of his lack of appreciation of the issues.

17. There can be no criticism of the Board's action. The history of its dealings with Mr Alexander demonstrated a significant degree of patience by the Board and a willingness to allow him excessive time to respond to complaints and to maintain the registration of the applicant. Ultimately that patience was exhausted when there was absolute silence from Mr Alexander from July 1988 to April 1999. The Board was left with no option other than cancellation or suspension.

18. I do not accept the submission that it was the refusal to respond to complaints which were minor in themselves rather than the actual complaints which should be held against the applicant. The complaints were not minor. It is not clear that the clients who complained ultimately incurred monetary losses. However, there were clear instances of neglect of the affairs of the clients which caused them sufficient concern to complain. It is generally likely that those clients who complain formally to the Board are a proportion only of clients who are critical of the tax agent. It is clear that Mr Alexander has a long history of failing to deal expeditiously with problems. It is difficult to believe that the experience of the Board, the ATO and the Institute of Chartered Accountants has not been reflected in responses to clients. Mr Alexander maintained that he was very busy with limited competent assistance and he put the demands of his clients first. That may well be right but a complete disregard for those bodies in authority who provide him with his capacity to carry on a tax practice is hardly in the best interests of his clients and may well be indicative of the way in which he services those clients.

19. There are two issues which have arisen since the cancellation of the registration which are, perhaps, of greater significance that the issues which led to the cancellation. They are the report of the ATO and the continuation of practice while not registered in contravention of section 251L of the Act. Whilst the stay granted by this Tribunal on 25 August 1999 may be seen as retrospectively resolving that contravention, the plain facts are that the registration was cancelled with effect from 30 May 1999. It was not until 20 July 1999 that the application for a stay was made and at that point, there could be no clear assumption that it would be granted. I am satisfied that the applicant continued the practice in the intervening period in the apparent view that if you ignored a problem it would go away. It is clear from the file note of the Board Secretary of 19 July 1999 that Mr Alexander had no understanding of his problem and thought that it was simply a matter of applying for re-registration. It was only the explanation provided by the Secretary which led to the application for review and stay of the decision lodged with the Tribunal the next day. Mr Alexander could not recall receiving the formal advice of cancellation of registration. Whether or not it was received, and I have difficulty accepting that it was not, it is clear that he was aware of the cancellation on 11 June 1999. There is clear evidence that he continued to practice normally thereafter and it was more than five weeks later before he lodged an application with the Tribunal.

20. The report of the ATO of 16 June 1999 does provide reason for concern. Mr Alexander cited some examples of disputes which indicate that he disagreed with the ATO view on deductibility of certain expenses and it may well be that his was a reasonably arguable position. However, in each of the four years of the WRE program the auditors concluded that the standard of returns was poor and a number of audit adjustments were required. Concern was expressed at apparent ambit and estimated claims without adequate recognition of substantiation requirements and ATO rulings. The documents attached to the report were littered with complaints of the failure of Mr Alexander to return phone calls or respond to letters. This failure was seen as creating the need for unnecessary objections and debt collection activity. All of these comments cast serious doubt on the applicant's ability to act in the best interest of his clients.

21. Mr Alexander maintained that he acquired much useful information to assist in managing his practice at the Public Practice Program which he attended at the direction of the Institute of Chartered Accountants. He said that he had implemented new procedures as a result. However, he attended the Program in April 1999. His new procedures did not appear to assist in dealing with the results of the Board's decision during May, June and July. His tardiness in making application to review the decision did not stop there. At the hearing of the application for a stay, the Tribunal directed that the applicant file and serve an outline of evidence of witnesses and a statement of facts and contentions by 15 September 1999. They were filed on 23 September, only two working days prior to the date set down for this hearing.

22. In Re Su and the Tax Agents Board of South Australia 82 ATC 4284, Davies J said (at page 4286):

"The function of a tax agent is to prepare and lodge income tax returns for other persons. A person is a fit and proper person to handle the affairs of a client if he is a person of good reputation, has a proper knowledge of taxation laws, is able to prepare income tax returns competently and is able to deal competently with any queries which may be raised by officers of the Taxation Department. He should be a person of such competence and integrity that others may entrust their taxation affairs to his care. He should be a person of such reputation and ability that officers of the Taxation Department may proceed upon the footing that the taxation returns lodged by the agent have been prepared by him honestly and competently."

In Statos v Tax Agents Board of NSW 90 ATC 4950, Hill J said (at pages 4958 and 4959):

"...the legislature has chosen in section 251L of the Act to confer upon registered tax agents a virtual monopoly on the preparation for reward of income tax returns and objections and in relation to the transactions of any business on behalf of a taxpayer in income tax matters for reward...

The conferral of this privilege upon registered tax agents carries with it a consequent set of obligations and responsibilities. ...

In addition to the tax agent dealing with his client, he will, almost invariably have dealings with officers of the Australian Taxation Office and perhaps the boards or tribunals to which I have already referred. Those dealings must be carried on in an atmosphere of mutual trust. The Commissioner and his officers must be able to accept that, to the best of the ability of the tax agent, returns have been prepared are true and accurate. This is particularly so now that the Commissioner has proceeded to a system of self-assessment, with inaccuracies only coming to light in case of random audit, or, presumably, other information coming to the hands of the Commissioner.

The Commissioner and his officers must be able, also, to accept the word of a tax agent when acting for a taxpayer in negotiations, and a fortiori in matters proceeding in a Board, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal or indeed a court it is imperative that the honestly and integrity of the tax agent not be called into doubt."

A continuing thread throughout the history of the dealings by Mr Alexander with the Board, the ATO, the Institute of Chartered Accountants, the Tribunal and, at least, some clients was his failure to deal properly and expeditiously with problems. There is no clear evidence of dishonesty or technical incompetence. The report from the ATO indicates suspicion of inappropriate claims but I do not take it as clear evidence that such claims were initiated and made by Mr Alexander. The report would appear to have resulted as much from frustration in attempting to deal with Mr Alexander as from clear concerns at potential revenue losses. There is no doubt that he suffers from a marked obtuseness at recognising warning signs and problems as they emerge. His "head in the sand" attitude to complaints from clients and the Board causes concern at his ability to properly service clients.

23. It would appear very likely that, if Mr Alexander had replied promptly to the Board's letter of July 1998 or, at least, to the "show cause" letter of February 1999, the applicant would have retained its registration with another warning. The subsequent report of the ATO would likely have led to a further "please explain" and, if the response was somewhat better than that provided to this Tribunal, the explanation may have been accepted by the Board. While this is conjecture it must be considered as a factor in determining whether the penalty of cancellation is appropriate. It should be said that I do not accept that the penalty of legal costs, time and anxiety suffered by Mr Alexander should be regarded as in any way adequate. These were brought upon himself by his inability to recognise and deal with problems. Neither do I believe that cancellation imposing undue financial hardship is sufficient reason for setting aside the decision of the Board. Mr Alexander has only himself to blame. He had adequate notice of the possible penalty from the Board with a month period of grace to reorganise his affairs after the decision to cancel was made.

24. Whilst I have no criticism of the Board's decision and would have no difficulty, particularly in view of the subsequent events, in affirming that decision, I am of the view that a somewhat lesser penalty is appropriate. It would not be unfair to describe Mr Alexander as "stupid" in his actions. However, I am not satisfied that he is dishonest or not of good fame, integrity and character at least to the extent of requiring full cancellation of the registration of the applicant. There is no doubt that the circumstances, particularly the offence against section 251L of the Act justify a penalty. In this case I am prepared to give Mr Alexander some slight benefit of the doubt by finding that an appropriate penalty is suspension of the applicant's registration for three months. Given the time of the year and the likely effect on his clients of such suspension to operate immediately, I am prepared to defer the suspension so that it commences on 1 January 2000. I recognise that such suspension will have an effect on some business clients whose accounting and tax returns may have otherwise been prepared in the January to March period. However, Mr Alexander will have two months in which to make appropriate plans and he is strongly urged to do so. He indicated that he proposes exploring the possibility of merging his practice with another accountant. This may well be the best course for him to undertake as every indication is that it would be preferable to place himself under supervision of another rather than seek to supervise any further staff. In addition he should heed a strong warning that further infringements, particularly any contravention of section 251L during the period of suspension are likely to lead inevitably to the penalty from which he has now escaped by the narrowest of margins. A study of that section would be advisable.

25. The decision of the Board should be set aside and, in its stead, the applicant's registration suspended for three months from 1 January 2000.
