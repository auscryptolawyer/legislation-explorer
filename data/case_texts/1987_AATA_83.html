QT86/1865 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 83; (1987) 18 ATR 3329; 87 ATC 424 (11 March 1987)

Last Updated: 15 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax Assessment Act - S23AD (3) (C)

Income Tax (International Agreements) Act -ss 4,11A

Goodfellow v. F.C. of T. (1977) 13 ALR 203, 77 ATC 4086

Case N45 81 ATC 220

Case P29 82 ATC 136

Case 12 86 ATC 107

DECISIONS AND REASONS

Re:

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAYATTON

RESPONDENT

No: QT86/1865

AAT Decision No 3306

Data: 11 March 1987

place: Brisbane

Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

The question at issue is whether an amount of $20,945 received as one payment in June 1979 is assessable income. In his return of income for the year ended 30 June 1979 the applicant/taxpayer described the payment as compensation from the Dutch Government for losses suffered during World War II and claimed that the payment was a non assessable capital receipt. Alternatively the applicant claims that the amount received should be assessable income of the year of income to which it relates. The Commissioner of Taxation asserts that the payment is assessable income, and is neither a capital receipt nor exempt income and that it is assessable in the year of receipt.

2. The specific statutory provisions applicable in respect of the relevant year are S23AD of the Income Tax Assessment Act, ss4 and 11A of the Income Tax (International Agreement) Act. The relevant part of s23Ad is paragraph 3(c) of that section which reads as follows:

"(3) The following payments are exempt from income tax:

(c) payments of wounds and disability pensions of the kinds specified in sub-section (2) of section 365 of the Imperial Act known as the Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970, other than payments that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, are of a similar nature to excepted payments;"

Section 365 of the Imperial Act reads as follows:

"365(1) Income from wounds and disability pensions to which this subsection applies shall be exempt from income tax and shall not be reckoned in computing income for any of the purposes of the Income Tax Acts.

(2) sub-section (1) above applies to -

(a) wounds pensions granted to members of the naval, military or air forces of the Crown,

(b) retired pay of disabled officers granted on account of medical unfitness attributable to or aggravated by naval, military or air-force service,

(c) disablement or disability pensions granted to members, other than commissioned officers, of the naval, military or air forces of the Crown on account of medical unfitness attributable to or aggravated by naval, military or air-force service,

(d) disablement pensions granted to persons who have been employed in the nursing services of any of the naval, military or air forces of the Crown on account of medical unfitness attributable to or aggravated by naval, military or air-force service, and

(e) injury and disablement pensions payable under any scheme made under the Injuries in War (Compensation) Act 1914, the Injuries in War Compensation Act 1914 (Session 2), and the Injuries in War (Compensation) Act 1915, or under any War Risks Compensation Scheme for the Mercantile Marine:

Provided that where the amount of any retired pay or pension to which subsection (1) above applies is not solely attributable to disablement or disability, the relief conferred by the said subsection (1) shall extend only to such part as is certified by the Secretary of State for Social Services, after consultation with the appropriate Government Department, to be attributable to disablement or disability."

  1. Section 4 of the Income Tax (International Agreement) Act provides that the Income Tax Assessment Act is incorporated and is to be read as one with the International Agreements Act. It also provides in subsection 4(2) that the provisions of the International Agreements Act have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent with those provisions contained in the Assessment Act. There is an exception in respect of section 160A0 of the Assessment Act which is not relevant for present purposes.
  2. Section 11A of the International Agreements Act provides for the incorporation into that Act of an agreement with the Kingdom of the Netherlands. That Agreement, commonly known as a Double Tax Agreement, was incorporated into the International Agreements Act with general effect on 1 July 1975. There are two relevant articles in the agreement which were brought to the attention of the Tribunal. Article 18 reads as follows:

" (1) Pensions, including pensions provided under the provisions of a public social security system, but not including pensions to which Article 19 applies, paid to a resident of one of the States, and annuities so paid, shall be taxable only in that State.

(2) The term 'annuity' means a stated sum payable periodically at stated times during life or during a specified or ascertainable period of time under an obligation to make the payments in return for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth."

The article deals with the taxation of a pension and annuities in general but Article 19 covers a particular class of pensions which are excluded from Article 18. Article 19 reads as follows:

"(1) Remuneration (including a pension) paid to any individual in respect of services rendered in the discharge of governmental functions to one of the States or to a political sub-division of one of the States or to a local authority of one of the States may be taxed in that State. However, any such remuneration, not being a pension, shall be taxable only in the other State if the services are rendered in that other State and the recipient is a resident of that other State who -

(a) is a citizen or national of that State; or

(b) did not become a resident of that State solely for the purpose of performing the services.

(2) This Article shall not apply to remuneration (including a pension) in respect of services rendered in connection with any trade or business carried on by one of the States or a political sub-division of one of the States or a local authority of one of the States. In such a case, the provisions of Articles 15, 16 and 18 shall apply."

5. The applicant appeared on his own behalf and the Commissioner of Taxation was represented by one of his officers.

6. The applicant, who was born in 1915, lived in Sumatra prior to the outbreak of World War II. Although it was not clearly established I infer that he was a Dutch citizen prior to taking up residence in Sumatra and that he remained a Dutch citizen at all relevant times until he became an Australian citizen. The applicant was employed by a public company carrying on business in Sumatra but had, at some unspecified time prior to the outbreak of World War II, spent four months in the Dutch Army training as a soldier in what the applicant described as "national service".

7. Upon the outbreak of hostilities with Japan and the perception that the then Dutch East Indies were threatened with invasion the applicant was mobilised into the Royal Dutch Indies Army (KNIL) on 10 December 1941. The applicant served on military duties with the KNIL until March 1942 when he was captured by the Japanese army.

  1. From March 1942 until August 1945 the applicant was incarcerated as a prisoner of war on Summatra.
  2. At the conclusion of hostilities and his release from the prisoner of war camp the applicant was then appointed as an Inspector of Police with special duties as a member of a British War Crimes Investigation team. He served in this capacity for 18 months and was maintained as to his food, accommodation, clothing etc by the British Army.
  3. After 18 months his former employer succeeded in gaining his release from his duties on the British War Crimes Investigation Team and the applicant returned to his employment with his former employer.
  4. Although not relevant to the issues arising in this application, for the sake of completeness it should be recorded that the applicant left the former Dutch East Indies upon the cessation of colonial rule by the Netherlands and settled in Australia. He has been a citizen of Australia since that time.
  5. About 1977 the applicant became aware that the Netherlands Government was making certain reparation payments to ex-Dutch citizens living abroad who were victims of persecution during the 1940-1945 War. Exhibit 1 includes a document entitled "Guide for the Victims of Persecution" and is issued by the Netherlands Ministry of Cultural Affairs, Recreation and Social Welfare and dated July 1975. That pamphlett sets out in some detail the scheme for reparation payments to the former Dutch citizens. The claim is generally described as "Act Benefits for the 1940-1945 Victims of Persecution" hereafter referred to as the ABVP. Its stated basic philosophy is the joint liability of the Dutch people towards their persecuted compatriots. The pamphlet's stated aim is to draw the attention of all who, as a result of the persecution they have suffered, are handicapped by disease or infirmities, including the next of kin of such victims, to the claims that can be made under the ABVP. The pamphlet states:

"The ABVP is actually designed to enable victims or their next of kin to maintain or restore, within reasonable limits, the standard of living they enjoyed or would have enjoyed if persecution had not taken place.

Persecution within the framework of the ABVP is stated to mean: all deprivation of liberty by imprisonment, detention in a concentration or internment camp."

That is the relevant ground of persecution for the purpose of this application. The ABVP is stated to not to apply to those who on the strength of persecution should lay claims under the provisions in force for members of the armed forces but specifically includes victims who were members of the former Dutch Indies Army (KNIL).

13. At paragraph 3 of his reasons in Case T2 86 ATC 107 Mr T J McCarthy of Taxation Board of Review No 1 has set out a fair summary of the provisions of the ABVP booklet (Exhibit 1). I adopt McCarthy's summary.

  1. It is apparent that the applicant qualified for payments under the ABVP after undergoing a number of medical examinations which satisfied the Dutch authorities that the decline in the applicant's health was as a result of his incarceration by the Japanese during the Second World War. That was the applicant's uncontested oral evidence before the Tribunal. The applicant explained that documentary evidence which would support his oral evidence had been destroyed in circumstances beyond his control.
  2. The applicant's case was that the amount of $20,948 was a capital receipt or in any event was not assessable income.
  3. The Commissioner assessed the applicant on the basis that the amount in question was assessable income describing it on the adjustment sheet as "Dutch persecution pension". As I have already explained the applicant was persecuted by the Japanese army and not by the Dutch who are the payers of the pension.
  4. The respondent Commissioner argues that the pension comes within the terms of Article 18 of the Double Tax Agreement and it is therefore assessable to the applicant in the year of receipt, it being quite clear that the applicant is a resident of Australia, the applicant not seeking to argue to the contrary as to his status as a resident. The respondent also argues that section 23AD does not apply to the circumstances of this case and relies in part to support that argument by the fact that in 1982 the Government announced and Parliament subsequently enacted paragraph 23(kca) which expressly exempted these pensions paid by the Dutch Government.
  5. I am indebted to Mr P M Roach, a Senior Member of this Tribunal who has dealt with the history of the exemptions for victims of persecutions in some detail in a recent decision dated 18 January 1987 relating to one "Visser" which Mr Roach describes as a false name, although apparently used by the applicant in that case as an alias during the 1940's. I need not repeat Mr Roach's historical commentary other than to note that the Government announcement which resulted in the enactment of s23(kca) was made about eight months after the decision of Taxation Board of Review No 3 in Case N45 81 ATC 220.
  6. The facts of Case N45 were that the taxpayer, a naturalised Australian, received a pension from the Dutch Government under the ABVP. The pension was paid to him as an officer of the former Royal Dutch Indies Army (KNIL) on the grounds that he had become medically unfit to earn an average income because of persecution suffered in the Second World War. The applicant claimed that the compensation paid to him under the Dutch Act was exempt from Australian tax under S23AD and relied in particular on paragraph (3)(c) of that section. The Board found by majority that paragraph 23AD(3)(c) only applies to wounds and disability pensions specifically listed under s365 of the United Kingdom Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970 and therefore only applied to pensions payable to members of the armed forces of the British Crown. Dr Beck, in dissenting reasons, took the view that although the taxpayer did not fall into the class or group of persons encompassed by s365, the pension he received was of the same kind as pensions received by that class or group and the taxpayer was therefore entitled to exemption under paragraph 23AD(3)(C). It is important to note that the report of that decision does not contain any reference to the decision of the High Court in Goodfellow v FC of T 77 ATC 4086.
  7. In Goodfellow's case the High Court was dealing with the provisions that were the predecessors of S23AD. In my view the Court's decision should be applied mutatis mutandis to S23AD. There must be some doubt therefore that the majority of the Board of Review reached the right decision in Case N45 and this is reinforced by the fact that in the present application the representative of the respondent specifically resiled from the reasoning of the Board.
  8. The Commissioner's representative conceded that the pension payable to the applicant under the ABVP is now exempt from income tax under paragraph 23(kca).
  9. The applicant's case gains considerable support from a decision of Taxation Board of Review No 2 made in April 1982 and reported as Case P29 82 ATC 136. In that case the taxpayer was entitled to a disability pension from the Dutch Government because of injuries he suffered as a member of the Dutch resistance forces during World War II. So far as is relevant to this application the Board found that the pension paid by the Dutch Government was a pension of the kind specified in s.365 of the United Kingdom Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970 and therefore the pension qualified for exemption from Australian tax. The Board therefore agreed with the dissenting reasons of Dr Beck in Case N45. In this situation it is not at all clear that the enactment of paragraph 23(kca) was made to change the law but rather to clarify an unsatisfactory situation which arose out of differing views expressed by the two Boards of Review. The differing views of the Boards of Review made it necessary for the Parliament to remove the doubts in Act No 76 of 1982 which received the Royal Assent on 13 September 1982.
  10. It should be noted that neither of the Board of Review decisions referred to above relied upon Article 19 of the Double Tax Agreement. This is understandable in Case P29 where the taxpayer in question was a member of the Dutch Resistance but the article certainly should have been raised before Board of Review No 3 in Case N45 where the facts are almost identical to the facts of the present application.
  11. Article 19 of the Double Tax Agreement provides that remuneration, which includes a pension, paid to any individual in respect of services rendered in the discharge of Governmental functions to one of the States or to a political subdivision of one of the State (in this case the Dutch East Indies) or to a local authority of one of the States may be taxed in that State.
  12. Article 19 is permissive but not mandatory in the sense that the specified State must tax the pension - it "may" tax it. The proviso makes it clear that certain remuneration coming within the Article, but not including pensions, shall he taxable only in the State not paying the remuneration if the recipient is a resident of that State and is a citizen or national of that Sate or did not become a resident of that State solely for the purpose of performing the Services in question.
  13. The intent of the Article, insofar as it applies to pensions of the specified kind is quite clear. They are to be taxed, if at all, by the State paying the pension. In view of the effect of section 4 of the International Agreements Act this clear intention must be given effect if the Tribunal is satisfied that the pension received by the applicant comes within the terms of Article 19.
  14. As noted above the applicant was a member of the Royal Dutch Indies Army at the time of his capture by the Japanese Army and I am prepared to assume that he remained a member of the Royal Dutch Indies Army during his time in the prisoner of war camp and until he was appointed an Inspector of Police with special duties described above at the cessation of hostilities in Sumatra. The appointment as an Inspector of Police following his release from the prisoner of war camp indicates that the applicant was in good standing with the Dutch authorities at the time of the cessation of hostilities on Sumatra.
  15. In considering the nature of the pension it is not necessary to determine whether the applicant was an employee of the Government of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. Almost certainly he was not. However, I think it is beyond doubt that the words "governmental functions" are words of sufficiently wide meaning to embrace the military forces of a Government including a colonial government. As the pension was paid for persecution suffered while a member of the Royal Dutch Indies Army (the ABVP specifically embraces such persons) I have no doubt that it is a pension coming within the terms of Article 19.
  16. The payments made under the ABVP have been paid to the applicant monthly (after the initial lump sum payment in respect of payments from the date of application to June 1979) and come within the ordinary meaning of "pension" being a periodical payment made in consideration of past services, injury, loss sustained etc. The payments are therefore in the nature of income.
  17. The view adopted by Taxation Board of Review No 2 as to the meaning of paragraph 23AD (3) (c) in Case P29 is consistent with Article 19 albeit that Article 19 did not apply to the facts of that case. I therefore take the view that I should follow that decision.
  18. Even if I am not right in respect of Article 19 I can see no reason for not following the decision of Board of Review No 2 in Case P29 and the dissenting reasons of Dr Beck in Case N45. I think the remuneration received by the applicant under the provisions of the ABVP is clearly of the kind specified in s.365 of the United Kingdom Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970 and therefore it is exempt from tax in Australia under paragraph 23AD(3)(c).
  19. In view of my finding that the amount of $20,945 is exempt income I do not need to consider the question as to when the income was derived.
  20. The respondent Commissioner's decision on the objection will be set aside and the objection allowed in full.

This and the preceding thirteen (13) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matter of QT86/1865
