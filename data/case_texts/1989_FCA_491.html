Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Eric Lawrence Ranson [1989] FCA 491; 89 Atc 5322 20 Atr 1652/90 ALR 533 25 FCR 57 (1 December 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: ERIC LAWRENCE RANSON

No. G206 of 1989

FED No. 741

Income Tax

89 ATC 5322

20 ATR 1652/90 ALR 533

25 FCR 57

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Davies(1), Jenkinson(2) and Hill(1) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - section 23(z) income tax assessment act 1936 - taxpayer receiving payments during university term and serving as employee during vacation under cadetship arrangement - whether payments during term were amounts "received ... upon condition" that taxpayer would continue to render services to employer within meaning of s.23(z)(i) - whether s.23(z)(i) requires legally enforceable agreement to provide services subsequent to receipt of payment - policy of exempting scholarship payments discussed.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936: s.23(z)

HEARING

SYDNEY

1:12:1989

Counsel and Solicitors I D Faulkner instructed by

for Appellant: Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel and Solicitors B J Sullivan instructed by

for Respondent: Gillard Ooi

ORDER

1. The appeal be allowed.

2. The order made on 5 April 1989 determining the appeal from the decision
of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal be set aside.

3. The decision of the appellant to disallow the respondent's objection against the assessment issued 28 August 1981 be confirmed.

4. There be no order as to costs.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The appellant, the Commissioner of Taxation, appeals against the decision of a judge of this Court affirming the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal constituted by a Senior Member, Mr P M Roach, that the objection of the respondent, Mr E L Ranson, against the assessment of his income tax for the year of income ended 30 June 1981 be allowed and that his taxable income be accordingly reduced by $2,025.

2. The amount of $2,025 represented moneys received by the respondent from the T & G Mutual Life Society Limited ("the Society") in the period from February to June 1981 in respect of a cadetship with the Society.

3. The respondent enrolled in 1979 for a degree of bachelor of arts in actuarial studies at Macquarie University and applied for "scholarships" from several companies including the Society. The Tribunal found that he was notified in writing by the Society of an offer of a cadetship and that he provided a written acceptance. The written offer was not adduced in evidence but the Tribunal found that the terms of the offer were mutatis mutandis adapted from another document which clearly had been used by the Society to advertise the terms of the cadetship. That document was in the following terms:

"A limited number of cadetships are available for

selected students intending to undertake full time

B.A. degree course majoring in Actuarial Studies or

already an undergraduate in this faculty.

The benefits and conditions relating to the course

commencing in 1978 are as follows:-

Benefits

1st Year Student $1,900

2nd Year Student $2,600

3rd Year Student $3,400

During vacation employment at T & G office, the

cadet will receive a salary in accordance with that

laid down in the Insurance Officers (clerical

Indoor Staffs') Award.

Conditions

1. The value of the cadetship will be subject to

an annual review, and the Society reserves the

right to withdraw it from the student in the

event of unsatisfactory examination results or

poor performance during vacation work.

2. Applicants will be interviewed at the State

Office concerned and the successful applicants

will be required to pass a medical examination.

3. (a) N.S.W. Students

Cadets will be expected to work at Head Office

during Christmas vacation. The Society will

meet the cost of the return air fare and a special

living-away-from-home allowance of $10.00 (sic)

while the cadet is working in Melbourne.

(b) Victorian Students

Cadets will be expected to work at Head Office

during the Christmas vacation. The Society

will meet the cost of the air fares to and

from Melbourne. A special

living-away-from-home allowance of $10.00 per

week will be payable during the Macquarie

University year.

4. On graduation from Macquarie University the

cadet would join the Society's staff and a

condition of employment will be willingness to work

in Sydney or Melbourne as the Society requires."

4. The Society made payments under the cadetship in the academic years 1979 to 1981. The assessability of the amounts paid from 1979 until February 1981 was not in issue before the Tribunal.

5. During the long vacations the respondent worked with the Society as a clerk and in particular, during the long vacation in the summer of 1980-81 he served as an employee of the Society working for four weeks in the Sydney office of the Society and for four weeks in its Melbourne office. When the 1981 academic year was completed the respondent was offered employment with the Society; it was mutually agreed that this offer be deferred to permit the respondent to travel overseas on the basis that he would take up the position at a later date and the respondent in fact did commence employment with the Society and remained in its service for something of the order of one year.

6. The Tribunal expressed the view that it was satisfied:

"that at all material times there was an

expectation on the part of both the Society and the

applicant that he would render services to the

Society as an employee during the long vacation and

that upon graduation, assuming that the "cadetship"

had not been terminated in the interim, he would be

offered a position in the service of the Society."

7. The Tribunal also found:

"that it was understood that, if he did not serve

the Society during long vacations in accordance

with its expectations, he would be liable to

suffer the forfeiture of his cadetship for the

future. However, he would not be in breach of any

legal obligation to the Society should he do so."

8. The reasons of the Tribunal then continued:

"I also find that the Society had no obligation to

offer, nor he any obligation to accept, any offer

of employment which might be considered following

graduation. I also find that, at all material

times, it was recognized on the part of both the

Society and the Applicant that it was quite likely

that he would serve as an employee during the

university long vacations and that employment would

be offered him following graduation and that such

an offer would be accepted."

9. These findings as to expectation and understanding arose, it would seem, from the acceptance into evidence of statements by the respondent as to his understanding of whether legal relations were created by the acceptance of the Society's offer. That evidence was irrelevant to the issue before the Tribunal. Whether or not a binding contract was entered into between the respondent and the Tribunal was a matter to be determined objectively from the terms of the offer made by the Society to the respondent and the respondent's acceptance of that offer. The state of mind of one of the parties to the arrangement (there being no evidence called by the respondent from any representative of the Society) or for that matter the state of minds of both of the parties to the arrangement would be irrelevant to the issue whether one of them was contractually bound to the other. While, as the learned authors of Cheshire & Fifoot, Law of Contract (2nd Australian ed.) pp 189-190 point out, if the parties to an arrangement clearly indicate by the terms of their arrangement that they do not intend to create legal obligations the law will respect that intention,

"if they use language which reasonable persons

would construe as importing mutual promises, their

real intention is irrelevant."

In Taylor v. Johnson [1983] HCA 5; (1982-3) 151 CLR 422 at 428 Mason A.C.J., Murphy and Deane JJ in a joint judgment indicated that the clear trend in decided cases and academic writings has been to leave the objective theory of contract in command of the field. Their Honours instanced Denning L.J. in Solle v. Butcher (1950) 1 KB 671 at p 691 where his Lordship had said:

"once a contract has been made, that is to say,

once the parties, whatever their inmost states of

mind, have to all outward appearances agreed with

sufficient certainty in the same terms on the same

subject matter, then the contract is good unless

and until it is set aside for failure of some

condition on which the existence of the contract

depends, or for fraud, or on some equitable

ground." (emphasis added)

See too Greig & Davis "The Law of Contract" Law Book Company Ltd 1987 Chapter 5 especially pp 187-191.

10. If it were necessary to do so, we would find that the arrangement between the Society and the respondent was one of contract. A cadetship involving obligations on both sides is a proper subject of contract, that is of binding mutual obligations. The respondent was given a written offer specifying the terms of the cadetship. The offer requested written acceptance and it was so accepted. All the indicia of a contract were present. The use of the word "expected" in Condition 3 is not inconsistent with that but reflects rather the problems which lie in the way of obtaining relief should the cadet fail to take up employment. As Smith's Law of Master & Servant, 6th Ed. states at p 87:

"...a master would rarely deem it worth his while

to bring an action against a servant who had

engaged to enter his service for refusing to do so."

The offer stated that employment with the Society was a condition of the cadetship and the offer in those terms was accepted. The respondent submitted that clause 4 was too vague to constitute a binding term of a contractual agreement between the cadet and the Society. However, the clause can readily be construed as imposing an obligation upon the cadet to work with the Society in a position commensurate with his graduate status and on the Society's usual conditions of employment. These views were not, however, those of the Tribunal.

11. The parties are in agreement that the amounts paid by the Society to the respondent were assessable income unless exempted by s.23(z) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") which sub-s. provides relevantly as follows:

"23. The following income shall be exempt from

income tax: ...

(z) income derived by way of a scholarship,

bursary or other educational allowance ... by

a student receiving full time education at a

school, college or university, but not including -

(i) an amount received by the student from a

person or authority upon condition that

the student will (or will if required)

render, or continue to render, services

to that person or authority;"

12. There was agreement that the sum of $2,025 was income derived by way of scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance and that the respondent was receiving full time education at Macquarie University. The issue between the parties was whether the amounts received by the respondent from the Society were received by him upon condition that the respondent would or would if required render or continue to render services to the Society. The Tribunal held that the words "on condition" imported words of legal obligation so that unless there was a contractual bargain between the respondent and the Society whereby he was to be legally obliged to render future service to the Society the allowance was not excluded from s.23(z). It was not enough in the view of the Tribunal to take a payment outside s.23(z) that there be an expectation or a likelihood or a possibility that the recipient of the payment would render services in the future. There being no legal obligation in the present case on the part of the respondent, in the view of the Tribunal, the scholarship income was accordingly exempted from tax pursuant to s.23(z) of the Act.

13. On appeal to this Court, the trial judge was of a similar view. His Honour asked himself what kind of condition was contemplated by s.23(z). He said:

"Any "condition" as to the use to which the amount

of a scholarship was to be put might be a condition

with some legal effect or it might be a stipulation

falling short of that. "Condition" is a term used

in differing senses in various areas of the law;

some of them are appropriate to the present

universe of discourse. A "condition" might be

imposed upon the holder of an "actuarial

cadetship" by way of a trust obligation (cf.

Barclay's Bank Ltd v. Quistclose Investments Ltd

(1970) AC 567) or a personal obligation enforceable

in equity (Gill v. Gill [1921] NSWStRp 39; (1921) 21 SR(NSW) 400;

Muschinski v. Dodds [1985] HCA 78; (1985) 160 CLR 583 at 605-606,

624-625). The stipulation might amount to

contractual promises by the recipient of the

scholarship (cf. Wickman Machine Tool Sales Ltd v.

L Schuler AG (1972) 2 All ER 1173 at 1180; Moreton

v. Montrose Ltd [1986] NZCA 30; (1986) 2 NZLR 496). Further, even

if the stipulation did not have effect as a

contractual promise, a student who took the money

but did not observe the stipulation might be

obliged to effect fair and just restitution for the

benefit derived at the expense of the Society, as

one of the new or developing categories of case

referred to by Deane J in Pavey and Matthews Pty

Ltd v. Paul [1987] HCA 5; (1987) 162 CLR 221 at 256-257. ... Or,

the stipulation might do no more than bind the

student as a matter of honour without any attendant

legal sanction."

14. In his Honour's view, the objective sought to be secured by the use of the words "upon condition that" was to exclude a case involving the rendering of services to the payer. Since the rendering of services ordinarily falls within the category of legal relations his Honour read the words as requiring:

"a stipulation, the fulfilment or non-fulfilment of

which has an effect on legal relations, whether

those relations be in terms of rights, duties,

powers or immunities."

15. His Honour then proceeded to consider whether the present case fell within the exclusion to s.23(z) on the footing that at all material times the relationship between the Society and the respondent was governed by the terms of the document headed "Actuarial Cadetship - Macquarie University" reproduced earlier in this judgment. While his Honour found that the student's right to the cadetship was dependent and "therefore conditional" upon his serving the Society during the Christmas vacation, the payment for the period February to June, being made after the last vacation employment had been undertaken and completed, could not be said to be conditional upon rendering service in the long vacation for no such service remained to be performed. His Honour found further that the provisions of the document did not, so far as they dealt with employment after graduation, constitute a "condition" in the required statutory sense of one affecting legal relations. Accordingly his Honour dismissed the Commissioner's appeal.

16. Before us, as before his Honour, the appellant argued that it was not necessary for a payment to be excluded from exemption under s.23(z) that the condition be one having an effect on the legal relations between the recipient and the payer. It was sufficient, it was submitted, if one could say as a matter of fact that the payment was one made on the relevant condition, whether or not that condition was legally enforceable. Alternatively, it was argued that on the facts of the present case there were legally enforceable conditions upon which the payment was made and received, these being both the obligation to perform vacation work and the obligation to commence employment on graduation and that either of them satisfied the statutory description.

17. The word "scholarship" is defined in the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary relevantly as "the ... emoluments of a scholar" and in the Macquarie Dictionary as "the sum of money or other aid granted to a scholar". The word "bursary", according to the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary referred in England to a particular kind of scholarship enabling a pupil at an elementary school to proceed to a secondary school, a meaning which was translated to Australia so that it referred to a particular kind of scholarship although not necessarily tenable only in a secondary school.

18. The concept of educational allowance may be wider. While the meaning of the word "allowance" clearly is dependent upon its context, its basic meaning as Jordan C.J. pointed out in Scott v. Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) (1935) SR(NSW) 215, 221 is of "a definite portion or amount allotted or granted to meet requirements", those requirements being in the case of an educational allowance the cost of education and perhaps support during education. See too in the context of payroll tax Mutual Acceptance Co Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 34; (1944) 69 CLR 389 per Latham J. at 396.

19. Payment of scholarships, bursaries or educational allowances could in any particular case be either voluntary or paid in discharge of an obligation to pay. They could be paid as an inducement to take up or continue employment as for example, the present case, or as recompense for training for future qualified employment as in the case of an apprenticeship. Alternatively, they could be paid as in the case of university scholarships or international scholarships such as the Rhodes Scholarship, the Fulbright or Ford International Fellowships in circumstances which are not only voluntary but also quite unrelated to any subsequent employment.

20. The common thread behind the words "scholarship, bursary or other educational allowance" as used in the section is the educational purpose in the person who provides the allowance and it matters not whether he who provides the allowance is motivated by purely educational aims (as would be the case with university and similar scholarships) or has in mind some collateral advantage, as no doubt was the case with the T & G Society here: see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hall (1975) 75 ATC 4156 at 4164 where Rath J said:

"...a condition requiring services from the

recipient, even present services, is not

incompatible with a purpose of educating the

recipient; indeed it is not incompatible with a

purpose of providing him with full-time education."

21. Section 23(z), it may be noted, is conditional upon the relevant scholarship, bursary or allowance being income. It may be possible to conceive of an educational allowance, paid in a lump sum or even periodically as being in a particular case a mere gift and accordingly as not having the character of income in ordinary concepts. Generally, however a scholarship or bursary will be income because it consists of a series of periodical receipts.

22. In Dixon v. Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, make-up pay, voluntarily paid to a serving member of the Australian armed forces during the war by a former employer was held to be income in ordinary concepts because the payments were regular and expected periodical payments arising out of circumstances which attended the war service undertaken by the taxpayer and because the payments formed part of the receipts upon which the recipient depended for the regular expenditure which he made upon himself and his dependants: per Dixon CJ and Williams J at 557 and per Fullagar J at 567-8, the latter regarding the periodicity of the payment in question as of some but not decisive importance. Reference may also be made to the decision of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Harris (1980) 80 ATC 4238 distinguished by the Supreme Court of Queensland in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Blake (1984) 84 ATC 4661 where the question of assessability of amounts paid to supplement pensions to retired persons was discussed.

23. As Professor Parsons in Income Taxation in Australia (Law Book Company Ltd 1985) points out, periodical scholarships like payments under maintenance orders or annuity payments or pension payments are examples of periodical payments which may be regarded as income in ordinary concepts within the periodicity principle without any showing of dependence or purpose. In the case of scholarships at least, the circumstances in which they are paid may permit an objective inference of both purpose and dependence to be drawn.

24. Until the introduction into the Act of s.23(z) by Act No 44 of 1951, scholarship payments were not exempt from tax. The 1951 legislation arose out of a recommendation made by the Spooner Committee in the "Report of the Commonwealth Committee on Taxation on Income Tax - Scholarships and similar Payments for Educational Purposes" (Reference No 11) published on 10 October 1951. That report pointed out that both the United Kingdom and New Zealand specifically exempted scholarship income. According to the Report there were in Australia three broad classes of payments for educational training:

(i) Awards to enable students to pursue a course

of study.

(ii) Payments in the nature of remuneration for

present or future service.

(iii) Living allowances.

Examples of each of these three classes as given by the Committee were:

"8. Examples of payments coming under Class (i)

are those received under -

(a) Fellowships, scholarships and bursaries

awarded at a university or school in Australia.

(b) Scholarships granted to Australians by

overseas foundations such as the Rhodes.

(c) United Nations fellowships and similar

scholarships, the holders of which are

pursuing a course of study in Australia.

9. Payments coming within Class (ii) include -

(a) allowances paid to students attending

State Teachers' Training Colleges;

(b) wages earned during a term of apprenticeship.

10. Class (iii) payments include -

(a) Living allowances paid to trainees under

the Commonwealth Reconstruction Training Scheme.

(b) Living allowances paid by the

Commonwealth Universities' Commission

under the scheme of financial assistance

to university students."

25. The Committee recommended that payments falling within classes (ii) and (iii) not be exempted from tax but that those falling within class (i) be so exempted. In so recommending the Committee said:

"13. Allowances paid to students attending State

Teachers' Training Colleges are substantially

similar to wages received during

apprenticeships or other training. Both types

of payment are in the nature of remuneration

and should be assessable accordingly.

14. Living allowances such as those paid under the

Commonwealth Reconstruction Training Scheme or

the Commonwealth scheme of financial

assistance to university students are

distinguishable from scholarship awards.

Exemption of such living allowance would lead

to anomalies and is not recommended."

26. The Committee's recommendation was substantially adopted although the language of the section introduced was not in the precise words of the Committee's draft. Commenting on the exclusion to the exemption, the explanatory memorandum circulated at the time stated:

"The exemption will not extend, however, to

payments, whether termed "scholarships" or

otherwise, which are in effect payments in the

nature of remuneration for present or future

services. Allowances paid to research workers, for

example, are indistinguishable in nature from

salaries and will continue to be subject to tax and

contribution. Similarly, allowances paid during

periods of training for future employment - such as

public service cadetships, teachers' training

college studentships, &c. - will not fall within

the new exemption."

27. It can be seen both from the terms of the section itself and from the extrinsic material to which we have referred that it was the legislative policy that amounts received under schemes in the nature of cadetships, where the training in question is designed to prepare the recipient for his future job being in substance remuneration for employment or future employment, would remain assessable and only those payments which were divorced from present or future employment would be exempt from tax.

28. In determining whether the condition to which s.23(z) refers must be one which is the subject of legal relations, regard must be had to the context of the exemption conferred by the sub-section. Although a scholarship, bursary or educational allowance could be the subject of a legally binding agreement between the payer and the recipient creating legally binding obligations between them, this is not necessarily the case. Scholarships, bursaries or educational allowances may be paid voluntarily and not be the subject of any legally enforceable arrangement between the donor and the donee and this may be the case whether or not the arrangement is one associated with existing or future employment of the recipient. That being the context in which s.23(z) operates, the words "received ... on condition" should not be given a meaning that requires there to be a contract between the payer and the recipient or indeed any other form of legal relationship between the parties. While the language of "condition" is apposite to the universe of discourse of contract it is equally apposite to the area of gift.

29. A gift may be made upon or subject to a condition. That condition may be a condition precedent to be performed before the gift takes effect or a condition subsequent to be performed after the gift has taken effect. The condition may, if unfulfilled, put an end to the gift. Or it may be a condition which is unenforceable. The condition may be void, e.g. as being contrary to public policy. As s.23(z) comprehends allowances granted in circumstances where no legal relations are brought into existence, the legislature cannot have intended to restrict the exclusion from s.23(z) to those conditions, the fulfilment or non-fulfilment of which is a matter relevant to the legal relationships of the parties and legally enforceable.

30. It may well be that in 1951 all teachers' training scholarships were bonded scholarships whereby the student undertook to repay the scholarship if he did not work for the State educational authority (see K S Cunningham and E A Morey Children Need Teachers, Melbourne University Press 1947 at 163; I S Turner The Training of Teachers in Australia: A Comparative and Critical Survey, Melbourne University Press 1943 at 97-101; Commonwealth Office of Education, Bulletin No 29, February 1963; Report for 1971-2 on the State Grants (Teachers Colleges) Acts 1967 and 1970, Commonwealth Parliamentary Paper No 185 of 1972 at 21) and that public service cadetships were similarly bonded; but it cannot be assumed that the legislature intended to confine the operation of the exemption to that class of case.

31. In our view, the Commissioner in determining whether an amount is or is not to be exempt income under s.23(z) is not required to determine whether the payer of the grant could obtain legal relief in the event of non-performance of the condition. Indeed, having regard to the difficulty of enforcing conditions relating to employment and to showing damages that flow in the event of breach of such conditions, it would be strange if the exclusion from the exemption of scholarship income conferred by s.23(z) would be made to depend upon a determination of the ability of the payer to enforce the condition relating to employment. In our view, it is sufficient to exclude the scholarship amount from the exemption contained in s.23(z) if, as a matter of ordinary language, it can be said that the receipt of the scholarship amount is conditional upon the recipient working with the payer if the payer so requires.

32. Our view is reinforced by the decision of Rath J in Hall's case to which we have already referred. In that case, Dr Hall desired to undergo training in epidemiology with a view to obtaining a doctorate degree. His supervisor at the university had conducted survey research for the Asthma Foundation of Tasmania and the data obtained by the supervisor formed a valuable database for further research. To fund the further research, the supervisor applied to the Foundation which had power to give financial assistance and donations for research although did not apparently have the power to conduct the research itself under its Memorandum and Articles of Association. The Foundation agreed to grant a research fellowship to Dr Hall for the analysis of the material obtained through the survey.

33. It is doubtful whether the terms of the fellowship constituted by a letter from the Foundation to Dr Hall created legally binding relations between them. What is presently important however is that Rath J, in considering whether the amounts of the fellowship were received upon condition that Dr Hall would render services to the Foundation (i.e. the research), did not proceed to analyse whether any contractual or otherwise legally binding relationship had been established between the Foundation and Dr Hall. Rather his Honour, speaking in terms of grants, an expression which connotes voluntary payments and the lack of legal relations said at 4165:

"On the evidence I think Dr Hall should be regarded

as receiving the money on the conditions laid down

by the Foundation in their letter to him. ...

Although the Foundation was not itself carrying out

the collation and analysis of data from the survey,

and may not have been authorized by its Memorandum

of Association to do so, it was concerned to ensure

that the work of collation and analysis was done,

and had the power to make a grant for that purpose.

In those circumstances it seems to me that the

person receiving the grant for those purposes is

receiving it upon the condition of rendering

services to the Foundation. There is work being

done, and that work is being done in furtherance of

a survey conducted for the Foundation at its

expense. This seems to me in the circumstances of

this case to be work done for the Foundation of

such a kind as to constitute the rendering of

services to it. ..."

34. Accordingly, we are of the view that it is not a necessary requirement that the condition upon which a scholarship is paid be one the fulfilment or non-fulfilment of which has an effect on legal relations, whether those relations be in terms of rights, duties, powers or immunities. It is sufficient that the condition satisfies the words of the sub-section, that is to say that it be one requiring that the student will or will if required render or continue to render services to the payer, whether or not there are legal relations created between the parties in respect of the fulfilment of the condition.

35. On the facts of the present case it seems clear that a legally binding relationship was created by the acceptance of the offer of cadetship, that relationship binding the respondent at the very least to work for the Society in the vacation periods. Whether the existence of that condition would have been of itself enough to take the scholarship out of the exemption conferred by s.23(z) is a difficult question. His Honour thought not, finding that, as the vacation services had been performed and no further vacation services remained to be performed, the scholarship moneys were no longer paid upon the condition that the respondent "will" render services. In so holding his Honour treated the word "will" as requiring that the relevant services to which the condition refers must remain as at the date of payment to be performed in the future.

36. The words "amount received upon condition that the student will render services", being elliptical, look to the receipt of the amount under an arrangement under which the student will render services, so that it is sufficient that the rendering of services lay in the future at the time the arrangement was entered into irrespective of whether the services had been performed at the time of payment. Any other interpretation would produce, as in the present case, a somewhat extraordinary result that the scholarship payments could be for part of the time in which they were paid exempt income and for part of the time assessable income. In our view, even if it were necessary that a legally binding relationship were created by the acceptance of the offer of cadetship and even if the condition that the respondent work for the Society on graduation be disregarded, the payments in the year of income would still have been excluded from the exemption to s.23(z) with the consequence that those payments formed part of the respondent's assessable income.

37. We are accordingly of the opinion that the appeal should be allowed. Counsel for the Commissioner informed us that the parties had an arrangement with respect to costs and sought no order. In these circumstances, there will be no order as to costs.

Appeal from the order of a single judge of the Court by which a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal was affirmed.

2. The circumstances out of which those proceedings arose and upon which this appeal is to be determined are disclosed in the reasons for judgment of Davies and Hill J.J., which I have had the advantage of reading.

3. I deal first with the construction of s.23(z). Gummow J. said:

"In my view, 'upon condition that' in sub-s.

23 (z) is to be read as a stipulation, the

fulfilment or non-fulfilment of which has an

effect on legal relations, whether those

relations be in terms of rights, duties,

powers or immunities."

Before stating that conclusion his Honour had observed:

"The conditions with which s. 23 (z) are

concerned are not conditions as to the use to

which the student is to put the amount

received; they are thus not conditions going

to the essential character of the scholarship,

bursary or educational allowance. In what

sense is the expression 'condition' used in

s.23 (z) where the stipulation is not as to

the application of the money by the student

but that the student will (or will, if

required) render or continue to render,

services to the persons or authority with whom

the amount is received?

Any 'condition' as to the use to which the

amount of a scholarship was to be put might be

a condition with some legal effect or it might

be a stipulation falling short of that.

'Condition' is a term used in differing senses

in various areas of the law; some of them are

appropriate to the present universe of

discourse. A 'condition' might be imposed

upon the holder of an 'actuarial cadetship' by

way of a trust obligation (cf. Barclays Bank

Ltd. v Quistclose Investments Ltd. (1970) AC

567) or a personal obligation enforceable in

equity (Gill v Gill [1921] NSWStRp 39; (1921) 21 SR (NSW) 400;

Muschinski v Dodds [1985] HCA 78; (1985) 160 CLR 583 at

605-606, 624-625). The stipulation might

amount to contractual promises by the

recipient of the scholarship (cf. Wickman

Machine Tool Sales Ltd. v L. Schuler AG (1972)

2 All ER 1173 at 1180; Moreton v Montrose Ltd.

[1986] NZCA 30; (1986) 2 NZLR 496). Further, even if the

stipulation did not have effect as a

contractual promise, a student who took the

money but did not observe the stipulation

might be obliged to effect fair and just

restitution for the benefit derived at the

expense of the Society, as one of the new or

developing categories of case referred to by

Deane J. in Pavey and Matthews Pty. Ltd. v

Paul [1987] HCA 5; (1987) 162 CLR 221 at 256-257. (The

Commissioner did not seek to rely on any such

principle in the present case.) Or, the

stipulation might do no more than bind the

student as a matter of honour without any

attendant legal sanction.

The objective sought to be secured by the

stipulation is the rendering of the services

to the person or authority from whom that

money is received. This is something

ordinarily seen as falling within the pale of

legal relations. Further, though the reliance

must not be pressed too far, it is to be borne

in mind that sub-para. (i) of s. 23 (z)

operates to identify a class of amounts which

are excluded from what otherwise would be

exempt income. One would ordinarly incline to

construe such a provision so as to give it a

specific rather than a loose meaning."

On the other hand, one may doubt that the legislative purpose which prompted the exclusion expressed in paragraph 23(z)(i) involved any concern with the nature, or with the absence, of any legal relationship between "the student" and "that person or authority". It seems unlikely that Parliament intended that an amount received upon a condition of a description expressed in the paragraph should not be excluded if the condition proved to be devoid of legal effect because it was impossible of performance, or because the services to be rendered were illegal acts, or because "the student" and "that person or authority" had declared that the arrangement into which they were entering and of which the condition was part was not to be binding in law, but in honour only. The first two instances I have given may be thought so rare that they may be ignored. But I doubt whether the third instance may be disregarded on that ground. The considerations against legally binding young scholars to undertake particular employment when full-time education shall have been completed were perhaps not so notorious in 1951, when s.23(z) was enacted, as they now are, but they were not then ignored.

4. The phrase "upon condition that" savours of legal discourse. But it is also plain English of common speech. On balance I think that it should not be understood, in s.23(z), as designating only a stipulation the fulfilment or non-fulfilment of which has an effect on legal relations. It does, however, denote a requirement which cannot be satisfied by subjective understanding, or even by mere statement of intention or of expectation. The first meaning given by the Shorter Oxford English dictionary for the noun "condition" conveys what the phrase requires : "Something demanded or required as a prerequisite to the granting or performance of something else; a provision, a stipulation".

5. I turn to the question whether the amounts aggregating $2,025 which the respondent received from T. & G. Mutual Life Society Ltd. after January in the year of income ended 30 June 1981 were of a description contained in paragraph 23(z)(i). The Administrative Appeals Tribunal's findings concerning the expectations and understandings of the Society and of the respondent do not in my opinion provide material relevant to the resolution of that question, which must be answered upon a consideration of the conduct of those two parties. The relevant conduct which the evidence disclosed was the publication by the Society to the respondent of a document in the terms of the document entitled "Actuarial Cadetship-Macquarie University", his written acceptance (in a document not in evidence) of an offer by the Society to admit him to one of the "cadetships" to which the document entitled "Actuarial Cadetship-Macquarie University" refers, on the terms stated in the latter document, the payment by the Society to him of moneys, both "benefits" of the kind which the document specifies and salary for his vacation work, and his performance of vacation work in the Society's employment while he was undertaking the B.A. degree course to which the document refers.

6. The respondent was at relevant times resident in New South Wales. The sentence in paragraph 3(a) of the document entitled "Actuarial Cadetship-Macquarie University" (hereinafter called "the cadetship document"), "Cadets will be expected to work at Head Office during Christmas vacation.", although in terms a mere statement of expectation, is in my opinion to be understood as stating, by means of a euphemism, a requirement made by the Society of the student as a condition of the Society's acceptance of the student as a cadet. That paragraph is under the heading "Conditions". Amounts received by the respondent from the Society as "benefits" before February 1981 were in my opinion received "upon condition that" he would render services to the Society during Christmas vacation and were amounts within a description contained in paragraph 23(z)(i).

7. Gummow J. based upon the circumstance that when the amounts aggregating $2,025 were received, after January 1981, no further services by way of Christmas vacation work remained to be rendered by the respondent, the conclusion that none of those amounts was received upon condition that the respondent would render services of that kind. The respondent was to complete the three year course of study, in respect of each year of which "benefits" were payable, at the end of the 1981 academic year, and his Honour held, correctly in my respectful opinion, that the Christmas vacations to which the cadetship document referred did not include that vacation which followed after the last of those three years. But I do not consider that the futurity of tense of the verbs in paragraph 23(z)(i) has reference to the time of receipt of the amount contemplated by that paragraph. I would understand the phrase "upon condition that" in the sense "upon a condition in the terms that", or "upon a condition providing that". The phrase being understood in that sense, the futurity of tense has reference to the time at which the condition came into existence. The "amount received" would, if that construction of the paragraph were adopted, be received "upon" such a condition as the paragraph specifies if it were such an amount, or an amount of such a character, as had been at the time the condition came into existence declared, expressly or impliedly, to be payable subject to the condition. The amounts here in question were of that character. In my respectful opinion that construction, being one which the words of the paragraph bear without undue strain, is to be preferred to that which Gummow J. adopted because it avoids the anomalous conclusion that some instalments of scholarship income or bursary allowance income or other educational allowance income fall within the paragraph and other instalments do not, notwithstanding that all of the income is of the kind which it is, as I think, reasonably clear the draftsman intended to bring within the paragraph. I therefore conclude that the amounts aggregating $2025 formed part of the respondent's assessable income.

8. The sentence numbered 4 in the cadetship document reads:

"On graduation from Macquarie University the

cadet would join the Society's staff and a

condition of employment will be willingness to

work in Sydney or Melbourne as the Society

requires."

Although the first clause of that sentence obscures its meaning in the subjunctive, I should be prepared to understand it as a euphemistic expression of a condition that the student will join the Society's staff on graduation, and therefore as a condition of a description contained in paragraph 23(z)(i), but for the doubt about that conclusion which the second clause of the sentence raises. That clause seems to state that the society will not employ the student unless the student is willing to work in Sydney or in Melbourne as the Society requires. If the student chooses to withhold an expression of that willingness when he presents himself for employment on graduation, he is free of any obligation to render services to the Society. On that understanding of the sentence, it could hardly be said to give expression to a condition of a description contained in paragraph 23(z)(i). If the second sentence be understood as a statement that it will be a term of the student's employment after graduation that he work in Sydney or Melbourne as the Society requires, the difficulty is resolved and the sentence is seen as giving expression to a condition which answers a description in that paragraph. Although I have held that that paragraph comprehends more than stipulations having legal effect, I do not intend so to construe the paragraph as to give it, in the words of Gummow J., a loose meaning, nor to treat as amounting to a condition of a kind which the paragraph specifies a sentence of uncertain meaning. In my opinion this is such a sentence and I am not able to conclude that it gives expression to a statement of an obligation on the "the cadet" to render services to the Society on graduation. I am prepared to hold that an obligation binding in honour only may satisfy the requirements of paragraph 23(z)(i), but not prepared to hold that what might well be intended to express an obligation of a kind specified in the paragraph, but does not, satisfies those requirements. Accordingly I could not base on that sentence a conclusion that the amounts aggregating $2,025 fell within the exclusion which the paragraph effects.

9. I would order that the appeal be allowed, that the order made on 5 April 1989 determining the appeal from the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal be set aside, that the appeal from that decision be allowed, that the decision be set aside and that the decision of the appellant to disallow the respondent's objection against the assessment issued 28 August 1981 be confirmed.
