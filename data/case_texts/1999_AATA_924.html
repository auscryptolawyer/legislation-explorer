Fox and Commissioner for Superannuation [1999] AATA 924 (8 December 1999)

Last Updated: 10 January 2000

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 924

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No N1999/1013

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re RUSSELL WALTER FOX

Applicant

And COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President) Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member) Mr G.D. Stanford (Member)

Date 8 December 1999

Place Sydney

Decision The decision under review is set aside and the matter is referred back to the respondent with a direction that the amount of the applicant's pension be re-calculated in accordance with these reasons and that any necessary adjustments be made.

(Sgd) BJ McMahon

..............................................

Deputy President

CATCHWORDS

SUPERANNUATION - Superannuation Act 1922 - section 13(1) - whether dependants allowances form part of the members salary - Fox v Commissioner for Superannuation [1999] FCA 372 - salary - application of War Financial (Military Forces) Regulations - implication of members mandatory allotment - allowance - part of members salary

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President), Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member), Mr G.D. Stanford (Member)

1. The applicant was born on 30 September 1920. He enlisted as a cadet in the Royal Military College, Duntroon, on 26 February 1938, graduating on 17 August 1940. He was thereupon appointed as a Lieutenant in the Australian Staff Corps, being part of the Permanent Military Forces. On 1 March 1942, he was seconded to serve in the Second Australian Imperial Force (2nd AIF). He subsequently saw active service in Australia and overseas. On 18 May 1944, he was appointed as a substantive Captain in the 2nd AIF with effect from 1 September 1942.

2. On 23 March 1945 he was retired from the Permanent Military Forces on the ground of invalidity. Section 60N of the Superannuation Act 1922 thereupon entitled him to "the full pension for which he was contributing at the time of his retirement". The applicant as a member of the Permanent Military Forces (not as a member of the 2nd AIF) was an eligible contributor under the Superannuation Act.

3. Section 13(1) of the Act provided for contributions to be made by employees in numbers of units corresponding with particular ranges of annual salaries. The Superannuation Act 1922, of course, applied throughout the Commonwealth Public Service. It was not designed particularly to accommodate members of the Permanent Military Forces. The definition of salary therefore did not pay particular regard to the legislation and practice in the military.

4. The Act defines salary as follows:

"Salary" means salary or wages, and includes the value of allowances such as allowances for rent, house allowed rent free, light, fuel, rations, and fees allowed regularly as emoluments of office, but does not include bonuses, overtime payments, or allowances for forage, equipment, climatic disadvantages, cost of living or travelling expenses;"

5. At the time of his retirement, the applicant's pay was 28 shillings per day, amounting to 511 pounds per annum. This would entitle him, under section 13, to contribute at the rate of 9 units of pension. At the time of his retirement, he had allotted 12 shillings per day from his pay to be paid to his wife. This was paid regularly to her account with the Commonwealth Savings Bank, Gordon. Because of the allotment, a further amount was also paid to her by way of dependants allowance. Initially this sum was four and sixpence per day, until their son was born in 1944, when it rose to seven and sixpence per day, being the rate appropriate for 2 dependants. The dependants allowance was also paid regularly to applicant's wife in the same bank account.

6. After his retirement on the ground of invalidity in 1945, the applicant was paid his pension. The amount of the pension was calculated with reference to the rate of pay he received as a Lieutenant in the Permanent Military Forces notwithstanding his substantive rank of Captain in the 2nd AIF. It also took into account the dependants allowance, which had amounted to 136 pounds 17 shillings and 6 pence per year.

7. The pension was suspended and reinstated at various times for reasons not here relevant. Ultimately, following investigations and advice, after the rate of pension had been raised, the present reviewable decision was taken on 20 October 1992 to reduce the amount of the pension again. That decision was affirmed on a reconsideration in January 1994. An application was then brought to this Tribunal to review that second reviewable decision. The principal issue before the Tribunal was whether the pension should have been calculated by reference to the applicant's salary as a Lieutenant in the Australian Staff Corps and not to the higher pay and allowances due to him as a Captain in the 2nd AIF. It was the respondent's contention that he was entitled only to the lower amount. It is not clear whether it was argued before the Tribunal that account should also not be taken of the dependants allowance in determining the applicant's appropriate salary, whether or not it was payable at the Lieutenant's or Captain's rate. At any event, the Tribunal made no finding on this aspect of the dispute.

8. The Tribunal found against the applicant, who then appealed to the Federal Court. The appeal was heard by a Full Court comprising Black CJ, Branson and Sackville JJ. Their judgement is reported in (1997) 78 FCR 151.

9. Subsequently, the applicant applied to the same court under order 35, rule 7 of the Federal Court Rules for an order setting aside that judgement and granting a re-hearing of that appeal. The principal ground for the application was that neither party had brought to the attention of the Court the determinative effect of section 45B of the Superannuation Act.

10. On the hearing of the second application before the Full Court, Black CJ, refused to grant a re-hearing, principally on the grounds that he did not believe that section 48B would have assisted the applicant and that even if it had been part of the "seemingly exhaustive examination of the relevant legislation", his judgement would not have altered.

11. A different view was taken by the majority, comprising Branson and Sackville JJ. For reasons that are no longer relevant in the present application, they found in favour of the applicant and held that, at the time of his retirement, he was liable under the Act to contribute in respect of the number of units related to the salary he received as a Captain in the 2nd AIF. This finding necessarily led to the conclusion that this Tribunal was in error when it had concluded otherwise. One of the remedies open to their Honours was to refer the matter back for re-hearing in accordance with law.

12. It appears from their judgement, however, that the question of the dependants allowance was also argued before the Court. This fact is attested to by references made in their judgements to the basis upon which the argument was put. We were also given extracts from the written submissions made by the parties to the Full Court which indicate that the question whether or not the dependants allowance was part of the applicant's salary was fully argued before the Court.

13. As to the dependants allowance, the majority said:

"The respondent contended that if, contrary to his primary submission, the applicant's entitlement under the Superannuation Act to units of pension was governed by the extent of his liability to make contributions, that liability was not affected by the dependants allowance paid during his period of service with the 2nd AIF. It is not entirely clear whether the AAT was invited to address this question. In any event, it did not find it necessary to do so and made no findings concerning the circumstances in which the dependants allowance was paid. Nor did the AAT consider the effect, if any, of payment of that allowance on the applicant's liability to make contributions under the Superannuation Act.

Documentation before the AAT records that, at the time of his retirement, the applicant received the benefit of a dependants allowance. If the value of that allowance were simply added to the applicant's salary as a Captain in the 2nd AIF (£511 per annum), it seems that the effect would have been to require the applicant to make contributions in respect of at least ten units of pension.

The dependants allowance was provided for in reg 34 of the War Financial Regulations. Regulation 34 stated that, where a member had one or more persons wholly or partly dependent upon him, a dependants allowance could be paid at a rate determined by a prescribed authority, subject to a statutory maximum. In general, it appears that dependants allowances were not paid to the member, but to the dependants themselves or, in some cases, persons responsible for the dependants: see reg 36(1), (2), (3). In certain circumstances, the person to whom the allowances was paid was bound to use it for specific purposes, such as the maintenance and education of a child: see reg 36(12).

We have referred earlier to the definition of "salary" in s 4(1) of the Superannuation Act. In the absence of findings by the AAT as to how the dependants allowance was calculated and to whom it was paid, we think it undesirable to determine whether the allowance fell within the definition of "salary". We would, however, make this observation. If the dependants allowance was paid on a regular basis to the applicant's spouse (as contemplated, for example, by reg 36(2)), it is, perhaps, a little difficult to see why it would not fall within the ordinary concept of "salary": see Commissioner of Superannuation v Carpenter [1983] FCA 148; (1983) 48 ALR 230, at 233-234, per Bowen CJ and Woodward J."

14. The full text of the judgement does not appear to have been reported at this stage. It is, however, accessible at [1999] FCA 372. The matter was remitted for further determination in accordance with law.

15. It will be seen that the reason why the matter was referred back to this Tribunal by the majority was the absence of findings by the Tribunal, as constituted earlier, as to how the dependants allowance was calculated and to whom it was paid. Had the Commissioner conceded at the Full Court second hearing that the allowance was calculated in accordance with Regulation 34 of the War Financial (Military Forces) Regulations, which prescribes the rates applicable depending upon the number of dependants, and had the Commissioner conceded that the whole of the dependants allowance was paid to the applicant's wife on a regular basis to her bank account, it is possible that the Court may have disposed of the litigation finally at that stage. We are unable to say why such concessions were not made. They form part of the agreed statement of facts in the present proceedings.

16. In our view, the matter has been determined by the Federal Court. It was the respondent's submission that the language of their Honours was merely "a tentative observation in guarded terms". We do not accept this. The fact that their Honours expressed themselves in a style of understatement is no indication that they held their views other than firmly. As it is now conceded that the dependants allowance was paid on a regular basis to the applicant's spouse, we consider that we are bound by the observations of their Honours. This alone should determine the issue.

17. In any event, this Tribunal should not be a party to the re-agitation of issues that have been dealt with possibly by this Tribunal before, and certainly at the second hearing by a Full Court. In their judgement (at page 4) the majority referred to the undesirability of re-trying issues and referred to the observation of Brennan J in Autodesk Inc v Dyason [No 2] [1993] HCA 6; (1993) 176 CLR 300, at 310 disapproving of attempts "to subvert the finality of litigation and to invite interminable arguments about the importance of the legal questions to be recanvassed ...".

18. Their Honours also quoted observations of Mason CJ in the same case at 302-303 as follows:

"These examples indicate that the public interest in the finality of litigation will not preclude the exceptional step of reviewing or rehearsing an issue when a court has good reason to consider that, in its earlier judgment, it has proceeded on a misapprehension as to the facts or the law. As this Court is a final court of appeal, there is no reason for it to confine the exercise of its jurisdiction in a way that would inhibit its capacity to rectify what it perceives to be an apparent error arising from some miscarriage in its judgement. However, it must be emphasized that the jurisdiction is not to be exercised for the purpose of re-agitating arguments already considered by the Court; nor is it to be exercised simply because the party seeking a rehearing has failed to present the argument in all its aspects or as well as it might have been put. What must emerge in order to enliven the exercise of the jurisdiction, is that the Court has apparently proceeded according to some misapprehension of the facts or the relevant law and that this misapprehension cannot be attributed solely to the neglect or default of the party seeking the rehearing. The purpose of the jurisdiction is not to provide a backdoor method by which unsuccessful litigants can seek to reargue their cases."

19. His Honour, of course, was speaking with special reference to the High Court. It is not for this Tribunal to encourage the re-opening of issues that have already been dealt with by a high authority, such as a Full Court of the Federal Court. The Tribunal, to use the words of Mason CJ, should not provide a backdoor method by which unsuccessful litigants can seek to re-argue their cases.

20. Had it not been for the fact that both parties have devoted considerable effort (and no doubt cost) to the assembling and presentation of further arguments on the dependants allowance question, we would have been inclined to terminate these reasons at this point. However, out of respect for these efforts, we will deal with the arguments, although ultimately we will come to the same conclusion as the majority of the Full Court. We are to determine a narrow, discrete point namely whether dependants allowances, as provided for in the Regulations, form part of the member's salary as defined in the Act.

21. Regulation 34 provides that where a member has one or more persons wholly or partly dependent upon him, a dependants allowance may be paid at such rate as a prescribed authority directs, but not exceeding the sums set out in that Regulation, by reference to the number of dependants. Regulation 32 empowers a member to make an allotment from his or her pay for the benefit of any person. Regulation 35 provides that if such an allotment is made at not less than the standard rate for benefit of dependants, then a dependants allowance is to be paid by the Commonwealth. Such an allowance requires that there be a member of the armed forces and that the member makes an allotment of an appropriate amount to a dependant. Without either of these preconditions, there would be no entitlement to the dependants allowance. Regulation 36 provides that the allowance is to be paid to a member's wife where she is the only dependant. A dependants allowance payable in respect of a child was to be paid either to the wife or the person directed by the "prescribed authority".

22. Assistance can be derived in the characterisation of a dependants allowance by a consideration of sub-regulation 36(13) which is in these terms:

"(13) Any payment of dependants allowance made to any person in pursuance of these Regulations shall, if that person has a claim for maintenance against the member who made the allotment in respect of which the dependants allowance is payable, or if a maintenance or affiliation order for the payment of alimony has been made against the member in favour of that person, be deemed to be a payment made to that person on behalf of the member."

23. From this provision it will be seen that the purpose of the dependants allowance is equated to payment of maintenance or of moneys ordered under an affiliation order or an order for the payment of alimony. In other words, the dependants allowance is visualised as a payment for the support of the dependants which otherwise would have been ordered by an appropriate authority. The payment is deemed to have been made on behalf of the member, who is thereby relieved of a possible liability to pay a second amount to a wife or child.

24. It is also important to note the phrase "member who made the allotment in respect of which the dependants allowance is payable". This clearly connects the dependants allowance with the member making the allotment. Indeed it does more than connect, it describes the dependants allowance as being in respect of the allotment. The words "in respect of" are said to have "the widest possible meaning of any expression intended to convey some connexion or relation between the two subject-matters to which the words refer" (The Workers Compensation Board of Queensland v Technical Products Proprietary Limited (1988) 165 CLR 642 at 646).

25. Regulation 46 provides that forfeiture of a member's pay is not to affect payment of any dependants allowance. The respondent sought to gain from this regulation support for the proposition that it was a completely independent and free-standing allowance. We see it rather as showing the continuing connection with the member's service. Although the member's pay is forfeited, so long as he continues to remain a member, the dependants allowance is payable. Similarly, Regulations 52, 53 and 54 make detailed provision for payment of allotments and dependants allowance where a member is reported missing, was reported to have been taken as a prisoner of war or was illegally absent. So long as he is in service or deemed to be in service, then the allowance continues to be paid. If, however, he is granted leave without pay, then Regulation 50 provides that the allowance is not to be paid. Thus, if the member leaves the service temporarily, possibly to take up a government position, or to write a book, and is given leave to do so without pay, then while he is not serving, the allowance is not payable. The intimacy of the connection between service and payment of the allowance could not be better illustrated.

26. The allowance with which we are concerned is the dependants allowance as defined and administered under the Regulations. The meaning of dependants allowance cannot be considered in the abstract. It must take its meaning from its context. Similarly, the use of the word "allowances" in the definition of salary must take its meaning from its context. As Dixon J said in Mutual Acceptance Company Limited v the Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 34; (1944) 69 CLR 389 at 402:

"Allowance" is one of the many words which take their meaning from a context rather than affecting or controlling the meaning of other words of the context in which they occur. For, considered alone and at rest rather than at work with other words, it means the allowing of a thing or a thing allowed. It is only by its application that you discover the kind of thing in mind."

27. The context of the word "allowances" in the definition of salary is not its association with salary or wages appearing earlier in the definition. In its application to the circumstances of this case, the context from which allowances in the salary definition will take its meaning, is the context to be found in the Regulations dealing with the particular allowance with which we are concerned.

28. The ordinary meaning of allowance was discussed by Latham CJ in Mutual Acceptance at pages 396 and 397 as follows:

"It is contended, however, that they are not "allowances" within the meaning of the section, and that the only allowances which are included within the definition are allowances which are in the nature of remuneration for services. "Allowance" in the relevant sense is defined in the Standard Dictionary as meaning:- "That which is allowed; a portion or amount granted for some purpose, as by military regulation, operation of law, or judicial decree: also, a limited amount or portion, as of income or food; as, an allowance of rations; an allowance for costs; an allowance for tare or breakage; an extra allowance for services; to put one on an allowance of bread." When the word is used in connection with the relation of employer and employee it means in my opinion a grant of something additional to ordinary wages for the purpose of meeting some particular requirement connected with the service rendered by the employee or as compensation for unusual conditions of that service. Expense allowances, travelling allowances, and entertainment allowances are payments additional to ordinary wages made for the purpose of meeting certain requirements of a service. Tropical allowances, overtime allowances, and extra pay by way of "dirt money" are allowances as compensation for unusual conditions of service."

29. It is not necessary that an allowance be paid directly to a person before it may be considered as part that person's salary. There is no reason why an allowance may not be paid to a third party but be treated as the salary of the person for whose benefit the allowance is paid, as the reference to "emoluments of office" shows in the definition of salary. Emolument is defined in the New Shorter Oxford English Dictionary as "profit or gain arising from office or employment, reward, remuneration". It is not essential to the working of this definition that the profit or gain should arise immediately in the hands of the holder of the office. The particular allowance with which we are concerned provides a profit or gain to the serving soldier in that it relieves him from the obligation of paying money for the support of his dependants and, indeed, protects him against any claim by them for support under any matrimonial statute.

30. We were referred to a number of cases which dealt with the treatment of allowances in other contexts. We found no assistance in determining whether a dependants allowance is part of a soldier's salary by considering the way in which this concept has been dealt with for payroll tax purposes (Terry Shields Pty Limited v Chief Commissioner of Payroll Tax (1989) 98 ALR 559), or in calculating entitlements for workers compensation (Commissioner for Government Transport v Kesby (1972) 127 CLR 274), or in determining movements in rates of labour in a building contract (London County Council v Henry Boot and Sons Limited [1959] 3 All ER 636), or in determining whether allowances formed part of the salary or income of a bankrupt and whether they formed part of his property (Re Shine; ex parte Shine [1892] 1QB 522), or whether allowances paid to seafarers formed part of the monies for which they had a maritime lien against a ship (The "Turiddu" (1999) 2 Lloyds Law Reports, 401). The allowance must be looked at in its own terms and the use of the word in the definition of salary must be governed by those terms and be considered in the context of the regulations.

31. The reality is that the applicant was entitled to regard the payment of a dependants allowance in respect of his wife and child, as an emolument of his office. It may well be that such an allowance, in any event, is eiusdem generis with the illustrative allowances in the first part of the definition of salary which deal with immediate sustenance needs. Certainly dependants allowances are not included in the specific categories that are excluded, a process that could easily have been achieved if Parliament had so wished.

32. If, as we find, the dependants allowance is part of the salary of the member whose allotment brought the dependants allowance into being, then it is not to the point to ask whether Parliament intended, when it enacted subsection 13(1) of the Act, to require an employee to contribute for additional units of pension on account of any allowance paid, not to the employee but to the employee's dependants. If, as we find, a dependants allowance is part of the employee's salary, then that consequence will follow, whatever the intention of Parliament might have been had its attention been directed to this consequence. It could, as we have said, have made provision in the definition of salary specifically to exclude this allowance. The respondent submitted that Parliament could not have intended to provide additional units of pension to retired members by reference to the number of their dependants. We do not consider this such an absurd proposition. We see no reason why pension entitlements should not be varied in principle to take account of obligations to larger numbers of dependants.

33. We consider that the original 1992 decision was wrong, both for reasons as to one aspect articulated by the majority of the second Full Court, and for reasons that the calculation accompanying the decision did not deal correctly with the dependants allowance paid to the applicant's wife. We therefore set the decision aside and refer the matter back to the respondent with a direction that the amount of the applicant's pension be re-calculated in accordance with these reasons and that any necessary adjustments be made.

I certify that the 33 preceding paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of Mr B.J. McMahon (Deputy President), Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member) and Mr G.D. Stanford (Member)
