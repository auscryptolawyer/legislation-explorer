DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - Allowable Deductions - "Carry forward losses" -Taxpayer bankrupt - All proving creditors paid in full - Claimed losses paid before bankruptcy - Annulment of sequestration -Whether paid "carry forward losses" deductible - Whether losses paid following bankruptcy deductible.

REASONS FOR DECISION

8 December 1989 Mr Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

THE APPLICANT

1. In the early 1970's John, a builder, and then and now a successfully married man, commenced carrying on business on his own account as a builder. His aim was profit but in that volatile industry he knew that profits were not always assured. But it is not the only industry to be so vulnerable. Because many income-

earning activities, instead of invariably generating a taxable income, sometimes return profits and in other years return losses, the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Tax Act") makes provision for losses to be "carried forward" to be set-off against later earnings. The Act also provided that, while John continued to carry on business alone on his own account, any profits would be assessable against him alone with greater cost to his family than would have been involved had he chosen to

carry on business in partnership with his wife, or might have been involved had the business been conducted by a company controlled by him in the interests of the family.

  1. In 1973 John and his wife jointly purchased a suburban development site with a view to its development. It was said that John, with the full consent of his wife, had proposed to carry out the development independently of her. It was said that her involvement in holding title was only to satisfy demands of the bank as a condition of its financing the purchase. The evidence was not challenged and I accept it. Some months later, John caused a company ("Johnco") to be incorporated. It arranged to borrow $260,000 to finance the construction project. The evidence was that Johnco was to enter into some construction and supply contracts on the basis of being indemnified by John; and that John would enter into other construction and supply contracts with persons who were not willing to deal with Johnco. I the latter instances John looked to the funds borrowed by Johnco to provide the monies to enable him to effect payment. Either way it was contended that John was carrying out the development proposal with funds provided through Johnco and with the work being carried out for John, either by Johnco through its( contracts with contractors and suppliers or by contractors and 1 suppliers dealing directly with John.
  2. The project failed disastrously at the time of the economic collapse in the mid-1970's. At that time, John became entitled to carry forward the losses he had sustained and to off-set them against his income in the years to come. The project having failed, John's sole source of income came to be remuneration paid to him by Johnco for his services to that company. Johnco availed itself of those services by providing advice to developers and others concerned in the building industry.
  3. The failure of the business was to result in John's bankruptcy but the bankruptcy did not result in any loss of principal to any person claiming to be a creditor of John. As a result of realisations from John's property (including after-acquired property) proving creditors were paid in full. The claims of non-proving creditors were also satisfied by payment or otherwise.
  4. John's defaults in relation to his tax affairs seem to have commenced before the collapse of the business. The evidence shows that no returns of income for the years of income ended 30 June 1978 and 1979 were presented to the Commissioner until May 1984. But the Commissioner had acted long before that. On 20 May 1980 he had assessed John as having derived a taxable income of $20,800 in each of the years ended 30 June 1976 to 1978 inclusive. The Applicant did not object, and those assessments stand conclusively according to their terms (s 177(1)), In July 1986 the Commissioner advised that, in relation to the 1979 year, no tax was payable and that requests for amendment of the assessments for the preceding three years were out of time.
  5. Of the five townhouses constructed, the first was sold during the year of income ended 30 June 1978. In May 1984 John returned a nett loss to that date of $20,433. In doing so, he claimed to have brought to account the nett proceeds of sale of that unit and one-fifth of all costs incurred to that date. In the following year of -income, that ended 30 June 1979, the remaining four townhouses were sold and, after bringing to account a further $1,223 as costs incurred during that year, John returned a loss for that year of $123,115. The carried forward loss of $20,433 claimed from the previous year was added to give an alleged available carry forward loss available to be set off by John against future income of $143,548. The Commissioner's responses to those returns were as previously mentioned.

THE CLAIMS

  1. It is not disputed that such loss (if any) as John had incurred to 30 June 1979 could have been available to be set-off against future income. But the Commissioner contends that, by reason of his bankruptcy, John is not so entitled. By a sequestration order made against him pursuant to the provisions of the Bankruptcy Act (1966), John became a bankrupt on 18 April 1979. In answer to that contention, the Applicant points to the circumstance that all creditors who maintained( their proofs of debt were paid in full and that, following payment, the sequestration order made against him was annulled pursuant to the provisions of s.154 of the Bankruptcy Act. The alleged consequence of the latter Order is that, from the date of that Order (23 December 1987), his affairs were to be dealt with under the provisions of the Tax Act as if he had never been a bankrupt.

9. It will be convenient to categorize into classes the creditors with whom John had dealt in the course of his building activities and otherwise. In considering the classification it is important to recognise that returns were presented on a "cash" basis. Those categories are as follows:

(a) "Carried forward loss Creditors";

The term refers to creditors for the supply of goods and services used by John in the course of carrying on business. These claims had been paid prior to sequestration, the payments so made not being set aside in the course of the bankruptcy, whether as preferential payments or otherwise. As they had been paid prior to 30 June 1979, they constituted the claimed "carry forward losses" of $143,548.

(b) Proving Building Creditors:

Those suppliers of goods and services in the course of the business whose claims had not been paid prior to sequestration and who thereafter proved in the bankruptcy. Being unpaid, their claims had not been brought to account

in the calculation of "carry forward losses". Their claims came to be fully satisfied in the course of the administration of the bankruptcy.

(c) Non-Proving Building Creditors:

Those suppliers of goods and services to building projects

undertaken by John in the course of carrying on business who had not been paid prior to sequestration but who did not prove in the bankruptcy. Their claims had not been brought to account in calculating "carry forward losses". It was contended that their claims came to be fully satisfied by the Applicant while a bankrupt, but not ink- the course of the administration of the bankruptcy.

(d) Financial Creditors:

Claimants for money lent, being either the bank in relation to John's overdraft or lenders whose monies had been deposited to that bank account. Those creditors included Johnco, and the lender to Johnco in respect of any amount for which its security proved to be inadequate. Those claims did not of themselves give rise to any claims for deductions. Rather, these creditors provided the funds which enabled the "carried forward loss creditors" to be paid. In that sense, they contributed to the-"carry forward losses" and did not give rise to any further claims to deductions. To the extent to which they sought it, they were paid in full, either in the course of the administration of the bankruptcy or independently, as for example when the bank received payment in full from John's father as surety and thereupon withdrew its proof of debt.

(e) Private creditors:

Other creditors, such as John's father who became a creditor upon satisfying his obligations as surety. Their claims did not give rise to any entitlement to, or claim to, any income tax deduction.

  1. The Applicant upon the hearing also sought to advance a

    second contention based upon the provisions of s.B0(4A) of

the Tax Act. The claimed losses already referred to were said to have been calculated by bringing to account all transactions recorded in the Cash Book of John. Given for the moment that that testimony was accurate, it followed that accounts of building creditors (other than the bank and lenders whose loans had been deposited to the bank account) which had not been paid had not been brought to account in those calculations. They formed no part of the

"carried forward losses". The bankruptcy released John

from liability to the Financial Creditors whose advances 12. The principal provision of the Bankruptcy Act to be

building creditors, whether in the course of the administration of his bankrupt estate or otherwise, he qualified for deductions pursuant to s.80(4A) of the Tax Act.

THE DEBTOR

13. Unless the Applicant establishes on the balance of probabilities that when the relevant debts were incurred he was the debtor, the present exercise an idle one. Resolution of that issue calls for findings as to matters which occurred some eleven years ago. That there has been such a long delay is not attributable to the Commissioner. It flows from the circumstance

of the bankruptcy of the Applicant. Further, insofar as the primary records relating to those transactions are no longer available for production that too, is a matter for which the

Applicant alone is responsible. It was contended for the

Applicant that some support for his contention was to be had in the circumstance that, when the Applicant did come belatedly - in May 1984 - to lodge income tax returns for the year of income ended 30 June 1979, the Commissioner did not challenge the figures then proposed. I reject the argument. Nothing more is to be inferred in the circumstances than that the Commissioner did not apply his mind to the question.

  1. Two features of the evidence favour a finding that the

Applicant was the debtor. First, he suffered bankruptcy by, reason of some claims arising out of the same circumstances as gave rise to the debts paid before sequestration. Secondly, his accountant gave evidence which I accept. That evidence was that what appears in the income tax returns had been prepared by him based upon the Cash Book of the Applicant: verified by him to be just that - the Cash Book of the Applicant and not of the

company. Against that there is the circumstance that in explaining matters in the course of his bankruptcy he sometimes spoke as if the debts were those of the company rather than himself; and the circumstance that one suspects that, had the project been profitable, it might have been accounted for to the Commissioner as a profit due other than to the Applicant as a sole trader: a basis for liability to tax more favourable to the family.

  1. However, weighing all of those considerations in the balance, I find that, more probably than not, it was the Applicant who was the debtor in relation to those transactions and that the liabilities so arising were liabilities which fell to him in the course of carrying on business for the purpose of producing assessable income.

THE DEBT

  1. I accept the evidence of the Applicant and his accountant that the quantum of the losses claimed in the returns of income up to 30 June 1979 was determined by an analysis of his Cash Book. Therefore I accept that the loss available to be carried forward at 30 June 1979 was in fact $143,548.

THE DEBTS - PROVED AND UNPROVED

  1. As to the debts I find that at sequestration -

(a) the Applicant was indebted to the bank for $56,105: that that amount formed part of the available carried forward loss already brought to account; that the debt to the bank had been guaranteed by the father of the Applicant; that the father performed his obligations as surety to the bank whereon the bank withdrew its proof; and that, whatever amounts the Applicant subsequently paid his father by way of indemnification, they did not exceed the debt to the bank at sequestration. Having regard to those findings the claims of the bank and arising from the claims of the bank at sequestration do not give rise to any claim which will not be provided for in the determination as to the deductibility of the "carried forward losses";

(b) Two other creditors claimed in the bankruptcy in relation to moneys lent. They too were subsequently paid. However, as was conceded by Counsel for the Applicant, those moneys had been made available to the bank account to be there drawn against so as to enable payment of claims within the "carried forward loss" category to be met. That being so they will not give rise to any, claim which will not already be provided for

determination of the "carried forward loss" claim;

(c) The lender to John-Co relied on its security and made no claim which has inflated the "carry forward loss";

(d) The evidence does not persuade me that the indebtedness arising on Bankcard at sequestration related to losses and outgoings incurred in the(- course of carrying on business rather than to'- matters of a private or capital character; and

(e) I accept that, at dates and times unknown, following sequestration the Applicant was able to arrange to satisfy claims of some creditors who might have proved in the bankruptcy, but did not do so. I am not able to find what those claims were nor how they were satisfied. In particular, I am not persuaded that satisfaction was effected by payment, whether from after-tax earnings or otherwise. It may well be -.I am not persuaded to the contrary - that the method of satisfying the unpaid claims of those creditors was to render a service which was accepted in satisfaction of the unsatisfied debt. If so the assessment was not excessive. There would have been a derivation of assessable income in an amount equal to the deduction now sought.

LIABILITY TO TAX

  1. Liability to income tax is an annual affair which calls for the measurement and assessment of taxable income for each financial year. Liability to such assessment depends upon a state of facts determined to exist at, or to have existed before, the close of that year. What findings of fact should be taken into account in effecting the assessment may be a matter of contention. If so, consideration of subsequent acts and events can play a significant part in determining what should be found to have been the actual state of facts at, or prior to, the close of the relevant year of income. But, in the absence of express statutory provision directing a changed basis for assessment by reason of subsequent events, once the relevant facts are a matter of history, no subsequent action or event can alter the basis for assessment. Perceptions may change; but the facts upon which

    liability to tax is founded do not change.
  2. So it was that the liability of the Applicant to

assessment for the year ended 30 June 1978 required that regard be had at the time of assessment to the facts as they had occurred to 30 June 1978. Those circumstances ultimately gave rise to a claim by John to a current year loss of $20,433 which was at that date available to be carried forward and brought to account in the assessment process in the following year of income. That contention was put notwithstanding the earlier, unchallenged assessment of a taxable income of $20,800 for the year of income ended 30 June 1978 (cf. above).

  1. However, when the time came to assess the income tax

liability of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1979 there had been a material change in circumstances. In that year he claimed he had suffered a current year loss of $123,115 but, by reason of having become a bankrupt, Section 80(4) of the Tax Act took effect to provide that the carried forward losses preceding bankruptcy were no longer available to be brought to account as deductions in the computation of taxable income to 30 June 1979. A similar result would flow at least up to and including 1 July 1987 in relation to the 1979 losses. As a matter of historical fact, the circumstance that the Applicant was a bankrupt at each 30 June from 1979 to 1987 would never change.

  1. The Applicant seeks to overcome the effect of that by pointing to the subsequent annulment of his sequestration in December 1987 and by contending that, not only is the effect of the annulment such as to indicate that he is to be considered as never having been a bankrupt, but also that, because the Tribunal is called on to exercise its power to review post-annulment, it should do what it was not open to the Commissioner to do had he assessed immediately upon the passing of each 30 June from 1979 to 1985: namely, ignore the circumstance that at the date of assessment the Applicant was a bankrupt. Thereby the Applicant contends that the effect of the annulment is that, had the Commissioner deferred assessment until the order of annulment had been made, the Commissioner could not have denied the Applicant the advantage of a deduction for carried forward losses.

THE ANNULMENT

  1. The effect of the order of annulment is said to be to have retrospectively rendered an assessment correctly raised as ineffective. As I understand it, it is put on the basis that the effect of the order of annulment was to bring about a situation as if there had never been a bankruptcy. Alternatively it was contended that, even if that was not so, the effect of the order was to create a situation whereby in determining post-annulment whether the assessment was excessive, the Commissioner, or in his place the Tribunal, should act as if there had never been a bankruptcy.
  2. In support of those propositions, Counsel for the Applicant relied on the authority of cases such as White v Chitty (1866) L.R. 1 Eq. 372; Bailey v Johnston (1872) L.R. 7 Ex. 263; Metcalfe v Metcalfe (1891) 3 Ch. 1; Re Forder (1927) 2 Ch. 291 and Moore v Moore (1962) 1 Ch. 424. In my view those decisions fall short of establishing the proposition sought to be made out. They all involve the resolution of competing claims in relation to property which constituted "available property" which might have been administered in the course of a bankruptcy later annulled. But the present issue raises no such claim. The question to be resolved is simply a question as to the correct assessment of taxable income and the tax payable thereon against a taxpayer in relation to a financial year during which, and at the close of which, the taxpayer was in fact a bankrupt. At the date to which each relevant assessment related, and at the date upon which the assessments in question before me issued, the Applicant was a bankrupt. By reason of that status he was not

entitled at either date to have the carried forward losses brought to account in the determination of his taxable income for any relevant period. Nor does the subsequent annulment of his bankrupcty alter those circumstances. Insofar as this review involves a consideration as to whether the assessments raised were correctly made when they were made, it must be in the affirmative.

24. But there was a second argument. It was contended that the review of the decisions under review must be carried out by the Tribunal having regard to the facts existing at the date upon which the decision of the Tribunal is made. That being so it is contended that, since the annulment has preceded the date of the decision, and since the annulment has the effect of deeming the Applicant never to have been a bankrupt, the question as to whether the assessments were excessive must be judged as if he had never been a bankrupt and without regard to the circumstance that the Applicant was bankrupt at 30 June, that being the date of the close of each financial year under review; was bankrupt when assessed; and was bankrupt when the objections to the assessments were reviewed.

  1. The submission acknowledges that had the review occurred before the order of annulment, the prospect that it might thereafter come to be annulled would have been irrelevant. Instead, the contention is that as in the circumstances the review is taking place after the annulment the review must proceed on the basis that the Applicant is to be deemed never to have been a bankrupt. The decision of a Full Bench of the Federal Court of Australia in Fletcher and Ors. v F.C.T. (88 A.T.C. 4834) was relied on in support of that proposition.
  2. In that case the Court held that in considering an objection to an assessment:

"The question for the Commissioner is the correctness of the original decision, that question being considered in the light of the terms of the objection but taking account of all the information then available to the Commissioner regarding the amount of the taxable income of the taxpayer and the amount of the tax payable thereon. It may well happen, for example, that, between the date of the original assessment and the date of determination of an objection, new information comes to the Commissioner or that there is some change in the relevant law. Subject to the limitations imposed by section 170 of the Act, these are matters properly to be taken into consideration by the Commissioner, in any case, in determining whether to issue an amended assessment (Ibid, 4845)".

In the particular case, neither at the time of issuing the original assessment nor at the time of reviewing an objection to it did the Commissioner make a determination under Section 177F of the Tax Act. One of the questions for consideration was whether upon a review of the decision to disallow the objection, this Tribunal might make such a determination. The Full Court held that it could. It said:

"By force of section 43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, the Tribunal has all the powers and discretions that are conferred by section 186 of the Income Tax Assessment Act upon the Commissioner. In exercising those powers and discretions the Tribunal was bound to consider the facts as they approved in evidence

before the Tribunal. Making the decision which, upon that evidence and at that time, was the correct or preferable decision to be made in considering the

objection. The Tribunal was not confined either to the material which was before the Commissioner, as primary( decision-maker, or the events which had occurred up to that time: (see Drake at 419 Nevistic v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1981] FCA 41; (1981) 34 A.L.R. 639; Commonwealth v Ford (1986) 65 A.L.R. 323 at 328; Freeman v Secretary, Department of Social Security (Davies J. 18 August 1988, not reported).

Once it is understood that, in exercising his powers under section 186, the Commissioner would have been free to exercise a discretion under section 177(F) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, it follows that, in reviewing the Commissioner's decision under section 186, the Tribunal is free of exercise that same discretion if, upon the material then before it, it seems proper to take that course (Ibid, page 4846)".

27. I respectfully adopt all that their Honours said in those passages. But in my view that will not avail the Applicant. The raising of the assessments under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act created liabilities in the Applicant. Those liabilities had to be determined by the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act as in force in relation to the Applicant in

relation to each relevant year of income. One relevant circumstance was the status of the Applicant as a bankrupt. The order of annulment had no effect upon that status for that purpose.



THE POLICY OF SECTION 80

  1. Section 80 of the Act is badly drafted in that it seems that there is no consistency in policy, or perhaps no policy, as to how to deal with the problem of "carried forward losses". The reason for that would appear to be that the draftsman failed to recognise the distinction between taxpayers who present their income tax returns on a "cash basis" and those who present their returns on an "accruals basis". The differences in presentation for fiscal purposes would seem to warrant distinct treatment in any matter of "carry forward losses".
  2. Section 80 (2) of the Act of itself creates no difficulty. When a loss has been incurred by a taxpayer, whether he presents his return on a "cash basis" or "accruals basis", if the loss so brought to account has not been allowed as a deduction for want of income against which to claim it, it remains available to be allowed as a "carry forward loss" in any of the next seven years of income. In the case of a "cash" return taxpayer, the loss would not be claimed until the year in which it is paid, with the result that no question of a release from liability for a claimed loss will ever arise. The difficulty addressed by Section 80(4) of the Act would seem to be confined to a situation in which the taxpayer has presented his return on an "accruals basis", but it is not limited to such claims presented on an "accruals basis" as have not been paid.
  3. It might have been expected that between them sub-sections (4) and (4A) of Section 80 would have provided that, if a taxpayer has been released from paying a debt which had generated a claimed carry forward loss, to that extent he would not be entitled to deduct the carry forward loss (Section 80 (4)); unless, despite the release, he pays the debt (Section 80 (4A)). On that basis it would have been appropriate that Section 80 (4) would have no application to any debt claimed as part of, the "carry forward loss" which had in fact been paid. That is because no question of a release under the Bankruptcy Act would arise. Further under such circumstances, no question of later payment would arise, such as is contemplated by Section 80 (4A). In those circumstances, it might have been thought that there was no reason at all why the taxpayer should be denied the benefit of a "carry forward loss" by the happening only of the bankruptcy; by reason of a release not availed of in relation to relevant debts.
  4. If income was accounted for for tax purposes on an "accruals basis", the debts constituting the claimed "carry forward loss" might be either debts which had been paid prior to, or debts which remain unpaid at, the date of sequestration (or other release) under the provisions of the Bankruptcy Act. As to the former situation, there would appear to be no reason why the taxpayer should be denied the benefit of the "carry forward loss", because nothing in the operation of the Bankruptcy Act avails the taxpayer at all in relation to those debts. On the other hand, it is not at all unreasonable that, in so far as a taxpayer is released from liability for unpaid debts, he should not be able to derive fiscal advantage by claiming those debts as part of a loss to be carried forward, so long as they remain unpaid. Yet, if they later come to be paid, it is consistent with common sense that, to that extent, the "carry forward losses" should be available to be claimed. That is reasonable because, despite the release, the situation comes to be indistinguishable from that of any other- person who has paid the debts.
  5. There is one further situation to consider. It is that of the debts of a bankrupt from which he has been released by the bankruptcy but which have not been claimed as losses or "carry forward losses" because the taxpayer accounts for his income on a "cash basis". With such a taxpayer there will be no claim to any loss, let alone any "carry forward loss", so long as the debts in question are unpaid. If such a person accepts his release under the Bankruptcy Act and never pays, no tax problem will arise. No claim to a deduction will be made. On the other hand, if he does pay notwithstanding the release, his claim will be made as a current year claim in the year of payment; and his entitlement to a deduction will be determined by the application of the principles expressed in Section 51 (1) of the Act, rather than Section 80.
  6. If such principles were to be applied in the case of the present Applicant, the result would be as follows:

( a ) In so far as he gained release from liability to his creditors in relation to debts which, but for the bankruptcy, would have been payable, he will be denied the benefit of the "losses" and of "carry forward losses";

(b) In so far as his debts had been paid and satisfied prior to sequestration, he would be entitled to the benefit of the "carry forward losses"; and

(c) In so far as he paid such debts after bankruptcy, whether in the ordinary course of the administration of his estate or otherwise, and so long as the payment can be said to be a payment by the taxpayer, he would be entitled to the benefit of the carry forward losses.

However, the issue is not to be resolved by reference to any such

generalisations. It has to be resolved by reference to the

provisions of the Act as enacted by the Parliament. Where the

Parliament has so expressed itself that a number of interpretations are possible, the interpretation best according with common sense and most likely to produce a just result is to be preferred. But it is not for the Tribunal to impute to the, Parliament intentions which the Parliament did not express in the Act.

THE APPLICATION OF S 80(4)



34. When one comes to consider Section 80 (4) of the Act, in my view it is clear that it is not limited in its operation to those debts unpaid by the bankrupt, or even to those debts from which he is released from personal liability by reason of the of the decision of the High Court of Australia in Clyne v F.C.T.

operation of the Bankruptcy Act, whether upon sequestration or otherwise. The Parliament has used as one of its criteria a clear, but not appropriate, standard: that the taxpayer "has become a bankrupt". Once that condition is found to exist then, subject to sub-section 80 (4A), of the Act:

"no loss incurred by him prior to the date on which he became a bankrupt ... shall be an allowable deduction".

THE APPLICATION OF S.80(4A)

  1. I next turn to consider Section 80 (4A) of the Act. It provides that when the other specified conditions are satisfied;

"the amount paid by the taxpayer in respect of the debt shall be an allowable deduction ...".

It was contended for the Commissioner that following bankruptcy, nothing was :

"paid by the taxpayer in respect of the debt".

The evidence clearly establishes that the Official Receiver, as trustee of John's estate, realised assets of John (including his interest in the family home) and received other monies from the after-acquired property of John and, having paid the charges for the administration of the estate, paid each proving creditor one hundred cents in the $1.

  1. Counsel for the Commissioner relied upon the authority ((1984) [1984] HCA 44; 154 C.L.R. 589). In that case the principal question to be determined was whether a Court had power to make a sequestration order on a creditor's petition when it appeared that the debtor had already become bankrupt by virtue of the presentation of his own petition. In considering that particular issue, the Court said (inter alia @ p594):

"The effect of the bankruptcy however is that the debtor is no longer obliged to pay his creditors; indeed he is disabled from doing so. If he offered payment they, could not safely accept it, their right is a right of proof against the estate".

But to suggest that that passage supports the proposition that payment cannot be made despite the disability of the bankrupt and nothwithstanding the risk that the payment might be set aside is to go too far. Furthermore, to take such a view would be to almost invariably deny all efficacy to Section 80 (4A) of the

Act. In my view, in the sense contemplated by Section 8(4A), John "paid" his proving creditors in full out of his own assets, (including after-acquired property) just as surely when the payment was effected on his behalf, and perhaps against his will, by the trustee of his estate exercising his power and authority under provisions of the Bankruptcy Act.

37. The difficulty facing the Applicant in the present case is that the condition specified in Section 80 (4A) of the Act is that:

"in the year of income, a taxpayer pays an amount in respect of a debt incurred by him in one of the seven years next preceding the year of income ..."

But because the entirety of the $143,548 sought to be carried forward and claimed as a deduction was "paid" before any of the years of income in question, there is no scope for the application of Section 80 (4A) of the Act to that sum.

  1. On the other hand, in so far as during the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1985 (inclusive), John, through the hands of the Official Receiver, made payment to creditors, there was potential for the application of Section 80 (4A) of the Act. But, on the evidence before me, there is no basis for the realisation of that potential. In so far as payments were made in that period, to the bank, to other lenders and to the bank in relation to bankcard, I am not persuaded that the payments were made in relation to "losses" which were deductible. Despite the absence of specific reference to deductibility of the losses so referred to, I am satisfied that the debts referred to must have, or have had, the quality of deductibility. On the other hand, as to the other alleged "payments" the evidence does not persuade me that such satisfaction as was made during that period in relation to debts which at the time of sequestration had been due to creditors for building costs, that satisfaction was effected in any particular sum, or indeed by "payment" at all.

THE S.177(1) ISSUE

  1. There was one other argument for the Commissioner which should be mentioned. In doing so, I am conscious that in view of the decision I have reached on other matters, it is not necessary to decide the point. However, it is a submission of both novelty and importance presented by Counsel and merits attention. The present references relate only to the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1985 inclusive. It was argued for the Commissioner that the fact that assessments of taxable income had been made in relation to each of the years of income ended 30 June 1976 to 1978 (inclusive) and that they had not been objected to was effective to bar any claims to carry forward losses to be brought forward in the year ended 30 June 1979 and the years following. The argument does not touch the carrying forward of the losses of the 1979 year.
  2. In those circumstances the submission of Counsel for the Commissioner was that Section 177(1) of the Act operated to prove conclusively that there could be no "carry forward losses" available from the earlier period as deductions in any of the years under reference because there had been assessments of taxable income in relation to earlier years. Section 177(1) so far as is material provides:

"the production of a notice of assessment ... shall bE conclusive evidence of the due making of the assessment and ... that the amount and all the particulars of the assessment are correct".

  1. I reject the argument. That the production of such

assessments is conclusive as to the due making of those

assessments and the amounts assessed and the particulars of the assessments is beyond dispute. But I do not understand

Section 177(1) of the Act to say anything about the due making of assessments in other years. The issue to be determined in the proceedings before me is whether or not a deduction is available in any of the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1985. Whether those assessments are excessive as contended for by the Applicant depends upon findings to be made as to the application of Section 80 of the Act. That Section defines the circumstances in which a deduction for "carry forward losses" will be available. Section 80 of the Act allows that carry forward losses may be claimed over a period of several years. The deduction is to be of:

"so much of the losses incurred by a taxpayer in any of the seven years next preceding the year of income as has not been allowed as a deduction from his income on any of those years..."

I find nothing in either Section 80 or in Section 177 of the Act to the effect that failure to successfully challenge the non-allowance of "carry forward losses" in any one year precludes any right to deductibility in relation to such "carry forward

losses" in any subsequent year. To hold otherwise could cause the most extraordinary and unjust results to taxpayers who omitted inadvertently or otherwise to challenge, or to realise their entitlement to challenge, the Commissioner's action in failing to allow "carry forward losses".

  1. As the decision of Deputy President Bannon Q.C. in Case W52 (89 A.T.0 486) suggests, a failure to claim carry forward losses in one year may have the effect of reducing the amount of available "carry forward losses" for future years by an amount equal to the taxable income assessed in the year. But in my view it has no other effect than that.

8)

CONCLUSIONS

  1. Fortunately the occasions upon which one is now called on to hand down a. decision which one considers to be manifestly unjust in result are few. That circumstance may ease the lot of the decision-maker but affords no consolation to the person suffering the injustice. In the days of the Boards of Review, the need to deliver unjust decisions was frequently occasioned by,

unjust procedural rules. Happily such procedural inadequacies

now less frequently have such effect. But, from time to time circumstances arise in which the substantive law as enacted by the Parliament is seen to work injustice. This happens to be one

such situation. I regret the result but, in my view, I have no

alternative than to confirm the assessments. Hopefully others

will take steps to effect a review of Section 80 of the Act.
