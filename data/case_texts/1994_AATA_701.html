Toms and Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] AATA 701 (14 December 1994)

Last Updated: 23 September 2008

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - Application to make late election to preserve rights. Short period before rejoining scheme. Ignorance of Applicant and supervisors regarding effect of preserving rights. Misleading information in Telecom booklet regarding superannuation contributions on retirement. Justice between Applicant, the Fund and other members of Fund.



Superannuation Act 1976 - Section 137, Section 157



Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450

The election made by PAUL VINCENT TOMS on the 15th day of April 1991 pursuant to s137 of the Superannuation Act 1976 be treated as if it was made within the period of time allowed by that section in the said Act.

MR ALLEN: The applicant, then aged 16 years, commenced employment with the then Postmaster General’s Department as a trainee technician in January 1969. Except for an interregnum of 10 months in 1979, he has since then, to date, been an employee of the Postmaster General’s Department and its successor, Telecom, now Telstra Corporation Limited. As an employee of the PMG, the applicant was accepted as a contributor to the superannuation fund established by the Superannuation Act 1922 and as such became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 197, from the date of its commencement, namely 1 July 1976..



On 23 February 1979, the applicant resigned from his position with the Postmaster General’s Department. At that time, he was in ill health and having difficulties with his marriage. At the time of his resignation, it was intended that he and his wife would move their residence from Albion Park, near Wollongong, to Gundagai, where his wife’s parents resided, and that he would, on the suggestion of his wife’s uncle, purchase and conduct a milk run.



The Gundagai arrangements did not work out and the applicant was re-employed by Telecom on 11 December 1979. On 12 December 1979, he wrote to his personnel officer in the following terms:



I hereby wish to have my entitlements to long service leave and any other benefits reinstated to me due to my resignation on 23/2/79 and re-employment on 11/12/79.



On 22 July 1980, the applicant was advised that his prior service with the PMG/Telecom, from 20 January 1969 to 23 February 1979, had been recognised and that his date for long service leave purposes had been determined as 7 November 1969. No mention was made of superannuation or any other benefits.



It is now impossible to say with any accuracy what information may or may not have been given to the applicant at the time of his resignation. A letter from Telecom to the respondent, dated 25 November 1922, being document T25, reads, inter alia:



I am unable to comply to your request to provide a copy of Mr Toms’ personnel file prior to February 23, 1979. Mr Toms’ archive files are located in a central registry where a request was made for them to be forwarded to this office. However, the records did not arrive and subsequent follow-ups have failed to locate Mr Toms’ files. From my own experience within the personnel section, staff resigning from the organisation were not given any formal exit interview to discuss options open to them regarding superannuation.



tomcom 14.12.9423.11.94 2



Details relating to preservation rights for superannuation would have only been explained if the exiting staff member had asked about their entitlements.



In evidence which was not contradicted, the applicant stated that at the time of his resignation he made inquiries from the accountant who was giving him advice regarding the purchase and setting up of the milk vending business as to what he should do regarding superannuation. That accountant was unable to assist and suggested he take the matter up with his supervisor at the PMG. The applicant did this and was told that there was an option of leaving his contributions in the superannuation fund where they would accumulate interest. He was not told that the employer’s contribution would also continue.



The tribunal accepts the applicant’s evidence that technicians employed by the PMG were discouraged from approaching personnel officers direct but that all contact was to be through their supervisors. The applicant stated, and the tribunal accepts this, that upon being informed that his moneys simply remained in the superannuation fund, accumulating interest, until a pension was payable at age 55, he decided that he could do just as well investing the money himself and so elected to receive a refund of his contributions.



When the applicant rejoined Telecom, he received a booklet regarding conditions of service. That booklet, which became exhibit A, apparently published in 1977, states, under the general heading of Superannuation:



6. Resignation



On resignation, a contributor is refunded all his or her contributions plus interest.



The above statement is both false and misleading but confirmed in the applicant’s mind that the only options were to either receive a refund of contributions or leave the money to accumulate interest.



At a later stage of his employment, namely in 1982, the applicant again resigned from his employment with Telecom and again chose to receive a refund of his superannuation contributions. That resignation was later withdrawn but the respondent pointed to the applicant’s election, at that time, as evidence as to what the applicant’s intentions would have been in 1979.



[9:41am]



tomcom 14.12.94 3

We do not accept that submission in that there is no evidence that the applicant was any better informed as to his options in 1982 and he was in 1979. The applicant’s letter of resignation dated 10 December 1982 does confirm, however, that he was most interested in transferring such rights as he could from the Commonwealth Public Service to the New South Wales Public Service; that is to say that he was more interested in preserving potential benefits than obtaining a cash settlement. The applicant’s evidence was that he did not become aware of the right to elect to preserve benefits until 1991 when he attended a series of presentations regarding the proposed new superannuation scheme for Telecom employees.



Once he was aware of his right to make a late election to preserve benefits he promptly made application. At all times the applicant has been employed by the Postmaster-General’s Department or Telecom in the category of a technician. Although he is in a more settled posting at the moment, most of his time and in particular up to his resignation in 1979 was spent as an itinerant worker installing telecommunications equipment in country areas away from his home base. This itinerant lifestyle has raised several factors which must be considered by the tribunal in assessing the applicant’s claim.



The first of these and, to our mind, the most important is that the applicant was away from any settled base depot and contact with administrative staff. We accept his evidence that any contact with administrative staff was to be through his supervisors and, as an itinerant worker, this contact with supervisors was usually by telephone. In passing we would comment that there was no evidence that his supervisors were any better informed than he was. Indeed the advice he did get when he asked his supervisor about superannuation upon resignation was incorrect.



Whereas the itinerant did place strains upon the applicant’s marriage it did allow him to accumulate funds from his travelling allowance. Like many such similar allowances Telecom is not concerned how the travelling allowance is expended so if, for example, an employee wishes to save money by say living in a caravan as the applicant did for one year rather than in a hotel or motel then any savings incurred are profit to the employee. The applicant was closely questioned regarding the source of his finances and we accept and can understand his evidence regarding gains from travelling allowance.



Although the applicant, at the time of his resignation, was on sick leave and was receiving treatment both for arthritis and depression we do not regard him as being so incapacitated either mentally or physically as to be incapable of understanding the full import of what he was doing. The decision to resign and purchase the milk vending business was not an



tomcom 14.12.94 4

irrational one although prompted by marital discord. At the time of that resignation and purchase he sought advice from an accountant and apparently was able to make a proper adjustment of his finances.



We find that the applicant’s decision to accept a refund of contributions was not due to any ability to make a decision but rather a choice made upon incorrect information. It was submitted by Mr Hastings of Queen’s Counsel, who appeared for the respondent, that a young man in the applicant’s position at the time of his resignation would have been more interested in obtaining the refund of contributions in order to boost his finances. It is, of course, impossible for anyone to say now with any certainty what the applicant’s choices would have been in 1979 had he been fully informed as to all options.



However, the applicant stated in cross-examination to that suggestion by Mr Hastings, “That’s what superannuation is all about; to plan for retirement”. We see nothing strange in a man of 26 having regard to his future retirement especially one in the situation of the applicant who knew he suffered from the potentially debilitating condition of arthritis. In addition, at the outset of his working life he was directed towards public employment by his father who emphasised the benefits of the security of government employment.



Once the moneys were received they were placed in a bank account. They eventually became intermingled with other moneys of the applicant and were used in property transactions but there is no evidence that they were used immediately upon receipt or applied then to any purpose. We accept the applicant’s evidence that the proceeds of the sale of his house at Albion Park generated sufficient funds to purchase the milk vending business.



The only other matter to be mentioned is the credit of the applicant. It is clear that in the application for re-employment with Telecom he was deliberately untruthful regarding his medical fitness for employment. We find that the real explanation for that was that the applicant was desperate to be re-employed by Telecom and was not going to admit anything which might jeopardise his chances of such re-employment. Given the evidence in this matter, the Commissioner for Superannuation should have further regard to any limitations which might be placed upon the applicant’s entitlement to claim against the superannuation fund for specific ailments.



Apart from this lapse, we found that the applicant was straightforward in his evidence and given the effluxion of time and the fact that he has suffered from depression which has been treated by psychiatrists with psychotropic drugs, he attempted to give the details of his property transactions and finances to the best of his ability. In appreciating the applicant’s ability to make inquiries on his own account the tribunal is



tomcom 14.12.94 5



conscious of the fact that he left school after year 10, age 15 years and his post-school qualifications have been as a tradesman.



He did undertake a course in small business at Wollongong TAFE and it would appear that this was non-examinable and can hardly be equated to tertiary education. Given the hierachial nature of the employer with whom he was employed, and his position in that hierarchy, it is not surprising that upon questioning his supervisor as to options, he accepted that advice and did not seek to investigate further. Had he sought to investigate further, then the booklet produced by Telecom, exhibit A, would not have enlightened him any further.



In Chalk v the Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450 at page 459, his Honour, Cooper J said:



The conduct of the commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under section 157. If it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration.



In the instant case, the tribunal found that the appellant did not know of the choices open to him under the act and that neither Telecom nor the commissioner advised him of the available choices. The tribunal correctly treated as a fact relevant to its deliberations that the appellant was ignorant of the choices available and that ignorance was partly explicable by the failure of Telecom and the commissioner to correctly inform the appellant of the available choices. However, section 157 does not require that the commissioner determine where there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time. Nor, that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault rather as explained by his Honour, Davies J in his reasons:



The section merely requires that the commissioner be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised.



Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for consideration and will include circumstances of the type identified by his Honour, Davies J as relevant to the statutory purpose for which the discretionary power in section 157, “to treat an election as within time,” was enacted. At page 456 of the same report his Honour, Davies J dealt with the various factors and said inter alia:



tomcom 14.12.94 6



In the application of section 157 it may be of little consequence that the employee did not inquire as to his or her right to elect under section 137, or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case frequently the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account when he or she decided not to elect subsequently turned out differently. Often times the discretions is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of section 157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances.



His Honour continued:



the superannuation scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the act confers are statutory rights but the entitlement to participate in the scheme and to receive the benefits of the scheme do not merely have a statutory base they form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision maker exercising the 157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee and to the scheme that that benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employees service and to the totality of his contributions including any sums which must be refunded.



In his reconsideration of the original decision to refuse the applicants request the delegate of the respondent had regard to the effect upon the statutory scheme should the applicant be granted the right to make a late election. At document T2 at page 17 he stated in paragraph 26:



The benefit to Mr Toms should his request for late election be granted is comparatively small, for example, should his 10 years contributory service from 1969 to 1979 be added to his period of current contributory service the following enhancement of Government financed age retirement benefits under the CSS would result.



And then is set out a table which reads: “Standard Pension of Percentage Salary” and reveals at age 55 with preservation the percentage of salary is 38.88 per cent as opposed to without preservation 35.42 per cent. At age 60 the figures are, with preservation 47.25 as opposed to 45.5 without preservation and at age 65, with preservation 52.5 vis a vis without preservation 51.81. The percentages involved are truly small.



It is for this reason given the applicant’s overall period of service with Telecom plus the fact he operated upon mistaken information given to him



tomcom 7



after he deliberately sought advice upon his resignation. And that he was an itinerant worker remote from the fixed depot and administrative staff in a very junior trades position with Telecom when he made his election that in this case we consider it fair both to the applicant and to the employer and to the scheme that the applicant be permitted to make a late election. A decision under review will be set aside and this matter be remitted to the respondent with the direction that the election made by Paul Vincent Toms on the 15th day of April 1991 pursuant to section 137 of the Superannuation Act 1976 be treated as if it were made within the period of time allowed by that section of the said Act.



MR CUSH: Thank you, sir.
