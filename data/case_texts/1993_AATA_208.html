ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P GERBER (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Assessable income - Estoppel - Employer submitted scheme for the issue of employee shares to Commissioner of Taxation for ruling whether an assessable benefit would be conferred on participating employees - Respondent gave advice, subject to several qualifications, that no assessable benefit would be conferred - Advice contrary to law - taxpayer allotted parcel of shares in issue subsequent to issue in respect of which wrong advice given - whether Commissioner estopped from taxing said benefit and/or whether decision to tax an abuse or excess of power - sec 26AAC of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended.

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

P. GERBER This matter ultimately proceeded on a Statement of Agreed Facts ("the Agreed Facts") which is set out below:

1. The Applicant is and was at all material times an employee of the

Northbridge Group Limited ("the Company").

2. The Company is and was at all material times a public company listed

on the Australian Stock Exchange Limited at Perth. The Company was

incorporated in Western Australia on 17 January 1936 achieved public

status in November 1965 and adopted its present name on 11 July 1986.

3. By letter to the Respondent dated 4 April 1977 the Company enclosed

copies of a draft deed and proposed resolutions of Directors and

Shareholders and requested advice as to whether shares issued to

executives and staff of the Company under the proposed Executive Share

Subscription Scheme ("the Proposed Scheme") would be "tax free" to

participating employees.

4. The Proposed Scheme provided for shares in the Company to be issued

to employees of the Company and its subsidiaries in the following

manner:

(a) the Trustee would subscribe for the fully paid ordinary shares in

the Company as applied for by the employees, and the Company would allot

those shares;

(b) the respective employers would provide the money necessary for the

subscriptions, and the amount so provided would in each case constitute

a debt for money lent owed by the employee to the employer;

(c) the subscription price for the shares would be the total of the

par value of the shares and an amount equal to 30% of the premium over

the par value as represented in the price of fully paid ordinary shares

of the Company on the Australian Stock Exchange Limited at Perth prior

to the date of application for the shares;

(d) the subscription price for the shares would be paid by the

employee by 8 equal consecutive annual instalments, the first instalment

becoming payable on the date of allotment; and

(e) subject to the other conditions of the Deed, the Trustee would

hold shares allotted on trust and transfer them to the respective

employees at the expiration of 7 years from the date of allotment,

provided the employee was still then in the employ of the employer and

had paid the subscription price.

5. By letter to the Company dated 2 May 1977 ("the First Letter of

Advice") the Respondent incorrectly advised that, subject to a variation

in the cost price of the shares issued under the Proposed Scheme, no

assessable benefits would be conferred upon participating employees. The

First Letter of Advice was in the following terms:

Consideration has now been given to the questions raised in your

letter dated 4 April 1977 relating to the proposed executive Share

Subscription Scheme whereby employees of the Company may acquire shares

in that company.

2. You will appreciate that, until such time as effect is given to the

proposed plan and relative facts are fully ascertained, it is not

practicable to give a firm ruling as to the liability to income tax of

employees who participate in the plan. In this regard, it is well

established in law that any such ruling given would not bind the Crown.

The following comments therefore should be regarded as an expression of

opinion and not as a finally binding decision.

3. Under the terms of the share issue as proposed, it is considered

that there would be an assessable benefit to the employee allottees.

4. However, on the basis of the information available it is considered

that, provided that the cost price of the shares issued to employees is

not less than $2.13 (representing par value plus 50% of the difference

between par and the current market price of $3.75), an assessable

benefit would not be conferred on the participating employees.

5. The above opinion is based on the proposal for the particular share

issue and should not be taken as a precedent for general application.

6. In due course, would you forward confirmation that the terms of the

allotment outlined in paragraph 4 have been observed.

6. The Company did not immediately proceed to establish the Proposed

Scheme following the receipt of the First Letter of Advice.

7. By letter to the Respondent dated 21 June 1979, the Company enclosed

copies of a draft deed and proposed resolutions of Directors and

Shareholders, incorporating amendments to the Proposed Scheme, and again

requested the Respondent's advice as to whether the shares would be

issued "free of tax".

8. The Proposed Resolution for General Meeting enclosed with the letter

dated 21 June 1979 stated:

(a) clause 2: "The total number of ordinary shares acquired under the

scheme by any executive may not exceed five thousand in any year";

(b) clause 3: "one hundred thousand ordinary shares of 50 cents each

in the capital of the Company be reserved for allotment under the

scheme"; and

(c) clause 4: "Shares may be allotted under the scheme at any time or

from time to time."

9. By letter dated 15 August 1979 ("the Second Letter of Advice") the

Respondent incorrectly advised the Company that no assessable benefits

would be conferred upon employees under the Proposed Scheme as amended.

The Second Letter of Advice was in the following terms:

"Consideration has now been given to the abovementioned scheme, and

provided shares issued to employees are subject to the conditions

specified in the attachment to your letter dated 21 June 1979, no

assessable benefit would be conferred upon employees concerned.

You will appreciate, however, that it is not practicable to give a

firm ruling as to the liability to income tax of employees who

participate in the scheme, until such time as effect is given to the

proposed plan. In this regard, it is well established in law that any

such ruling would not bind the Crown. The above comment should

therefore be regarded as an expression of opinion and not as a finally

binding decision.

The above opinion is based on the proposal for the particular share

issue and should not be taken as a precedent for general application.

In due course, would you forward confirmation that the terms of

allotment outlined in paragraph 4 have been observed."

10. Before writing the First Letter of Advice and the Second Letter of

Advice ("the Letters of Advice"), the Respondent failed to consider the

provisions of section 26AAC of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the

Act") pursuant to which benefits conferred on employees from the

acquisition of shares under such schemes were assessable.

11. Following resolutions by the Company's Directors on 14 November

1979, and Shareholders on 21 November 1979, the Company Executive Share

Subscription Scheme ("the Scheme") was established by deed dated 21

November 1979. The terms of the deed and the resolutions of the

Directors and Shareholders were the same as those contained in the draft

amended deed and proposed resolutions of Directors and Shareholders

which were enclosed with the letter from the Company to the Respondent

dated 21 June 1979.

2. It was conceded before me that, for purposes of this application, it is not in issue whether or not sec 26AAC of the Act has the effect of rendering the benefit conferred by the issue of employee shares below their market price under the instant scheme assessable income in the hands of the applicant; the sole issue being whether, in all the circumstances, the respondent is estopped from taxing the benefit; alternatively, whether the respondent's conduct was an abuse of power.

3. The question whether the Commissioner of Taxation can be estopped from taxing what the Act has deemed to be "income" is ultimately a question of law. However, in the event that this matter goes further, I propose to set out a summary of the documents, the evidence and the considerable argument by both sides that was presented during the hearing.

4. It would be appreciated if you would advise whether these shares

"T11" is a letter from Dwyer and Thomas, the Company's solicitors, to the respondent dated 6 July 1990, from which it emerges that the Company had, by then, become aware that the Commissioner had reconsidered his position and had assessed the benefits on shares beyond the original 100,000 or so shares allotted. The author of the letter sought a conference "to discuss it further", and in a subsequent letter by Dwyer and Thomas, dated 6 August 1990, it was stated:

We refer to the recent "without prejudice" conference held at your

offices.

We enclose Schedule detailing Issues made pursuant to the Company

Employee Share Scheme ("the Scheme"). You will note from that Schedule

that all issues made were in accordance with the principle contained in

paragraph 4 of your letter dated 12 May 1988, i.e. the cost price of

shares issued to employees represented par value plus 50% of the

difference between par and the then current market price. Our client

has at all material times relied upon the principle as contained in your

letter in making the issues. We refer to your comments on paragraph 5

of the letter dated 12 May 1977, which paragraph was reiterated as

paragraph 3 of your letter dated 15 August 1979.

We respectfully submit that the statement was intended as a general

statement rather than a specific one in that:

1. It is repeated verbatim in both letters.

2. It merely reflects the practice of your Department, which practice

was enunciated in Income Tax Ruling IT1 wherein it states:

"Furthermore, where a ruling is given in respect of a particular fact

situation it will be operative in the circumstances of that fact

situation. Taxation rulings are issued subject to these necessary

reservations".

3. It is the principle as enunciated in paragraph 4 of the letter dated

12 May 1977 which is relevant and which our client reasonably relied

upon as there is no significant difference between a share issue where

the cost price is $2.13 and the market price is $3.75 and one where the

cost price is $2.14 and the market price is $3.78. Statements as the

applicability of relevant Income Tax laws are statements of principles

not limited to dollar amounts.

4. We further enclose photocopies of letters dated 4 April 1977 and 21

June 1979 by our client to your Department. Again, it is clear that

reference is made to the principles upon which a share issue will be

made rather than to specific dollar amounts. In neither letter is there

a reference to a specific cost price or a specific market price, the

only reference being to "the terms of the issue as par plus 50% of the

difference against the market price."

In the circumstances we believe you should reconsider your preliminary

opinion and substitute a view more favourable to our client and its

employees.

We look forward to your comments.

11. On receipt of the above letter, the respondent, through one of his officers, carefully re-considered his position, taking into account his previous advice. Included in the "T" documents is a nine page report dated 8 August 1990 ("T13") by an officer of the respondent. Relevant for present purposes is the recognition by that officer that the earlier advice tendered by the respondent had clearly overlooked the effect of sec 26AAC and that, in response to the earlier advice, "the Company advised its employees that the share issue would not result in an assessable benefit".

12. Although not strictly relevant to these proceedings, the officer usefully set out the sequence of events that occurred subsequent to the respondent's first advice dated 15 August 1979. After setting out the history of previous correspondence, the Officer continued:

... The next approach to the ATO from the Company was via telephone from

a Mr B, on 25/11/86, querying whether the original agreement between the

ATO and the Company still applied. This call resulted in a review of

the matter, and alerted the Department to the fact that considerably

more shares had issued to employees than had originally been approved.

(my underlining)

Additional information was sought, and on 17/3/87 was provided by the

Company's agents Bentley and Co. The agent also requested confirmation

that the issue of 1/3/80 would not result in an assessable benefit to

the Company employees. Further consideration was given to the matter

and it was felt that, notwithstanding a wrong decision had been made in

1979 in not applying s26AAC, the Department would honour its commitment

and no benefit would be conferred on the employees from the first issue

of 100,000 shares under the scheme. On 20/6/89 the Company was advised

by letter of this decision and that subsequent allotments may result in

conferred benefits to the employees.

Although the Commissioner erred in his original decision, it was made

sufficiently clear to the Company that the matter was confined to the

particular issue of 100,000 shares, and for this reason it is considered

that the Company was negligent in advising its employees that no

assessable benefit would arise from any issue under the scheme.

The qualification in the letter of 15/8/79 should have alerted the

Company to the fact that future issues would require a separate review.

In any case, the method used to calculate the benefit would suggest a

review was necessary for each subsequent issue. In addition, there is

doubt that the Company was ignorant over such a long period (1974-88) of

the provisions of s26AAC, and this should have suggested that further

issues on the same basis as the first issue would need to be resubmitted

to the Commissioner.

13. A further report by the same officer, dated 13 August 1990 ("T14"), notes that:

It is difficult to accept any other interpretation of para 3 of this

office's letter dated 15/8/79 (cf para 7 above) than that it is

restricting the opinion to the particular share issue (in this case

100000 shares) and cannot be summarily dismissed as a general statement.

That letter already carries a disclaimer at para 2 and, notwithstanding

the arguments on whether the statement at para 3 can be classified as

general or specific, the fact is that the Company chose to ignore the

implied warning.

14. Pausing here, it is by now apparent that the respondent had finally concluded that the benefit conferred by the scheme did indeed constitute assessable income in the hands of the Company's employees. However, in view of his previous advice, the respondent decided not to resile from his previous advice to the extent that he elected not to tax any benefits that arose from the 1980 share issue. This information was supplied to Messrs Dwyer and Thomas on 10 September 1990 ("T15"). That communication stated:

The matters raised at the recent meeting between yourselves and Messrs F

and H of this office have received further consideration and, it has

been decided to extend the decision conveyed to the Company in our

letter dated 20 June 1989 to the share issues of July and December 1980

and May 1981.

This decision has been taken notwithstanding the fact, that the original

opinion given in our letter of 15 August 1979 was made in error.

However, your view that the decision should extend to all issues under

the scheme is unacceptable as paragraph 3 of the letter of 15 August

1979 specifically restricted the opinion to the particular share issue

which, in this case was 100,000 50 cent ordinary shares.

Paragraph 3 is not a general statement and, the company should have been

alerted to the fact that future issues of shares in excess of the

particular issue of 100,000 would need to be referred back to the

Commissioner for confirmation of the probable tax treatment of the

employee benefits.

Accordingly, all issues from June 1981 may result in assessable benefits

being conferred on participating employees.

18. On 6 August 1991, the taxpayer lodged a 21 page objection which, inter alia, raised the issue of estoppel and abuse of power.

19. The objection resulted in a careful and detailed objection report, which set out the whole historical background ("T20"). The officer concluded:

The Commissioner did err and provide an incorrect opinion to the Company

involved. However, when alerted to the error in the decision, attempts

to rectify the matters on grounds of fairness and equity did not succeed

as the Company did not wish to hold the decision to the one issue of

shares as proposed. The Commissioner has therefore no alternative but

to administer the law correctly.

As is supported by FCT v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105:

"No conduct on the part of the Commissioner could operate as an estoppel

against the operation of the Act."

This was most recently upheld in AGC (Investments) Ltd v FCT (21 ATR

1379-1397). (The Officer set out the relevant passage from his Honour's

decision).

RELEVANT CASE LAW/REFERENCES/RULINGS

(1). Taxation Ruling No. IT 2500

(2). FCT v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105

(3). AGC (Investments) Ltd v FCT 21 ATR 1379

20. On 25 August 1992, the taxpayer was informed that his objection against the assessment issued on 20 June 1991 for the income year ended 30 June 1990 "has been considered and has been disallowed". The decision included the objection report and the reasons for decision set out in the previous paragraph.

21. The taxpayer, who was a director of the Company at all relevant times, gave evidence as to his awareness of the Company's intention throughout to introduce an employee share scheme and to seek advice from the respondent because "the Company is a very conservative company and we, as a Board, at that time expressed the concern that it must be within the law and, therefore, it must conform with the rules of the Tax Department." He stated that he was generally aware of the negotiations with the respondent and had read the correspondence to and fro between the parties. His understanding, as a result of the correspondence was to the effect "that we could now invest in our own company without being taxed for the shares that we would be purchasing." (p 26 tr).

22. The following then took place in evidence-in-chief between Mr O'Connor QC, who appeared for the applicant and the applicant:

What did you regard as the extent of operation of the letter?---Well, it

was wide open that we could issue to whoever we pleased, as a Board, any

number of shares with a tax benefit to the staff.

As you saw it, was there any restriction imposed by this letter?---None

whatsoever. We had submitted a deed which they had ratified, and that

deed covered all the relevant points.

At that time did you have any view, and if so what, as to what the tax

position would be, based on this letter, after the issue of 100,000

shares?---No.

Well my question was, after the 100,000 shares - when the number

allotted exceeded 100,000 - what was your view as to what this letter

said as to the position?---We would just go to the shareholders to seek

more shares to issue to the staff.

Did you have a view then as to what the tax position would be in

relation to those further allotments?---They'd be tax free.

And why did you form that view?---Because our deed - and the deed was

discussed at length - that indicated that all the shares in the hands of

the staff at the end of the seven years or the eight payments would be

40. I have set out the evidence in more detail than is customary since Mr O'Connor impressed upon me that the case raised important issues of law and may well go on appeal. Be that as it may, it seems to me that the case raises only one issue: The Tax Act having rendered the applicant's benefit liable to tax, is the respondent, in all the circumstances, nevertheless estopped from taxing it?

41. Both counsel addressed me at length on the doctrine of estoppel and delved into numerous authorities. I am satisfied that both the doctrine of common law estoppel by representation and promissory estoppel, extending as they do now, to representations or promises as to future conduct, have undergone substantial re-examination and extensions in this country since the judgments of Denning J (as he then was) in Central London Property Trust Ltd v High Trees House Ltd (1947) KB 130, Denning LJ in Coombe v Coombe [1952] EWCA Civ 7; (1951) 2 KB 215; cf the majority judgments in Legione v Hateley [1983] HCA 11; (1983) 152 CLR 406, Waltons Stores (Interstate) Ltd v Maher and Anor [1988] HCA 7; (1987-8) 164 CLR 387, Foran v Wight [1989] HCA 51; (1989) 168 CLR 385, and The Commonwealth of Australia v Verwayen (1990) 170 CLR 394. The fact that the High Court failed to reach unanimity in each of the above decisions is surely indicative of the complexity of the doctrine and its application to a given set of facts. However, if one thing emerges from the Verwayen, it is that the Commonwealth is no different from anyone else, in that it, too, is subject to the equitable doctrine of estoppel; per Deane and Dawson JJ, if its conduct is such that it should be held to the state of affairs it induced by its conduct. At the risk of oversimplification, it may be said that the difference between the majority and the minority ultimately turned on weighing up the degree of "unconscionability" which would result from depriving one party of his/her/its statutory and common law defences when balanced against the detriment suffered by the other party in reliance on the former's conduct. Put in terms of the facts of this case: is the Commissioner precluded from assessing the applicant to tax if it is found to be unjust to allow him to rely on, say, sec 26AAC of the Act having regard to the dealings which have taken place between the parties?

42. The reason that I have referred to the Commonwealth as being subject to the doctrine of estoppel by conduct arises from the reliance by the Commissioner on FC of T v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105, where Kitto J stated:

No conduct on the part of the Commissioner could operate as an

estoppel against the operation of the (Tax) Act. (at p 117)

43. The above view follows English authority; see for example IRC v Brooks (1915) AC 478 where Lord Parker of Waddington stated:

It was further suggested that, on the general principles of law

governing estoppels, neither the subject nor the Crown ought to be at

liberty to go behind the amount of profits and gains when once

determined by any competent authority. I do not dispute these general

principles, but it seems to me that where there is a statutory provision

requiring an estimate to be made for a statutory purpose and by a

statutory authority, the principle of estoppel cannot be invoked to

render the provision nugatory in cases where such principle might

otherwise have applied. (at pp 491-2)

44. More to the point, the issue of estoppel vis-1/4-vis The Commissioner was recently reviewed by Hill J in AGC (Investments) Ltd v FC of T 91 ATC 4180, where his Honour, after referring to both the decisions in Waltons Store and Verwayen, and prefacing his observation with the comment:

Even if it be assumed that the doctrine of estoppel could have

application to the Income Tax Assessment Act, it is difficult to see how

the elements necessary to equitable estoppel have been made out.

Equitable estoppel precludes a person who by a promise has induced

another party to rely on the promise and thereby to ace to the other

party's detriment from resiling from the promise without avoiding the

detriment: Waltons Stores at p 427. Estoppel, by representation,

existed both at common law and at equity. It required a representation

of existing fact to have been made, and was intended to provide:

"protection against the detriment which would flow from a party's change

of position if the assumption (or expectation) that led to it were

deserted."

(See Waltons Stores at 404 and 419, Commonwealth v Verwayen (1990) 64

ALJR 540 at 544.)

As the judgments of Mason CJ, Brennan and Deane JJ in Verwayen all point

out, the modern decisions show the emergence of a single principle of

estoppel, rather than a series of discrete rules. Deane J (at 560),

described the central features of the doctrine as follows:

"2. The central principle of the doctrine is that the law will not

permit an unconscionable - or, more accurately, unconscientious -

departure by one party from the subject matter of an assumption which

has been adopted by the other party as the basis of some relationship,

course of conduct, act or omission which would operate to that other

party's detriment if the assumption be not adhered to for the purposes

of litigation.

3. Since an estoppel will not arise unless the party claiming the

benefit of it has adopted the assumption as the basis of action or

inaction and thereby placed himself in a position of significant

disadvantage if departure from the assumption be permitted, the

resolution of an issue of estoppel by conduct will involve an

examination of the relevant belief, actions and position of that party.

4. The question whether such a departure would be unconscionable relates

to the conduct of the allegedly estopped party in all the circumstances.

That party must have played such a part in the adoption of, or

persistence in, the assumption that he would be guilty of unjust and

oppressive conduct if he were now to depart from it..."

What is the representation, whether of existing or future fact, or

indeed of law, that is said to have been made by the Commissioner?

Presumably, the applicant would have it that the Commissioner by

assessing on a basis which is wrong in law, represented that the correct

view in law of the facts stated in the applicant's returns was that the

gains were of a capital nature. What was the basis of action or

inaction that the representation by the Commissioner or the adoption of

the assumption by the applicant induced? There is no evidence of any at

all. There is no evidence which suggests that the applicant would have

acted in any way differently from the way it did, had no such

representation been induced. It is difficult to see what detriment the

applicant is said to have sustained, particularly when its assets have

been increased by the tax properly exigible, which it has not had to

outlay. Why is it either unconscionable or unconscientious for the

Commissioner, having assessed upon a wrong basis, to depart from that

course and assess on a correct basis? To me, the question is

unanswerable, other than to say it is not.

The last question merely reflects the reasons why there is no room for

the doctrine of estoppel operating to preclude the Commissioner of

Taxation from pursuing his statutory duty to assess tax in accordance

with law. The Income Tax Assessment Act imposes obligations upon the

Commissioner and creates public rights and duties, which the application

of the doctrine of estoppel would thwart. In my view the doctrine of

estoppel cannot be invoked by a taxpayer so as to prevent the

Commissioner assessing pursuant to his duty so to do. The cases

certainly support that view: FC of T Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105 at 117;

Inland Revenue Commissioners v Brooks (1915) AC 478 at 493; Maritime

Electric Co Ltd v General Dairies Ltd (1937) AC 610 at 620-624; North

West County District Council v JI Case (Australia) Pty Limited (1974) 2

NSWLR 511 at 523-524; Commissioner of Inland Revenue v Lemmington

Holdings Ltd (1982) 1 NZLR 517 at 523; David Jones Finance and

Investment Pty Ltd and Anor v FC of T 90 ATC 4730 at 4733. In my

opinion this submission is of no substance. (91 ATC 4194-5)

45. The conclusion reached by Hill J follows a long line of decisions of superior courts in this country. Thus, in David Jones Finance and Investment Pty Ltd and Anor v FC of T 90 ATC 4730, O'Loughlin J, after noting that the liability to pay tax is imposed by the Tax Act and the Commissioner is merely a tax-collecting agency, not a tax-imposing authority, stated:

In other words, the process of assessment that is undertaken by the

Commissioner through his officers is merely "the method of

ascertaining the extent" of a particular taxpayer's liability to

tax. ... Furthermore, "the Commissioner cannot be estopped by past

conduct from performing his statutory obligation to make assessments

as and when he thinks proper". (at p 4733).

His Honour cited august authority for each of the above quotations. I should add that the decision was reversed on appeal on other grounds (90 ATC 4315).

46. Mr Le Miere referred me to Attorney-General (NSW) v Quin (1990) 170 CLR 1. The facts need not concern me. cf Attorney-General (Hong Kong) v Ng Yuen Shiu [1983] UKPC 2; (1983) 2 AC 629 at 638:

47. Sitting as an administrative tribunal, it goes without saying that I am bound by the Australian authorities, mirroring as they do, recent developments in the United Kingdom, with the result that the applicant must fail, at any rate before this Tribunal.

It is therefore solely out of courtesy to Mr O'Connor that I deal with the law in greater detail, leading to a result - in the words of Hill J - "even if it be assumed that the doctrine of estoppel could have application to the Income Tax Assessment Act", the doctrine has no application on the facts of this case.

49. I find as a matter of law, that to found an estoppel in reliance on a representation, that representation must be clear, certain and unambiguous. That, it seems to me, has been the law since time immemorial.

Every estoppel, because it concludeth a man to alleage the truth,

must be certaine to every intent, and not be to be taken by argument

or inference. (Coke on Littleton, 352b)

decision.

53. I am satisfied that anyone relying on a letter which states (i) that it is not practicable to give a firm ruling as to liability to income tax until the proposed scheme is put into effect, and (ii) that, in any event, such ruling would not bind the Crown, is truly volens as to the fiscal consequences. The submission that reliance on a "ruling", hedged with such qualifications, can found an estoppel does not lack boldness.

54. There is a further hurdle in the applicant's path. At its highest, the Company sought a ruling with respect to the issue of some 100,000 employee shares on certain terms. As will appear from the Agreed Facts, by 1 June 1981, various executives - which did not include the applicant - had been allotted in excess of 100,000 shares in the Company. Indeed, it was not until 1 August 1982 that the applicant received his allotment of 5000 shares, to be held in trust, out of a total allotment of some additional 39,000 shares. In other words, it is sought to be argued here that the respondent's representation was "open ended", enabling the Company to issue as many employee shares as it liked, and entitling the applicant to rely on a ruling given with respect to an allotment of 100,000 shares notwithstanding that by then, the Company had exceeded by some 200,000 shares, the amount of issued shares for which a ruling had been sought. Since the issue of additional shares has an impact on a company's share structure, this is a not without some significance. Thus, at least one of the Tax Officers called by the applicant deposed that he took account, when considering the Company's application, the number of shares for which a ruling had been sought.

55. I am satisfied that even if it can be maintained that the Commissioner was bound by his ruling that an issue of 100,000 employee shares in the Company would be tax free in the hands of the recipients, I am satisfied that he cannot be bound with respect to any issue which exceeds the amount for which the ruling was sought.

56. Mr O'Connor QC laboured valiantly through 13 decided cases to try and distinguish his case from the mass of authorities which were against him. In deference to his valiant effort, I propose to deal briefly with his attempt to extricate himself from the dilemma of the doctrine of stare decisis. Wade's case was sought to be distinguished on its facts and that the views of Kitto J did not constitute the view of the majority. Alas, any view expressed by so eminent a judge - whether or not that view is reflected by the rest of the Court - cannot be lightly dismissed. In any event, his Honour's observation on estoppel has been frequently cited with approval and never questioned. The AGC (Investment) case was lightly put aside on the basis "that it does not seem as though the matter (i.e. estoppel) was argued very fully, as I said, it was only before one Justice anyway". True it is that Hill J conceded that "the submission received little elaboration save that it was said to be based upon equitable estoppel rather than common law estoppel". Be that as it may, his Honour, unassisted by argument, nevertheless proceeded to review the position of the Commissioner of Taxation, and concluded that the doctrine of estoppel - whether equitable or common law - does not operate to preclude him/her from pursuing his/her statutory duty to assess tax according to law. Having said that, whilst I would hesitate to disagree with a dictum of a judge of the High Court, I would be departing from the hierarchy of our judicial system if I ignored a ratio decidendi of the Federal Court because "it was only before one Justice anyway".

57. Next, Mr O'Connor submitted that in the instant case, an "expression of opinion" is sufficient to found an estoppel. It will be recalled that in the letter from the respondent, dated 15 August 1979 ("T"6), after stating that "no assessable benefit would be conferred upon employees concerned", the letter went on to state:

You will appreciate, however, that it is not practicable to give a

firm ruling as to liability to income tax until such time as effect

is given to the proposed plan. ... The above comment therefore

should be regarded as an expression of opinion and not as a finally

binding decision.

58. In support for his submission, Mr O'Connor placing much reliance on the decision of the High Court in L Shaddock and Associates Pty Ltd and Anor v The Council of the City of Parramatta [1981] HCA 59; (1980-1981) 150 CLR 225 and in particular to the statement by Gibbs CJ in which his Honour noted:

The question then is whether there was a duty to answer carefully the

questions put to the Council orally and in writing. It is now settled

by the decisions in Hedley Byrne and Co Ltd v Heller and Partners Ltd

(15) and Mutual Life and Citizens' Assurance Co Ltd v Evatt (16) (High

Court) and (17) (in the Judicial Committee) that a person can be liable

for financial loss resulting from a negligent mis-statement of fact or

opinion, although the mis-statement was honestly made, and there was no

fiduciary or contractual relationship between the parties. The question

that is not settled by those authorities is what is the principle by

which the courts are to determine whether a duty of care exists.

59. It is with no disrespect to counsel that I am of opinion that Shaddock has no application to this case. That case was decided on what may be called the "Hedley Byrne" principle, i.e. that but for a disclaimer of responsibility, given the relationship between the parties, there was a duty to take reasonable care that the information provided was correct. The case was pleaded and decided on "negligent misstatement", not estoppel. In any event, it is fashionable nowadays to cite Hedley Byrne as having established a new principle of law whilst ignoring the true ratio decidendi of the case, viz, that a disclaimer of responsibility for the correctness of the decision negatives the duty of care. Thus the law of negligence may well have taken a different turn had Mr Stevenson put a label on his jars to the effect that: "The manufacturer takes no responsibility for any snails that may be in this beverage" (Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) AC 562).

60. I am therefore satisfied that the opinion contained in the letter of August 1979 is so hedged - it is not "practicable" to give a firm ruling, any such ruling would not bind the Crown, the comments "should be regarded as an expression of opinion and not as a finally binding decision" - as to come within the "true" principle of Hedley Byrne, rather that its exegesis.

61. There may be a further hurdle in the applicant's path. Thus, Mr Le Miere submitted that a representation of present opinion cannot found an estoppel requiring the representor to act in the future in accordance with the opinion expressed. Any estoppel, founded upon a representation as to present opinion, will, he submitted, extend no further than precluding a denial that the represented opinion was truly held. Counsel relied on a passage in the decision of Deane J in Foran v Wight [1989] HCA 51; (1989) 168 CLR 385 where, at p 435-6, his Honour noted:

A representation can found an estoppel by conduct only to the extent

that it is clear. It can, however, be reduced to what is clear by

discarding so much of its content as is equivocal or ambiguous. That

being so, a representation of future fact and a representation of law

will often, upon analysis, involve no more, for the purposes of the

doctrine of estoppel by conduct, than a representation of present

opinion. In a case where that is so, any estoppel founded upon the

representation will ordinarily by of no use to the representee since it

will extend no further than precluding a denial that the represented

opinion was truly held (cf. Waltons Stores (1988) 164 CLR at p451.

62. Fortunately, being satisfied that the Commissioner of Taxation cannot be estopped by his own conduct from collecting all taxes lawfully due - save where the statute so provides - and that, in any event, the disclaimers in his letter of 15 August negative estoppel, I am spared the necessity of having to grapple with the weighty dicta of Deane J in the above case other than to note in an aside that, were it necessary to apply it in this case, it may well be fatal to the applicant. 63. Mr O'Connor, in an alternative submission - albeit heavily relied upon - relies on an alleged abuse or excess of power. I was referred to a decision of the House of Lords in R v IRC Ex p Preston [1984] UKHL 5; (1985) AC 835. The facts in that case, taken from the headnote, were as follows:

In 1978 an inspector in the special investigations section of the Inland

Revenue informed the taxpayer that he did not intend to raise any

further inquiries on his tax affairs if the taxpayer withdrew certain

claims for interest relief and capital loss. The taxpayer withdrew the

claims and paid capital gains tax on a transaction about which the

inspector had been inquiring. Following the receipt of new information

in October 1979 relating to the same transaction, the Inland Revenue

Commissioners concluded that the taxpayer had received from the

transaction a tax advantage of a kind to which section 460 of the Income

and Corporation Taxes Act 1970 applied, and in July 1982 they issued the

taxpayer with a request for further information. On 14 September 1982

the Inland Revenue Commissioners gave the taxpayer formal notification

initiating the procedure under section 460 of the Act of 1970 for the

cancellation of a tax advantage. On an application by the taxpayer for

a judicial review of the notification, the judge held that the

commissioners had acted unlawfully. On appeal by the commissioners, the

Court of Appeal allowed the appeal.

On appeal by the taxpayer:

Held, dismissing the appeal, that the Inland Revenue Commissioners were

amenable to the process of judicial review and a taxpayer could

challenge a decision taken by the commissioners in exercising their

statutory powers and duties if he could show that they had failed to

discharge their statutory duty towards him or that they had abused their

powers or acted ultra vires; that unfairness in the purported exercise

of a power could amount to an abuse or excess of power if it could be

shown that the commissioners had been guilty of conduct equivalent to a

breach of contract or breach of representation but that in the

circumstances the taxpayer had failed to discharge the burden placed

upon him.

64. The case is authority for the propositions (i) that the principle of fairness has an important place in judicial review and that in an appropriate case, it is a ground upon which the court can intervene to quash a decision made by a public officer or authority in purported exercise of a power conferred by law; (ii) unfairness in the purported exercise of power can be such that it is an abuse or excess of power if there is unfairness; (iii) taxing authorities are not immune from the process of judicial review. I am prepared to extend the principle of "judicial review" to include the administrative review process created by the advent of this Tribunal. Applied to this case, Mr O'Connor submits that the Commissioner's refusal to extend his "tax free zone" beyond the issue of 100,000 shares constitutes an abuse of power such that it is open to review by this Tribunal.

65. It seems to me that, at its highest, it may be said that once the Commissioner discovered his error in failing to advert to sec 26AAC, he might have notified the Company of this fact, but he was under no duty to do so, the more so since he was not on notice that the Company intended to extend its share issue beyond the amount for which a ruling had been sought and by which the Commissioner abided notwithstanding a subsequent realisation that the ruling was wrong in law.

66. The "unfairness" argument is enterprising, but it cannot succeed. A road sign which states that there are kangaroos for the next 10 kilometres provides no undertaking that there may not be kangaroos beyond that distance. If a motorist collides with a kangaroo 20 kilometres beyond, he may grumble that he was misled, but he cannot plead estoppel or assert that the public authority abused its power, committed an error of law or a breach of natural justice. If the motorist suffers damage in such a collision, both he and the kangaroo must bear their loss with whatever fortitude each can muster; neither law nor equity provides a remedy. Applied to this case, I accept that the Commissioner must act fairly at all times. However, I have not been persuaded that what occurred in this case constituted unfairness or an excess of power. In the circumstances, it is with no disrespect to Mr O'Connor that I do not advert to the many authorities he relied on to establish what can no longer be disputed. Nor is it of any comfort to this taxpayer to note that the principle enunciated by the House of Lords in Preston has since been adopted in this country; cf Century Metals and Mining NL v Yeomans and Anor [1989] FCA 273; (1991) 100 ALR 383.

67. Let me say once again, I have dealt with the law in far greater detail than is strictly necessary out of courtesy to the well argued submissions by both counsel. It is with no disrespect that I note that their argument should more properly be addressed to the Federal Court rather than to an administrative tribunal bound by precedent in pari materia. Applied to this case, I am satisfied on the facts that no estoppel could be raised against the Commissioner, even assuming that he has no immunity from the principle. To found an estoppel, the representation must be clear, unambiguous and unequivocal, and be such that the representor cannot withdraw from his representation; cf Legione v Hateley (supra). Where, as here, the representation said to found an estoppel (cf "T6") is qualified - not just once, but three times - it is impossible to argue, at any rate before this Tribunal, that the Commissioner can be estopped from taxing the benefit this applicant received from a scheme, which, in any event, had already exceeded the "tax free zone" for which the representation relied on had been sought.

68. In case this matter goes further, I will merely add that - although the matter gave me some concern since the evidence was vague, to say the least - I am satisfied on the probabilities that the belief that no assessable benefit would be conferred upon staff purchasers was one of the factors which induced this applicant to acquire the shares in dispute, other factors being that they were offered below market value and payment was deferred over a period of seven years. Again, it was briefly sought to be argued by the Crown that there was no "detriment" demonstrated in the instant case sufficient to found an estoppel. I disagree. Whatever other reasons may have induced the applicant to buy these shares, he acquired them in the belief that it involved no taxable benefit, a belief contributed to by the respondent's representation to that effect. It is thus in a real sense a "detriment" if he is subsequently taxed on that benefit.

69. For the above reasons, the objection decision under review is affirmed.
