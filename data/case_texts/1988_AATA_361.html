Re Mehraban and Farideh Yazdani and Commissioner of Housing [1988] AATA 361 (16 December 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: MEHRABAN and FARIDEH YAZDANI

And: COMMISSIONER FOR HOUSING

No. A88/122

AAT No. 4819

Housing - Words and Phrases

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

J.O. Ballard(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Housing - applicants resident in melba flats - seeking priority transfer to a house - offers of priority transfer to two alternative flats - criteria to be applied in allocating housing once priority granted - relevance of Public Rental Housing Assistance Program to Tribunal's decision - relevance of need based on religion

Words & Phrases "special need"

Housing Assistance Ordinance 1987 s.12 Schedule 1

Harris and Secretary, Department of Social Security 7 ALN N356

Bull v. Attorney-General of N.S.W. [1913] HCA 60; 17 CLR 370

Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (No. 2) [1979] AATA 179; 2 ALD 634

Mansted and Secretary, Department of Primary Industry 12 ALD 227

Neal and the Commissioner for Superannuation 6 AAR 529, 13 ALD 460

HEARING

CANBERRA

16:12:1988

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This is a claim under the Housing Assistance Ordinance 1987 ("the Ordinance") of the Australian Capital Territory. Section 12 of the Ordinance, so far as relevant reads:-

"12.(1) The Commissioner may, for the purpose of this

Ordinance, from time to time prepare, in the form of an

instrument in writing, a housing assistance program or a

variation of such a program.

(2) . . . .

(3) . . . .

(4) . . . .

(5) . . . .

(6) . . . .

(7) . . . .

(8) . . . .

(9) A relevant instrument prepared by the Commissioner

and approved by the Minister in accordance with this

section may provide for application to be made to the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of such

decisions as are specified in the instrument.

(10) . . . ."

The Public Rental Housing Assistance Program was published in the Commonwealth Gazette on 12th August 1988; clause 21 provides for review of decisions of the Commissioner for Housing (established under the Ordinance) under the Program by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal.

2. Clause 2 states that the object of the Program is to provide rental accommodation to eligible persons in the Australian Capital Territory who are unable to afford or obtain adequate and appropriate housing. Clause 4 of the program prescribes eligibility. Clause 8(5) requires the Commissioner to allocate assistance in the order in which applications have been registered. Clause 10 provides priority assistance in the following circumstances:-

"10.(1) An applicant, who is eligible for assistance or

whose application has been approved under clause 5 or 6,

may apply for the priority allocation of assistance, in

advance of their position on the waiting list, where the

applicant has a special need for the early allocation of

assistance due to:

(a) ill-health or disability;

(b) the inadequate nature of their current

accommodation;

(c) severe financial difficulties;

(d) domestic violence;

(e) imminent eviction from their accommodation; or

(f) other similar problems.

(2) The Commissioner shall not grant priority

allocation of assistance unless:

(a) the applicant has been resident or employed

in the A.C.T. for the six month period

immediately prior to the time at which the

application for priority allocation is

considered by the Commissioner; or

(b) in any other case, the Commissioner considers

that the circumstances are exceptional."

(my underlining)

3. Mrs Yazdani alone attended this Tribunal, as her husband was overseas because of illness of his father. Mr and Mrs Yazdani were joint applicants (where "the applicants" are referred to in this decision this denotes both Mr and Mrs Yazdani, where "the applicant" is referred to Mrs Yazdani alone is denoted). It is not in issue that the applicants were allocated a flat at Melba. This was unsatisfactory and they applied for a transfer. This brought into issue clause 19(2) of the Program:-

"19. (1) The Commissioner may:

(a) upon application by a tenant who is eligible

for assistance; or

(b) where it is reasonably required for the

purposes of good administration;

transfer a tenant to other accommodation under the

Commissioner's control, provided that notice of the

transfer has been served on the tenant not less than 14

days prior to the date of the transfer.

(2) In determining whether to approve an application

for transfer the Commissioner shall have regard to,

amongst the other circumstances of the particular case:

(a) the availability of suitable accommodation;

and

(b) the reasons stated in the application."

They were offered alternative flats. They applied for priority for a house. This was refused. They now seek review by this Tribunal.

4. Paragraph 1 and 2(a) Schedule 1 of the Ordinance provides:-

"1. The primary principle is to ensure that every

person in the Australian Capital Territory has access to

adequate and appropriate housing at a price within his

or her capacity to pay by seeking to:

* alleviate housing-related poverty; and

* ensure that housing assistance is, as far as

possible, delivered equitably to persons

resident in different forms of housing tenure.

2. In implementing this principle, assistance provided

under housing assistance programs will also reflect the

following detailed principles:

(a) Assistance generally

the primary consideration in delivering

housing assistance will be the needs of

people, rather than to attach assistance to

particular dwellings or categories of

dwellings;

housing assistance provided will be available

to all sections of the community irrespective

of age, sex, marital status, race, religion,

disability or life situation. However,

priority in granting assistance shall be

determined by the need for assistance;

in delivering housing assistance, as far as

possible, people should be given an equal

choice between the types of housing assistance

available;

housing assistance programs should be designed

so that maximum social benefit is derived from

previous investment in housing;

housing assistance programs under this

Ordinance should be co-ordinated with other

housing assistance programs."

This is applied by clause 1 of the Program.

5. Mrs Yazdani gave evidence. She said they came to Perth in 1986 from India. She had left Iran as a student. They came to Canberra on the 9th January 1988; she had been offered a job here. Her husband had to study to convert his Indian pharmacy qualification to an Australian one. The applicant had previously studied in Perth to gain an Australian computing diploma while her husband had worked. After she had accepted the job offer in Canberra she discovered that she was going to have another baby. They also discovered that her husband could take his pharmacy examination in Canberra. When they arrived they first lived in hostels and then got a unit in Queanbeyan. Then they applied for a government house. At that point they had one child who was two and a half and another child was expected. The applicant had continued to work until she was eight and a half months pregnant. They then subsisted on her husband's unemployment benefit, which produced $222.00; with two children it had now gone up to $244.00.

6. The applicant said that when they saw the Housing Trust they were told that there was a one year wait. They were also told that there was a priority scheme and they were told that they could apply for this because she had received an offer whilst still in Perth. However they were refused priority because their stay in the A.C.T. had been less than six months. They had not heard the reputation of the Melba flats and when they were told that there was a quick allocation there they changed their preference so that they could get housing more quickly. They were told that they could go to these flats in six days. Because it was so quick they never knew what the Melba flats were like. She had mentioned to her boss after the move was arranged that they were going to Melba flats and he told her to be careful of drug addicts and alcoholics. As soon as they arrived on the 14th June 1988 a neighbour told her their flat had just been robbed of a television and a radio; that was their very first impression. Her fears started then. The second child was born on the 7th July 1988. They could see the type of people walking around; the Trust just did not bother to consider their special circumstances or human dignity before suggesting that they change their preference to Melba. Once they had moved, although they had been in Canberra the 6 months necessary to get priority, they were no longer eligible for priority. They were not informed of the existance of the rent-relief scheme under which they could have stayed in private housing until their allocation came up on the wait turn list. Since going to Melba they had practically stopped any social contacts because they could not invite their friends over because of drunks and bad behaviour. If they belonged to another calibre of people it would not be so bad but they were Bahais; their religion and culture prohibited drinking, drugs, gambling and free sex. The applicant continued that living in Melba flats was degrading, they had been lowered in the eyes of their friends. What hurt her was the education of her children. Bahais believed their children were the trusts of God. They put the education of the female first because the female had to educate the next generation of children. The applicant had approached her doctor, Dr Beryl Rich, and she had certified that it was urgent that the applicant be given the opportunity to get a house somewhere else. Dr Rich considered continuing in Melba was a threat to their health and well being; the applicant produced another certificate from the same doctor and a certificate to the same effect from a Dr Crawford of the Queen Elizabeth II Home. This certificate however refers just to "better housing" rather than a house. The applicant said that because of the strain she had lost her breast milk. The applicant continued that her real concern was her three and a half year old daughter. Bahais teach chastity but if one goes to the balcony you can see nude people, you can see boys and girls on the grass kissing and hugging and she could not stop a small girl going onto the balcony; it was the only place she had to play. Bahais considered the first five years the most important formative years and five months of those years have now gone by in those flats. The applicant continued that when she applied for transfer she sought a house in Woden. On the 22nd August 1988 her husband wrote a letter to the Commissioner setting out their case for priority housing. They were offered another flat but decided not to make the same mistake they had made in moving to Melba and asked for the address so that they could go and have a look. They were told they could not be given a specific address, only a general area, so they went to all the flats in the area that had been suggested and spoke to people living there. Reports suggested there were troubles there too. They saw one flat at Mawson and felt that it was very small and could not cope with the family's special needs, there were other flats very close and they decided that they would have to go to the Tribunal. They had tried to see the Commissioner for Housing but were told that she was too busy to give them an interview.

7. In cross-examination the applicant said she was eleven when she left Iran, her husband was 23. The applicant had been back to Iran from India twice a year until the revolution in Iran. Bahais could not return to Iran after the Islamic revolution. She was from a wealthy family in Iran, her father owned a business and lots of properties. Her husband had done military service in Iran. He had originally decided to go to Europe for education but changed to India for religious reasons. He came from an even wealthier family than she did. She had spent 14 years in India and he spent eight or nine or years there; her husband had been studying pharmacy in India, but had worked part-time as well. They had had a very good life in India. They had had domestic servants there. The applicant did not want a flat because they did not know what sort of neighbours they would have. They had been to see flats in Griffith, Forrest, Hughes, Mawson and Lyons, they had been told that a flat would be in either Woden or the southside. They had originally asked for a three bedroom house but the applicant said that she would accept a two bedroomed house. The present flat was crowded because they had a trampoline and it had to be stored on the balcony and then put into the hall for her daughter to use; there was no space to move. The applicant agreed that she did visit a flat in Northbourne Avenue; it was not satisfactory. She agreed that the last thing she had complained about was that there was no carpet, but denied this was why they refused the flat. It was on the second floor and there was nowhere for the children to play. They also saw that other flats had two locks which caused them to worry about the security of the area and they thought that the flat might be hot in the summer. In Perth they had had a two bedroomed house. When questioned by the Tribunal the applicant said that her husband had to take the whole set of examinations again, he had applied for many jobs in Canberra and failed to get them. She did not know when he would finish his exams, he had not been able to concentrate in the past five months. He had finished his exams in India but they had not had permanent residence in India and had to come to Australia when immigration made this possible. The applicant had been a computer systems officer in Canberra; if she went back to work it would be as a part-timer but she would prefer to stay at home to look after the children. It is important to the family that her husband got a recognized degree. It was her responsibility to bring the children up. Questioned further by the respondent the applicant said that at the Northbourne flats they met one person who looked as though he was a drug addict. Another neighbour had said that in general Northbourne flats had a very good reputation but the block they would be moving into would be the worst block.

8. The respondent had accepted that the applicant was eligible for priority transfer to a flat, so the only issue was priority transfer to a house. The Northbourne flat had been a firm offer, it was a three bedroom flat. Since then the applicant had been offered another flat at Mawson.

9. The applicant called Ms. Le Sueur, a community development worker at the Neighbourhood Centre at Melba, who had lived in Pakistan and held a degree in Islamic History and Arabic. She had advised tenants since February 1988. There were 410 flats at Melba for low income people. It was a diverse mix, lots of single mums and quite a few migrant families. The flats were all clumped together in one distinctive area. Tenants identified themselves with the area in a very negative way. Most did not feel at all good about living at Melba flats. The flats were close and overlooked each other; people had to share stairs and ramps and there was a real feeling of everybody being clustered together. The Neighbourhood Centre had had contact with the applicant for some months. The applicant found life in the flats particularly difficult, some people were just not suited to live at Melba flats and the applicant was one of them because of her cultural background. Ms. Le Sueur would think that another block of flats would be better. The reality was that the applicant was from another culture; things which Australians might find moderately offensive the applicant found sinful. Ms. Le Sueur felt a house would be much more suitable because the applicant would have more control over the environment in which she brought up her children, for example whether they came into contact with alcoholism. This was important to her because of the responsibility her religion placed on the mother for the children's upbringing. There were other tenants at Melba who wanted a house. In cross-examination, Ms. Le Sueur said that she lived in government flats in Northbourne flats. At Melba one was confronted more with alcohol, drug abuse and sex than in other government flats and one could not avoid close contact with the neighbours. A change to other flats would be an improvement, but the applicant would still have problems with neighbours if they were alcoholics or drug users. There were no drugs, sex or alcohol in her block in the Northbourne flats but she was lucky.

10. The respondent called Mr Yvanovich, the manager of rental operations of the A.C.T. Housing Trust. The Trust had a 11,500 tenancies operating through three branch offices in the City, Woden and Belconnen. If a person applied for priority housing they completed an application; then a report was sent to the priority housing committee and that committee made a recommendation to the Commissioner or his delegate. Mr Yvanovich was aware of the application in this matter; the applicants were eligible for priority to move to another flat. The applicant had been offered and had seen two, a three bedroom flat in Northbourne flats which was declined and a two bedroom ground floor flat in Mawson. The applicant was interested in that one and the Commissioner had said that they would hold this offer open for a few days. It was still vacant but the Trust did not want to keep it vacant indefinitely both because of other applicants and because of the loss of rent.

11. Mr Yvanovich continued that the Commission had 7,688 houses; 1,719 group flats with two to three bedrooms, 1,815 single room flats and 550 aged persons units. There were 1,975 on the ordinary list for houses and 621 on the ordinary list for group flats. At present the length of wait for houses was 16 to 18 months, this was not regular and was cyclical. There were now 14 applicants for priority housing in the Woden region, they currently had about a six week wait. In Tuggeranong there were 5 cases for priority housing and in Weston Creek four. An example of the top priority were a couple with three children under the age of four all living in one bedroom of a parent's house; three other sisters were living at that house, there were ten people in the house with five in one bedroom. In Weston Creek the top priority was a single parent with a child with epilepsy which made it impossible for her cope with stairs. The top priority in Tuggeranong was a single mother with three children who was facing eviction from other accommodation. In relation to priority allocations the Commissioner's objective was adequate not optimal housing. They would not allow people to specify an area too closely. If people on the priority list reject an offer they are returned to the normal waiting list. Mr Yvanovich exhibited lists with characteristics of people on the priority lists. As to overcrowding Mr Yvanovich said the Trust allowed for two people per bedroom except where they were two children of opposite sex at around puberty and beyond. Another consideration was whether current housing was inadequate, for example a caravan or living with elderly parents. Other considerations were domestic violence or inadequate income. He referred to a very similar case at Melba of a family who wanted to get out, also new Australians. Mr Yvanovich exhibited the guidelines issued to the branch officers for the grant of priority housing. He stressed that an allocation to people on the priority list displaces somebody on the ordinary list, or at least puts them one house further away from achieving allocation. He said that people do take flats while waiting for a house on the normal list, they did that to avoid clogging up normal waiting lists. The applicants had applied for a house in June and could expect the allocation of a house in about 14 months. Mr Yvanovich continued in cross-examination that they had originally offered a two bedroomed flat to the applicants, then a three bedroom one came up and it was offered. It was refused and it had now been re-allocated. There was another two bedroomed one waiting for her but they could not hold it too long. The applicants remain on the waiting list for a three bedroomed house; he reaffirmed that the applicants had a priority for a flat. When asked about the material that went before the Priority Committee Mr Yvanovich said that they could have social workers reports, medical certificates, police reports, or solicitor's reports depending on the sort of application being dealt with and the type of reports that were appropriate.

12. When the matter resumed the Tribunal, the parties and their legal advisers visited the applicant's existing flat in Melba, the area of the flats she refused in Northbourne and the flat now on offer in Mawson. The Tribunal indicated that it would not comment on these flats except to say that the Mawson flat has a large verandah, which was probably able to take the applicant's daughter's trampoline, and was on the ground floor. Counsel were invited to make additional comments resulting from the viewing and did so on transcript.

13. The applicant then called Dr Beryl Rich, a general practitioner, who had twice given her a certificate recommending transfer to a house. Dr Rich gave evidence on a telephone hook-up. She had treated the applicant at her Dickson surgery since the 24th June this year but she had first treated her earlier this year when the applicant was living in Queanbeyan. Dr Rich had treated her in relation to pregnancy during the anti-natal period and the applicant had come back for post-natal checkups. She also came back for nerve problems arising from the housing situation. The applicant was finding stress after moving from Queanbeyan to Melba, she had lost her milk and was not sleeping. It had not improved. Dr Rich had seen her last week and she was still in much the same situation. Dr Rich said that many people lived happily in the circumstances in which the applicant and her family were living. The applicant had said she thought it was so stressful to her because of her upbringing and background. In cross-examination Dr Rich said on one occasion the applicant's husband came to see her because he was worried about her condition. He indicated dissatisfaction with the housing on account of his wife and that he felt the housing was why she was upset, nervous and tense. The applicant first consulted Dr Rich for nervous stress a couple of weeks after the baby was born in July. The applicant had said it was the housing which caused her problems. Dr Rich did not ask whether there was anything else that was distressing her. The applicant had been very different when she saw her earlier while the applicant was living in Queanbeyan. The applicant said she found she could not relate to people in Melba because they drank, were noisy and were unpleasant to her. Dr Rich did not specifically check for problems about racism. Dr Rich said she had had other patients with problems living in Melba, it was a particular source of problems. She herself would not walk through there on her own at night. Dr Rich had discussed the possibility of moving to other flats with the applicant. The applicant had said that they were worried that other neighbours might be just as difficult as the ones in Melba. The applicant felt that a house was necessary where they could be away from neighbours. Dr Rich had thought that a quiet flat with reasonable neighbours would solve the problems but the applicant seemed quite convinced she needed a house. The applicant specifically mentioned the need to move to a house and that was why the doctor had suggested this in her letters of reference. On the 24th July 1988 the applicant specifically came to see Dr Rich distressed about the flat situation and asked for a letter of reference so they could be moved from Melba. The applicant's milk had dried up in October 1988.

14. The respondent called Mr Knight, the acting general manager of housing services in New South Wales, who also gave evidence on a telephone hook-up. Mr Knight had dealt with priority accommodation in New South Wales for twenty years. He said that on transfer on a priority basis applicants must satisfy the N.S.W. Authorities that the present accommodation affected their life so that there was a major threat of domestic violence, conflict with neighbours amounting to crime or that they had become dysfunctional, eg. a quadraplegic who could not make it up steps. Mr Knight said that a conflict between the accommodation and tenant's religious beliefs was not accepted as a criteria for priority allocation. It had been advanced, normally by old people wanting to be near their place of worship, and in every case it had been rejected, there was just no delegation authorising acceptance on these grounds.

15. In cross-examination Mr Knight said that the transfer guidelines were set out by way of delegated authorities from either the head of the Department or from the General Manager, Housing Services. They had not been before the current Minister but this was not necessary as the head of the Land and Housing Corporation in the state had full power to delegate to officers to make the decision. For priority transfers to be granted the officer had to be completely satisfied that a person could no longer function in the dwelling. Mr Knight explained that when he answered the question on religious questions he was merely responding to the question. He said that people can get transfers on a non-priority basis on grounds such as that they were incompatable with highrise living, or a priority transfer could be given where it was a matter of not merely disliking highrise buildings but where there was evidence of a psychological problem giving a prospect of the applicant defenestrating or where a family has increased in size and the accommodation is too small. He reiterated that there is no delegation to approve a priority transfer on religious grounds.

16. Questioned by the Tribunal Mr Knight agreed that there were particularly bad areas in New South Wales. He said that there was no delegation to allow the transfer on the grounds of incompatability with a particular area. Where people were initially seeking housing they may nominate three areas of choice; they may also nominate the type of dwelling after discussing what the authority had and where they had it. There was no point in seeking a cottage where the Corporation had none. In that case they either had to look in an area where cottages were available or a townhouse or a flat nearer to where they wanted to be. In the case of transfers they got this choice but only if they could not stay where they were any longer. They can indicate the place and type of accommodation they want and then the Housing Commission would tell them what they had and where. However in priority transfers the critical issue was necessarily to move them from where they were. The need for speed compromises the degree of choice. People who get a priority transfer may have to take what is offered to be able to move quickly. Questioned further for the applicant Mr Knight agreed that if someone was moved because they were unsuitable for a high density area they would not just put them back into another high density residence. Mr Knight said that the position east of the Blue Mountains was quite different from the rest of the state rather than there being a demarcation between country and city housing. He did not purport to know the situation in the Capital Territory. Re-examined Mr Knight said that he did not think aversion to alcohol or overt sexual relationships were a ground for a transfer because the authority could not control the environment and could not guarantee that the matter giving rise to the request for transfer at the outset could be averted by a move.

17. Counsel for the applicant then said that she had intended to call a Ms. Deponte. However her office had been telephoned to say that Ms. Deponte's daughter had fallen ill and had to be taken to a doctor. The applicant intended to call Ms. Deponte because in her evidence the applicant had said that she had gone to the Northbourne flats and seen a neighbour, Ms. Deponte was that neighbour. The Tribunal was told by agreement, in the absence of Ms. Deponte, that there was a conversation between the applicant and Ms. Deponte; in that conversation Ms. Deponte had made negative comments about those flats; at the end of the conversation the two applicants looked confused. Ms. Deponte had just been divorced and lived at Flat 3 at Northbourne flats.

18. In submissions the respondent's counsel put it to the Tribunal that section 12 of the Ordinance gave the Commissioner the power to prepare a housing assistance program for the purposes of that Ordinance (i.e. providing housing assistance in the Australian Capital Territory). It was submitted that the Program should be accepted as a valid exercise of the Commissioner's power and binding on the decision-maker. Clause 10 of that duly prepared Program (paragraph 2 supra) states that for the Commissioner to provide priority assistance there must be a special need for early allocation. The criteria need to be objectively considered; for example the applicant had to be ill - not merely feel ill. How a person was affected by their religion could not be objectively determined, religion could not be a ticket to special accommodation. The applicant's special background had to be set against a background of thousands of people in need with a limited supply of housing available. The case of Harris and Secretary, Department of Social Security 7 ALN N356 was cited in support of the view that before the applicants could be said to be in special need of assistance their circumstances would need to be such as to distinguish their case from the ordinary case and that it was appropriate to assess their circumstances by reference to the standard of living of the country in which they had chosen to live. The need for quiet and the absence of alcohol, drug abuse and free sex were needs felt by the vast majority of parents raising children. Clause 19(2) of the program required the Commissioner to have regard to availability of suitable accommodation and went some way to authorising priority for a flat but not a house; in the applicants circumstances offering the choice of two flats was a reasonable way to alleviate their need as balanced against the needs of others. In New South Wales the applicants plainly would not get priority. The evidence was that there were a particular problems in Melba. Having wealthy and religious backgrounds the applicants expectations of housing in this country were unreasonably high.

19. For the applicants it was said that they need only meet one of the criteria in Clause 10 of the Program to be eligible for priority assistance. It had been demonstrated in the evidence of Dr Rich that the applicant had a special need due to ill-health (Clause 10(1)(a)). Further, they had a special need due to the inadequate nature of their current accommodation (clause 10(1)(b)). They had been granted priority and had been offered flats on that basis. Counsel submitted that the applicant had suffered stress, social isolation and fear at Melba; she felt she was unable to fulfill her duties as a mother. It was submitted that the test should be applied subjectively and the applicant's background together with her experience at Melba flat should be taken into account as how Melba had affected the applicant was now part of the problem. Her enquiries at the other flats about the factors causing problems at Melba showed her concern and that she felt she needed a house. The list of cases where priority had been granted had been tendered by the respondent should not be compared with the applicant's situation as they were all cases of initial grants, not transfers. Nor was the New South Wales situation comparable with that in the Australian Capital Territory. This was a remedial Act; where there was any ambiguity it should be interpreted beneficially (Bull v. Attorney-General of New South Wales [1913] HCA 60; 17 CLR 370). The issue was no longer whether priority should be granted or not - it had been granted. The question now was what type of housing the applicants should be given, and this, it was submitted, was not covered by the legislation.

20. As to the argument that the Ordinance is a beneficial Act it is helpful to refer to Neal v. Commissioner for Superannuation 6 AAR 532 at p 540 (see also 13 ALD 360):

"It seems clear that the purpose was to provide some

protection for the superannuation scheme established by

the Act against claims due to the premature invalidity

or death of an eligible employee who, at the time when

he became an eligible employee, was suffering from a

potentially disabling or fatal physical or mental

condition which, in the event, results in his early

retirement or death. We say some protection because the

protection affored to the scheme is not absolute. Thus,

a benefit classification certificate ceases to have any

effect at all after the eligible employee has completed

twenty years' contributory service (s.16(5)) and has a

diminishing effect in the event of death or retirement

on the ground of invalidity where the period of

contributory service is not less than eight years (ss 66

and 81). But the existence of the purpose of providing

limited protection to the scheme militates, in our view,

against the construction of the various subsections of

s16 put forward on behalf of the applicant."

Similarly the Ordinance provides for the Program for the needs of specific applicants to be balanced fairly against those of other applicants. The Ordinance cannot therefore be regarded as remedial in relation to these applicants to the detriment of others in relation to a limited resource of housing.

21. In Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (No.2) [1979] AATA 179; 2 ALD 634 it was held that a Minister may form and promulgate a policy to guide the exercise of his discretion where the policy so formed was consistant with the legislation and its terms and did not purport to limit the range of the statutory discretion; the Tribunal could depart from Ministerial policy but any departures should be made cautiously and sparingly. This principle has been applied in other jurisdictions (Mansted and Secretary, Department of Primary Industry 12 ALD 227) and is applicable in relation to the Program. I accept that the Program should be applied where it does not conflict with the legislation. In doing so it is necessary to have special regard to the provisions of paragraph 1 of the Schedule of the Ordinance requiring that assistance be given "equitably to persons resident" and to the provision of paragraph 2(a) requiring that housing assistance is to be provided to "all sections of the community irrespective of . . . . . race, religion, disability or life situation". On the other hand the comments of Counsel for the applicant on the evidence concerning N.S.W. practice are valid; what happens in that State must be governed by the laws of N.S.W.; plainly the Tribunal must apply the legislation in force in the A.C.T.

22. I accept the evidence of Dr Rich and the applicant that the Melba flats have affected the applicant adversely. I further accept the evidence of Ms. Le Sueur that social conditions at the Melba flats are poor. That is accepted by the respondent who has already granted the applicants priority for another flat elsewhere. Consideration of special circumstances arising from adherance to the Bahai faith are specifically excluded by paragraph 2 of Schedule 1 of the Ordinance. So are any consideration arising from race. Alcohol, drug abuse and free sex are frowned upon by many religions and by many residents in the Territory; concern for the education of children coming into contact with these things is a frequent cause of stress and anxiety among other residents regardless of religion or race. There is no evidence that this stress has caused actual psychiatric illness in the applicant or the child.

23. The case for the applicants is, as I see it, one which relies upon grounds for special treatment which are expressly precluded by Schedule 1 of the Ordinance. That Schedule is of general application to the Housing Assistance Program and I cannot accept that, because priority transfer has been approved to another flat because of the unsuitability of the Melba flat, these principles can then be disregarded in determing the type of priority allocation that should be offered.

24. Quite apart from my finding that the present application is one which, as a matter of law, contravenes Schedule I of the Ordinance Dr Rich said in evidence she thought a flat elsewhere would have sufficed. I accept her evidence in preference to that of the applicant. Thus, even if my conclusion as to the effect of Schedule I of the Ordinance is in error the conclusion would be the same on the facts.

25. I find, in light of the need to balance their needs against the needs of others, another flat is suitable accommodation to fulfill the applicants' needs.

26. The decision under review is affirmed.
