Last Updated: 21 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - whether depreciation of of personal computer an allowable deduction pursuant to s. 54(1) - nexus between employment and use of computer.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 54(1)

Case P66 (1964) 14 TBRD 295

Griffin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1986) 86 ATR 4839

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60

Case L52 (1979 79 ATC 384

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mrs. Rosemary Balmford, Senior Member

Mr. J.E. Stewart, Senior Member

Mr. H.C. Trinick, Member

  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent disallowing an objection by the applicant to the inclusion in the respondent's assessment of his taxable income for each of the years ended 30 June, 1983, 1984 and 1985, of an amount claimed as a deduction in respect of depreciation on a personal computer.
  2. The applicant requested that each of the objections be referred to a Board of Review, and these requests were complied with by the respondent. By virtue of Section 223(1) of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 and sub-section 189(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") that referral of these decisions on the applicant's objections is deemed to constitute the making by the applicant of applications to the Tribunal for review of the decisions. As the issue was the same in respect of each year of income, it was agreed that all three applications be heard together.

3. The applicant claimed the deduction under sub-section 54(1) of the Act which reads:

"Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction."

4. At the hearing, evidence was given by the applicant, who was unrepresented. The applicant and the representative of the respondent produced the following statement of agreed facts, which it is convenient to incorporate in these Reasons:

"1. The applicant was employed by the Australian Taxation Office during the years of income ended 30 June, 1983, 1984 and 1985. He was a Team Leader in the Company Assessing Section.

  1. In June 1982 the applicant purchased a 'Dick Smith Super 80 computer' at a cost of $848. It was purchased in kit-form and assembled by the applicant in his principal residence, where it remained at all material times. It was installed ready for use as of August 1982.
  2. In his returns of income for the years ended 30 June, 1983, 1984 and 1985, the applicant made claims for depreciation at the rate of 30% per annum (diminishing value method) in respect of the computer. The claims were made against income derived from the applicant's employment with the Australian Taxation Office. The claims consisted of $233 (1983), $185 (1984) and $129 (1985).
  3. The claims for depreciation were disallowed by the Commissioner as private expenditure.
    1. The applicant gained an accountancy qualification in 1973, but has undertaken no formal courses or training in computers, either from his employer or privately.
  4. At all material times the computer contained no taxation information or data, nor was it linked to any taxation-related subscription database.
  5. At all material times, the applicant in his position of Team Leader was not required by his employer to directly or indirectly operate a computer facility in the course of his duties."

5. The applicant submitted that it was necessary for him, as an accountant, to keep abreast of modern trends, which meant that he should become familiar with computers. The computer was not suitable for games. He produced to the Tribunal a simple program which he had written for use in the preparation of a balance sheet. Asked by the Tribunal about the connection between the depreciation on the computer and his work as an employee of the Taxation Office he said:

"There is no way that I could argue that it is actually relevant to the particular work I would be doing at the moment. But it could be relevant say if I was to go into the audit or investigation type areas."

He hoped that, at some time after the next twelve months, he would move into the investigations area of the Taxation Office, where he felt that it would be desirable to have some familiarity with computers.

6. In Case P66 (1964) 14 TBRD 295, Mr. Davies (as he then was) as a member of the No. 2 Board of Review, said (at 298) that the phrase "used ... for the purpose of producing assessable income" in sub-section 54(1)

"requires the application of principles analogous to those applied when considering, under s51 of the Act, the purpose for which an outgoing is incurred. The word 'purpose' does not appear in the first limb of s 51(1), but it is commonly used in relation to that section to connote the connexion which must appear between the outgoing and the derivation of the taxpayer's assessable income. In s 54, the word 'purpose' has a similar connotation and the principles established for the determination of the relationship between an outgoing and the derivation of assessable income may, with safety, be applied by analogy when considering whether plant or articles are used by a taxpayer for the purpose of producing his assessble income.

Thus the state of mind of the taxpayer at the time of the use of the plant or articles, and the factors which motivate such use, are no more than relevant to the enquiry whether the use of the plant or articles has the attributes which characterize it as a business activity rather than a private activity and if the former as an activity which is in the course of and relevant and incidental to the gaining or producing of the taxpayer's assessable income. In this context, reference may usefully be made to what was said in relation to s25(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1934 by Dixon J. , as he then was, in Robert G Nall Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 79; (1936-1937) 57 CLR 695, at p711): 'In matters of income tax, purpose is an elusive and indefinite criterion. The purpose of a payment when a deduction is claimed for it becomes an attribute of the transaction rather than a state of mind in some actual person.'"

7. The applicant relied on the decision in Griffin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1986) 86 ATR 4839. In that case, Yeldham J of the Supreme Court of New South Wales applied the decision in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60 to the travel expenses of a professional engineer employed by the State Rail Authority of New South Wales who travelled abroad for the purpose of widening his knowledge and making himself familiar with developments in overseas railway electrical systems. Although the travel was not required or recommended by the employer, and no question of possible promotion was relied upon, His Honour found that the deduction should be allowed.

8. However, in our view, there is not, on the evidence before us, a sufficient nexus between the employment of the applicant with the Taxation Office, which was the source of his assessable income at the relevant time, and the use of the computer, to support the applicant's claim for a deduction. In reaching this conclusion we have not been unmindful of the well-known words of Dixon CJ in Finn (supra) at page 64:

"It will be seen that the question involved in the case is of an important description. For it is indeed important that officers and employees engaged at a salary in the exercise of a skilled profession should not be in a worse position in respect of the costs of better equipping or qualifying themselves in point of knowledge and skill than are those exercising the same profession as a calling remunerated in fees paid by clients or by the members of the public who, under whatever style, enlist their services."

  1. For the reason given, we find that the applicant's computer was not used by him during any of the years in question for the purpose of producing assessable income.
  2. The applicant, rightly in our view, did not seek to rely on the second limb of sub-section 54(1). His argument was that he was using the computer in order to fit himself better for future duties with his employer. Whatever the intended meaning of the provisions (as to which we would refer, by way of example, to the views of the majority in Case L52 (1979) 79 ATC 384 at 394) it cannot be intended to apply to plant or articles actually in use, even though that use is related to the production of income in future years rather than the year of income.

11. The decision under review will be affirmed.
