ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. QT88/640, 912-913; QT91/34/81

AAT No. 8186

Number of pages - 27

Taxation

[1992] AATA 254; (1992) 24 ATR 1025

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Miss S A Forgie(1) (Deputy President), Mr K J Lynch(1) (Senior Member) and Dr W A De Maria(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - income tax - assessment by asset betterment method - decision affirmed.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Sections 6(1), 166, 167, 170(2)(b), 226(2)

Case D47, 72 ATC, 276-277; 300-302

HEARING

BRISBANE

21:8:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal:

1. affirms each of the objection decisions under review; and

2. in respect of the year 1981, imposes a penalty pursuant

to sub-section 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 in

the amount of 100% of the taxation payable for that year.

DECISION

The applicant appeals against assessments made by the Commissioner of Taxation for the years 1976, 1980, 1981, 1983. While statements of findings on the facts and reasons for decision were prepared separately for 1976 (Exhibit 1), 1981 (Exhibit 2) and 1980 and 1983 combined (Exhibit 3) the matters were heard as one. There were two issues to be considered, first, whether the Commissioner has correctly calculated the applicant's taxable income and second, whether she is liable to pay additional taxation.

2. The relevant background facts are that as a result of certain information obtained in 1983 to the effect that the applicant and her husband were receiving property rental income undisclosed in their tax returns, the Commissioner re-opened assessments on the applicant and her husband for a number of previous tax years.

3. These assessments, issued on 14 May, 1985 by virtue of section 170(2)(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, were objected to by the applicant in July 1985. The objections were disallowed and subsequently the applicant appealed to this Tribunal. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by her husband and the Commissioner by Mr C. Newton of Counsel.

4. We will first set out the new assessments from the Commissioner, along with the applicant's original objections, and do so on a year to year basis.

1976

5. For this year, the Commissioner increased the applicant's taxable income from $0 to $2564. Upon investigation, the Commissioner concluded that the applicant had "failed to include in her return of income her assessable income from all sources". As a result the applicant was expected to pay additional tax of $552.55 as a penalty under sub-section 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

6. In an undated letter of objection (attended by 17 statutory declarations), the applicant argued:

(i) that the Commissioner misunderstood the arrangement she or her

husband had with their children with respect to the purchase and

rental of certain houses.

(ii) that the original taxable income as originally declared should

now be reduced on the basis of certain losses that the applicant

claimed were detailed in material accompanying the undated letter of

objection.

(iii) that the re-assessments were issued more than six years from the

date upon which tax became due.

(iv) that certain assets (caravan $5,500; boat $5,500; building

materials $15,500; furnishings and carpets $40,000 were "omitted as

from 30.6.75".

(v) that certain accounts should be eliminated from the asset

betterment statement.

(vi) that their liabilities were understated.

1980> 7. On re-assessment, the Commissioner calculated that the applicant's taxable income for 1980 was $7778 and the penalty was assessed at 100% of the tax payable on that amount. The applicant did not lodge an Income Tax Return for the year ended 30 June, 1980. Upon investigation, the Commissioner came to the following conclusions:

(i) all investment properties belonged to the applicant and her

husband.

(ii) living/domestic expenses calculated were considered reasonable

and appropriate.

(iii) the increase in assets was not as a result of gambling wins.

(iv) the applicant was liable for additional tax for not furnishing a

tax return for the year ended 30 June, 1980.

8. The applicant challenged these findings in the same undated letter of objection that was used with respect to the 1976 re-assessment.

1981

9. On re-assessment, the Commissioner calculated that the applicant's taxable income for 1981 was $7620. We have been unable to find any record of the penalty imposed for this year. The findings behind the calculation were the same as for the year ending 30 June, 1980. Similarly, the applicant's objections were similar to those raised for the previous years.

1983

10. On re-assessment the Commissioner calculated that the applicant's taxable income for 1983 was $6029. The penalty was assessed at 80% of the tax payable for that year. Again the findings and the objection to the assessment were the same as for previous relevant years. For 1983 financial year a partnership return was lodged for the first time, to cover the rental activity.

11. In conclusion the respondent has calculated through the Asset Betterment Test that the applicant's taxable income in the relevant years of 1976, 1980, 1981 and 1983 amounted to a total of $23,991.

Asset Betterment Test

12. By comparing the net worth of the applicant at the end of each relevant year with her net worth at the beginning of each of those years, an estimate of annual asset growth is obtained. Non deductible expenditure (often in the form of estimates) is added to this estimate and liabilities and exemptions are subtracted. A figure is then computed for total taxable income (combined total taxable income in this case). When taxable income previously declared is subtracted we are left with the difference, which is the outstanding taxable income (Case D47, 72 ATC, 276-277; 300-302). This amount is then apportioned between applicant and husband.

13. Asset betterment investigations are done through the authority given by section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Section 167 provides:

"If -

(a) any person makes default in furnishing a return; or

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied with the return furnished by

any person; or

(c) the Commissioner has reason to believe that any person

who has not furnished a return has derived taxable income,

the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his judgment income tax ought to be levied, and the amount shall be the taxable income of that person for the purpose of section 166."

14. Section 166 provides:

"From the returns, and from any other information in his

possession, or from any one or more of these sources, the Commissioner

shall make an assessment of the amount of the taxable income of any

taxpayer, and of the tax payable thereon."

15. As one can see the Asset Betterment method is used when a taxpayer does not honour his or her obligation to furnish accurate taxation returns and/or fails to co-operate with subsequent tax investigations, even to the extent of attempting to undermine such inquiries. The facts before us provide ample evidence that the applicant, through her husband, failed to provide returns, frustrated inquiries and jeopardised the investigative processes of the respondent. The use of the Asset Betterment method was justified in her case. Having said that, we are under no misapprehension that it was the applicant's husband who was the principal architect of the family's finances, and sole strategist of this drawn out and costly campaign of non co-operation.

16. We find that the respondent's calculation of taxable income for the applicant in the 1976, 1980, 1981 and 1983 financial years using the Asset Betterment method were as sound as the circumstances permitted. In the step by step approach used to reach this conclusion the Tribunal has been mindful of the injustice of imposing excessive tax. In the words of McGarvie J.

"There are several ways in which wrong information could produce a

result which showed an excessive amount of taxable income. If in a

given year, the amount shown for net assets betterment or for other

expenditure is too high that would have the effect of increasing

taxable income accordingly. If the amount shown for capital receipts

or concessional deductions is too low or the amount for capital loss

too high that would also have the effect of inflating taxable income.

Other errors in amount used in making adjustments could also operate

so as erroneously to increase the taxable income shown by the assets

betterment statement" (L'Estrange v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation

78 ATC 4766).

17. We are persuaded to accept the Commissioner's figures as accurate because they were not attacked or contradicted by the applicant's offering persuasive countermanding evidence, thus leading to an inference that the figures are correct (L'Estrange v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 78 ATC 4764-4765). In any event the applicant bore the onus of proof (McCormack v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4543) and she has not discharged it. She cannot do so by non-specific attacks on the Asset Betterment calculations or on the investigation process itself. She must establish that if there are errors in the Commissioner's calculations, that these directly led to an excessive assessment (Trautwein v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63; Dalco v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 90 ATC 4093-4; AAT Case 6807 (1991) 22 ATR 3096, 3098, 3110). Nor can she do so by simply referring to correspondence or statutory declarations in which she or her husband have asserted facts to be true. In some cases, this could lead us to find, at least, consistency in the evidence over a long period of time. In this case we cannot do even that on many occasions. That the applicant had plenty of time to contradict the Asset Betterment calculations is beyond dispute. By the hearing date (July 1991) the applicant had access to the Asset Betterment Statement for just over seven years (Exhibit "3", page 191).

ASSETS

Cash on Hand

17. Mr Gary was the respondent's principal investigator in this matter and the person who drew up the Asset Betterment Statement. The applicant's husband had told him that in the relevant years there was nil cash on hand. Mr Gary stated this in his evidence to us on 23 July. Yet in the applicant's husband's evidence to us through exhibit "C" (which he prepared) he tells us that there was cash on hand in all relevant years bar 1983. This contradiction in his evidence to the Commissioner and to us was not resolved at the hearing. Accurate cash on hand figures are very difficult to collect, particularly from a taxpayer who presents shoddy financial records, let alone from one who is obstructive when under investigation and less than truthful before this Tribunal. There was, most probably, always cash on hand for both the applicant and her husband.

18. For most of the relevant years, the applicant's husband was in regular salaried employment with either a caravan company, a land company where he received bonuses and termination pay or a corporate finance and a business development company. Bond money and rental income were being regularly obtained from the properties in streets we will call Quality Street, Wonderland Street, Strike Street, Wish Street and Half Street. In addition there was income from two properties which were sold in the relevant years (Quality Street and Wish Street). There is also an unsubstantiated statement (Exhibit "5" page 61) that the applicant's husband paid her wages in the 1976-1983 period for her role in managing the rental properties. He claimed in his tax forms that he paid $40,300 to his wife in the relevant years for her secretarial services to the rental business. This was remarkable given that she is on a Blind Pension and, according to her husband, speaks, writes and reads very little English. The respondent concluded that these payments were a fiction "a means of making both tax payers non-taxable" (Exhibit "3" T12). We also know that the husband claimed $2080 in 1982 as wages to his wife and $520 in 1983 for secretarial duties performed by her during his political campaign. There is no evidence corroborating the payment to the applicant for such duties. Finally, there is the undisputed evidence of a Blind Pension being paid to the applicant. The applicant's husband told us at the hearing that his wife's Social Security pension was her sole source of income in the relevant years. It appears from the Assets Betterment Statement that this is incorrect in that the applicant did not get the pension until 1980. Further, he claims to have paid the applicant for secretarial services as we have seen. We accept the respondent's cash on hand figures.

Savings Accounts

19. The Asset Betterment Statement deals with two accounts under this sub-heading; Westpac account 1307 and ANZ account 58643, both bearing the initials and surname of the applicant and her husband. The accounts appear to be low level and fairly inactive. We accept the amount in these as they are based on unchallenged bank statements and bank information (transcript page 437).

Savings and Credit Union Accounts

20. The majority of accounts under this heading are in the applicant's name. The evidence from her was that family allowance money was deposited in them and that it was saved on behalf of the children. In the absence of any corroboration, we prefer Mr Newton's submission that these accounts were part of a complex deposit and withdrawal system developed by the applicant and her husband, for their own financial benefit. From this we find accordingly, that the credits in the accounts belonged to the applicant and her husband equally. As such, the money in these accounts is part of the cash assets of the applicant and her husband. We find this view of the accounts persuasive for a number of reasons, including the fact that the husband deposited $24,185.74 in account A with the National Australia Bank for 30 days at 11.25% on 6 July, 1984 (at the start of the tax investigation) in the names of his children X, Y and Z (Exhibit "AW"). While we are content to accept that this is only an example of how the accounts were used, we find that the low balances in these accounts could not possibly indicate the true cash flow in their house selling and rental business. The conclusion is that the applicant and her husband either carried a high level of indebtedness or put their cash in other sources not discovered by the tax investigation.

Freehold Properties

21. In the relevant years (1976, 1980, 1981, 1983) nine freehold properties were part of the assets of the applicant and her husband. Notwithstanding the husband's protests, these assets are taken as cost price (L'Estrange v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 78 ATC 4765). The applicant's husband had not returned income on any of his rental houses. Of these, only three (Sword Street, Strike Street and Arnold Road) are the subject of dispute between the applicant and the Commissioner of Taxation, insofar as the addition of value is concerned. We have not taken into account the property in Day Street because we agree with the submission made by Mr Newton that it had been bought and sold within a year and, therefore, does not find a place on the Asset Betterment Statement. Day Street needs to be taken more into account. On the Asset Betterment Statement no asset values are recorded. The statement explains that Day Street was purchased in July 1979 for $37,800 and sold in August. We have in evidence a contract for the sale of 2 shops and 2 flats at 14 Day Street to the applicant and her husband for $30,000 on 19 June, 1979 from an export company (connected to the Ocean Land Co). There is another contract with the same date, for the purchase from Ocean Land Company of vacant land on the corner of Day Street and Hole Avenue for $12,000. We know both sales went through (Exhibit "AP" show statements of interest due on both loans). In Mr Newton's final submission he said, and we accept, that Day Street was bought and sold "within a year" and that the Assets Betterment Statement is right in not disclosing any asset position (section 26AAA).

Sword Street

22. The Sword Street land was purchased by the applicant and her husband in May 1974, on a ten year loan from the Queensland Lands Department, for $6650. The Asset Betterment Statement records this value until 1980 when a valuation by Mr Rathbone was done. From then on through 1981 and 1983 the value is $65821.

23. The applicant's argument that she and her husband are entitled to deductions for expenditure on the house from 1980 as it was a rental property, is a totally false proposition. While certain plumbing and drainage work was carried out and inspected to the City Council's satisfaction in September, 1980 (Exhibit "AK"), the house was not connected to electricity supply until 8 March, 1984 (Exhibit"13"). A valuation from Mr Hair of May 1983 says "... heavy expenditure needed before council would allow building to be occupied" (Exhibit "AI"). In November 1983 the property was inspected by Mr Gary and Mr Homer of the Taxation Department along with an estate agent from Sunshine Real Estate. Mr Gary reported "The electrical wiring, bathroom and several other details, were incomplete". If the house were indeed occupied, as the applicant's husband contends, prior to this date by an aged relative, that in itself is no evidence of the commercial viability of the house as it may well be that the relative (if indeed she existed) was probably supported by the applicant and her husband as, on their evidence, they later did.

24. Similarly one could accept that there may have been some desperate souls ready to rent the Sword Street house before electricity was connected. However there is no evidence of rental transactions prior to 19 March, 1984.

25. Exhibit "AJ" is a building application to the City Council to construct a 120 square metre brick and timber carport and storage unit for $18,000. A departmental valuer noted a

"detached open brick garage with a ribbed metal skillion roof

which provides accommodation for four vehicles and a lock-up tool shed

is built along the rear boundary (Exhibit "AI" page 2).

26. The valuer also noted that steel supports to the garage were not attached, nor was it painted, nor was there a high standard of workmanship throughout.

27. This evidence is still not sufficient to include it as part of the asset growth at Sword Street because we do not know when the garage was constructed or for what price. It may even be that it was absorbed in the increased value allocated to Sword Street in 1980. We simply have no evidence on which to vary the valuation.

Strike Street

28. The dispute over correct value of Strike Street, indicates the applicant's husband's poor understanding of the Asset Betterment method and the quality of the evidence he often put before the Tribunal. His argument is that a sum of $3500, comprising $3000 deposit and a $500 option fee, was paid to Alliance Acceptance in 1975 and, therefore, should be entered in the 1975 column and subtracted from a subsequent year when the money was repaid to him. Even if there were evidence of this payment and repayment, we accept Mr Gary's evidence (transcript, page 439) that such a give and take would have no effect on the assets betterment.

29. We reject the applicant's husband's view of Exhibit "AN" as being a maintenance contract between him and a tenant. What the tenant contracted to do (i.e. painting of an internal wall, replacement and painting of verandah floor and dismantling verandah rails to enable the husband to re-galvanise them) were jobs more in the nature of capital improvement.

Arnold Road

30. The applicant's husband incorrectly stated on his tax return that the house at Arnold Road was the first home that he bought. In so far as the Tribunal was able to ascertain, Wonderland Street was. The Arnold Road land was purchased by the applicant and her husband in 1966. His evidence was that a house on that land was essentially completed by June 1975, and included from that time, a separate work office (transcript pages 117, 309). We can comfortably reject both propositions.

31. His evidence that "... the third storey was up" (transcript page 310), "... 99% complete ..." (transcript page 311) etc, is contradicted by exhibit "5" page 301 and exhibit "6" page 262). Approval for a third storey was granted by the Shire Council on 25 October, 1976 (transcript page 122). In March 1977 the council inspected third floor walls and frame. Exhibit "5", page 123 is a statement from an ex-councillor of the Shire Council supporting the husband's submission that the third floor work was done prior to Council approval. This evidence is not satisfactory as to the time of performance of the work.

32. The husband also said that a third of Arnold Road was rented (transcript page 305). We do not know to whom, for how long, or for what price. Even if his evidence were satisfactory on this point, the abovementioned Shire Council approval for a third floor was conditional upon his not renting the house (transcript page 317). Consequently, claims by the applicant and her husband to offset interest payments to Suncorp against the entire rental income are rejected.

33. The applicant's husband asserted that money received from overseas was put into the Arnold Road property and evidence of the receipt of this money is in the letter from the National Bank dated 8 November, 1974 (Exhibit "L"). We agree with Mr Newton's submission that it is not relevant to consider whether or not it was used in that way in June 1975, as the husband contends, for the increased value would simply be reflected in the 1975 year which provides the base figure for the calculations. The increase would then be reflected across the other years.

34. We can see no reason not to accept the respondent's assessment of asset value at Arnold Road, which were as follows:

1975 $10985 - $ 200 for land

- $8332 National Australia Bank loan

- $2403 Credit Union

1976 $18335 - $5910 loan to pay out National Australia Bank

1977 $21335 - addition of $3000, outgoings from

Q3000 account

1978 $36335 - $1500 for third storey

From 1978 there was no increase in the asset value of Arnold Road during the relevant period. Exhibit "T" is a building approval to construct a carport at Arnold Road. We could find no evidence that the carport was indeed constructed and, consequently, it should not be included in the Asset Betterment Statement.

Day Street

35. There was no dispute between the parties that this property was bought and sold within a year. The only issue was whether a deposit had been paid for, if that was so, it would have been a loss and be reflected as such on the Asset Betterment Statement. The contract of sale dated 9 June, 1979 for the property shows that a deposit of $1,200 had been paid (Exhibit "AO"). This is contradicted by the evidence from the principal of the Ocean Land Company, who has stated that the amount required for the deposit was forgiven (Exhibit "O", page 7). Faced with contradictory evidence from the applicant's husband and in view of our conclusion regarding his credibility, we prefer the evidence of the Ocean Land Company.

Remaining Properties

36. We accept that the respondent's Asset Betterment Statement in respect of those properties on the basis that there is no evidence to satisfy us that it is incorrect.

Motor Vehicles

37. The respondent's evidence here comes from uncontested tax returns and bank loan statements.

(i) Subaru: The Asset Betterment Statement shows that a Subaru car

was purchased in 1977 for $3000 and remained the property of the

applicant and her husband throughout the relevant period. The husband

declared in a statutory declaration dated 21 June, 1985 that he

purchased the car for $20 in 1974 and sold it for an undisclosed sum

in 1980. The Asset Betterment Statement under "capital loss",

We have no reliable evidence on which we can begin to consider varying

(ii) Holden: Tax returns and bank statements show that a Holden car

(iv) Falcon: In the Asset Betterment Statement a Falcon is listed as

(v) Unknown Make No. 1: This car, according to the respondent's

(vi) Unknown Make No. 2: This car was owned according to the

Miscellaneous Assets

38. The figure under this heading are from bank and stock broker statements. The applicant (Exhibit "C") asks us to believe that the furniture is worth $12,000 for each of the relevant years. While this would not affect the Asset Betterment Statement and as no supportive evidence as to value was furnished we are not prepared to accept the applicant's figure of $12,000. Also in Exhibit "C", the applicant said that she and her husband owned a boat and caravan, each valued at $5,500. The boat was supposed to have been disposed of before 1976 and the caravan before 1979. No satisfactory evidence came before the Tribunal to confirm the existence, price, selling date or selling price of these items.

39. Similarly the exhibit states that $5,500 worth of building materials was on hand until 1979. Again there was no satisfactory evidence for this. Unless the applicant and her husband re-sold the second hand building material it was (on their view of things) either destroyed or put into their houses. If the latter then it would of been seen as an asset improvement. As we have said, however, no satisfactory evidence came forth to prove its existence, value or method of write-off. We can make no finding other than that in the respondent's Asset Betterment Statement.

Additions

Private/Domestic Expenses

51. The difference between the applicant and the respondent on this item is in terms of the quantum and the nature of the calculation.

52. The applicant's husband, in a statutory declaration dated 21 June, 1985 says that their expenses amounted to a total of $9,156 for the relevant year. The applicant in Exhibit "C" stated that this figure was incurred by her husband alone. The respondent's estimates amount to a total of $22,200 for the relevant years. This information was deduced apparently from a number of sources during the investigation. The applicant's husband told us that their living expenses were minimised because they grew vegetables and killed their own meat. Even accepting this, his estimate of $2,914 for 1983 is very low and, without further proof, is difficult to accept. In that year, there would have been at least three people in the household; the applicant and her husband together with their youngest child, who would have been 13 years old. This figure means only $2.60 was spent on average on each person per day for all domestic expenses including food, electricity, gas and school fees. Having said that the respondent's figures are also low. We do not consider that there is sufficient evidence on which we can disregard the respondent's figures and we accept them.

Concessional Expenses

53. The figures under this heading are taken straight from the applicant's husband's tax return. While he accepted the figures (except for 1978), he argued that they should be removed from the Asset Betterment Statement as they do not concern his wife and to have them on would amount to what he called "double-dipping". We do not understand what he meant by that term.

Motor Vehicle (private expenses)

60. In various tax returns, the applicant's husband has claimed motor vehicle expenses because of his export business, political campaigning and employment with the financial and business development company. With respect to the meat export business, on the evidence we conclude that it was operated in such a half-hearted and amateur way so as to be unable to be regarded as having any commercial validity or viability. The applicant's husband claims that he administered this business at the same time he was employed full time by the finance and business development company. With respect to political campaigning, the respondent has followed Federal Commissioner of Taxation and Wilcox 82 ATC 4411 which allowed political expenses incurred from date of pre-selection to the date of the election. We accept that the applicant's husband had to use his vehicle while employed by the finance and business development company.

Telephone, Telex, Advertising, Stationery, Travel and Entertainment, Corporate Registration, Salesman Licence

61. These items can be taken together because they were claimed as deductible expenses by the applicant's husband, incurred through his alleged meat export business and through his unsuccessful attempt to contest a political election.

62. As the applicant's husband failed to discharge his responsibilities to provide credible countervailing evidence on these points, we would treat all claimed telex, telephone, travel and entertainment expenses accounted with the meat export business as personal charges. With respect to the election campaign, advertising and telephone expenses are allowable only in the period between pre-selection (December 1981) and the election (February 1983).

Salesman's Licence and Corporate Fees

63. We would amend the Asset Betterment Statement by including as personal expenditure $205 and $207 for the 1981 and 1982 years respectively, accounted with registration fees for the meat export business. Our previous comments on the status of the company are relevant in this regard. A $30 salesman fee for 1981 is already on the Asset Betterment Statement.

Partnership and Apportionment

69. Section 6(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act defines a "partnership" as meaning an association of persons carrying on business as partners, or in receipt of income jointly. Some characteristics regarding partnerships are:

(i) joint owners of property are understood for tax purposes

to be partners;

(ii) partners, through their economic behaviour, share profits;

(iii) joint bank accounts with operating rights bestowed on both

partners;

(iv) evidence of mutually consented investment of capital in the

business;

(v) evidence of services rendered to clients (tenants) through

the joint but differentiated work of the partners;

(vi) evidence of the drawings on profits by both partners from

the partnership business.

70. The evidence satisfies us that the applicant and her husband were in a partnership business for the sole purpose of gaining income through the purchase, sale and rental of houses. Other sources of income, such as the applicant's husband's salaries and the sale of capital items were secondary, and complementary, to the main commercial enterprise i.e. renting and selling houses.

71. We find that this partnership was started at least as far back as 1966 with the Kim Street purchase. We also find that the partnership has continued from that time uninterrupted. That the partnership faltered around 1984 with a brief separation in the marriage was an implication which the applicant and her husband asked us to draw. We reject it as a ploy to avoid tax, by inventing a reason to transfer Kim Street and Arnold Road to the applicant. Similarly, we reject the proposition that the houses were controlled by a family trust for the benefit of the children. We comment further on the trust shortly.

72. As we find the facts at the date of hearing, the current matter, the partnership owned (despite what Titles Office records would show) nine rental properties. Five of these: 15 Caramello Crescent, Pecker Point, Cnr Caramello Crescent and Denise Street, Rothwell, 55 Caramello Crescent, Pecker Point, 202 Ruler Street, Chiswell, 40 Farmland Avenue, Scarborough do not directly concern us. These properties are mentioned, notwithstanding the fact that they are outside the relevant period, to indicate the strength, continuity and single mindedness (in the commercial sense) of this partnership.

73. We also need to consider the issue of apportionment. We need to address the issue of apportionment because it was a long running source of dispute between the applicant and the Commissioner. On behalf of his wife the husband sought to characterise a number of heads of expenditure as personal to himself.

74. The evidence confidently indicates that when the Commissioner was able to apportion he did so, when he couldn't (because of the state of the evidence) he made a 50-50 split between the applicant and her husband. Anything but a 50-50 apportionment in this case would contradict the finding of a true and equal partnership between the applicant and her husband (see the joint judgement of Latham C.J., Reid, Dixon, McTiernan and Webb JJ. in Ronpibon Tin NL and Tongkah Compound NL v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 8 ATD 431 at 437; [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 59).

Credit

76. The Tribunal has reached the obvious conclusion that the applicant and her husband were untrustworthy witnesses who assumed a cavalier and opportunistic attitude to the truth. The applicant's "selective memory" was made apparent in the course of her evidence and cross examination. She could not remember whether she has ever bet on a horse. She could not recall what she did with the loan from her mother. She could not recall why she borrowed money from one of her sons in 1976, 1981 and 1983 and why she borrowed from her husband in 1983.

77. Nothing exemplifies the untrustworthiness of the applicant and her husband more than the Trust Minute Book issue. On request from the Tribunal, the applicant's husband presented what purported to be the minute book of the Family Trust. He denied that it was created for the purposes of the hearing or the tax investigation. The minute book duly presented and tendered showed entries that allegedly recorded Trust meetings prior to and after 1984. The respondent called the Manager of the firm that has sole importation rights on the minute book the applicant's husband used. He told us that this type of book was not imported into Australia until 1984.

Penalties

78. In view of the various comments which we have made about the manner in which this matter has been conducted over a number of years, we find no grounds to justify our remitting the penalties detailed in paragraphs 5 to 11 above. We appreciate that the applicant and her husband are of Greek background but that does not detract from her responsibilities under the taxation laws of this country. She has chosen to allow her husband to advise and represent her and to rely upon his own advice rather than to seek separate professional advice. That is understandable but she must also accept responsibility for that choice. There is nothing in the evidence which persuades us that she is, in any way, less responsible for the obstructive manner in which the whole matter has been conducted from the start. We note that in paragraph 9 above, we are unable to find any record of the penalty. In line with the lowest penalty imposed in respect of the other years, we consider that the penalty should be 100% of the tax which is properly payable for that year.

79. For the reasons we have given, we:

(1) affirm each of the objection decisions under review; and

(2) in respect of the year 1981, impose a penalty pursuant to

sub-section 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 in the amount

of 100% of the taxation payable for that year.
