DZ and Anor and Commissioner of Taxation [1998] AATA 1026; (1998) 42 ATR 1062; 99 ATC 175 (27 October 1998)

Last Updated: 17 September 2009





Administrative

Appeals

Tribunal

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION NO 13473

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

TAXATION APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) QT97/404



Re DZ

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



QT98/158



Re PB PTY LTD

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal Mr. D.W. Muller, Senior Member

Date 27 October 1998

Place Brisbane



Decision The Tribunal:

1. Sets aside the objection decisions under review.

2. Determines that a loan of $1,500,000 made to DZ by way of a loan to PB Pty Ltd was a genuine loan and not a sham.

3. Remits the matters to the respondent for re-assessment in the light of these decisions.

............(Signed)..................................

D.W. MULLER

SENIOR MEMBER

CATCHWORDS

TAXATION - company loan to shareholder - genuine loan - not a sham

REASONS FOR DECISION

27 October 1998 Senior Member D.W. Muller      



  1. The two applications for review relate to the same matter and were heard together. The applications seek reviews of decisions that certain transactions between Z Industries Pty Ltd, PB Pty Ltd and DZ, designated by the Directors of Z Industries Pty Ltd., PB Pty Ltd and DZ, as loans from Z Industries Pty Ltd to either PB Pty Ltd or DZ, were not genuine loans, that they were shams, that they should be treated as a distribution of dividends and taxed accordingly.
  2. DZ’s father, EZ, is, and was at all material times, a Director of Z Industries Pty Ltd. EZ is now 74 years of age. He left school after passing grade 5 and started working for his father on a farm. From there, he worked in a garage, operated buses, operated a road transport company and, in 1966, formed Z Industries Pty Ltd. In the meantime, EZ married his wife, MZ. They produced six children: four girls and two boys (DZ and SZ).
  3. Z Industries Pty Ltd became a very profitable company. Other Z enterprises came into existence and acquired extensive holdings of flats and other properties. By the early 1990s, the six children of EZ and MZ had become shareholders in Z Industries Pty Ltd to the extent of owning 49 per cent of the shares between them. EZ and MZ owned the balance of 51 per cent of the shares of Z Industries Pty Ltd.
  4. Although EZ and his son DZ are not well-educated men, they are not fools. They have worked hard and they have been very successful in business. Nevertheless, they employed an in-house accountant, a private accountant and a solicitor to attend to their accounting and legal affairs.
  5. The Tribunal heard evidence from EZ, DZ and their private accountant. The Tribunal has no hesitation in accepting all three witnesses as honest and reliable.
  6. The oral and documentary evidence placed before the Tribunal leads the Tribunal to find as follows:
    • (i) In 1992, EZ and MZ decided to give a large portion of their assets to their children. They decided that it would be better for all concerned if the re-arrangement of assets was made before they died. The family would get the benefit of the assets sooner. The parents would enjoy seeing their family enjoying the assets. It would be far less messy than the alternative of an eventual and, perhaps inevitable, dispute over the interpretation of the parents’ wills.
    • (ii) EZ reckoned that he and his wife had assets sufficient to allow them to give to each of their children assets to the value of $1.5M, without giving up their 51% share in Z Industries Pty Ltd.
    • (iii) EZ decided to re-arrange his assets and the assets of his children in such a way that the following factors were to be taken into account:
      • (a) DZ left school at the age of 15 and has worked in his father’s business ever since. He was the only child of the family who was interested in, and had the knowledge to operate the business of Z Industries Pty Ltd.
      • (b) The four daughters worked occasionally and casually for Z Industries Pty Ltd in clerical jobs but were not really interested in working in that industry.
      • (c) SZ, brother of DZ, works for a large Australian company in a city which is a distance of over 1,000 kilometres from the city where Z Industries Pty Ltd operates. SZ was not interested in being part of Z Industries Pty Ltd.
      • (d) Z Industries Pty Ltd had a net worth of just over $6M. The 49 per cent share owned collectively by the six children was worth about $3M. The company had most of its assets tied up in property or loans but had little in the way of cash.
      • (e) EZ and MZ owned other property worth in excess of $6M.
      • (f) DZ owned a modest house and he had shares in Z Industries Pty Ltd representing 8 per cent of the company but he had no other assets of significant value.
    • (iv) To achieve parity between the children, to take account of the factors set out above and to ensure that each child received property, shares or cash to the value of $1.5M each, EZ and his family set out to bring about the following re-arrangement of assets:
      • (a) The four daughters and their brother SZ to transfer their shares in Z Industries Pty Ltd to DZ. Thus giving DZ a 49% interest in Z Industries Pty Ltd worth $3M.
      • (b) The four daughters to receive property and other assets to the value of $1.5M each.
      • (c) DZ to pay $1.5M in cash to his brother SZ.
    • (v) The general plan of asset re-arrangement outlined above was put into the hands of the accountant and solicitor to sort out and bring to fruition. The intended re-arrangement took place in mid-1993.
    • (vi) Transfers of shares and properties were carried out to accommodate the plans for the daughters of EZ and MZ. (None of those matters are relevant to the central issue of this review.)
    • (vii) The matter of the proposed payment of $1.5M by DZ to SZ was more complicated because DZ did not have the money, nor did Z Industries Pty Ltd. An outside loan was considered for a brief moment but the solicitor and accountant came up with an alternative and more satisfactory arrangement. The solicitor and accountant put in train the following transactions to achieve the desired result:
      • (a) Z Industries Pty Ltd made a loan of $1.5M to DZ, effected by Z Industries Pty Ltd sending a cheque for $1.5M to SZ, on the condition that DZ repay the money to Z Industries Pty Ltd.
      • (b) SZ deposited the cheque for $1.5M from Z Industries Pty Ltd into the account of one of his companies, Z Investments Pty Ltd. (Z Investments Pty Ltd had been incorporated many years before these events and had been used by SZ for rental property purposes).
      • (c) Z Investments Pty Ltd loaned $1.4M to Z Industries Pty Ltd at 8 per cent per annum.
      • (d) DZ and his wife acquired the total shareholding between them of a shelf company, PB Pty Ltd, which company became a 49% shareholder in Z Industries Pty Ltd. PB Pty Ltd became the debtor owing $1.5M to Z Industries Pty. Ltd.
    • (viii) On completion of the said re-arrangement DZ and his wife were worth in excess of $3M, less the $1.5M owing to Z Industries Pty Ltd.
    • (ix) As at mid-1993, Z Industries had franking credits in excess of $3M.
    • (x) In a five year period from and including 1992 to and including 1996, Z Industries Pty Ltd paid tax of over $1M.
    • (xi) The in-house documentation shows DZ as being the debtor to Z Industries Pty Ltd instead of PB Pty Ltd, but nothing turns on that fact because DZ and PB Pty Ltd were used interchangeably by the in-house accountant for the purposes of keeping track of the loan.
    • (xii) In mid-1993, DZ and his wife sold their family home. From the proceeds of sale, they paid $90,000 to Z Industries Pty Ltd on 8 July 1993. The money was paid to lower the debt owed to Z Industries Pty Ltd.
    • (xiii) In 1994, EZ and his wife MZ, decided to go overseas for a holiday for two months. Before they left for overseas, EZ prepared a document which he distributed to his six children. He told his children that if he and his wife did not return from overseas, he wanted his remaining assets divided between the children in a certain way. EZ clearly envisaged in 1994 that DZ would have to account to the other five children for the $1.5M owing to Z Industries Pty Ltd.
    • (xiv) EZ and his wife MZ intend to retain their 51 per cent share in Z Industries until the death of both of them. Upon their death the 51 per cent is to be distributed amongst the children in equal shares. There was never any question of DZ being put into a position of being able to forgive his own debt. It was never envisaged that the other five children would allow DZ to keep the full $1.5M when with the passage of time they will be entitled to five sixths of it.
    • (xv) The accounting affairs of the family went into a state of semi-limbo in 1995/96. Their in-house accountant became ill and died. Their external accountant developed a life-threatening illness, had major surgery and luckily, he eventually survived.
    • (xvi) All loans have now been paid out.
  7. As a result of what was really a re-arrangement of the Z family assets, there has now been visited upon DZ, or in the alternative, PB Pty Ltd, a tax plus penalty plus interest bill of $1,137,828.55. This state of affairs has come about because the respondent has determined that the loan transaction involving DZ, or PB Pty Ltd, was not a real loan but a sham. It has been determined by the respondent that the payment by Z Industries Pty Ltd to SZ should be treated as a distribution of dividend to either DZ or PB Pty Ltd, and taxed accordingly.
  8. The respondent’s decision is based on three main contentions. These contentions were also pursued by the respondent’s counsel at the Tribunal hearing:
    • (i) DZ was impecunious as at mid-1993 and had no hope of paying back a loan of $1.5M.
    • (ii) Eventually, DZ will become the sole shareholder of Z Industries Pty Ltd and when he does, he will forgive his own debt.
    • (iii) It was not until the Australian Taxation Office conducted an audit in 1996 that any effort was made to pay back the loan.
  9. The Tribunal is satisfied that the points raised by the respondent have no validity at all. Apart from the fact that the Tribunal accepts the witnesses DZ, EZ and their accountant as truthful there are other factors which tell in favour of the taxpayers.
    • (i) If the loan had been a sham there was no point in DZ and his wife paying $90,000 to Z Industries Pty Ltd on 8 July 1993. They need not have paid a single cent if it had been envisaged that they not pay back the loan.
    • (ii) Z Industries Pty Ltd had franking credits worth about $3M in mid-1993.
    • (iii) DZ and his wife may have had insufficient funds to repay a loan of $1.5M before the transactions took place, but upon completion of the re-arrangements they a 49% interest in a very profitable company which gave them assets of more than $1.5M after the loan was taken into account, plus a potentially high income.
    • (iv) It is unfair to suggest that nothing was done about the loans in 1994/1995, that is, until the tax audit in 1996. The family accountants (both of them) had serious health problems.
    • (v) It flies in the face of common sense to suggest that the other five members of the family would allow their brother, DZ, to keep assets of $1.5M for himself, when they will eventually be entitled to five-sixths of those assets.
  10. The Tribunal finds that there was a genuine loan to DZ through the company PB Pty Ltd. It was not a sham.
  11. The decisions under review are set aside.
  12. The matters are remitted to the respondent for re-assessment in the light of these decisions

Mr. D.W. Muller, Senior Member
