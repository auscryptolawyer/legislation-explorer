ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - assessable income - allowable deduction - manufacturer of tobacco products - expenditure to fund campaign against passing of legislation - proposed legislation to affect advertising and increase fees - whether incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or incurred in carrying on business for purpose of gaining or producing such income - whether capital expenditure - Income Tax Assessment Act s 51(1)

Decision : The Tribunal sets aside the objection decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent with the direction that the applicant is entitled to a deduction of $449,406 for the year ended 30 June 1988 under s 51 (1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No NT90/248

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re

Applicant

And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 August 1991 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)

1. The applicant is a manufacturer of tobacco products. In the year ended 30 June 1988, it paid a sum of $449,406 to an industry association in payment of special levies requested by the association to fund a public relations campaign against the passing of legislation then pending in the parliaments of Victoria and South Australia. That legislation proposed to affect the industry mode of advertising and to increase certain licence fees. The applicant claimed a deduction for the amount pursuant to s 51 (1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, particularly under the second limb. The respondent considered that the preservation of the right to advertise in a manner desired by the applicant was part of a "profit yielding subject" and that money paid to that end was capital expenditure, falling within the exception set out in s 51. This application is brought to review an objection decision which affirmed that assessment.

  1. Evidence was given by a person who was a director, general manager and chief executive of the applicant at the relevant time. I accept that, whatever their relevance, the witness' beliefs and purposes in relation to the expenditure were the beliefs and purposes of the applicant.
  2. The industry association comprised 4 manufacturers, although one of them did not contribute to its funding to the same extent as the other 3. The activities of the association were funded by the payment by members of regular subscriptions and, where necessary, by the payment of special levies imposed by resolution of the directors of the association. Each of the 4 members of the association had a right to appoint a director. The regular subscriptions moneys were provided bi-monthly by the members on receipt of an invoice from the association. On occasions, where unbudgeted activities were considered appropriate but the association had insufficient funds to pay for these activities, the board of the association would resolve that each member would pay an additional levy to provide the necessary funds. Evidence was given that although there was no obligation upon members to pay these levies, there was an understanding that they would be paid, particularly as they were not made unless unanimously approved by the 4 members.
  3. The association is a company limited by guarantee. Its principal objects contained in Clause 3 of its Memorandum of Association are as follows -

(i) to promote understanding of the tobacco industry in Australia by the public, by all levels of government and of public administration and by other authorities, institutions or associations whether public or private

(ii) to represent and to assist the tobacco industry in Australia in the legitimate maintenance, support and furtherance of its interests

(iii) to take part in, carry on, develop and encourage stimulate and aid research into tobacco and its products, into smoking and into any type of environmental factor which may affect human health and to do so by way of direct participation or by providing or contributing to funds to enable such research to be undertaken by others or in such other manner as may seem desirable

(iv) to engage in discussions and negotiations with and to determine or assist in the determination of matters of policy by or for all or any authorities, institutions or associations, whether governmental, public or private on matters relating to or affecting directly or indirectly any of the foregoing matters."

  1. Evidence was given that the association sought to advance the interests of the tobacco industry and its members by protecting their rights (as it was put) to enjoy commercial freedoms, such as advertising and sponsorship and to market, by accepted commercial means, tobacco products. The association was a vehicle which protected the interests of the companies in the commercial environment by allowing the ideas of the members to be promulgated without attributing them specifically to any particular member. The association also aimed to foster public understanding of the tobacco industry by increasing public awareness of the agricultural, manufacturing, marketing and economic and social role of tobacco in Australia.
  2. In order to achieve these objectives, the association customarily advertised material relevant to issues concerning the tobacco industry, provided information for particular groups such as politicians, newspaper editors, journalists and special interest groups, and lobbied politicians for the purpose of making representations concerning legislation affecting the tobacco industry.
  3. In October 1987, there was introduced into the Victorian Parliament a Bill entitled Tobacco Bill. In March 1988, a Bill entitled Tobacco Products Control Bill was introduced to the South Australian Parliament. The applicant perceived that if these Bills became law they would affect its ability in a number of ways to market its tobacco products. Specific sections of the Bills had the following effects -
    • (a) Advertising - a selective ban was proposed to be placed on tobacco advertising covering theatres, sale of films and video tapes, billboards, external displays, leaflets, free samples and competitions which were connected with the sale of tobacco products. Point of sale advertising, newspapers and magazines were excluded from the restrictions (Victorian bill clauses 6, 7, 8; South Australian bill clause 12);
    • (b) Sponsorship - there was to be a prohibition of sponsorship of sporting and cultural events, with a power for the Governor in Council to make exemptions in Victoria and limited exemptions in South Australia for the Australian Formula One Grand Prix and Sheffield Shield and international cricket matches (Victorian bill clauses 9 and 10; South Australian bill clause 12);
    • (c) Licence Fee - there was to be an increase in the State licence fees from 25% to 30% in Victoria and from 25% to 28% in South Australian (Victorian bill clause 46; South Australian bill clause 18);
    • (d) Vending Machines - there were to be restrictions on the location of vending machines, inter alia, on licensed premises (Victorian bill clause 13);
    • (e) Packaging of Cigarettes - there was to be a ban on the sale of cigarettes in packages containing less than 20 cigarettes (Victorian bill clause 14); and
(f) Smokeless Tobacco Products - there was to be a ban on the manufacture and sale of smokeless tobacco products (Victorian bill clause 15).

8. Many of the then current activities of the applicant would have been directly affected by the proposed legislation. A number of marketing methods were adopted by the applicant and were summarised in evidence as follows -

(i) General Methods - these consist of advertising in newspapers, magazines and other publications and vending machines, and advertising on shopping trolleys.

(ii) Outdoor Advertising - this consists of contracted advertising space that gives exposure by being either fixed to buildings, or freestanding (such as billboards) or at event venues.

(iii) Point of Sale Advertising - this consists of advertising signage that is placed within a Tobacco selling outlet or a sign of a limited size that is affixed to the outside of a Tobacco selling outlet.

(iv) Co-operative Advertising - this consists of promotion by means of price "specialing" that is done in co-operation with a retail selling organisation.

(v) Promotion by Casual Promotional Representatives - this consists of "giveaways" by personnel who are engaged to promote products to smokers at retail outlets, sporting functions or other venues;

(vi) Sponsorship - this consists of providing funds or merchandise to support a sporting, cultural or educational activity. The funds might be provided either to the participants in the activity, or to support the costs of organising and running the activity itself.

  1. As a result of the ultimate passage of the Bills, the applicant was obliged to change the emphases in its marketing activities. There was a marked shift to co-operative advertising in Victoria although, as the chief executive said, it was not in the opinion of the applicant as efficient as outdoor advertising or general methods advertising. The new emphasis was necessary to correct lost marketing opportunities.
  2. Evidence was given of the applicant's perception of the role of advertising and sponsorship. It was seen as a marketing tool to assist the applicant to improve its level of sales and to reverse any downward market trends for individual products. Evidence was given that, according to the applicant's perception, advertising did not increase the total market consumption of tobacco products. The Australian market, the chief executive said, is a mature market as distinct from a developing market. That is to say it is a market in which there are not likely to be rapid changes in the level of disposal income and where the continued advertising of a particular class of product ceases to affect further market penetration. The various methods of promotion were not so much intended to increase the overall level of consumption of tobacco products as to achieve an increase in the applicant's market share at the expense of its competitors.
  3. Sponsorship was seen as a means of creating a positive perception of the applicant and its brands within the community, of building brand recognition among the existing smokers, and creating greater brand awareness and brand loyalty among existing smokers.
  4. The applicant's market share was lower than that of its competitors in both South Australia and Victoria. The proposed restrictions would therefore have had a particular effect on the applicant, especially where billboard advertising was concerned. Furthermore, the applicant's share of the market was represented by sales of more expensive brands, whereas its competitors had a greater dominance of the cheaper end of the market. Experience had shown that any increase in taxes which increases the prices of cigarettes affected the more expensive ones more than the cheaper ones.
  5. Accordingly the legislation presented a two-fold threat to the business interests of the applicant. The proposed increase in tobacco licence fees would impact to a greater extent upon premium brands of cigarettes of which the applicant held a prominent market share and secondly, the proposed billboard advertising restrictions would make it more difficult for the applicant to increase its market share through the promotion of cheaper brands of cigarettes.
  6. On 5 November 1987 and 12 April 1988 the association resolved to impose the levies. The amounts involved were designed to finance advertising campaigns which had been discussed and approved prior to the meetings. To fund the Victorian campaign, the association imposed a special levy upon each of the 3 substantial members totalling $768,219-19. Each of the 3 members was required to contribute a one- third share being $256,073-06. Invoices were sent to the applicant and were paid. The amount that the association received by way of special levy was in excess of the amount of the costs which it subsequently incurred in relation to the Victorian campaign in the sum of $250,423-17. The amount which it received in relation to the South Australian campaign exceeded the amount spent by $148,422-34. Both of these sums were retained in the association's "general funding account" or "general revenue account".
  7. There was some debate in the hearing before me as to the role of advertising and its relationship with the consumption of tobacco products. The applicant tendered in evidence a number of articles which, it was alleged, demonstrated that restrictions on advertising did not lead to a decline in consumption but had a substantial effect on market shares. The respondent tendered in evidence a number of articles contesting that proposition. Some of the articles dealt with trends in cigarette consumption in Australia and perceived patterns of tobacco smoking. Others dealt with the activities of groups totally opposed to smoking as such. The purpose of the tender by the respondent was to demonstrate that the tobacco industry, as such, was under attack and that the moneys expended, when properly viewed, should therefore be considered as moneys expended in defence of a total industry. I will come to this submission later in these reasons. Suffice to say that for present purposes, I do not feel called upon to decide whether or not advertising increases total consumption. I accept the applicant's perception as reasonable that in its experience in the industry, it sees advertising more as a tool for gaining market share than for increasing total consumption. I do not regard any of the material in any of the articles that were tendered in evidence by either party as evidence of the truth of the matters asserted in those articles. For present purposes, it is not necessary to do so. Furthermore, the effect of smoking on health, be it beneficial or deleterious, in my view, has nothing to do with the issues to be decided in this application and I will not examine the contentions of both sides in the debate.
  8. The association had mounted a similar advertising campaign in Western Australia in 1983. The applicant believed that the Victorian and South Australian campaigns could be equally successful. Its optimism was not justified by the events and both Bills became law. Financial information tendered in evidence showed, however, that the fortunes of the applicant do not appear to have been affected by the legislation, at least so far as overall profits and sales are concerned. This evidence was given to answer the suggestion by the respondent that the proposed legislation constituted such a serious affront to the industry, and to the applicant in particular, that it threatened the very legality of its continued trade. The respondent submitted that the legislation should be seen as a continuing skirmish in the battle which had included such salvos as the banning of electronic advertising some years previously, the move to prohibit smoking in the workplace, the prohibition of smoking in hospitals and other public areas and the increasingly prominent warning labels on packages. If sales and profitability are to be the criterion, however, the evidence shows that the assault (if the legislation can be figuratively regarded) did not mortally wound the applicant. If it suffered any passing disorder the figures, it was submitted, show that it had certainly recovered.
  9. On the basis of this evidence I was asked by the respondent to bring in certain findings. The first was that the expenditure was voluntary. This was deposed to by the applicant's own chief executive and was clearly the case.
  10. The second finding requested was that the object of the payment, the subject of the objection, was to defeat the passage of the 2 Bills. It is true that the applicant sought to assist their defeat by making the payment. It would not be correct, however, to say that the only object of the payment was to defeat the legislation. The object or purpose of the payment could well be viewed as an attempt to preserve the applicant's right to continue to advertise in the pre 1988 manner.
  11. The respondent then asked for a finding in respect to the objectives which the legislation was likely to achieve. These are set out in the various sections of the Bills. Concerning them, there can be no doubt that the objectives were to inhibit advertising in a manner which the applicant had regarded as particularly advantageous (eg billboards), to limit sales by vending machines, to increase the price of cigarettes by substantially raising the "franchise", to limit the sale of small packets and to inhibit new smokers.
  12. Fourthly, the respondent requested a finding that advertising was an important part of preserving brand names and preserving an expanding market share. It had a particular importance in an ability, therefore, to earn revenue. I am prepared to make this finding but I would also add that the legislation proposed affected the whole of the market and not just the applicant. It would not have impeded the applicant's ability to increase its market share any more than it would have impeded its competitors.
    1. Fifthly, I was asked to find that the Australian market for cigarettes was "mature". As evidence was given to this effect by the applicant's chief executive, I see no reason to reject it. Nevertheless, the financial information tendered on behalf of the applicant shows that annual sales of cigarettes since the passage of the legislation has increased by approximately one billion.
  13. For this reason, I am unable to accede to the respondent's request for the sixth finding, namely, that the only avenue for expansion of sales was into the youth market and it was the prevention of infiltration into this market that the legislation was designed to achieve. There is no evidence to support this requested finding. The hard financial evidence from the applicant (although in general terms as to the age of customers) is clear evidence to the contrary.
  14. Finally, the respondent asked for a finding that the tobacco industry itself was under seige and that there was a consistent pattern of opposition to it throughout the western world. It was submitted that if this opposition went unchecked the pressure against the smoking habit would eventually force legislators to ban smoking altogether. I was asked to infer from the evidence put before me that for this reason, the legislation imperilled the foundation of the applicant's business. In my view, this would be a gross speculation. The business is continuing. The applicant is continuing to trade profitably. There is no basis for the extreme inference which I have been asked to make. As it happens, the preamble to the Victorian legislation contained a sub-paragraph in these terms -

"(iii) Tobacco use is a widely accepted practice amongst adults which is inappropriate to ban completely"

  1. The South Australian legislation recited as its principal object: "to recluse the incidence of smoking and other consumption of tobacco products in the population, especially young people". I am not prepared to infer that the legislation constituted a threat to the very legality and continuing existence of the applicant's business. I am not even prepared to infer that such a result would be accelerated by the passing of the legislation. There is no basis for drawing any inference of probative value as to the likely outcome of similar legislation in the New South Wales Parliament if it were ever to be introduced. The legislation, in my view, was intended to limit and control, but not abolish contact between, the applicant and its present and future customers. It was, on its face, not designed to put the applicant out of business.
  2. On these facts, it is now necessary to categorise the payments for the purposes of s 51.
  3. The respondent's first submission is that the money paid by the applicant was to seek an enduring advantage, namely, the right to advertise in the manner which it desired. It was submitted that this right formed part of an income yielding structure. The classic discussion of the nature of capital payments is to be found in Sun Newspapers Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 -

"The distinction between expenditure and outgoings on revenue account and on capital account corresponds with the distinction between the business entity, structure, or organization set up or established for the earning of profit and the process by which such an organization operates to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the difference between the outlay and returns representing profit or loss. The business structure or entity or organization may assume any of an almost infinite variety of shapes and it may be difficult to comprehend under one description all the forms in which it may be manifested. In a trade or pursuit where little or no plant is required, it may be represented by no more than the intangible elements constituting what is commonly called goodwill, that is, widespread or general reputation, habitual patronage by clients or customers and an organized method of serving their needs. At the other extreme it may consist in a great aggregate of buildings, machinery and plant all assembled and systematized as the material means by which an organized body of men produce and distribute commodities or perform services. But in spite of the entirely different forms, material and immaterial, in which it may be expressed, such sources of income contain or consist in what has been called a "profit-yielding subject', the phrase of Lord Blackburn in United Collieries Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioners (1930) S.C. 215 at 220" (at 359-360).

...

"In the attempt, by no means successful, to find some test or standard by the application of which expenditure or outgoings may be referred to capital account or to revenue account the courts have relied to some extent upon the difference between an outlay which is recurrent, repeated or continual and that which is final or made 'once for all', and to a still greater extent upon a distinction to be discovered in the nature of the asset or advantage obtained by the outlay. If what is commonly understood as a fixed capital asset is acquired the question answers itself. But the distinction goes further. The result or purpose of the expenditure may be to bring into existence or procure some asset or advantage of a lasting character which will enure for the benefit of the organization or system or "profit-earning subject". It will thus be distinguished from the expenditure which should be recouped by circulating capital or by working capital" (at 361).

...

"There are, I think, three mattters to be considered, (a) the character of the advantage sought, and in this its lasting qualities may play a part, (b) the manner in which it is to be used, relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and under the former head recurrence may play its part, and (c) the means adopted to obtain it; that is, by providing a periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payment or by making a final provision or payment so as to secure future use or enjoyment" (at 363)."

  1. Dixon J reiterated his views expressed above in Hallstroms Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at 646 and 647. An income yielding asset (or subject) or a structural asset does not import notions of proprietary rights which belong to the law of property. To emphasise this, Dixon J preferred the term "advantage".
  2. I find it difficult however to accept that a right (or liberty) to advertise in common with others in an already restricted manner, can be regarded as a capital asset, even if this term does encompass incorporeal advantages and an "infinite variety of shapes". It was not a special privilege like the right to practise law (Case V140 88 ATC 875). Certainly it was not a tangible right. It was not exclusive. It could not be said to attach to the goodwill of the applicant's business as distinct from the goodwill of the business of other companies. This distinguishes the facts in the present case from those in Sun Newspapers. Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 85 CLR 423 and John Fairfax & Sons Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30. Here, no advantage was pursued for one particular company to distinguish it from another company. If any advantage can be said to have been sought, it was to preserve the existing rights of all the companies in the industry to continue to advertise and carry on business in the same way that they did prior to the introduction of the legislation. As in Hallstroms (at page 641 per Latham CJ) the expenditure, it seems to me, was made not in order to secure an actual asset to the company but to enable the applicant to continue, as it had in the past, to carry on the same business unfettered by a particular difficulty which had arisen in the course of the year.
  3. No additional right was sought by the applicant. No advantage over competitors was pursued. What was being defended was a right enjoyed in common with others. The respondent submitted that the threatened change in advertising procedures was a permanent ban of some advertising modes. As I see the facts, however, what was proposed was yet another degree of regulation in an already highly regulated industry. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Snowden and Willson Pty Limited [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 Dixon CJ had no difficulty (at page 437) in assuming that similar expenditure could not be said to be "an affair of capital". In Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4542 at 4554, Brennan J came to a similar conclusion where expenditure was "calculated to achieve the same results and also to vindicate the appellant's business methods..." In the present circumstances, the applicant by outlaying the levies sought to remove an impediment of a kind which had recurred regularly in the past, to its desired trading practices. They were not, to use Dixon J's words quoted above, made "once for all", but were "recurrent, repeated or continual".
  4. As an alternative submission the respondent sought to categorise the money as a payment made to preserve the applicant's business.
  5. In "Income Taxation in Australia", Professor Parsons at paragraph 7.69 discussed the expenses of defending as distinct from maintaining a structure. I endorse his views -

"Expenses of defence which are structural will relate to a special challenge to the continuance of a business or the holding of property whence income is derived. It may be appropriate to refer to them as expenses of defence in a situation of peril, though the word "peril" may be too limiting. It is enough that a significant element of a taxpayer's business operations are the subject of some special challenge. Expenses of a builder in resisting an action brought by a customer will generally be working expenses. But expenses of appearance in proceedings brought by a licensing authority to deregister the builder may well be non-working. In Snowden and Willson the High Court expressly negatived a view of the facts that would have seen the appearance before the Royal Commission as a matter of defence in a situation of peril."

31. I do not view the facts that I have found as constituting a situation of peril for the applicant. In BP Australia Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386 at 397 the Privy Council (referring to the words of Dixon J in Hallstrom) affirmed their view that a "practical and business point of view" should be taken in analysing the facts. in McLennan v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 90 ATC 4047 at 4055 Hill J said -

"In the end, as the Privy Council pointed out in BP Australia Limited the application of a rigid test will not necessarily provide the right solution. "It is a common sense appreciation of all the guiding features which must provide the ultimate answer." The process is one of balancing all of the considerations looking at the matter from "a practical and business point of view".

In Commissioner of Taxation v Consolidated Fertilizers Limited (unreported 6 August 1991) the majority (at page 17) recognised that the characterisation of the expenditure as revenue or capital depended upon the weight or emphasis to be placed on particular factors, about which reasonable minds might differ.

32. The material tendered on behalf of the respondent indicates that the tobacco industry had been subject to criticism and increased regulation for a number of years. The industry had fought against the introduction of further regulation by statute in Western Australia with some success. It was unsuccessful in the case of the subject legislation. It may be successful in other circumstances. In any event, the evidence of the applicant makes it clear that it has adapted to the additional restrictions with which it must now live in Victoria and South Australia, and has adapted quite profitably. Both in retrospect and in prospect, the legislation could not now, and could not then have been viewed as putting the business of the applicant in peril. The evidence of the activities of the association, over the years, and of the history of levies shows a certain recurrence in the nature of the expenditure, one of the hallmarks of defence expenditure chargeable to revenue account. Dealing with critics, financing public relations exercises and lobbying Members of Parliament, are all aspects of the business of being a tobacco products manufacturer. Moneys paid for these purposes are, therefore, in my view, working expenses.

33. Not all the moneys paid to the association were expended by it in the 2 campaigns. A question arises therefore whether the applicant's undivided interest in the surplus (if any) can be said to have been expended in gaining assessable income. It was argued by the respondent that this part of the levy could not be said to be a business outgoing. it submitted that the surplus should simply be viewed as a putting aside of some funds for some indeterminate purpose. The contributions, it was submitted, could be distinguished from those considered in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Gordon [1930] HCA 49; (1930) 43 CLR 456 in that the present association was small, that it had no significant existence of its own, and that there was no prospect of it doing other than what the 4 members desired. In short, the respondent submitted that there was no nexus between the payment and the applicant's income earning activities so far as these surpluses were concerned.

  1. I do not see the character of these surpluses in that light. They form part of moneys paid to an incorporated association. I am not entitled to assume that the directors of the association would ignore their fiduciary obligations to the company. The moneys continued to be held by the company to be applied in the furtherance of its objects in the manner prescribed by its Articles of Association. There is no evidence that there was some obligation on the part of the company to refund any surplus to the subscribers of funds.
  2. If the payments to the association are properly deductible, it seems to me to be irrelevant that the association has not yet disbursed all the moneys that it received. The association, in principle, is no different from the Graziers Association of New South Wales, which was considered in Gordon by Dixon J at page 462. That association attempted to promote and protect the general interests of the businesses of graziers and pastoralists and extended its activity into politics. Dixon J considered that a subscription should be viewed as having been paid to secure these advantages to the business by which assessable income was earned and for no other purpose or reason. There is no difference, in my view, between annual subscriptions and special levies, in this context. A similar view was taken in relation to similar contributions by Mr JD Davies (as he then was) in Case B870 ATC 38 at 41.
  3. I have concluded, therefore, that the whole of the moneys paid by the applicant should be viewed as a working expense. The objection decision under review is therefore set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that the applicant is entitled to a deduction of $449,406 for the year ended 30 June 1988 under s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.
