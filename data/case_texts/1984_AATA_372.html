Re Ellen Gertrude Campbell and Commissioner of Superannuation [1984] AATA 372 (31 August 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ELLEN GERTRUDE CAMPBELL

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A83/100

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr. Ewart Smith (Senior Member)

Dr. D.J. Howell (Member)

Mrs. J.H. McClintock (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - retirement on grounds of invalidity - whether applicant failed to furnish information - test to be applied for that purpose - issue of benefit classification certificate - whether condition specified in benefit classification certificate connected with ground for retirement

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16(11)(d), 66(1) and (2)

Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation (Federal Court, ACT G86 of 1983, decision given 31 July 1984) applied

HEARING

CANBERRA

31:8:1984

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the applicant is entitled to superannuation benefits in accordance with sub-section 66(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

1. The applicant has sought review of a decision of the respondent confirming a decision by his delegate on 3 March 1981 -

(a) to issue in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") under s.16(11) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") specifying the condition "History of Depression treated with tricyclic antidepressant"; and

(b) to be of the opinion under paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant was caused or was substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the BCC or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions.

2. Section 16 of the Act provides for the conduct of medical examinations for the purposes of the Superannuation Fund. By s.16(2) a person who proposes to become, or becomes, an eligible employee may be required to undergo such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner for Superannuation requires. Section 16(3) provides that a report of the result of the medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner. Section 16 provides:

"(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or

reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of

the opinion that the person is not likely, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical

or mental condition or conditions referred to in

the report or reports, to continue to be an

eligible employee until the person attains his

maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue

a benefit classification certificate to that

effect, being a certificate in which the relevant

condition or conditions is or are specified."

3. The abovementioned provisions enabling the issue of a BCC cater for the situation of persons becoming contributors to the Superannuation Fund whose physical or mental condition prevents unqualified entry as a contributor to the Fund. Under the system of BCCs specific provision is made in relation to the case of a person failing to make appropriate disclosure of relevant information at the time when he or she is medically examined. This is effected by s.16(11) which provides as follows:

"(11) Where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by

reason of death or retirement on the ground of

invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less

than 20 years and, on the day on which he

ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not

attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) a benefit classification certificate is not in

force in respect of the person immediately

before his death or retirement or, if a

benefit classification certificate is in force

in respect of him at that time, the

Commissioner is not of the opinion that the

death or incapacity which was the ground for

his retirement was caused, or substantially

contributed to, by a physical or mental

condition or conditions of the person

specified in the certificate, or by a physical

or mental condition or conditions connected

with such a condition or such conditions; and

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied -

(i) that, at or in connexion with a medical

examination which the person was

required to undergo under this section,

the person failed to furnish any

information required to be furnished by

him or furnished false information; and

(ii) that, if the person had not failed to

furnish that information or had not

furnished that false information, there

would have been in force in respect of

the person, immediately before his death

or retirement, a benefit classification

certificate in which there would have

been specified the physical or mental

condition or conditions which caused, or

substantially contributed to, the death

or retirement or a physical or mental

condition or conditions connected with

such a condition or such conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the

person a benefit classification certificate in

which there is or are specified the physical or

mental condition or conditions of the person which,

in the opinion of the Commissioner -

(e) in a case where a benefit classification

certificate is in force in respect of the

person at the time he dies or retires - would

have been the physical or mental condition or

conditions of the person specified in the

certificate at that time; or

(f) in any other case - would have been the

physical or mental condition or conditions of

the person specified, at the time the person

dies or retires, in the benefit classification

certificate that would have been in force in

respect of the person,

if the person had not failed to furnish that

information or had not furnished that false

information and, for the purposes of this Act, the

certificate shall be deemed to have been in force

in respect of the person immediately before his

death or retirement."

4. The respondent claims that the provisions of s.16(11), and in turn the provisions of s.66(2) of the Act, are applicable in the circumstances of the present application. The relevant portion of s.66(2) is that which relates to the formation by the Commissioner of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement "was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions". Where such opinion is formed the benefit entitlement of the retiree is limited.

5. The Tribunal received in evidence the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (as Exhibits T1-T5) and heard oral evidence from the applicant and, in order of appearance, Dr D.E.H. Lawrence (a psychiatrist), Dr Phillip Greenhalgh (a general practitioner), Dr B.J.W. Hughson (a psychiatrist with the Capital Territory Health Commission), Dr R.G. Moorhead (a general practitioner), Dr B.C. Bromhead (senior medical officer, Department of Health, Canberra) and Dr Howard Peak (Director of the Cardiology Unit at the Royal Canberra Hospital). It also had before it various medical reports, notes of Dr. Bromhead on his examination of the applicant on 4 August and 3 September 1980 and correspondence from the Canberra Hospital to the applicant.

6. The applicant commenced employment with the Capital Territory Health Commission as a Nursing Sister Grade 1 on 1 February 1979. On 15 March of that year the applicant underwent a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") pursuant to s.16(2) of the Act. A report as a result of the medical examination was furnished to the Commissioner for Superannuation in accordance with s.16(3) of the Act.

7. In providing details of her personal medical history the applicant was required to complete a form (T4, 25-28) which included the following questions:

"Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered

from any of the following disabilities:-

1(a) nervous fatigue or neurasthenia

(b) mental or nervous conditions

(c) anxiety state

(d) depression or difficulty in sleeping."

To each of these questions the applicant answered "No".

8. No answer appeared opposite the question 12(b) "Have you had any complaints, illness or injury not mentioned above?" and she gave the answer "No" to question 17 which reads "Do you have any menopause (change of life) problems?".

9. On the basis of the medical examination and the answers to these questions the senior medical officer (SMO) considered that the applicant met the general medical standard. A benefit classification certificate was accordingly not issued in respect of her under s.16(4) of the Act.

10. Following a considerable amount of time off work because of various illnesses, on 3 September 1980 the applicant was medically examined to determine her fitness for continued employment or retirement on invalidity grounds. It was recommended that she was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on grounds of invalidity. The basis for this was expressed as mitral prolapse 50% and depression 50%. Subsequently, Dr Peak having advised that in retrospect he did not feel that her prolapsed mitral valve was causing her symptoms and that it was of a minor nature, the SMO recommended that the basis of the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement be changed to "depression 50%, menopausal symptoms 50% mitral prolapse 0%". An investigation was undertaken by the Commissioner in relation to the question of when the applicant was first treated for the condition which caused her retirement and a report from Dr Moorhead dated 5 January 1981 was forwarded to the SMO, who commented that she had been depressed as far back as 1975 and that in 1978 Dr Moorhead had prescribed an anti-depressant drug. Therefore, the SMO said, the applicant would have known in 1979 that she had a history of depression. On 3 March 1981 a BCC was issued specifying "History of Depression treated with tricyclic anti-depressant" and her entitlements determined to be in accordance with sub-section 66(2) of the Act.

11. The respondent's case is based on the applicant having either failed to furnish information required to be furnished by her or having furnished false information. A BCC cannot be issued after her retirement (as was in fact done) unless one of those conditions is fulfilled. The case was argued on the basis of a failure to furnish information rather than the furnishing of false information and in particular in relation to her answer to question 1(d) referred to in paragraph 7 above. We therefore proceed to consider the matter on that basis. There is the further requirement, as the Commissioner himself pointed out in paragraph 13(a) of his Reconsideration (Exhibit T2/4), that the Commissioner (and hence this Tribunal) be satisfied that the condition that would have been specified in a BCC but for the failure to furnish the information caused, or substantially contributed to, her retirement or that there is a relevant connection between the condition which caused or substantially contributed to her retirement and the condition that would have been so specified.

The Test to be Applied in Respect of Failure to Furnish

Information

12. The proper meaning to be given to s.16(11)(d) in relation to failure to furnish information required to be furnished has, since this case was heard, been considered by the Full Federal Court in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation (No. ACT G86 of 1983 - decision given 31 July 1984). In that case the Court was dealing with the same form and the same set of questions as in the case now before the Tribunal, in particular, questions 1(a) to (d). The test formerly adopted by this Tribunal in Re Watts and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD 267 and Re "Z" and Commissioner for Superannuation, (decision given 16 September 1983 and the decision the subject of the appeal to the Federal Court) in considering the question of failure to furnish information was rejected by the Federal Court.

13. Counsel for the Commissioner had submitted that the appropriate test was a hybrid one; it involved, the Federal Court said, looking to see of what facts and matters the applicant in question was actually aware, and then deciding what opinions a reasonable man with such awareness would have held, how he would have answered the questions posed and what he would have disclosed.

14. Sweeney J., with whom Blackburn and Neaves JJ. agreed, observed:

"Under such a test an employee, who did in fact

hold a particular opinion which a reasonable man in

his position would have held, would fall short of

what was required of him under the section if an

answer which he gave according to his actual

knowledge differed from the answer which a

reasonable man would have given. If an employee,

being of opinion that he had not suffered from a

mental or nervous condition, answered 'No' to a

question asking whether he had suffered from such a

condition, he would fall short of what was required

of him if a reasonable man in the employee's

position would have been of this opinion and would

have answered 'Yes', even though it would have been

a lie for the employee, given the actual state of

his mind, to answer 'Yes'."

15. His Honour said that "the absence from the present Act of the words 'to the knowledge of the contributor' does not justify the conclusion that the obligation resting upon a person in the position of the applicant should be determined by reference to the knowledge which might fairly have been attributed to him".

He went on to say:

"The language of s.16(11)(d) makes it clear

that the Act does not impose an obligation upon an

employee to furnish information generally but

limits his obligation to information required to be

furnished by him 'at or in connexion with' a

medical examination. Under s.16(2) the

Commissioner may require the employee to undergo

such medical examination or examinations as he

determines. A medical examiner is at liberty to

ask such questions as he wishes.

There must be a requirement made of the

employee to furnish information before any

obligation arises. The extent of that obligation

depends upon the questions asked of him and the

actual state of his knowledge in relation to them.

The obligation upon him is to 'furnish

information'. 'Furnish' means 'to provide or

supply with (something necessary, useful, or

desirable)' and 'information' is something 'of

which one is apprised or told.' (see The Shorter

Oxford English Dictionary).

To fall within the scope of the section an

employee must have failed to provide or supply

something required of him of which he has been

apprised or told."

His Honour concluded:

"In my opinion the language of the section does not

support the hybrid test for which the Commissioner

contends. There is no justification for approaching

the obligation placed upon the employee on the basis of

'the knowledge which might fairly have been attributed

to him' or upon what 'he should have known'."

16. In the present case the basis of the issue of a BCC is failure to disclose a history of depression or difficulty in sleeping and treatment for depression. The relevant question (1(d)) asked whether the applicant was now suffering or had ever suffered from any of the following, namely, depression or difficulty in sleeping, and she had answered "No". This is a question that may mean different things to different persons and to quote from Sweeney J's judgment again:

"An employee complies with the section (s.16) if he

(or she) answers the questions truthfully according

to his (or her) actual knowledge and state of

mind."

The Evidence

17. We turn now to consider the applicant's medical and other history and, in particular, that history before she answered the questions on the form. Dr Moorhead, who, as it later transpired, had (in his capacity as a private medical practitioner) certified the applicant to be fit for nursing in his opinion on her Medical Record Form in November 1978, explained his attitude to her employment as follows:

"I think I would have felt she was fit for nursing

because she was well at the time, and I thought she

was taking her medication and felt that somebody

who was coping well and who wanted to be back in

the workforce should be allowed to work."

Dr Moorhead admitted that the Medical Record Form the applicant presented to him in November 1978 had been answered "No" by the applicant to the question whether she had suffered from mental illness, depression or psychiatric treatment and had stated only Thyroxin to the question whether she was taking tablets. He said the first answer was not correct according to his notes and the latter was not complete. He said he had read the Form quickly.

18. Dr Moorhead said that he had been the applicant's general practitioner. He had provided a report to the respondent dated 5 January 1981 (T4 p.50). In that report he said "in terms of depression, she gave me a history of depression lasting for 3 years when she first attended the surgery on 5 April 1978". He also said he then prescribed Tryptanol, an anti-depressant. From his clinical notes, he said that on 5 April 1978 he had taken a general history and the things he had written down about the depression were simply "depression three years, early morning insomnia, psychomotor retardation". He added a comment in those notes: "Dislike of depending on Tryptanol but needs it". His notes did not make mention of Dr Fetterplace (the applicant's former general practitioner). Subsequent relevant consultations were on 10 April 1978, where there were comments about conflict concerning her previous marriage and that he had prescribed Tryptanol tablets 25 milligrams, three at night time. He assumed he had prescribed these because of depression but nothing was written down as to the reason. The next consultation was 26 April 1978 where he had written down "Tryptanol, three at night time. Less depression, sleeping better". On 29 May 1978 there was a further reference to Tryptanol 25 milligrams and three, but no comment about the depression. On the next consultation, 12 June 1978, he had written "repeat Tryptanol 25 milligrams, three". The next was on 14 July 1978, the only comment being "Tryptanol tablets three at night". He said that was relevant to the depression. His locum saw her on 4 August and 11 August 1978. On the consultation of 25 September 1978 (which was for an upper respiratory condition) the comment "still on Tryptanol" was written. On 3 October 1978 a further reference to "Tryptanol 25 milligram, three at night time" appeared. On 20 October 1978 the entry is "cough, generally tired, no energy, is still on Tryptanol three at night". Dr Moorhead said he had written "Try Concordin, two at night", explaining that this was another anti-depressant. On 10 November 1978 he had written "is on Concordin". On 5 January 1979 he had written "is enjoying nursing again - for weaning off Concordin". On 20 March 1979 he had written "Enjoying nursing. Recurrence of early morning insomnia", followed by "recurrence of depression. Concordin tablets, two at night". He said he had also noted "socially isolated". In cross-examination, Dr Moorhead said he did not know that the applicant took the Tryptanol and he only assumed that she was taking it. That would also be true of the Concordin he had prescribed. He said that "obviously it was my view she was suffering from depression" and he must have thought there was an endogenous component. He said that it was not his practice to prescribe Tryptanol for insomnia.

19. The applicant's explanation of Dr Moorhead's diagnosis and prescription of Tryptanol was given in the following question and answer:

"MR PURNELL: In relation to the Tryptanol, how

many times did you take Tryptanol on

prescription from Dr Moorhead?---Well, as I

recall, on the first consultation visit, I went

to see the doctor for a post-nasal problem, and

he took a case history of my previous years

dating back to the time of the divorce and

things were discussed, and on that occasion, as

I remember, he made a comment. He thought I

might be depressed and it would be a good idea

to have something like Tryptanol, and he

prescribed it on that occasion and gave me a

book to read regarding depression because I was

not really in agreement with what he was saying;

however, I took the book and I took the

prescription of Tryptanol. To my knowledge, I

remember taking the Tryptanol probably about two

nights. At the time I felt that I had some

tension with the selling of my home and the

purchasing of another one, and so, as I remember

that occasion, I did take it, but I was not very

happy about taking Tryptanol. I went back to

the doctor on the next visit and returned the

book and said I was not happy about taking it.

Now, I do not recall continuing on Tryptanol."

As to Concordin, she said:

"My recollection is somewhat faint but I do

remember after I moved house in '78, July '78,

that I had occasion to go to him again, and I

cannot remember what the occasion was, but he

had suggested a new drug because he knew I was

not happy about Tryptanol, and I kept saying I

did not think I needed it and was not happy

about it, and he suggested Concordin, but to my

knowledge I did not take it."

20. The applicant's rather unhappy situation after 1974 arose out of her divorce. This had, indeed, caused her a great deal of stress and unhappiness, as her husband had become involved with a friend of hers. She had, over a long period, a great deal of nursing experience and it was not surprising that she thought to return to that vocation. She was, as she put it, "a reluctant petitioner" in the divorce proceedings. While she was employed at the then Canberra Community Hospital as a ward sister in 1972 and thereafter, she did a part-time adult matriculation course and commenced an arts degree at the Australian National University. The marital problems arose in April 1974 and ended with a decree absolute in 1975. She was, not unnaturally, in a state of shock at what had happened and she had consulted Dr Fetterplace, who had been her family doctor for some eight years. This happened when her husband had planned to re-marry the other woman and she was advised by Dr Fetterplace to take three months off and not to make any irrational decisions while she was in an emotional state. She had broken down when she consulted Dr Fetterplace. Dr Fetterplace gave her a certificate stating "Mrs Campbell is unable to work for three months as she is in a depressed state". He had, the applicant said, advised her to have the three months off and said "I will give you a certificate to cover it". She also said that not at any time did he discuss with her or treat her for depression and she said that he wrote "depressed state" in her opinion to protect her private life. Dr Fetterplace, regrettably, is now deceased and attempts to obtain his clinical notes have not been successful. She had gone to Adelaide and had in fact stayed six months, not three, partially due to serious surgery - a hysterectomy not involving removal of the ovaries. When she left Adelaide, she returned to Canberra because her son (who had gone to live with his father) had come over to Adelaide in September and asked her to come back "as things are not working out ". When she returned to Canberra she found she could see the house in which her husband and her former friend were living in from the kitchen window and that this "was a bit distressing". She stayed in this house for some twelve months and then moved to Kambah and ultimately to Wanniassa.

21. The applicant was asked in cross-examination about the prescription of Tryptanol by Dr Moorhead and when asked to explain how it could be that such a discrepancy could exist in her view and the doctor's view said:

"I am really at a loss to explain that, other than

the fact that after the initial prescription of

Tryptanol and I returned to the doctor and said I

did not feel I needed it, I was not depressed and

perhaps there was some stress situations as a

single parent, but I was coping, and I told him I

was not happy about taking Tryptanol other than

perhaps from the point of view of the mild sedative

properties it contained. I have worked on the

assumption that he knew I was not taking them and

whether he was assuming I was continuing - I am

really, as I say, at a loss to explain."

She did not remember a locum prescribing Tryptanol and she did not remember taking the Tryptanol. She did remember discussing Concordin, but "taking it, no". She could not recall why Dr Moorhead had made a note that there was a recurrence of depression and early morning insomnia, and could not understand why he made a note at one stage that she "should be weaned off Concordin".

22. The applicant was unequivocal in her evidence as to her honest belief in the answers she had given to the questions on the form of Medical Report she had completed for superannuation purposes. Her evidence as to this was given in her answers to the following questions asked of her by her Counsel in respect of that form:

"I turn you now to category B on the same page, the

Applicant's Statement of Personal Medical

History. Did you cause the entries to be

made in that category?---Yes. That is all my

handwriting.

When you filled that in, did you believe that to be

true and correct?---Yes, I did.

I turn now to the next page which is headed

Applicant's Statement of Personal Medical

History Continued. Did you cause the entries

to be made in that category?---Yes, I did.

When you filled that in, did you believe that to be

true and correct?---Yes, I did.

I take you to category C, details of yes answers by

applicant in CMOs comments. Under the

category of applicant to furnish details of

yes answers, did you cause those entries, by

reference to the question and the year it

occurred to be made?---Yes, I did."

23. Of her disagreement with Dr Moorhead's view as to her sleep problems, she said:

"No, I do remember, even on the first visit, he

said, 'You look tired', and questioned me about my

sleep pattern and I said, 'I really do not have any

difficulties.' Occasionally I woke in the

mornings, but I would go back to sleep and if not I

would take a hot glass of milk. I do remember him

always feeling that I looked tired and I explained

it was my single parent situation and coping on my

own with teenage children and he seemed to think it

was a symptom of depression, but I did not agree.

I can understand him thinking that it is a symptom

of depression, but it is something that did not

bother me. He was entitled to his opinion and I

did not agree."

24. In cross-examination, she maintained her stand. Asked concerning her answer "No" to question 1(d), she responded as follows:

"Do you consider that was the correct answer to

say, no, to that question?---Yes, I did, yes.

Even though Dr Moorhead had prescribed Tryptanol

for you?---Yes.

And Concordin?---Yes. That was his opinion. It

was not mine. I did not go to him

complaining of difficulty in sleeping.

Surely you would defer to your treating medical

practitioner in such matters?---Not always.

Do you regard your professional calling sufficient

to come to your own conclusions?---I consider

that some GPs do have skill in diagnosing;

other can make errors on the basis that there

is conflicting symptoms. There would be a

lot of diseases that have similar symptoms.

Therefore, it can be difficult if they do not

know all the facts, and Dr Moorhead had only

seen me once and made a diagnosis on the

history that I supplied. So I really did not

take his diagnosis seriously."

25. The applicant's view was supported by Dr Lawrence. Dr Lawrence was asked as to the answers the applicant had given in the form she had filled in for superannuation purposes. In particular, he said that in his opinion she was not then suffering from depression and that, as to difficulty in sleeping, this did not seem to be a very serious problem in her case because it could sometimes be settled by a glass of milk. He said he did not think that she had a history of depression treated with tricyclic anti-depressants. From 1975 until April 1978 when she was given the label depression, he was of the opinion "that this lady was coping with massive loss, and was in an acute grieving process, which is well recognised and well documented". He was asked what she was suffering from as at 15 March 1979 and he answered:

"I think not a lot. By then in my view she had

come through some pretty traumatic events in her

life and was beginning to surface again and pick up

some threads and actually could tolerate living

back in Canberra. So there seemed to be a

resolution to me of many of the things that she had

been coping with before. She had changed her

house. She had done a lot of positive things to

her life in an attempt to reconstitute what she had

lost, which is a very healthy thing to do."

He also said that, in his opinion, the applicant suffered from "a classical grief reaction and not a depressive illness". He added:

"Now, that is my other contention that nowhere do I

see Mrs Campbell having been specifically treated

in any sense of the term for depression. She was

given some anti-depressants, but as far as I know

she only took them for a few days or nights, or

whatever. That does not constitute a treatment of

depression."

He said he took the applicant's story that she did not proceed with the Tryptanol. He had not seen anywhere that she had been treated by a psychiatrist, although he agreed that it was not necessary that a psychiatrist should have been called upon before the Commissioner has a condition that would satisfy the answers to the questions in the medical form.

26. Dr Lawrence thought it "remarkable that the big improvement came with the introduction of oestrogen". When asked about the oestrogen deficiency, he said: "... You see, as I said, if an oestrogen deficiency limits your life capacity and you cannot function, you can actually get depressed about that loss, the loss of function. So it is not simple."

27. Dr Greenhalgh (the applicant's present general practitioner) had found in April 1982, having ordered a hormonal assay, that she was "markedly deficient in oestrogen hormone". She was commenced on a high dose of oestrogen tablets, one milligram, taking three a day and then reducing gradually to a lower dose, the usual maintenance being one a day. He found there was "quite a dramatic response" in two weeks. He has seen her approximately fifteen times since then and not once has she mentioned lethargy, which was her original complaint to him.

28. Dr Greenhalgh was asked what, in his view, the applicant was suffering from as at September 1980. He replied:

"She was suffering from lethargy at that stage

which a definite diagnosis had not been put on.

It is possible that the oestrogen deficiency that

was diagnosed in April 1982 could have been present

then, but it is impossible to say."

Then followed the exchange with counsel for the applicant, set out below:

"Impossible to say without a hormonal assay?---That

is right.

Is it possible that she could have been suffering

from oestrogen deficiency in March 1979?---Yes.

And again is it impossible to say without a

hormonal assay?---That is correct.

Is it likewise the case for April 1978?---Yes.

And April 1975?---Yes.

I suppose as the general practitioner of Mrs

Campbell you have got to know her pretty well,

with all the times that you have seen

her?---Yes, I think so.

In the time that you have known her does she, in

your view, present a picture of a person with

the medical condition of depression?---No, I

have not been impressed with that at all.

In your view was she suffering from depression as

at 3 September 1980?---I think it would be

unlikely."

The final question put to Dr Greenhalgh by counsel, and his reply, were as follows:

"Doctor, taking into account the medical history of

Mrs Campbell, is it your view that whilst you

cannot be certain, it is more likely than not that

she has been suffering from oestrogen deficiency

for at least since 1975?---It is likely, yes."

In cross-examination, Dr Greenhalgh conceded that "I think I would say it was possible or it was likely".

29. Dr Peak, a cardiologist in charge of the Cardiology Unit at the Royal Canberra Hospital, confirmed that he did not regard the mitral prolapse of any real importance in the applicant's case. He said that the applicant presented with symptoms of tiredness and lethargy and was having difficulty in carrying on with her work. In retrospect, he said the symptoms that she had when she presented to him in 1980 "were probably a combination of stressful circumstances with an underlying oestrogen deficiency or menopausal symptoms". The stressful circumstances he was aware of were a long period of excess work and coping with the new home and shift work and other multiple stressful situations which related principally to the marital breakdown and the surrounding problems. He said that, in his view, as at April 1978 it is probable that she was oestrogen deficient but that was not proven. She certainly would, because of her age, "naturally be a high contender for menopausal symptoms commencing". He was then asked if there were symptoms of depression present in April 1978, whether they would be attributable to menopausal depression as a result of oestrogen deficiency. He answered: "It is likely, yes, aggravated by the other circumstances". By "other circumstances" he explained he meant the environmental circumstances of the marriage and that kind of thing.

30. Dr Peak went on to say that it is likely she would have been oestrogen deficient in March 1979 and in his view, as at that date, with the wisdom of hindsight, she was suffering from menopausal depression as a result of oestrogen deficiency. It was also likely that she was oestrogen deficient during 1980 and 1981. He thought that was the "underlying cause".

31. Dr Peak said that the applicant showed some improvement with treatment for depression with anti-depressant treatment and that with the correction of the hormonal imbalance, her symptoms improved considerably. He said that "menopausal symptoms and depression both co-exist and can be subtle in their onset and may be present for some years before they are recognised, and hormonal menopause is a time when people may be particularly prone to depressive symptoms which are dramatically improved with hormonal replacement".

Finally, counsel for the applicant asked him whether it was his view that what caused the applicant to be unable to continue in her job was menopausal depression due to oestrogen deficiency. He answered:

"Again, with the wisdom of hindsight, yes."

32. Dr Peak maintained, throughout cross-examination, that the underlying problem was oestrogen deficiency and the depression was a manifestation of it rather than a completely separate entity. He said that, as to the cause of the incapacity, "It was not really recognised, at that time, how much she was oestrogen deficient and perhaps, at that time, an assessment of some depression and menopausal symptoms being equally to be blamed might have seemed reasonable". However, he explained "in retrospect, in point of fact it is probably more like 100 per cent hormonal deficiency as the prime cause, the symptoms being depression".

33. Dr Peak said he had prescribed Sinequan in July 1980 for the applicant and that this was an anti-depressant. By February 1982 she had come off Sinequan.

34. Questioned by the Tribunal on the relationship of her symptoms to her difficulties in respect of shift work, Dr Peak answered:

"As things have turned out, it seems the

fundamental cause of virtually all the incapacity

was menopausal symptoms. It was not as clear at

the time, but it has become apparent with her

response to oestrogen treatment and the fact she

did not need anti-depressant any more and felt well

and was able to return to the workforce. It meant

the menopausal symptoms were really the main cause

of the problem."

35. Dr Denis Appel, a gynaecologist, was asked to express an opinion on several questions set out in Exhibit T4/100. He did not specifically answer those questions but expressed his view as being that "it is highly likely she was suffering from a hormonal deficiency at the time" (the time referred to was 1975). But he added that, since no appropriate tests were done in 1975, it would be impossible to say that she was definitely lacking in oestrogen then.

36. Dr Hughson, a psychiatrist adviser to the Capital Territory Health Commission, took a different view to Dr Lawrence and Dr Peak. He felt that an acute grief reaction would not extend beyond a matter of months and a gradually diminishing form could continue for up to one year. A significantly longer period than that seemed to him to take it out of the category of acute grief. He did not believe that there was any established association between oestrogen deficiency and the development of psychiatric forms of depression, stating that his own view was the same as the majority view in the psychiatric literature. When asked in cross-examination that he did not say there was no association between oestrogen deficiency and depression which he put under another label, Dr Hughson answered:

"I do not think I have expertise in the area and so

I do not have an opinion. That is why I made the

qualification before."

37. Dr Hughson said that the question as to the nexus between the treatment of the hormonal deficiency and the symptoms which have been diagnosed as depression was "the impossible question". No certain answer could be given to it. But on the basis of probabilities - and he did not know that he could even estimate the probabilities - he explained there were three possible choices. The first was that oestrogen deficiency is responsible for the applicant's symptomatology over the last few years or some longer period and the application of correct treatment has led to the remission of all those symptoms. The second possibility was that there was a "neurotic" symptomatology and there was waxing and waning of a very marked degree and people recover to a point where they have no symptoms for quite long periods of time. The third possibility was "the notorious problem of a placebo response". He was reluctant to concede that oestrogen deficiency was the answer.

38. Dr Hughson said that the applicant did not have a primary depressive illness, but that she had reactive depression in 1975 and intermittently after that time. Informed that Dr Lawrence had not agreed that the applicant suffered from either primary, neurotic or reactive depression, Dr Hughson said that "if that is what he said, then we are in disagreement at that point".

39. Dr Bromhead said that he was now senior medical officer with the Department of Health. He said that Dr Turner had carried out the original medical examination for the Superannuation Fund on 15 March 1979 and that he had conducted a medical examination of the applicant on 4 August 1980 for consideration of her fitness to continue duty. He said that the applicant had stated she had not coped well since being back at the Hospital, that she was too tired and needed extra rest. He said these were the main causes of her excessive sick leave. He had recommended four weeks sick leave.

40. Dr Bromhead saw the applicant again on 3 September 1980 and on that day recommended her retirement. He said it was based on mitral prolapse 50% and depression 50%, the former being based on Dr Peak's report and the latter partially on his own assessment in respect of depression. Dr Peak's report had said that the applicant was responding to treatment "for depression, hypertension and mitral prolapse". He explained that Dr Peak did not normally consider a prolapsed mitral valve as being of any clinical significance and he had assumed that Dr Peak had, by specifying it, given it a different significance than he usually placed on it. Dr Bromhead, having subsequently received a further report from Dr Peak, amended the incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement as "Depression 50%, menopausal depression 50%, prolapse mitral valve 0%". Dr Bromhead said that he was aware of Dr Appel's report and had given consideration to whether oestrogen deficiency could have been the complete cause of the applicant's retirement, but he had concluded that oestrogen deficiency by itself is not the cause of mental symptoms. It was also his view that depression and oestrogen deficiency could co-exist.

41. Dr Bromhead was asked whether he accepted a condition known as "menopausal depression" as a common description. He said he regarded that condition as a mental disorder and he would regard it as such for the purposes of the guide lines for examination of the Central Nervous System as appearing in the Handbook for Commonwealth Medical Officers.

42. In his clinical notes of 4 August 1980 Dr Bromhead had noted, under the heading "medical conditions", inter alia, the following:

"4. Probably depressed - menopausal. On Sinequan."

At the examination on 3 September 1980, the entry was:

"2. Depression. Social history suggestive that this

reaction may have been going on for some time.

Refers frequently to her situation as a single

parent."

Dr Bromhead conceded that by the use of the expression "probably depressed" he could not make his mind up about whether she was depressed or not and could not remember whether he believed it was menopausal depression or something else that she was probably suffering from. He said that the comments next to "depression" meant that it certainly stemmed from her marital situation. It was related, amongst other things, to the fact that she was unable to be home when the children were home because of shift work.

43. Dr Bromhead said that he was now of the view that not only was "mitral prolapse" not 50% but that it was irrelevant. He agreed with the applicant's counsel that this was an enormous jump and that Dr Peak was of the opinion that there was no depression as of April 1982.

44. Dr Bromhead also conceded that with the information now available to him he thought it was "probable" that the applicant was suffering from an oestrogen deficiency in 1978, and also in 1979 and 1980 and he would "certainly agree" that when the oestrogen assay was done in April or May 1982 she was suffering from an oestrogen deficiency. He did not agree that oestrogen deficiency, however, was the cause of what he had referred to by the term "menopausal depression".

45. Dr Bromhead said of the change of the cause for her retirement being made to 50% menopausal, that he had come to this view not by his own diagnosis "but rather (he) agreed with the medical evidence presented". One of the matters on which the change was made was the report from Dr Appel.

46. The Tribunal asked Dr Bromhead as to the relationship of the guidelines referred to in paragraph 41 above to an applicant who had an emotional disturbance associated with marital discord and subsequent nervous symptoms which are then controlled by the use of oestrogen. Dr Bromhead answered that if there was no interference with the person's normal life patterns, so that they continued at work if they are working and are not prolonged more than two to three months, he would not regard that as indicating that they are likely to suffer from depression. He said it would depend on the degree of nervous symptoms at the menopause as to whether or not he "would connect those two within these guidelines". He agreed that if people had symptoms which are relieved by therapy which is relatively simple that he would probably not regard them as fitting in those guidelines.

Findings and Conclusions

47. We do not see the omission on the applicant's part to answer question 12(b) on the form as requiring any inference adverse to the applicant to be drawn. It appears to have been an oversight on the applicant's part and the examining medical officer ought to have picked that up at the examination. Nor do we find that any such inference can be drawn from the negative answer to question 17 - not even experienced medical practitioners had identified menopausal symptoms as relevant as at March 1979. The real question is whether, in simply answering question 1(d) in the negative, there was a failure to furnish information on the applicant's part within the meaning of s.16(11)(d) of the Act. We do not understand the decision of the Federal Court in Fry's case as meaning that, if a person denies all knowledge of having suffered from a condition, that is an end of the matter. Obviously, there is at least a question of credibility and whether the Tribunal is able to accept her testimony in the light of all the evidence. Did she answer the questions truthfully according to her actual knowledge and state of mind? That is the question that we understand needs to be answered in the light of Fry's case. We have concluded that, on the balance of probabilities, her evidence that she believed her answers given on the form to be true should be accepted. Her answers are supported by Dr Lawrence and by the evidence given, with the benefit of hindsight, as to the effects of oestrogen deficiency. She explained Dr Fetterplace's certificate and did not accept Dr Moorhead's diagnosis of depression - a fact supported by Dr Lawrence, who was certainly more qualified to give an opinion on the matter. Having so found, it follows that there was no basis for the issue of the BCC and the decision under review must be set aside.

48. That is, of course, enough to dispose of the matter. However, we have also concluded that, even if we had found otherwise on that question, the result would not have been different.

49. Having considered all the evidence in relation to the applicant's retirement, and in particular accepting Dr Peak's views as given in evidence, supported by Dr Lawrence and Dr Greenhalgh, we have concluded that the real cause of the applicant's retirement was oestrogen deficiency and its consequences. We think that, on the balance of probabilities and in the light of the evidence given at the hearing, that is the most likely explanation and we do not think that the other diagnoses that have been made of depression independent of the menopause and oestrogen deficiency, while no doubt made in good faith in the light of the knowledge the medical practitioners then had, are to be supported.

50. We do not think that the condition specified in the BCC that was in fact issued could be said to have caused, or to have substantially contributed to, the applicant's retirement or that there was a relevant connection with the condition so specified and the condition which we have found caused her retirement. On the facts as we have found them, the real cause of the applicant's retirement was not discovered until after she had retired and after a BCC had been issued. Her case is an extraordinary one for that reason alone; and of course the BCC cannot be issued after her retirement unless the condition specified in it was related to the cause of retirement in the manner stated at the beginning of this paragraph. Having regard to the applicant's history, we are satisfied that the condition that brought about her retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the BCC. Her retirement is explicable on other grounds that we accept. There remains the question whether the condition specified in the BCC was "connected with" the condition which caused or substantially contributed to her retirement. This is a more difficult question. We accept that the connection is not required to be a causal connection, but it is necessary that it be a real or demonstrable connection and that the two conditions must be linked together on some sustainable medical basis (Re Hegedues and Commissioner for Superannuation (1982) 4 ALN No.82; Re Vaughan and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN No.36 and Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN No.1). In the light of all the evidence, we are not satisfied that the condition specified in the BCC of 3 March 1981 was "connected with" the applicant's oestrogen deficiency for the purposes of the relevant provision. While oestrogen deficiency can result in some symptoms of depression, the earlier history of depression specified in the BCC related to a different conception of her problems. Indeed, had her oestrogen deficiency been recognized and treated earlier, her retirement may never have occurred.

51. One matter in relation to this case that should perhaps not pass without comment is the place that shift work played in the applicant's retirement. Both Dr Peak and Dr Bromhead had apparently taken the view that she was unable or unsuitable to perform shift work and appear to have concluded that there was no option but to retire her on medical grounds. It seems curious that this should have led to her retirement on such grounds. Be that as it may, the applicant told the Tribunal that she had completely recovered now and was willing and able to resume her former duties, including shift work. As the effect of the Tribunal's decision will be to make the applicant a pensioner, it will seemingly be open to the respondent to have her medically examined for the purposes of returning her to employment if she is now found fit, in accordance with ss.74-77 of the Act.

52. The decision under review will be set aside and in lieu thereof a decision substituted that the applicant is entitled to superannuation benefits in accordance with s.66(1) of the Act.
