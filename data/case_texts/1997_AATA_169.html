Teresa Prudzinski and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria [1997] AATA 169 (21 May 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TERESA PRUDZINSKI v. TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF VICTORIA

No. V95/570

AAT

No. 11882

Number of pages - 5

Taxation



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

G.L. McDONALD (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - registration as a tax agent - agent committing crime against State law - agent pleaded guilty - no conviction recorded - whether such matter may be considered in decision to deregister tax agent

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s37, 43(1)

Crimes Act 1914 (Cth) ss6, 7, 8A, 86(1)(a)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss251A, 251BC, 251K

Sentencing Act 1991 (Vic) s8

Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) ss8P, 8T, 8U

Jebb v Repatriation Commission [1988] FCA 105; (1988) 80 ALR 329

Re Greenham and Minister for Capital Territory (1979) 2 ALD 137

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 20 May 1997 (hearing), 21 May 1997 (decision)

21:5:1997

Solicitor for the applicant: Tetley McNamara

Solicitor for the respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

All matters concerning the commission and determination of the offences with which the applicant was charged in the County Court of Victoria in December 1996 may be considered in the hearing of this application.

DECISION

G.L. McDONALD

1. This is an application for review of a decision made by the respondent, the Tax Agents' Board ("the Board") on 4 April 1995 to cancel the registration of Ms Teresa Prudzinski ("the applicant") as a tax agent. The decision was made pursuant to ss251K(2)(b)(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Tax Act"). In preparation for the hearing of this application the parties lodged statements of facts and contentions with the Tribunal, and several directions hearings were held. In the course of these preparations it became apparent to the Tribunal that there was disagreement between the parties as to whether the Tribunal could properly take the determination of two criminal charges against the applicant into account in its determination of this application. Accordingly, the Tribunal requested that the parties provide written submissions on the issue. The Tribunal has also received documents filed for the purposes of s37(1) (the "T" documents) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act"), from which it drew general information.

2. Many of the facts surrounding this application are not in dispute, and may be summarised as follows. The applicant was a registered tax agent. She was first registered in 1982, and has subsequently worked as the principal in a tax agents' business for a long period. Over a period of several years the Board received various complaints, from clients of the applicant, about the conduct of her business. The respondent made several investigations into these complaints. The respondent also made investigations about whether the applicant had properly discharged her statutory responsibilities concerning the level of supervision and control of returns that were prepared on her behalf. The Board eventually sent the applicant a "show cause" letter, by which she was required to show why she should not be deregistered as a tax agent. According to the Board, the applicant did not respond to the letter. No appearance was made on her behalf at the meeting of the Board, on 4 April 1995, despite an invitation being extended to her to attend, at which it considered whether to cancel or continue her registration as a tax agent. The Board cancelled the applicant's registration, and notified her accordingly. The notice stated that the Board had decided to cancel the applicant's registration on the basis that she was "not in a position to look after her tax agents business", and that she was "not a fit and proper person" to be registered as a tax agent (T docs pp.194-7). The applicant is seeking review of that decision. There is, however, one further relevant issue.

3. On 9 December 1996 the applicant pleaded guilty in the County Court of Victoria to two charges of theft. The trial judge adjourned the proceedings for a period of 12 months, and released the applicant on an undertaking that she be of good behaviour during the period of the adjournment. His Honour did so without recording a conviction against the applicant on either charge. Neither the T documents, nor the submissions of the parties precisely explain the basis of the two offences, but it is clear that the charges arose from the applicant's dealing with a cheque that had been mistakenly credited to her business account for a sum vastly larger than intended. The incident occurred in December 1992, but there was a significant delay in the resolution of the charges, due largely to the applicant's ill health. The charges were known to the Board; the minutes of the Board's meeting for the day of the decision under review indicates that it was aware of the charges (TB, p.xi). It is also clear that the Board was aware that the charges had not been finalised. The notice of the Board does not clearly specify the weight, if any, that was given to these charges. But in the opinion of the Tribunal the status of the charges at the time of the decision under review is no longer relevant, because the charges have since been resolved. As a general rule, the Tribunal determines an application according to the facts as they exist at the time it reaches its determination: Jebb v Repatriation Commission [1988] FCA 105; (1988) 80 ALR 329; Re Greenham and Minister for Capital Territory (1979) 2 ALD 137. The Tribunal is not, therefore, limited to an examination of the material that was placed before the decision-maker. It may consider any relevant material or events that occur or arise subsequently to the decision under review.

4. The Tribunal wishes to emphasise that the present decision is concerned only with the issue of whether the resolution of the theft charges may be taken into account in the determination of this application. The Tribunal is not presently concerned to decide how the resolution of the theft charges might be used when undertaking the substantive determination of the application, but only whether these facts may be properly taken into account by the Tribunal. Due to the narrow focus of the present decision, the Tribunal will also not consider other matters raised by the respondent (in various parts of the T documents) in support of the decision, such as the applicant's alleged failure to lodge her own income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1989 to 30 June 1993 inclusive, the lodging of completed income tax returns by the applicant's office using the applicant's tax agent number even though the returns were alleged to have been prepared by someone else, and the various complaints made to the respondent about the applicant's conduct. It should also be noted that the decision of the respondent, and various findings of material fact upon which it was based, was mostly concerned with these other issues.

5. On appeal the Tribunal may affirm, vary or set aside and substitute another decision for, or remit a decision with a direction or recommendation for further consideration to the original decision-maker (s43(1) of the AAT Act). It is, therefore, useful to examine the provisions dealing with the cancellation of registration of tax agents. Part VIIA of the Tax Act makes provision for the registration and regulation of tax agents, which is supervised by the Board. The Board is required to suspend or cancel the registration of a tax agent where the agent commits various offences against the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) ss8P, 8T or 8U, or the Crimes Act 1914 (Cth) ("the Crimes Act") ss6, 7, 8A or 86(1)(a). The former provisions deal with various offences committed in the course of, or connection to, the preparation and submission of income tax returns. The latter provisions concern the general Federal offences of attempting, inciting, conspiring or acting as an accessory to the commission of a Federal offence. The express mention of these offences, in the provisions dealing with the automatic suspension or cancellation of the registration of a tax agent, is clearly intended to require the Board to act in relation to agents who are convicted of offences that are committed in the performance of tasks that directly involve taxation work. It is agreed by the parties that the offences in question do not constitute serious taxation offences. The Tribunal accepts that there is no question that the applicant may be eligible for automatic disqualification on this ground.

6. The written submissions from the parties appear to be somewhat at cross purposes and, therefore, have been of limited assistance to the Tribunal. In essence the submissions made on behalf of the applicant argue that an offence under a State statute, for which a defendant is found guilty but no conviction is recorded, cannot be taken into account in the determination of the cancellation or suspension of the licensing of a tax agent. The submission effectively suggested that the applicant had not incurred a "conviction" of any kind, thus the outcome or the order of the County Court yielded nothing that could be properly considered by the Tribunal. The submission pointed to s8(2) of the Sentencing Act 1991 (Vic) ("the Sentencing Act"), which provides: "Except as otherwise provided by this or any other Act, a finding of guilt without recording a conviction must not be taken to be a conviction for any purpose." That Act further provides that a finding of guilt without the recording of a conviction does not prevent a court from making any other order that it is authorised to make in consequences of the finding by any Act: s8(3). It was argued that the Tribunal's status as an administrative body, rather than one exercising the judicial power of the Commonwealth, renders s8(3) irrelevant to the determination of this application. The applicant further submitted that neither the Sentencing Act, nor the Crimes Act enable the Tribunal to consider a finding of guilt, where a conviction has been not recorded against a defendant, in the determination of this application.

7. The submissions made on behalf of the respondent essentially argued that the terminology applied to the resolution of the charges did not prevent the Board, or the Tribunal, from taking account of the finding of guilt against the applicant, nor the circumstances surrounding the offences. The respondent also submitted that the convictions should be taken into account by the Tribunal in deciding whether the applicant was a fit and proper person pursuant to s251BC(1)(d) of the Tax Act. Further to this point, it was submitted that, whilst the offences were not within the class of offences that would automatically trigger a cancellation or suspension, the nature of the circumstances surrounding the offences left the Tribunal with no choice other than to affirm the decision under review, because the applicant could not possibly be said to be a fit and proper person to be registered as a tax agent.

8. At this point it should be noted that there is no suggestion that the applicant did not commit the offences. Indeed, it is apparent from the transcript of her sentencing in the County Court that there can be no question that the applicant had admitted her guilt of the offences. It should also be noted that, when deciding not to record a conviction on either charge, the learned Judge commenced his sentencing remarks with the following statement: "Being satisfied that you are guilty of the offences to which you have pleaded guilty . . ." (see p1 of the Judge's sentence).

9. The Tribunal is of the opinion that, as a matter of law, it is entitled to have regard to the findings of guilt made against the applicant. The Tribunal does not accept that s8(2) of the Sentencing Act casts as wide a net as the applicant suggests for the following reasons. First, it is not clear that the provision is intended to extend to the determination of disciplinary proceedings in general. Second, it should be noted that the Sentencing Act does not purport to bind the Commonwealth (as far as that may be possible by a State statute). Accordingly, it is doubtful that, even if it was accepted that s8 was intended to place some type of limitation on the consideration of criminal proceedings in other proceedings, such a limitation could extend to the Tribunal. Third, s8 states that ". . . a finding of guilt without the recording of a conviction must not be taken to be a conviction . . .". The section is directed to convictions, and makes no mention of the factual circumstances surrounding a proceeding. If the provision was given a literal interpretation, the Tribunal could take account of the applicant's plea of guilty (with its implicit admission of guilt), and the facts necessarily implied by that admission, but not the subsequent decision of the learned Judge. Alternatively, the applicant's submission could be taken to imply that the Tribunal should disregard the whole of the proceedings. The Tribunal rejects any suggestion that either outcome was intended. In respect of points two and three, the Tribunal draws general support from s251BC(2) of the Tax Act. That provision expressly states that the generality of the sub-section enabling the Board to cancel or suspend the registration of an agent, on the grounds that the person is not of good fame and integrity, is not to be limited by the provisions that require automatic action in respect of serious taxation offences. Section 251BC(2) indicates that, in respect of issues of good fame and integrity, the considerations of the Board may extend to criminal proceedings that are not expressly covered by other provisions.

10. The Tribunal is also of the opinion that the categorisation of the result of a criminal proceeding, in which the defendant has admitted guilt of the charge or charges before the court, and the proceedings have been adjourned without a conviction having been recorded against the defendant, is of limited relevance to an application such as the present one. In an application for review of a decision to cancel or deregister a tax agent, the Tribunal is concerned with the nature of the acts alleged against the applicant, rather than the terminology that may be attached to the means by which any charge was resolved. This view is consistent with the character of the merits review undertaken by the Tribunal, whereby the Tribunal examines the whole of the relevant facts surrounding an application, and determines the application upon consideration of all of those facts. Accordingly, the Tribunal rejects the submission of the respondent, that the serious nature of the two offences, and the conduct of the applicant during and after the commission of the offences, leaves the Tribunal with no option but to affirm the decision under review.

11. Both parties made submissions as to the weight that the Tribunal should ascribe to the facts surrounding the offences. In the opinion of the Tribunal, this issue cannot, and should not, be resolved at this point. The submission of the respondent has clearly not provided the type of detail on the facts surrounding the offences that would normally be provided in a hearing. Nor has the Tribunal heard any evidence of the parties involved in the offence, particularly the applicant. Furthermore, there are many documents in the T documents which suggest that the decision of the Board took various professional complaints against the applicant into account. The Tribunal has not received detailed submissions on these issues, or direct evidence from any member of the Board. The applicant cannot attempt to rebut such evidence until it is clearly presented to the Tribunal. The Tribunal believes that all of these matters should be considered at any hearing.

12. In light of the above reasoning the Tribunal concludes that all matters concerning the commission and determination of the offences with which the applicant was charged in the County Court of Victoria in December 1996 may be considered in the hearing of this application.
