Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Loke Sun Sid Lau [1984] FCA 401; 57 ALR 107 / 84 Atc 4929 / 8 FCR 203 (18 December 1984)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: LOKE SUN SID LAU

No. G92 of 1984

Income Tax

[1984] FCA 401; 57 ALR 107 / 84 ATC 4929 / 8 FCR 203

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fox(1), Jenkinson(2) and Beaumont(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - outgoings incurred by taxpayer in connection with afforestation scheme - whether allowable deductions under s.51 Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ("ITAA") - investment in scheme largely funded by borrowings - whether outgoings actually "incurred" within meaning of s.51 - whether outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or in carrying on a business - deductibility of prepaid expenses - relevance of taxpayer's motives to deductibility - whether outgoings of capital rather than revenue nature - applicability of s.82KL(1) of ITAA - whether value of additional benefit in relation to eligible relevant expenditure and expected tax saving in connection with that eligible relevant expenditure equal to or greater than the amount of the eligible relevant expenditure - applicability of s.260 so as to deny deductions.

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ss.51, 82KH, 82KL, 190, 260

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Limited [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 C.L.R. 492 - con.

Ure v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 34 A.L.R. 237 - con.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ilbery [1981] FCA 188; (1981) 38 A.L.R. 172 - con.

Clowes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1954] HCA 10; (1954) 91 C.L.R. 209 - con.

Cecil Bros. Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 82; (1964) 111 C.L.R. 430 - con.

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Losses and outgoings - "Incurred" - Afforestation scheme - Prepayment of management fees - Loan transactions with special features - Tax avoidance scheme - "Additional benefit" to taxpayer - Onus of proof - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), ss 51(1), 82KL(1), 260. Held: (1) That a taxpayer, who entered into a long-term commercial venture involving the growing of pine trees with a view to the future sales of timber therefrom, was entitled to a deduction under s 51(1) for a lump sum "management fee" paid to the managers of the venture in return for recurring services to be rendered in connection with the venture for a period of twenty-one years.

Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 FLR 183, applied. Ferguson v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 29; (1979) 37 FLR 310 referred to with approval. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ilbery [1981] FCA 188; (1981) 58 FLR 191; Clowes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1954] HCA 10; (1954) 91 CLR 209; Milne v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 2; (1976) 133 CLR 526, distinguished.

(2) That the taxpayer's claim for deductions under s 51(1) could not be denied on the basis that the lease of land, on which the venture was to be carried out, was said to have been granted in contravention of the subdivisional requirements of the local legislation.

(3) That s 260 did not apply to defeat or reduce any deduction to which the taxpayer was otherwise entitled under s 51(1).

Cecil Bros Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 82; (1964) 111 CLR 430, applied.

Per Beaumont J (Jenkinson J agreeing):

(4) That a taxpayer who bound himself, by enforceable obligations, to pay management fees and other expenses "incurred" those outgoings within the meaning of s 51(1), and the fact that payment was later effected by a procedure involving an exchange of cheques was not to the point.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 CLR 492; Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 CLR 616; Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] FCA 240; (1984) 15 ATR 982, applied. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Steeves Agnew & Company (Victoria) Pty Ltd [1951] HCA 26; (1951) 82 CLR 408, referred to with approval.

(5) That where, as here, parties to a transaction are at arms length and it is concluded that the moneys were outlaid by the taxpayer for a real or genuine commercial purpose, any inquiry as to the manner in which those funds were subsequently applied by their recipients is immaterial for the purposes of s 51(1).

Ure v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 50 FLR 219, distinguished.

(6) That, for the purposes of s 82KL(1), when assessing the amount or value of an "additional benefit" which depends on what will happen in the future, an estimate must be made of the chances that a particular thing will happen and these chances must be reflected in the amount determined to be appropriate.

Chaplin v. Hicks (1911) 2 KB 786; Howe v. Teefy [1927] NSWStRp 41; (1927) 27 SR (NSW) 301, applied.

(7) Onus of proof in establishment and quantification of "additional benefits" under s 82KL(1), considered.

HEARING

Brisbane, 1984, October 11-12; December 18. 18:12:1984

APPEAL

Appeal from judgment and orders of the Supreme Court of Queensland (Connolly J).

H G Fryberg QC and C Newton, for the appellant.

G L Davies QC and B J Clarke, for the respondent.

Cur adv vult

Solicitor for the appellant: Australian Government Solicitor.

Solicitors for the respondent: Clarke & Kann.

FPC

ORDER

1. The appeal be dismissed.

2. The appellant pay the respondent's costs of the appeal.

Appeal dismissed

DECISION

Fox, J: This is an appeal from the Supreme Court of Queensland in an income tax matter. For the year ending 30 June 1981 the respondent claimed deductions as follows under s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"):

(a) a sum of $39,200 described as a management fee; and

(b) rental of $640, and legal fees and stamp duty of $180.

The outgoings were all incurred in connection with the one plan, which related to the growing of pine trees for the respondent taxpayer. The Commissioner disallowed all three deductions (which totalled $40,020) because of the complexion he gave to the relevant transactions. The learned judge from whom this appeal comes held that the three sums were deductible, and that, on a calculation made under s.82KL(1) of the Act, the total amount claimed was not to be reduced. The Commissioner now appeals.

The respondent agreed to participate in a scheme promoted by Messrs. Aschman and Ray in which land would be planted with pine trees (or pine tree seeds) and cared for on behalf of the taxpayer. The scheme was attractive because it offered the prospect of tax deductions, as well as the benefit of the sale of the plantation, the trees it contained, or the timber in them. It was of course obvious that some years would elapse (at least seven) before the trees were sufficiently developed to be separately marketable.

The promoters arranged for the purchase of a large parcel of land represented to be suitable for the purpose. Participants (eighty in all) were to have stated acreages of this land set aside for the growing of trees for them, the particular areas to be identified, and become the subject of leases to them. Each participant was to pay a rent for his (or her) land, and was also to pay a management fee for the clearing and tending of trees on the area allocated to him (or her). In the case of the taxpayer, the lease rental was $640 for the first year, and $240 for each of the next two years, with recurring options for three years at $240 per annum with a maximum of twenty-one years. The management agreement was to run for twenty-one years, and the management fee was $39,200 payable on the execution of the agreement. This latter arrangement was intended to put the lessor in funds to pay for the land.

If the facts were simply as I have stated them, there would probably be little doubt that the rental, and the management fee, should be treated as deductible under s.51. The Commissioner relies on a number of factors as producing a contrary result, but the principal one relates to the way in which the taxpayer financed and paid the management fee. It is therefore necessary to provide some more details.

The purchaser of the land was a company known as Paragon Nominees Pty. Ltd. (which I will call "Paragon"), and it paid $146,550 for it in April 1981. The financial arrangements for this purchase are not clear. On 23 April 1981 Paragon and the taxpayer executed a document which was described as a lease, but was defective as a lease, and as an agreement for lease, because the land was not then delineated, or then capable of ascertainment. The area was described as being of "approximately fourteen hectares", and as lot 16 in a schedule, but there was not any effective subdivision, or any delineation of that, or any other, lot in the area.

On 24 April 1981 a management agreement was entered into between the taxpayer and a company known as Mount Sugarloaf Forest (N.Q.) Pty. Ltd. (which I will call "N.Q."). The same people (Messrs. Aschman and Ray) were in control of both Paragon and N.Q.

The agreement recites the lease, but, in effect, accepts that the parcels were not at that time defined. The area, according to the recital, was to consist of "no less than fourteen hectares". The agreement was to run for a period of twenty-one years commencing on 23 June 1981. I set out clause 2:

"2. The Forest Owner will pay to the Manager a management fee calculated at the rate of $3920.00 per hectare to be prepared, planted and maintained as hereinafter provided and payable as follows:-

Area to be Planted no less than ten Hectares at a total cost of $39,200.00 payable on execution hereof."

Although at one stage of the argument I referred to this amount of $39,200 as if it were an undiscounted pre-payment of annual sums, this is not correct. It certainly was largely an advance payment but it was calculated as a lump sum on the basis of ten hectares. I set out several of the relevant clauses of the agreement:

"3. In consideration thereof:

a) The Manager undertakes to carry out all operations necessary in

order to prepare, improve and maintain the Said Lands as to obtain the best possible plantable area for the growing of the species Pinus Elliotti and Pinus Caribbean.

b) The Manager will seed or plant directly at suitable intervals and spacing as to obtain an average density of not less than 1,250 (one thousand two hundred and fifty) trees per hectare over the said plantable area.

c) The Manager shall establish, maintain and manage the Pine Tree Plantation with due expertise, so as to attain an average lineal growth of not less than 2.70 metres over a growing period of any three years up to the age of twelve years, excluding the first 2 years after seeding or planting.

. . .

7. The Forest Owner shall not rent, sublet or otherwise dispose of the
whole or any part of the said lands other than in accordance with Clause 11 hereof.

. . .

11. However, Clause 10 does not imply that the Manager will have the
sole right to cut and harvest the timber. The Forest Owner will always be at liberty to appoint a harvester and or seller of his own choice other than the Manager without affecting the duties and liabilities of the Manager on any balance of the Management still left under this agreement.

. . .

14. Notwithstanding the complete performance by the Manager of its
obligations in accordance with this agreement, the Forest Owner may at any time discontinue this agreement by giving thirty days notice in writing to the Manager as long as the Forest Owner has not assigned or encumbered these presents to another Party. If notice to discontinue this Management Agreement would be issued by the Forest Owner after harvest has commenced, then the Manager will be entitled to payment of all harvesting and transportation costs incurred plus the ten percent of the Nett Proceeds of such harvest up to the date of termination of this Management Agreement. This entitlement shall be payable by the Forest Owner to the Manager. The Forest Owner shall not be entitled to any refund of Management Fees.

15. In the event that the Forest Owner appoints the Manager to cut, harvest and sell the whole of the timber under this Management Agreement, the Manager irrevocably guarantees that the aggregate nett proceeds from such sale(s) will be no less than four dollars per fifteen year old tree harvested."

During the financial year the taxpayer paid by cheques out of his own moneys a total amount of $3,900 to N.Q. as deposit for, or on account of, management fees. He also paid $640 as rental to Paragon, and $180 to N.Q., apparently for legal fees in relation to the preparation of documents, and for stamp duty.

The taxpayer did not provide the balance due to N.Q. ($39,200 - $3,900 = $35,300) out of his own moneys. Instead he obtained finance from a company known as Liberton Finance Pty. Ltd., which was associated with the other two. The details are recorded in a document called "Memorandum of Indebtedness", dated 23 June 1981, which was in fact a deed. The term of the loan was twenty-one years, the whole of the loan to be repaid at the expiration of that time. Interest was payable in the meantime at the rate of 2.4 per cent per annum, calculated from 1 July. The terms of the loan agreement reflect clearly its place in the overall scheme. Clauses 4 to 9 are as follows:

"4. If the Borrower defaults or fails to comply with any or all of the conditions detailed under clauses, 1, 2 & 3, and continues to be in default or non-compliance for a period of thirty days, then The Lender may at his option cause the seizure and transfer forthwith in its own name or its nominee of:-

a) The Lease Agreement dated the twenty third day of April 1981 between the Borrower and PARAGON NOMINEES PTY. LTD. (copy attached)

b) The Management Agreement dated the twenty fourth day of April 1981 between the Borrower and MOUNT SUGARLOAF FOREST (N.Q.) PTY. LTD. covering a Pine Tree Plantation located on the land leased referred to in the lease agreement under 4(a) hereof.

c) If the Lender transfers the documents mentioned under (a) and (b) of this clause under the terms of the clause 4 then all agreements, rights and liabilities in respect of this Memorandum and Leases and Agreements specified therein have for the borrower come to an end, with the exception of an amount of ($500.00) for transfer, stamp duties and costs, payable by and recoverable from the Borrower.

5. The Borrower undertakes to pay promptly and punctually the lease falling due under the Lease Agreement referred to in Clause 4(a) and to exercise the optional renewals of the said lease for at least the period that the loan is in force. Default or non-compliance with this clause may cause seizure and transfer as detailed in clause 4.

6. If the afforestation manager referred to in the management agreement mentioned in clause 4(b) shall cease to perform its obligations under the said agreement in a material particular and shall continue not to perform its obligations after having been requested in writing by the borrower to do so then (and only then) The Lender hereunder, being satisfied as to the foregoing, shall accept, if so requested by the Borrower, the transfer of the Borrower's rights, title, and interest under the Lease and Management Agreement referred to in clause 4(a) and clause 4(b) to the Lender or its nominee and on the execution of such transfer the Borrower shall be completely, absolutely and fully discharged of any and all liabilities under this Memorandum of Indebtedness.

7. The Borrower hereunder expressely acknowledges for the purpose of clause 6 above that certain acts of afforestation management can only be or can most efficiently be carried out during certain seasons of the year and that the afforestation manager shall not for the purpose of clause 6 above be regarded as not having performed its obligations until the expiration of one year from the date of issue of the request by the Borrower to the Manager to perform its obligations referred to in clause 6 hereof.

8. Notwithstanding anything set out in clauses 5, 6 and 7 above if the afforestation manager is ordered by the Court to be wound up or if the Members of the afforestation manager shall resolve to wind up the Company and prior to the commencement of the winding up of the afforestation manager the manager has failed to assign the management agreement and the duties thereunder to a third party capable of continuing the management agreement then the afforestation manager shall for the purposes of these presents be regarded as having not performed its obligations under the afforestation management agreement and The Borrower may at his option request the Lender to accept the transfer or assignment of the Lease and Management Agreement detailed in clause 4(a) and clause 4(b) in full and final satisfaction of the debt hereunder and the Lender shall be bound to accept such transfer forthwith.

9. Herewith The Borrower irrevocably appoints The Lender as its Special Agent for the purposes of receiving and disbursing proceeds from the sale of the timber and will be accountable to The Borrower for all monies received and expended."

The taxpayer had no relationship with the companies I have mentioned; he and they were at arms length. The amount of $35,300 (as part of a larger sum) was paid by Liberton to N.Q.. Liberton did not, however, have funds to meet that cheque. It was put in funds by N.Q. which gave it a cheque for the same total amount. This advance by N.Q. was at interest. While any submission that any of the transactions was a sham has been expressly disclaimed, it is submitted by counsel for the Commissioner that there was not a loan to the taxpayer, and not a payment by him to N.Q.. There were simply cross-cheques passing between the companies. It is submitted, as part of the argument, that Liberton's cheque was not honoured on presentation. I think this latter argument is refuted by the bank statements themselves. The transaction with the taxpayer was only one of many similar transactions, the total amount paid to and by Liberton being $2,656,120. The total amount received in cash by N.Q. from participants was $362,560. The cheque from N.Q. (for $2,656,120) was in fact cleared through Liberton's bank, and the corresponding debit was made when Liberton's cheque was cleared. As the learned judge has observed, N.Q.'s bank presumably gave N.Q. credit for "virtually an instant of time". The net result of the dealings so far as concerned the taxpayer was to make meaningful the loan to him, as well as to provide a record. The latter had performed his obligation to pay N.Q. the balance of the management fee, albeit he had incurred a liability to Liberton for the same amount. Liberton had also incurred a liability to N.Q..

The early lack of definition of the land is not in my view of any consequence. It was intended that the subject area be defined, and this was in fact done, although not in the same taxation year. It can hardly be said that the rent was anything else but a payment for the use of land to be used for the growing of pine trees for the taxpayer. Nor can the management payments be regarded as anything but what they purported to be. The initial lack of certainty about the bounds of the land, or, for that matter, its precise area, doubtless would have created legal problems if dispute had arisen between the taxpayer and the companies, but their common purpose was clear enough, and certainty could be, and was given to the arrangements.

A further matter raised in this connection on behalf of the Commissioner is that a subdivision of the large area of land purchased by Paragon to permit fourteen acres to be leased to the taxpayer was forbidden by law. The correctness of this view and of the consequences have not been fully examined in argument before us. There are many situations arising in the application of the Act where it is necessary that the legal effect of a document or dealing be determined precisely. In the application of s.51, as with the application of s.25, what is to be looked at is the nature of an outgoing, on the one hand, or the nature of a receipt, on the other. The mere fact that there was an illegality is often not critical, and would not be critical in the present case. It is necessary to see what was done, and to what end, and to relate that to the concepts being dealt with. If, as is submitted on behalf of the Commissioner, the "lease" part of the transaction rested in contract, this would not affect the fact that the moneys were paid for management services, and would not convert what was deductible into something not deductible.

It is submitted that the payments were of a capital nature, and therefore not deductible. The management fees included the cost of purchasing and planting seed, or young pine trees, but it has not been sought to make any distinction or apportionment along those lines. The amount of rent of $640 was doubtless applied, in part at least, in the purchase of the land by Paragon, but again this aspect has not been explored. One is left, as it seems to me, with a situation in which an amount has been paid, albeit by a lump sum, for services to be rendered in connection with property which is, as between the parties, treated as that of the taxpayer. The services are to be rendered over a period of up to twenty-one years, and will vary as occasion requires. It is true that the manager is given some special rights in relation to the growing timber, and in relation to its sale, and there are other special provisions, but there can be no doubt as to the general nature of the agreement. I see no basis for treating the payments as being of a capital nature.

It is submitted that his Honour erred in the application of s.82KL(1). That sub-section is as follows:

"82KL. (1) Where the sum of the amount or value of the additional benefit in relation to an amount of eligible relevant expenditure incurred by a taxpayer and the expected tax saving in relation to that amount of eligible relevant expenditure is equal to or greater than the amount of the eligible relevant expenditure, notwithstanding any other provision of this Act but subject to this section, a tax benefit is not and shall be deemed never to have been, allowable in respect of any part of that amount of eligible relevant expenditure."

It was not contended before his Honour, and has not been contended before us, that the sums disallowed ($39,200 + $640 + $180 = $40,000) were not "relevant expenditure". It should be noted, nevertheless, that the definition of "relevant expenditure" in s.82KH(1) (which is in the same subdivision as s.82KL) involves 19 alternative sub-heads (in paras. (a) to (u)). It is not clear which one (or more) was treated as applicable here, and there has been no argument on the matter.

The terms "tax avoidance agreement" and "relevant expenditure" are defined in s.82KH(1). Paragraph (p) of this latter definition refers to a loss or outgoing incurred by the taxpayer in respect of the growing care or supervision of trees on behalf of the taxpayer, to the extent allowable under s.51. The matter of "eligible relevant expenditure" is dealt with in s.82KH(1F):

"82KH . . .

(1F) For the purposes of this Subdivision, an amount of relevant

expenditure incurred by a taxpayer shall be taken to be an amount of eligible relevant expenditure if -

(a) that amount of relevant expenditure was incurred after 24 September 1978 by reason of, as a result of or as part of a tax avoidance agreement entered into after that date;

(b) by reason of, as a result of or as part of the tax avoidance agreement the taxpayer has obtained, in relation to that relevant expenditure being incurred, a benefit or benefits in addition to -

(i) the benefit in respect of which the relevant expenditure was incurred; and

(ii) any benefit that resulted directly or indirectly from the benefit in respect of which the relevant expenditure was incurred and is a benefit that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, might reasonably be expected to have resulted if the benefit in respect of which the relevant expenditure was incurred had been obtained otherwise than by reason of, as a result of or as part of a tax avoidance agreement; and

(c) in a case where the relevant expenditure is relevant expenditure to which paragraph (s) of the definition of 'relevant expenditure' in sub-section (1) applies - that amount of relevant expenditure was incurred by reason of, as a result of or as part of a tax avoidance agreement entered into before 28 May 1981."

His Honour was of the view that at least part of the purpose of the agreements, and particularly the memorandum of indebtedness and the management agreement, was the avoidance of tax. There was, therefore, a "tax avoidance agreement".

A definition of "additional benefit" in relation to an amount of eligible relevant expenditure is supplied in s.82KH(1), and a reference to s.82KH(1F)(b) (above) indicates what the benefit is to be additional to. An additional benefit is one obtained by reason of or as a result of or as part of the tax avoidance agreement, which is additional to the benefit in respect of which the "relevant expenditure" was incurred, and other benefits resulting directly or indirectly from that benefit if the Commissioner is of the view that they don't owe anything to the tax avoidance agreement. Put shortly, the inquiry is one as to what benefits, if any, were additional to those for which the relevant expenditure was incurred. The meaning of "benefit in respect of which relevant expenditure was incurred" is delineated by s.82KH(1G). There are 18 different situations dealt with in this sub-section, but again there was no discussion as to which one or more applied. The amount involved in the present case (leaving aside rent and stamp duty) was $39,200. Paragraph (p) of the sub-section is as follows:

"(p) in a case where the relevant expenditure was incurred by the taxpayer in respect of the growing, care or supervision of trees on behalf of the taxpayer - the growing, care or supervision of the trees on behalf of the taxpayer".

Paragraph (d) deals with interest on money borrowed:

"(d) in a case where the relevant expenditure was incurred by the taxpayer in respect of interest - the availability to the taxpayer of the money borrowed by the taxpayer".

An early question is whether, when looking at the agreement under which the amount was paid, the examination of "benefits" should go beyond the particular agreement to associated agreements, or, put another way, they should all be regarded as one agreement. In this case the amount is claimed under the management agreement and not under the loan agreement. In years after that in question interest on the loan may be claimed as a deduction, but the low rate benefits the revenue. However, regard must be had to the definition (in s.82KH(i)) of "agreement", which includes "arrangement", and to the fact that s.82KH(IF)(b), which is a key section, refers to "tax avoidance agreement", which itself suggests that attention can be paid to a group of associated arrangements or agreements.

The Commissioner claims that there were three additional benefits. The first was the prospect of Liberton not enforcing payment of the principal debt, or the whole thereof, on the due date (23 June 2002). This was said to arise under clause 2 of the Memorandum of Indebtedness:

"2. The term of the loan shall be for a period of 21 years from the date hereof and shall be discharged by the borrower in full on the due date being the twenty third day of June 2002 providing always that interests, costs and other dues have been paid promptly and punctually during the term of the loan and providing further that the Lender shall accept the proceeds from every and all harvest(s) of the timber described in the management agreement attached hereto in full satisfaction of the principal loan and any accumulated interest with the proviso that any returns exceeding the amount of principal and interest and commission and fees and costs are to be paid to the borrower."

Although there was evidence as to how well the trees might do, this benefit seems to me wholly conjectural. There is no evidence that it was an intended, or a desired result. There was simply something in the nature of an underwriting by the creditor, but nothing was paid for it, and no value can be put on it.

The next benefit alleged was the low rate of interest charged. This was a matter which the learned judge considered, and he did a sum taking 11% as the realistic commercial rate, as compared with the 2.4% interest charged. The result did not reduce the deduction claimed. In my view this was an approach unduly favourable to the revenue. Looking at the whole arrangement there were many relevant possibilities (see clauses 4, 6, 8 and 11 of the Indebtedness Agreement; clauses 11 and 15 of the Management Agreement; clause 5 of the Supplementary Management Agreement; the original duration of the lease, and successive options to renew it, and clause 2 thereof). The low rate of interest is explicable by the early lump sum payment. It is not as if the borrower kept the money for his own use. It was momentarily treated as his, but under the scheme, he was immediately deprived of its use, without recompense. Nevertheless, he paid interest at 2.4% per annum on it. I would not treat him as gaining any additional benefit in this way. The benefit he gets is the tax he avoids, and this has been calculated. In my view he does not gain any further benefit, or, at least, any quantifiable benefit.

Reliance was placed by counsel for the Commissioner on clauses 6 and 8 of the indebtedness agreement, as showing that the debt may be discharged early, with consequent benefit to the taxpayer. I have already mentioned these clauses. A brief answer is that his interest in the enterprise will cease at the same time. This can hardly be a benefit. To put values upon the possibilities such as these, whether they are beneficial or detrimental, would, I think be impossible.

A ground in the notice of appeal was that the "scheme" was a fiscal nullity. In Oakey Abbatoir Pty. Ltd. v. The Commissioner of Taxation, (unreported, 5 October 1984) this Court held that the doctrine referred to had no application under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and counsel, without arguing the matter, reserved his right to rely upon the ground in the event of an appeal to the High Court. Special leave to appeal was refused by the High Court in that case.

It was also submitted that s.260 of the Act applied, but in my view there is no scope for the application of that section. The scheme was unusual in a few respects, but the taxpayer was not party to the establishment of it. He, with many others, responded to it, particularly by making the early lump sum payment for maintenance services. This (with the attendant loan arrangement) is the only transaction of which the Commissioner can complain, but it was promoted by others and not one into which it was unbusinesslike for him to enter into. Certainly, it achieved for the taxpayer a large deduction and an income tax loss, part of which he could carry forward, but, even if a deduction can be the subject of s.260, it is far short of satisfying the requirements of that section.

It seemed to me in connection with this argument, as with others, that what it was really being sought to allege was that the parts of the overall transaction, or some of them, were shams. As I have said, there was an express disclaimer of any intention to make such an allegation.

I am of the opinion that the appeal should be dismissed, with costs.

JENKINSON, J: I agree that the appeal should be dismissed with costs. I concur in the reasons given by Fox J. and in those given by Beaumont J..

BEAUMONT, J: The Commissioner of Taxation appeals to this Court from orders made by a single judge of the Supreme Court of Queensland allowing an appeal by the respondent taxpayer against an assessment by the Commissioner for the income year ended 30 June 1980 and remitting the matter to the Commissioner to amend the assessment by allowing three deductions claimed. The deductions, in the aggregate amount of $40,020.00, were disallowed by the Commissioner but were claimed by the taxpayer to have been incurred by him in the income year as outgoings in connection with an afforestation scheme, being a management fee of $39,200.00, rental of $640.00 and legal fees of $180.00. The taxpayer claimed that each outgoing was a deduction allowable under s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ("the Act").

In the Supreme Court, the Commissioner sought to uphold the assessment on a number of grounds. He argued that the transactions entered into by the taxpayer should be ignored as a sham (a ground not pressed in this Court); that the transactions were, in material respects, void by statute or otherwise ineffective under the general law; that the payments made were capital payments which fell outside s.51; that any deduction should be denied by virtue of the operation of s.82KL(1) of the Act; and that s.260 of the Act applied so as to disallow the deduction. The Commissioner also urged the Supreme Court to adopt the "fiscal nullity" doctrine applied by the House of Lords in Furniss v. Dawson (1984) 2 W.L.R. 226. However, whilst reserving his position for another day, the Commissioner did not press that argument before this Court because of a recent Full Court decision rejecting it (see Oakey Abattoir Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 15 A.T.R. 1059).

There is little room for dispute about the facts. At the end of 1980, the taxpayer, a medical practitioner engaged in full-time practice, turned his mind to the possibility of acquiring a long term investment - a "primary industry type situation", as he put it. He consulted his accountant, Mr. Ting, in this connection, with a view to investment in pine trees "desirous . . . of making provision for the years when the income from his professional practice might be expected to diminish but not unmindful (as the learned judge found) of the advantage of having a substantial tax deduction for the year ended 30 June 1981" (see (1984) 54 A.L.R. 167 at p.169).

On Mr. Ting's recommendation, the taxpayer executed an instrument bearing date 3 December 1980 headed "Application for Lease and Forest Management at the Burdekin Softwood Project Tondara Plantation". The application stated that the taxpayer wished to acquire a three year lease with a maximum of six three year renewals, together with "a 15 year forest management" (scil. contract) for the purpose of establishing and maintaining a "Pinus Caribeae Plantation". The application form required payment of a deposit in the sum of $320.00 payable to M.M.V.C. Pty. Limited. On 5 December 1980, the taxpayer drew a cheque in that amount in favour of Mr. Ting, who was known to the promoters of the Burdekin Softwood Project, Messrs. Aschman and Ray. In the events which happened, the scheme with which the taxpayer in fact became involved was not the Tondara Plantation but one known as Berejondo. His deposit was applied to that account, together with an additional sum of $4,400.00, paid by three cheques drawn by him between 22 April and 11 June 1981.

In April 1981, the land for use in the Berejondo scheme, situate near Lowmead and approximately 75 kms. from Bundaberg, was acquired for the sum of $146,550.00. The land was partly freehold and partly leasehold. Messrs. Aschman and Ray purchased the leasehold portion and Paragon Nominees Pty. Limited ("Paragon"), a company controlled by Messrs. Aschman and Ray, acquired the freehold portion. By an instrument of lease bearing date 23 April 1981, Paragon purported to lease certain lands to the taxpayer. The lease, stated to be for pine tree growing purposes only, was for a term of three years commencing on 23 June 1981 at a yearly rental of $640.00, payable in advance for the first year of the lease, and then of $240.00 for each of the second and third years of the lease, payable on or before 30 June 1982 and 1983 respectively. The leased premises were described as "an area of approximately fourteen hectares and located as shown in the attached schedule under lot number 16". Although a plan was attached to the lease, no attempt was made in the plan to subdivide the subject lands into lots with the consequence that lot 16 was not there identified. Lot 16 was subsequently located, in circumstances which I later mention. Under the lease, there was reserved to the lessor the right to remove any indigenous timber growing on the land (c1.8); the lessee was given the right to give access to a manager, his agents, servants or workman in order to carry out all the necessary action for the establishment, maintenance, felling and marketing of a pine tree plantation (c1.9). The lessee agreed not to use the land for any purpose other than pine tree growing without the lessor's approval (c1.10). Options for renewal for three year terms up to a maximum of 21 years were granted to the lessee at an annual rental of $240.00 (c1.12; c1.13).

A management agreement bearing date 24 April 1981, made between the taxpayer as "The Forest Owner" and Mount Sugarloaf Forest (N.Q.) Pty. Limited ("N.Q."), another company controlled by Messrs. Aschman and Ray, as "manager", recited that the taxpayer was desirous of establishing a pine forest plantation for commercial purposes on the leased land. The term of the agreement was a period of 21 years commencing on 23 June 1981 (c1.1). The taxpayer agreed to pay N.Q. a management fee calculated at the rate of $3,920.00 per hectare to be prepared, planted and maintained as therein provided, payable on execution of the agreement. The area to be planted was no less than ten hectares so that the sum of $39,200.00 was then payable (c1.2). This is the management fee now in question. N.Q. undertook to carry out all operations necessary in order to prepare, improve and maintain the subject lands so as to obtain the best possible plantable area for the growing of the species Pinus Elliolti and Pinus Caribbean (c1.3(a)). The manager promised to seed or plant directly at suitable intervals and spacing as to obtain an average density of not less than 1,250 trees per hectare (c1.3(b)). The manager further promised to establish, maintain and manage the pine tree plantation with due expertise, so as to attain an average lineal growth of not less than 2.70 metres over a growing period of any three year period up to the age of 12 years, excluding the first two years after seeding or planting (c1.3(1)). The manager further promised, as from 12 years after tree establishment, if so required, that it would cut and market from the plantation such timber as the taxpayer should direct. From the gross proceeds of sale, the manager is to reimburse itself for the cost of harvesting the timber and transport; the residue is to be divided as to 10% to the manager and the balance to the taxpayer or his assignee (c1.10). The taxpayer may at any time discontinue the agreement by giving 30 days notice in writing of his intention (c1.14). In the event that the taxpayer appoints the manager to cut, harvest and sell the whole of the timber under the agreement, the manager irrevocably guarantees that the nett proceeds from such sale(s) will be no less than four dollars per 15 year old tree harvested (c1.15).

This agreement was supplemented by a further management agreement between the same parties dated 24 April 1982. Under that agreement, a supplementary maintenance programme was instituted over a period of 20 years (c1.1). The taxpayer agreed to pay a supplementary management fee of $20,000.00, calculated at the rate of $2,500.00 per hectare to be fertilised, thinned and pruned (c1.2). In the event that the taxpayer appoints the manager to cut, harvest and sell the whole of the timber under the agreement, the manager irrevocably guarantees that the aggregate net proceeds from such sales will be no less than $10.00 per 15 year old tree harvested (c1.5).

The learned judge found that the funding of the management fee of $39,200.00, and similar fees payable by the other 80 odd participants in this scheme, required special financial arrangements, because the taxpayer did not have the sum in cash and that to have borrowed at a commercial rate of interest would have made the scheme "wholly unworkable". It was arranged that he borrow from Liberton Finance Pty. Limited ("Liberton") a company owned and controlled by Mr. Worrell, an accountant, and his partner, so much of the management fee as the taxpayer had not already paid, together with smaller amounts for rent and stamp duty.

This accommodation was documented by a memorandum of indebtedness dated 23 June 1981 made between Liberton and the taxpayer. It recited that Liberton had agreed to advance certain funds to the taxpayer for the purpose of financing the afforestation project. The principal amount advanced was $35,300.00 (c1.1). The term of loan was expressed to be a period of 21 years from the date thereof. Discharge was to take place in June 2002 "providing always that interests, costs and other dues have been paid . . . punctually during the term of the loan and providing further that (Liberton) shall accept the proceeds from every . . . harvest . . . in full satisfaction of the principal loan and any accumulated interest with the proviso that any returns exceeding the amount of the principal and interest and commission and fees and costs are to be paid to the (taxpayer)" (c1.2). Interest on the principal sum only is to be paid by the taxpayer at a simple rate of 2.4% per annum, payable quarterly (c1.3). In the event of default by the taxpayer, Liberton may cause the "seizure" and transfer to its own name of the lease agreement with Paragon and the management agreement with N.Q. (c1.4). Upon such transfer, all agreements, rights and liabilities in respect of the memorandum and leases and agreements specified therein will for the taxpayer come to an end, with the exception of an amount of $500.00 for transfer, stamp duties and costs, payable by and recoverable from the taxpayer (c1.4). The taxpayer is required to provide instruments of assignment in accordance with the foregoing provision (c1.10(b)). (Such an assignment, conditioned on his default, was executed by the taxpayer, bearing date 23 June 1981.) If the afforestation manager (N.Q.) defaults in a material particular in the performance of its obligations under the management agreement, Liberton shall accept, if so requested by the taxpayer, the transfer of the taxpayer's rights, title and interest under the lease and management agreement in complete discharge of his liabilities under the memorandum of indebtedness (c1.6). The taxpayer irrevocably appoints Liberton as its special agent for the purposes of receiving and disbursing proceeds from the sale of the timber and will be accountable to the taxpayer for all monies received and expended (c1.9).

In fact, however, as the learned judge found, Liberton had no funds to advance. It was put in funds for the purpose by N.Q. by way of an interest bearing advance and immediately paid back to N.Q. the same amount, being the aggregate of the sums owing by the participants on account of management fees, rent and stamp duty. On 26 June 1981, Liberton and N.Q. exchanged cheques in the sum of $2,559,620.00. On 30 June 1981, a further exchange of cheques took place in the sum of $96,500.00, giving a total of $2,656,120.00, being the aggregate amount due by the participants including the taxpayer.

Of these dealings, his Honour observed ((1984) 54 A.L.R. 167 at pp.171-2) that the participants, including the taxpayer, owed almost the whole of the management fees to Liberton, which in turn owed the same amount to N.Q. The participants were under an obligation to pay interest to Liberton, in all, in excess of $70,000, the latter paying it on to N.Q. less 10 per cent, so that N.Q. would receive something more than $60,000. This was considered to be sufficient to meet the annual costs of the scheme. Mr Aschman gave evidence that the interest rates were fixed with this in mind. The participants had a continuing debt to Liberton, as did Liberton to N.Q., in the aggregate sum of $2,656,120.00 which was to be discharged from the proceeds of the harvests. Although Liberton's right to take over the management agreement and lease on default by a participant was not framed as an obligation, the learned judge thought that it was an inevitable inference that the participants were never intended to recoup the full amount of the loan by Liberton from which the management fee was paid to N.Q. if the proceeds proved to be less than the amount of the loan. That inference is not challenged here.

Although the taxpayer has been paying interest of $200.80 per quarter, on 30 June 1981, N.Q. paid to Paragon the sum of $50,560.00 on behalf of the participants, being the aggregate of their rent for the first year. The sum included $640.00 on account of the taxpayer. The learned judge then rationalised the series of transactions as follows (at p.172):

"One asks oneself why the scheme took this form. Plainly enough NO needed a substantial amount in cash to establish the afforestation project. Payment for the land called for nearly $150,000 and, in addition to this, substantial costs would obviously be required for the initial setting up of the scheme including clearing and planting in the first year. There were some 80 participants and Mr Aschman gave a figure of $3,018,000 as the aggregate of their commitment. His evidence was that $362,560 was received in cash. If that sum be added to the continuing loan commitment of $2,656,120 it leads to virtually the precise figure given by Mr Aschman namely $3,018,680. The immediate question which poses itself is why Liberton was inserted in the scheme. NO's commercial needs would have been satisfied if the cash payments to which I have already referred had been made and if the management fee had been owed by the participants to NO for the same period of two years at the rate of interest which is payable under the scheme by Liberton to NO. Mr. Aschman's explanation was that he had been advised that it was desirable for NO to be, as he put it, at arms length from the participants. However, he was perfectly frank in conceding that one of the attractions of the scheme was the tax advantage to those who participated in it. To my mind it is an inevitable inference that the object of interposing Liberton was to create a situation in which each of the participants made a large cash payment in the initial year, the whole of which he could claim as a deduction on the footing that it was a prepayment of the management fee charged by NO for its management services over the next 21 years. In addition it was no doubt expected that the interest payments to Liberton and the rental payable to Paragon, a total in the case of Dr Lau for years after the first year in excess of $1000, would also be tax deductible."

Notwithstanding the absence of any real movement of money, his Honour held that the transactions were not shams. On the contrary, he found (at p.173) that it was the common intention of the participants in the scheme, of Liberton, and of N.Q., that the various instruments should take effect according to their tenor. Once it is concluded that the documentation is real enough and that the transactions are otherwise genuine, there is no room for any suggestion of a sham (see Mullens v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 C.L.R. 290 at p. 316). Essentially, this is a question of fact and, as has been said, the Commissioner now accepts this finding.

The learned judge also held that the three items concerned were allowable deductions which fell within s. 51 of the Act, notwithstanding that, in his view, the afforestation project was unlikely to make a major contribution to the taxpayer's income in the years of his declining medical practice. There was expert evidence that the harvest was unlikely to exceed, on current values, an amount of approximately $20,000.00. His Honour recognised that it may not be open to make a direct comparison between that figure and the sum of $35,300.00 which must be repaid in the currency of the year 2002. Nonetheless, he did not think that "a business is any the less a business because, on a more accurate analysis than has been carried out by the person undertaking it, its profits are unlikely to realise his expectations".

The learned judge also rejected each of the other grounds relied upon to justify the assessment.

Section 51

The Commissioner challenges the application of s. 51 on a number of grounds. He first submits that, of the deductions claimed, the amount of $35,300.00 was not an outgoing "incurred" by the taxpayer because it was never paid by or on behalf of the taxpayer. The amount in question - equal to the principal amount advanced to the taxpayer by Liberton - represents the balance of the aggregate sum owed by the taxpayer in respect of the management fee ($39,200.00), first year's rent ($640.00) and stamp duty ($180.00) after the payment by the taxpayer of the sum of $4,720.00. The Commissioner suggests that, because the procedure adopted involved the exchange of cheques, in truth, no payment was ever made by Liberton to N.Q., nor was any loan ever effected by N.Q. to Liberton. All that happened, the argument runs, was that the bank, at the request of those companies, made certain entries in its records and no cashflow occurred.

In my opinion, the argument cannot be sustained. Assuming, as we must, that no sham is involved, the sole question, for present purposes, is whether, on the documentation, the outgoing was "incurred". No proof of payment or actual disbursement need be established. Section 51 covers outgoings to which the taxpayer is "definitively committed" in the year of income in the sense that he is then under a presently existing liability on that account (see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Limited [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 C.L.R. 492 at pp. 506-7: Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty. Limited v. The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 C.L.R. 616 at p. 627: Australian Guarantee Corporation Limited v. Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 84 A.T.C. 4,642). Under the contract instruments, the taxpayer bound himself, by enforceable obligations, to pay to N.Q. and Paragon respectively the management fees and rental now in question. He thus "incurred" those outgoings in that year. The accommodation granted to him by Liberton, and, indirectly, by N.Q., is beside the point. The question is whether the taxpayer incurred the outgoing rather than how he financed it. This is not to say that, in any event, the exchange of cheques should not be treated as a payment made by each party to the exchange. Clearly it should be so regarded (see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Steeves Agnew & Company (Victoria) Pty. Limited [1951] HCA 26; (1951) 82 C.L.R. 408 at p. 421 and cases there cited).

Then the Commissioner says that the taxpayer's expenditure was not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or in carrying on a business. He argues that, in assessing the character of the payment of the sum of $35,300.00, the court should not confine its attention to the management agreement since, it is said, it is proper to look at the use to which the money is put. The suggestion is that Liberton, the taxpayer's agent, had agreed in advance with N.Q., his manager, that the amount of the payment would immediately be returned to Liberton, to be held by it for 21 years. The return of the money was a requirement, in the sense of a financial necessity, of the taxpayer's agent for the purpose.

In order to say what relationship to the undertaking or business is borne by the incurring of expenditure, it is necessary to identify what the expenditure is for (see Magna Alloys and Research Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 33 A.L.R. 213 per Brennan, J. at p. 221). Thus, in Ure v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 34 A.L.R. 237, it was held that an interest payment was obtained by that taxpayer to obtain not only a lesser amount of interest from an on-lending transaction with a company controlled by him, but also to achieve non-income producing objects and advantages which included a reduction of his income tax liability. In the circumstances, an apportionment was made for the purposes of s. 51. In that context, Brennan, J. said (at pp. 241-2):

". . . where a question arises as to the purpose for which money is laid out by a taxpayer, it is erroneous to exclude as irrelevant evidence of the use of that money, albeit by others, in conformity with arrangements made by the taxpayer. That is not to deny the separate identity of those who use the borrowed money, nor to attribute vicariously to the taxpayer the activities of others. It is simply to take some relevant factors into account in determining the purposes for which money is laid out. Those purposes do not depend upon the state of the taxpayer's mind but upon what the taxpayer in the circumstances of the case is ascertained to have done in using and arranging for the use of the borrowed moneys.

The purposes for which money is laid out is an issue of fact, turning upon the objective circumstances which human experience would judge to be relevant to the issue . . . In the present case, there is an air of unreality about the proposition that the borrowed moneys were laid out wholly for the purpose of earning a return of 1% pa. Rather it is right to say that the purpose for which the borrowed moneys were laid out included all the purposes earlier mentioned, only one of which was to earn a return of 1% pa.

If the borrowed moneys had been laid out soley for the purpose of gaining assessable income, the interest would be wholly deductible; but as they were laid out in part for that purpose, and in part for other purposes, the interest charges must be apportioned."

But, in Ure's Case, the parties were not, as in this case, at arms' length, so that the interposed family company was not in any sense striking a bargain with its controller. This taxpayer was dealing with parties, N.Q., Paragon and Liberton, to whom he was in no way related. Those parties were prepared to deal with the taxpayer on certain terms which he was at liberty to accept or reject. It was not a case where, as in Ure's Case, the taxpayer was in a position to mould the transaction so as to maximise its tax advantages from his standpoint. Whilst tax benefits were never overlooked in this case, the transactions were negotiated in a genuine commercial setting in which, for reward fixed by themselves, Paragon offered a lease of the site, N.Q. offered management services and Liberton offered financial accommodation.

It is a truism that it is not for the court or the Commissioner to say how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income but only how much he has spent (see Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 47 at p. 60). Once it is concluded that the monies were outlaid by the taxpayer for a real or genuine commercial purpose, any inquiry as to the manner in which those funds were subsequently applied by their recipients is immaterial for the purposes of s. 51. The reason is that, where, as here, the parties are at arms' length, the use made of the funds by the other parties to the transactions is not capable of throwing any light upon the purpose for which the taxpayer incurred the outgoings. The suggestion that, in this case, a tracing exercise should be embarked upon in order to establish, in the context of s. 51, the "true" purpose of the liabilities incurred by the taxpayer, should be rejected. Whether these matters bear upon the application of s. 260 of the Act is a different question.

In support of his contention that a deduction under s. 51 is not available, the Commissioner points to a number of features of these transactions which, he says, afford important clues as to their true character, and, in particular, the absence of any substantial commercial flavour in what was done: the prepayment of a large sum was involved; no security was given for the repayment of this amount or for the performance of the manager's obligations; the taxpayer had little legal control over the manager; and the manager was granted grazing rights under the management agreement (cl. 4). But, with the possible exception of the prepayment, none of these considerations can cast any serious doubt upon the essentially commercial character of the transactions entered into. The prepayment apart, the matters complained of are at best equivocal on the point sought to be made. The more likely explanation is that the taxpayer, not unnaturally, preferred to rely upon the business judgment and expertise of Messrs. Aschman and Ray rather than to impose tight legal constraints upon the conduct of their business activities. For the taxpayer to insist upon rigid legal guidelines for the operation of the project may well have been commercially counterproductive in the long run.

There is more force in the Commissioner's complaints about the prepayment. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ilbery [1981] FCA 188; (1981) 38 A.L.R. 172, a prepayment of interest was disallowed under s. 51. It was held that the taxpayer's object in prepaying the interest was to obtain a tax advantage rather than to gain income. Two features of the case, not here present, should be noted. At the time of prepayment of interest, that taxpayer had not acquired any property from which he hoped to derive income. Further, he prepaid the interest voluntarily: he was under no obligation to accelerate the payment of interest, but elected to do so pursuant to an option granted to him in that behalf. This may be contrasted with the present case, where N.Q. was only prepared to deal with the taxpayer upon the footing that he was contractually obliged to pay the management fee in advance of performance. The taxpayer said in evidence that his preferred course was to pay the management fee by instalments rather than by a lump sum in advance. He explored the possibility of doing this but N.Q. was not prepared to treat it with him on that footing.

The prepayment of a relatively large management fee may be unusual in business dealings. Against this, it can be said that N.Q. anticipated that the major development expenses would be incurred in the early stages of the project. The question is whether the prepayment of the fee is so significant as to work a transformation of the transaction from something which had a commercial objective into an arrangement which lacks any such purpose. In my opinion, the question should be answered in the negative. Although the transaction was structured so as to offer tax advantages to participants, it would be wrong to say it was not a real business transaction. The arrangements were underpinned by genuine commercial considerations and, in my view, these considerations are decisive for the purposes of the application of s. 51 (cf. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v. Walker (1984) 15 A.T.R. 847).

The Commissioner also argues that other circumstances of the case indicate that, in truth, the taxpayer was concerned only to obtain a tax deduction and was indifferent to the viability of the project as a business concern. He relies, in this connection, upon the failure of the taxpayer and his accountant, Mr. Ting, to peruse carefully the documentation submitted for his execution and their failure to seek legal advice as to their purport. Then he points to their failure to seek the advice of the Forestry Department and the circumstances that the taxpayer visited the plantation on only one occasion. He also refers to the failure to obtain any credit check on N.Q. and the absence of any attempt to obtain a registrable lease. But the evidence was not all one way, even on these aspects of the matter. For instance, there was evidence that the taxpayer chose the project because it was to be operated by the persons who had previously managed a similar and apparently successful plantation at Stanthorpe. There was also evidence that the taxpayer relied on the judgment of Mr. Aschman. His opinion was that the project would be profitable. In these circumstances, an issue of fact or, more precisely, of prophecy, arises. The learned judge was inclined to the view (at p. 175) that, perhaps the Commissioner's pessimism as to the prospects of the project was justified. Yet, even if this be so when viewed objectively with the benefit of hindsight and expert opinion evidence not then available to the taxpayer, it does not follow that the matters now relied on indicate that the actions of the taxpayer were no more than a fiscal exercise. In my view, given the findings of fact made by the learned judge, in particular, the findings that there was here no sham and that the dealings between the parties were actuated by genuine commercial considerations, none of the matters now relied on by the Commissioner can deny to the payments in question the character of outgoings which fall within s. 51 (see Thomas v. Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth (1972) 46 A.L.J.R. 397 at p. 401).

On this branch of the argument, the Commissioner says that, although the lease in question required the approval of the local authority, no such approval was sought or given, with the consequence that the lease is void (see Pelovic v. Malinic (1960) 60 S.R. (N.S.W.) 184). The consequence, he submits, is that, absent the lease, no deduction can be allowable under s. 51. It is common ground that the Local Government Act, 1936 (Q.) and By-Law 40 required Council approval to the subdivision. Illegality of the lease itself is a different matter. If it were to be treated as an agreement for lease entered into subject to a condition that approval be obtained, illegality would not necessarily ensue, at least not at that stage (see Local Government Act, s. 34 (19) (iv); Brown v. Heffer [1967] HCA 40; (1967) 116 C.L.R. 344 at pp. 349-50). But whatever be the precise status of any subdivision effected by the lease, the taxpayer's claim for deductions under s. 51 cannot be rejected on that score. Even if it be assumed (and I express no view on the matter) that the lease was purportedly granted in contravention of the subdivisional requirements of the local legislation, it does not follow that any deduction on that account is to be ignored for taxation purposes anymore than would income derived from that activity (see Madad Pty. Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation 84 A.T.C. 4,739).

Another difficulty raised by the Commissioner, already adverted to, is the parties' failure, in the first instance at least, to identify the leased premises. It is common ground that, taken alone, the lease instrument did not sufficiently identify its subject (see Lancaster v. de Trafford (1862) 31 L.J. CH. 554; cf. Akot Pty. Limited v. Rathmines Investments Pty. Limited (1984) 1 Qd.R. 302). It would seem that, at an uncertain later date, the taxpayer's area was identified as Lot 16. There can be little doubt that, considered as at 23 April 1981 and subsequently, there were vested in the taxpayer equitable proprietary rights in respect of the area to be planted or, at the least, equities, by virtue of which he could have proceeded to protect his interest in the land. Whether these rights or equities were in the nature of a leasehold interest or by way of a profit a prendre need not be pursued here (see Mills v. Stockman [1967] HCA 15; (1966) 116 C.L.R. 61 at pp. 76-77; Australian Softwood Forestwood Forests Pty. Limited v. Attorney-General [1981] HCA 49; (1982) 148 C.L.R. 121 at pp. 130-2). Taking the worst position from the taxpayer's standpoint, even if the monies in question were laid out by him in connection with no more than a de facto lease, the expenditure was nonetheless incurred in what the learned judge found to be a genuine commercial context. Given the business character of the expenditure, any failure of the taxpayer to perfect his title to the premises could not, in my view, disqualify the taxpayer from claiming the outgoings as allowable deductions.

Then the Commissioner says that the payments fell outside s. 51 because they were affairs of capital. He relies on a number of matters as indicating that, as in Clowes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1954] HCA 10; (1954) 91 C.L.R. 209 at pp. 218, 223 (and see Milne v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 2; (1976) 133 C.L.R. 526), the expenditure was not incurred on revenue account: the payments were made "once and for all"; a long term benefit, with little element of recurrence, was contemplated; and the expenditure bore an investment character consistent with a capital outgoing.

In my opinion, both on their facts and in point of their reasoning, Clowes' Case and Milne's Case may be distinguished here. In Clowes, the question was whether the taxpayer had earned, within the meaning of s. 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936-1945, a "profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property . . . or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme". The taxpayer there, along with others, entered into an agreement which imposed upon the other party an obligation to establish a plantation of pine trees. The agreement provided that the plantation was to be dealt with as a whole and upon sale of the timber, lot-holders were to take only a distributable share of the total net proceeds. The taxpayer paid to the other party the sum of 75 pounds upon entry into the agreement. Many years later, the taxpayer received a distribution of 105 pounds. It was held that the sum of 30 pounds was a mere enlargement of capital and was not to be deemed assessable income under s. 26(a), because the investments, not forming part of any system or practice, were not themselves a profit-making scheme, while the operations which produced the profit were not carried on by or on behalf of the taxpayer as a lot-holder, but by the other party on its own behalf.

In Milne's Case, a forestry company issued to the taxpayer one of a series of bonds under which it promised to plant and tend softwood plantations on described land. Each bondholder became entitled as tenant in common with other holders of bonds to one acre of freehold land in the described area. The Commissioner in that case unsuccessfully attempted to distinguish Clowes on two grounds: that in Milne, an acquisition of an interest in land was involved; and that the taxpayer in Milne received a number of payments over a period of years by way of distributions of proceeds of sales among bondholders. Again it was held that, whether or not bondholders acquired an interest in land, the amounts received were not assessable income because the payments were not income according to ordinary conceptions and they were not profits arising from the carrying on or carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme.

In the present case, the structure of the relationship between the parties is different from either Clowes or Milne. Here, the arrangement contemplates that the taxpayer will be granted, by way of lease, a specific area of land, and, thereby, an identifiable interest in specific trees in that area. Further, N.Q.'s role under its agreement with the taxpayer is one of management on behalf of the taxpayer and not management in its own right. The agreement gives the taxpayer the right at any time to terminate the manager's services, admittedly without any refund of the management fee (cl. 14). Further, N.Q. did not own the subject land and the management agreement gave it no right of entry onto the land.

Since neither Clowes' Case nor Milne's Case is squarely in point here, the question remains whether, in principle, the amounts in question should be viewed as paid on revenue rather than capital account (see Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 C.L.R. 337 at p. 363). The learned judge found that the fee paid to N.Q. was, in truth, paid on account of management services which were to be rendered, if not continuously, then recurrently, over a period of 21 years. In this context, it is appropriate to refer to the contractual quid pro quo to determine the nature of the outgoing (see Magna Alloys & Research Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 F.L.R. 183 at pp. 191, 208) and, prima facie, monies outlaid in return for such recurrent services are paid on revenue account. In my view, the circumstance that the fee is to be paid as a lump sum in advance is not sufficient to displace this presumption. The important considerations are the nature of the services to be rendered and the periodic manner in which they are to be rendered. In my opinion, the outgoings fell within s. 51, being directed not to the profit-yielding subject of the taxpayer's business but to the process of operating it (see Ferguson v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 29; (1979) 26 A.L.R. 307 at p. 314).

Section 82KL(1)

Next, the Commissioner relies on the provisions of Subdivision D of Division 3 of Part III of the Act and, specifically, s. 82KL(1). It provides that a deduction is not allowable in respect of certain recouped expenditure: where the sum of the amount or value of the additional benefit in relation to an amount of eligible relevant expenditure incurred by a taxpayer and the expected tax saving in connection with that amount of eligible relevant expenditure is equal to or greater than the amount of the eligible relevant expenditure, a deduction is not allowable. The taxpayer accepts that the amounts in issue of $39,200.00, $640.00 and $180.00 respectively are "relevant expenditure" (see the definition of "relevant expenditure", para. (p) in s. 82KH(1)). By s. 82KH(1F), these amounts are "eligible" relevant expenditure if, by reason of, or as a result of or as part of a tax avoidance agreement (as defined), the taxpayer has obtained, in relation to that relevant expenditure being incurred, a benefit or benefits in addition to (i) the benefit in respect of which the relevant expenditure was incurred; and (ii) any benefit that resulted directly or indirectly from the benefit in respect of which the relevant expenditure was incurred and is a benefit that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, might reasonably be expected to have resulted, if the benefit in respect of which the relevant expenditure was incurred had been obtained otherwise than by reason of, as a result of or as part of a tax avoidance agreement (s. 82KH(1F)(b)). By s. 82KH(1), a tax avoidance agreement is defined so as to include an agreement one of the purposes of which is to reduce the amount of income tax to be paid by a taxpayer. It is not disputed that the management agreement and the loan agreement evidenced by the memorandum of indebtedness fall within the definition since one of their purposes was to reduce the taxpayer's liability to income tax.

The relevant expenditure incurred was $40,020.00. It is common ground that the expected tax saving would be $11,417.30. The next question is to determine the amount of any additional benefits obtained by the taxpayer for the purposes of s. 82KH(1F)(b). The Commissioner points to a number of supposed additional benefits. First, and this is not disputed, he fastens on the making of the loan at a low, non-commercial rate of interest. The taxpayer accepts the finding of the learned judge that, given a realistic commercial interest rate of 11%, an actuarial valuation indicates that the taxpayer must be regarded as having had a benefit of $24,514.00 on this account.

Next, the Commissioner invites the Court to assess an additional benefit said to accrue from the operation of cl.2 of the memorandum of indebtedness. The suggestion is that, because that provision obliges Liberton, in certain events, to accept the harvest in full satisfaction of the taxpayer's liability under the memorandum of indebtedness, the taxpayer should be credited with an amount that reflects the likelihood of that contingency occurring.

Although the taxpayer bears the general onus of proof under s. 190(b), the provisions of s. 82KH(1F) can only operate if there is a benefit which can be assessed in monetary terms. But this does not mean that the matter is to be tested on the balance of probability. As in the case of the law of damages, s. 82KH(1F) is concerned with evaluating, in monetary terms, future possibilities and chances. In assessing an amount which depends on what will happen in the future, an estimate must be made as to what are the chances that a particular thing will happen and these chances, whether they are more or less than even, must be reflected in the amount determined to be appropriate (see Chaplin v. Hicks (1911) 2 K.B. 786 at pp. 794-5; Howe v. Teefy [1927] NSWStRp 41; (1927) 27 S.R. (N.S.W.) 301; Halsbury's Laws of England, 4th Ed. vol. 12 p. 437, para. 1137; Luntz, Assessment of Damages, 2nd Ed. at p. 82, para. 1.1.05).

The learned judge made the following findings in this connection ((1984) 54 A.L.R. 167 at p. 176):

"If the scheme runs its term, Liberton must accept the proceeds of harvests in full satisfaction up to the amount of the debt. To quantify the extent to which this benefit is additional to the prospects of a valuable harvest is conjectural. I pause to say that on the evidence before me there is no reason to suppose that the scheme will not run its course. Mr Aschman and Mr Ray promoted a similar scheme in the Stanthorpe area some ten years ago and on the evidence it is operating and presents external indications of success. If Dr Lau were to default in payment of the rent or the interest this benefit would crystallise but at the expense of his participation in the surplus of harvests over the debt he owes to Liberton. The prospect of Dr Lau's defaulting is not, in my opinion high. If he does he will again lose whatever prospect he has of making something from the harvesting of the timber. His annual payments are around $1000 tax deductible. It has not been demonstrated that it may reasonably be expected that Liberton will release, abandon or fail to demand repayment of its debt so as to bring into operation s. 82KH(1J)."

Having assessed the benefit to the taxpayer of the low interest loan at the figure of $24,514.00 and having taken into account the expected tax saving of $11,417.30, the learned judge turned to the question whether he was satisfied that the value of the possibility of Liberton's being in a position in which the loan would not be recovered was demonstrated to make up the difference, viz., $4,088.70 (i.e. $40,020.00 - ($11,417.30 + $24,514.00)). His Honour's response was negative (at p. 177). The Commissioner attacks the finding as contrary to some of the expert evidence called. He further submits that, in so holding, the learned judge reversed the onus of proof imposed upon the taxpayer by s. 190(b).

When his Honour's remarks are read in context, it does not appear that any impermissible reversal of onus has occurred. The learned judge was dealing with a subject which was necessarily a matter of conjecture. His reference to his being not being "satisfied" on the issue was not intended to deal with the onus of proof but rather to indicate that there was simply no concrete evidence on the point. The expert evidence, controversial as it was, could do no more than proffer an opinion as to the future. I am not persuaded that his Honour's finding on this score should be disturbed.

The Commissioner further contends that the learned judge failed to deal with another submission put by him in this connection, that a benefit to the taxpayer arose from the prospect that his liability to repay under the memorandum of indebtedness may be discharged prematurely by the operation of cl. 6 or cl. 8 hereof as follows:

"6. If the afforestation manager referred to in the management agreement mentioned in clause 4(b) shall cease to perform its obligations under the said agreement in a material particular and shall continue not to perform its obligations after having been requested in writing by the borrower to do so then (and only then) The Lender hereunder, being satisfied as to the foregoing, shall accept, if so requested by the Borrower, the transfer of the Borrower's rights, title, and interest under the Lease and Management Agreement referred to in clause 4(a) and clause 4(b) to the Lender or its nominee and on the execution of such transfer the Borrower shall be completely, absolutely and fully discharged of any and all liabilities under this Memorandum of Indebtedness.

. . .

8. Notwithstanding anything set out in clauses 5, 6 and 7 above if the

afforestation manager is ordered by the court to be wound up or if the members of the afforestation manager shall resolve to wind up the Company and prior to the commencement of the winding up of the afforestation manager the manager has failed to assign the management agreement and the duties thereunder to a third party capable of continuing the management agreement then the afforestation manager shall for the purpose of these presents be regarded as having not performed its obligations under the afforestation management agreement and The Borrower may at his option request the Lender to accept the transfer or assignment of the Lease and Management Agreement detailed in clause 4(a) and clause 4(b) in full and final satisfaction of the debt hereunder and the Lender shall be bound to accept such transfer forthwith."

The learned judge made no reference to the argument. Yet, he did find (at p. 176, supra) that there was "no reason to suppose that the scheme will not run its course". Although that finding is not inconsistent with the mere possibility of an early determination, it is another matter to attribute a money sum to that contingency.

Whilst it may be appropriate to allow more than a nominal amount on account of this benefit, it does not necessarily follow that as much as $4,088.70 should be assigned to it. Again the quantification is conjectural. Given the findings of the learned judge, it would be wrong to attribute to this item any substantial amount, since interest payments are in the order of $845.00 per annum only. In other words, I do not think that it is appropriate, even as a possibility, to reckon on the taxpayer's being relieved of that commitment four years or so before the due date.

Section 260

Finally, the Commissioner invokes s. 260. There are obvious difficulties in seeking to apply s. 260 to deny a deduction to a payment which qualifies as an allowable deduction. The question was considered by a Full High Court in Cecil Bros. Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 82; (1964) 111 C.L.R. 430, per Dixon, C.J., at p. 438; per Kitto, J. at p. 438; per Taylor, J. at p. 438). Although, in strictness, their Honours did not need to decide that s. 260 could not apply to defeat or reduce any deduction otherwise truly allowable under s1.51, their expressions of opinion on the point should be treated as authoritative for present purposes.

The Commissioner sought to find support for his argument in Hollvock v. The Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 43; (1971) 125 C.L.R. 647. Gibbs, J. (as he then was) there spoke of an arrangement designed to secure both the avoidance of tax and another purpose and held that, in such a case, the arrangement does not escape from s. 260 simply because it cannot be held that the avoidance of tax is the principal purpose of the scheme (at p. 657). But, significantly for present purposes, his Honour also observed that if tax avoidance is an inessential or incidental feature of the arrangement, that may well serve to show that the arrangement cannot necessarily be labelled as a means to avoid tax.

The Commissioner also presses upon us the recent decision of this Court in Oakey Abattoir Pty. Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation, supra, so far as it applied s. 260. But that case did not involve a deduction truly allowable under s. 51. Further, the arrangement to which s. 260 was there applied was wholly artificial and circular and lacked any commercial foundation whatever. In my opinion, once it is concluded that a deduction under s. 51 is available, the reasoning of Cecil Bros. applies here so as to deny s. 260 any application.

I would dismiss the appeal with costs.
