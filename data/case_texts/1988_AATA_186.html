Evidence

The Tribunal had before it documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as modified by section 14ZG of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. It received exhibits during the hearing. Sworn evidence was heard from the Applicant and his wife; from a former employee of W.A. Discount Corporation, part of the group of entities operated by Mr. C.T. Moll; from a former Senior Investigation Officer with the Australian Taxation Office; from an executive officer in the Compliance Branch of the Australian Taxation Office; from a wholesaler and exporter of diamonds and other jewellery; from a Certified Practising Accountant who was formerly a Senior Investigation Officer in the Australian Taxation Office; and from the managing director of a printing company.

Principal relevant statutory provision

The principal relevant statutory provision of the Act in this proceeding is sub-section 51(1). The substantive questions which arise are whether the losses and outgoings claimed as deductible by the Applicant were incurred by him in gaining or producing assessable income or were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income. It is not contended that the losses or outgoings in issue fall within the exception in sub-section 51(1).

Onus of proof

Pursuant to section 190(b) of the Act the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive lies on the Applicant. In Danmark Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation; Forestwood Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1944) 7 ATD 333, at p. 337, Latham C.J. stated:

"I agree that upon an appeal the onus rests upon the taxpayer of establishing the facts upon which he relies and if it is necessary for him to establish a particular fact in order to displace the assessment he must satisfy the Court with respect to that fact."

The standard of proof is the "ordinary civil onus of proof": Allied Pastoral Holdings Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1983) 44 ALR 607, at 612-619.

The Respondent has the evidentiary burden of pointing to any relevant issues to enable the taxpayer to deal with them in the discharge of his overall burden of proof: Boyded Industries Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4047 at 4056.

Management fees under the MANS Contract

The Applicant, who was born in England in 1937, graduated as a medical practitioner in that country in 1961 and was there employed for three years by the National Health Service. In 1964 with his wife and family, he settled in Australia and became one of three partners in a medical practice. He practised medicine in partnership until 1972 when the partnership dissolved and he became the sole proprietor of one of the branch surgeries previously conducted by that partnership.

The Applicant's wife qualified in England as a physiotherapist. Whilst the Applicant was in partnership she was involved in the practice to the extent of answering telephone calls and dealing with patients. From 1972 she worked with her husband in his practice. She was asked:

"But, basically in the year ended 30 June 1974 your husband was working as a doctor and you were managing the non-medical side of the practice? --- Yes."

On 2 September 1974 the Applicant entered into a contract ("the MANS contract") with Management and Nursing Services Pty. Ltd. ("MANS") one of a group of companies controlled by a Mr. C.T. Moll. Under the MANS contract, MANS accepted a qualified commitment

"to carry out such duties for the medical practice of the Client [the Applicant] and for such period (if any) as shall in each case be advised by the Client to the Employer [MANS]."

The Applicant's wife resigned from her employment with her husband (apparently shortly after the MANS contract was made) and became an employee of MANS. She continued her work at the Applicant's surgery as before and in particular this included her managerial work as referred to above.

MANS was remunerated by recoupment of its outgoings plus 12 1/2 per cent of such outgoings. In the financial year ended 30 June 1974, the Applicant paid total wages of $6995. In the subsequent financial years the Applicant claimed as deductions the following amounts for payments made to MANS, in each case described as "Administration and Nursing Services".

  1. $13,277
  2. $27,324
  3. $26,907
  4. $32,314
  5. $25,274

All but $740 in 1976 and $6989 in 1978 of the above amounts have now been allowed as deductions.

Management fees under the Moll Contract

Before the Tribunal was an agreement ("the Moll Contract") dated 1 July 1974 between the Applicant (therein described as "the Employer") and C.T. Moll & Co. (therein described as "the Manager"). Clause 5 of that agreement provided:

"5. The remuneration of the Manager for the services of the Manager hereunder shall be FIVE HUNDRED DOLLARS per calendar month payable calendar monthly in arrears on the last day of each calendar month during the term hereof and commencing with a payment of that amount on the thirty-first day of July One thousand nine hundred and seventy-four. The Employer shall reimburse the Manager for all out-of-pocket expenses reasonably incurred by the Manager (including entertainment expenses incurred by the Manager in connection with the duties of the Manager hereunder) in performance of the duties of the Manager hereunder."

Although the agreement bore the date of 1 July 1974 the Applicant agreed it had been backdated but could not recall on what date he had executed it. The agreement was stamped on 13 April 1976. The Applicant's cashbook shows the sum of $91.50 paid by way of solicitor's fee on 29 April 1976. That is the amount claimed as legal costs for the preparation of the agreement. The Tribunal accepts the submission on behalf of the Respondent that the inescapable inference is that the agreement was backdated from approximately March or April 1976 to 1 July 1974.

The first amount forming part of the $9000 claimed by the Applicant in the 1975 year - the sum of $6000 - was paid on 27 June 1975. According to Folio 155 of the documents before the Tribunal the balance of $3000 was "Accrued by journal", although at Folio 157 this journal entry appears to have been reversed. No evidence has been given on behalf of the Applicant as to the date or other particulars of these journal entries. The Applicant was asked:

"Why was it that the management agreement provides for $500 per month, which comes to $6000 and in your case you paid $9000, why was that? I really do not know. I cannot remember why this was. We have looked at this many times since - recently we have looked at it many times - and I just cannot recall why. I have been unable---".

Whether the payment of this $3000 was ever made has not been established by the Applicant.

The Respondent seeks to characterise this record of payment as haphazard and to say that it is therefore indicative of a voluntary rather than a compulsory payment. Despite variations in the pattern of payment the Tribunal does not accept that the payments are haphazard and even if they could be correctly so described, does not consider the pattern of payment suggestive that the amounts paid were not being paid pursuant to the contractual obligation in the Moll contract or a prior agreement which that contract recorded. The history of payment whether viewed from the Day Book or the folios of the Tribunal documents, to which the Respondent has also addressed argument, suggests the records maintained by the Applicant's wife were being maintained, as it were, by an amateur as opposed to a professional accountant. Having considered the Respondent's submissions concerning the modes of keeping account, the items which the Applicant's wife failed to highlight in the Day Book, the explanation that the $250 related to repayment of airfares and the entry for 17 December 1975 as a personal drawing, the Tribunal does not consider these matters lead it to conclude the Applicant did not make the above payments in the manner referred to.

The evidence of the Senior Investigation Officer included in part the following exchange:

"Mr. [....], some questions were put to you in cross examination as to the basis of your conclusion in relation to the management fee. Was the basis of your conclusions in relation to the management fee that they were actually refunded to [the Applicant] or was it that there was an arrangement that they would be? ---Yes. The basis was that I felt that there was an arrangement whereby the fees would be refunded back to [the Applicant]; in fact, as far as I could ascertain I did not come across any fees which were actually refunded but the point was that we felt that there was the initial arrangement."

The Respondent contends it is therefore necessary for the Applicant to satisfy the Tribunal that there was no arrangement whereby these payments or any part of them would find their way back to the Applicant or any associate of his, whether in cash or kind and whether in Australia or overseas. Reading that answer of the Senior Investigation Officer in the context of all his evidence the Tribunal does not consider that the answer has that effect. It is apparent from the evidence of that officer that many matters which he thought to be the case at the time of preparing his report concerning the Applicant were based upon untested assumptions. While the documents before the Tribunal suggest that the consequence of the modus operandi of Mr. Moll there must have been an arrangement for the Applicant to receive such payments, there is not evidence before the Tribunal from which that possibility receives even the slightest support. The following exchange occurred in examination of the Applicant:

"And, were you getting any money back from either Moll or MANS? — No.

Was there any agreement made between you and Moll, or MANS, or any of his entities at all, for any agreement made by you with anyone, under which you had received back the money that you paid to Moll or MANS?--- No, I ad no such arrangement.

Did you receive back any money that you paid to Moll or MANS? — None."

In the opinion of the Tribunal, in the absence of any evidence to the contrary the Applicant discharges the onus upon him of establishing that no payments were received back and that there was no arrangement whereby that would be the case.

On 1 January 1977 the Applicant took a partner into his practice. The evidence before the Tribunal establishes that the partner was not required by the Applicant to pay any portion of the management fee. The evidence was that the partner shared in profits but was not liable for losses. The profits were shared equally. The Applicant said he had overlooked passing the costs to his partner; that he, the Applicant, was alone totally responsible for the administration of the practice; and that in any event, the fee was not just for help with management of the practice but also for management of his other financial arrangements such as Jason Trading Co. (to which reference will later be made) and for advice as to how to invest money in the short term money market in the name of the children (said to be for the reason that they would be paying their tax at a lower rate). Whether or not such moneys remained the property of the Applicant or whether they were gifted to the children so that the interest was properly receivable by them as income was not conclusively established on the evidence. On the evidence of the Applicant's wife, the investment was made in the children's name so that the tax would not come home to the Applicant. The position is, therefore, that the Applicant had no income generating activities other than his medical practice and Jason Trading Co. Apart from interest earned on a bank account in relation to which Mr. Moll was not said to have given any advice, the Applicant stated he had no business affairs to manage.

The evidence of the Applicant and his wife in relation to what the Applicant received from C.T. Moll & Co. pursuant to the obligations on the Manager under the Moll contract requires further examination. The Applicant's evidence in chief on this was as follows:

"Can you recall what objectives you had in mind in retaining Mr. Moll?---Yes, I wanted to shed the burden of the administration of the practice to as large extent as was possible. Mr. Moll had promised that he would be able to do that for me. He also said that he would help and advise me in investments, which I can expand on if you like, he would give me more time to practice, he would streamline the administration of the practice, I think that is it.

Yes, all right. Now if you can expand on the second aspect, the help and advice on investments. At the time all disposable money that we had left over after expenses was being banked or kept in building societies, whatever the interest rate of the day was, Mr. Moll made available to us at that time investment on the short-term money market which as I understand it, would not have been available to small investors like myself then, and we were enjoying the benefits of 2 per cent greater interest rates than were available through building societies or banks. He made investment available to us on an on-call basis, we did not have to tie our money up for any length of time. He advised us, I think Mr. Hawkins mentioned earlier on, we were looking at the purchase of a unit in [...] as an investment, as a rental proposition, and he advised us very strongly against this at the time, told us to leave our money where it was on the short-term market. I cannot think of anything else offhand."

He said that contact with Mr. Moll was usually by telephone and most of the contact was made by his wife after he had asked her to sort something out. He was asked:

"... what sort of things would you ask your wife to contact him about?---1 can think of two examples if that is of any help after all these years. One was a typewriter that was on the blink? should we get it repaired yet again or should be replace it? If we replace it, should we do it this financial year or next financial year? If the decision is to replace it, should be buy it or lease it? If we were to lease it, would it be better off - would Mr. Moll be able to help us and advise us in getting the best deals on the lease. Bad debts were often a problem; they still are a problem. We would phone him and say Mr. J. Patient had run up bills of $100, should be chase him for this, should we wait until he had run up a few more bills, should we in fact refuse to see him in anything other than an emergency until this bill was paid; ordinary day to day office administration which we had no experience in ourselves. We got a great deal of advice from him too on the actual running of the accounting system in the office. We had a very cumbersome one before he came along and streamlined it for us. It involved - before he came along it would take a week to ten days to get the accounts out at the end of each month. After he had reorganized the system for us they were all out by the third of the month. I could sit here a long time, I think, trying to remember small things like this that he did for us over the years.

Unfortunately I cannot produce documentation for any of them.

Why is that? Most of it was done over the telephone. The accounting system we spoke to him about and somebody from his office, and I do not know who at this stage because I cannot remember, but somebody from his office came down and showed us how we should be doing it, but most of our communication with Mr. Moll was by telephone."

In cross-examination it emerged that the request for advice concerning the purchase of the unit appears to have occurred in or around April 1979.

In her evidence the Applicant's wife said she spoke to Mr. Moll "about queries within the medical practice, queries about accounting, advice on investments of money and buying property". Her evidence in chief continued:

"Can you give some specific examples of the questions that you asked of Mr. Moll?---Well, I asked questions of Mr. Moll about buying office equipment - replacing typewriters and adding machines, those sorts of things. He gave me advice on bookkeeping. I think before Mr. Moll was around, the accountant that we used had to make do with the cheque stubbs and work his way around that. Mr. Moll organized a day book for me to keep. I asked Mr. Moll about investing moneys for lithe Applicant] in order to get a good return. I asked Mr. Moll about investing in property. But, a lot of the things were just general sort of housekeeping queries, I suppose, in management of a business."

The Day Book which was commenced in 1975 apparently on the advice of Mr. Moll, bears no comparison to any day book preceding June 1975 and referred to as existing prior to that date in the evidence of a Senior Investigation Officer.

The impression left by the evidence of the Applicant and his wife was that they were satisfied with the advice which they had received from Mr. Moll and apart from the fact that they would not want to continue with Jason Trading Co. they would be prepared to continue receiving advice from Mr. Moll.

Nevertheless, it is said on behalf of the Respondent that the fee claimed was a very considerable sum in relation to the net profit from the Applicant's practice. The Applicant's gross professional income decreased from $115,625 in 1976 to $75,070 in 1977 to $56,892 in 1978 and marginally rose to $62,583 in 1979. It is said that no person earning that amount would pay out management fees of $9000 every year in addition to the fees already allowed in respect of MANS. Furthermore, the practice did not need much managing in any event because it was a single surgery, suburban practice. In the view of the Tribunal these factors may well show that the Applicant received a poor commercial bargain in that the return for the amount paid was relatively insubstantial. It does not result in the Tribunal considering that the Applicant's evidence of the services received in return for the payments made should not be accepted.

Nor does the Tribunal accept the Respondent's submission there is a lack of credibility in the evidence of the Applicant's wife.

Having considered the evidence and argument in relation to these matters the Tribunal concludes:

(i) that in relation to the 1976 year of income the Applicant cannot succeed as to $3000 of the amount claimed for management fees in this year of income because the payment of that amount has not been established and has in any event ( only established payment of $5500;

(ii) nor can the Applicant succeed in relation to the management fees in the 1975 year of income where again payment has not been established;

(iii) the Applicant cannot succeed in his claim for a deduction in relation to that portion of the remaining management fees in issue as relates to management advice concerning investments producing assessable income in the name of a person other than himself, namely his children: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 at 170. While the relative proportion of management advice in relation to such investments and in relation to the Applicant's medical practice was not addressed in evidence in detailed terms, the conclusion to which the available evidence leads the Tribunal is that it is appropriate to regard the advice as given equally between those two objectives. Accordingly, the Applicant cannot succeed in relation to 50% of the remaining amount of fees sought by way of deduction;

(iv) with regard to the remaining 50% of management fees, it is apparent that they were also incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income not only of the Applicant but also of the Applicant's partner as from 1 January 1977. From that date to the close of the 1978 year of income the Applicant is therefore only entitled to a deduction in respect of one half of that remaining 50%, the assessable income of the practice having been shared equally by the Applicant and his partner.

1975 year of income

Passing reference was made on behalf of the Respondent to the possible application of section 260 to the Moll contract and the arrangement which it represented. The matter was not argued in any detail and the Tribunal thinks correctly so. On the evidence before the Tribunal the arrangement is not one which bears on its face an indication of a purpose to avoid tax. It is fairly referable to ordinary business dealing.

Losses on trading

In the 1978 year of income the Applicant claims a deduction in respect of a loss on diamond trading. Carry forward losses are also claimed in the 1979 year of income.

There are two documents central to this aspect of the Applicant's case. The first is a partnership agreement between the Applicant and his wife constituting them as a trading partnership ("Jason Trading Co."). In that agreement it was recited that the partners had, since 1 January 1977, carried on the business of dealers in various commodities including particularly diamonds.

The other agreement ("the Mobit contract") is one between the Applicant and his wife on behalf of Jason Trading Co. and Mobit (W.A.) Pty. Ltd. (a company controlled by Mr. Moll) whereby the latter is employed as the manager of the former from 1 July 1977. Clause 1 of the Mobit contract states that Mobit "has been" so employed "to and including the 30th day of June 1978". The Respondent contends that is the limit of the appointment. The Applicant contends that the contract is effective beyond that date.

Clause 3 of the Mobit contract provided for a fee to be paid to the manager for the services during the term of the agreement. The amount provided for was $90,000. Although it was as between the Applicant and the Respondent at one time in issue as to whether this amount had actually been paid by the Applicant, from the evidence at the hearing including particularly the bank statement and cheque tendered at the conclusion of the Applicant's wife's evidence, the Tribunal is satisfied that fee was in fact paid.

There are a number of unusual features concerning the partnership. Copies of the partnership agreement in the documents before the Tribunal show the agreement to be undated but stamped on 2 December 1977. Despite the recital that the parties have been carrying on the partnership since 1 January 1977, the partnership did not file a return for that year. The certificate of registration of the business name was dated 2 December 1977. Legal fees in respect of the agreement appear to have been paid in January 1978. The Tribunal therefore accepts the Respondent's submission that it is a reasonable inference that the partnership agreement was executed in December 1977 and backdated to January 1977.

Clause 5 of the agreement states that the capital and assets of the partnership as at the date of commencement constituted $10,000 contributed as to 90% by the Applicant and 10% by his wife. The Applicant's evidence was that he did not know where this capital was paid so that there is no evidence of the contribution of capital.

In addition, the partnership operated no bank account.

The Applicant did not see any books or records of the diamond (trading partnership. His position was stated as follows:

"... in 1978 and 1979 what was your belief as to the diamond trading partnership?----I believed that diamonds were being traded on my behalf by Mobit who were managing the diamond trading partnership for us - for me."

The Applicant stated the basis of his belief was because his auditor said that that was so. The Applicant admitted that neither Mr. Moll nor any person in his organisation consulted the Applicant prior to the alleged sale of a large quantity of diamonds in one parcel nor indeed prior to the purchase of the diamonds. The Applicant was asked:

"And you have no knowledge personally whether these purchases or sales were ever, in fact, made is that right?---That is right, yes."

At the same page the Applicant confirmed that neither he nor his wife had the funds to pay for the diamonds and neither he nor his wife ever made arrangements to be provided with credit to the extent required for such alleged sales and purchases. Neither did the Applicant know where the diamonds were when they were bought or where they were when they were sold. The Applicant was given the opportunity to state whether the diamonds were ever insured or whether storage charges were ever incurred. He stated he had no knowledge of either type of expense being incurred.

No records have been produced showing payment for the diamonds when they were allegedly purchased or receipt of funds when they were allegedly sold and no stock records have been produced. No valuation certificates have been produced.

It is in the context of these facts that two alleged purchase invoices and two alleged sale invoices must be assessed. It is to be noted that this is a one-off transaction. The Applicant was cross-examined as to the circumstances in which he came to write the word "approved" on the invoices and to sign them. His evidence was that he could not recall the individual circumstances for signing any of the documents because he did not have time to read any of them.

Examination of the invoices suggests they originated from Hong Kong. The evidence of the printer raised the possibility, without establishing it as a probability, that Mr. Moll had had letterheads printed in Western Australia for use in this manner. The diamond merchant's evidence established that. the invoices confused terminology of at least two different types of diamond grading systems.

Furthermore, in evidence the Applicant said that the $90,000 fee had been assumed by him to be required to buy stock in trade. The transcript shows the following exchange, among others, on this point:

"How was the $90,000 calculated?---It was the amount which he asked - that he would need to set up a diamond dealing company, and do the dealing on our behalf. I am not aware of how it was calculated. Well, what was it for - was it for the stock in trade, when you --?--I believed it was for the stock in trade. Well, that is a bit different from a service fee is not it?---It is very different to a service fee. I assumed later that it had been called a service fee, for reasons - what, because it was to my advantage to call it such. I trusted the man, I had no reason to question his every action. It was to your advantage to call it at such, before it would be a tax deduction, is that the reason?---I do not know. At the time, I did not know. As the years have gone by, I realise that that may well have been the advantage - may well have been the reason.

But, at the time, I think you said a moment ago, it was to your advantage to sign it, is that right? I signed it because I believed it was worded this way, because it would be to my advantage to word it that way. The advantage being, the tax deductibility of the $90,000? — I was not aware of that at the time. What other advantage,... would there have been? -- I do not know. There is no other advantage, is there, other than the tax deductibility of the fee? — I now know that there is no other advantage."

The Applicant's wife in her evidence said the payment of $90,000 was made to Mobit for the setting up of the entity which was to trade in antiques and jewellery. All this leads to the conclusion that the sum of $90,000 was indeed initially represented by Mr. Moll as the moneys required to purchase commodities and that the execution of the documentation was designed to characterize the payment of the $90,000 as a deduction.

Clause 7 of the Jason Trading Co. partnership agreement provided that the partners would share the net profits as to 9/10ths to the Applicant and as to 1/10th to his wife. The Applicant as a medical practitioner was paying tax at a considerably higher marginal rate than his wife. The result would be that if the partnership had made a profit, he would have been paying substantially more tax than would have been the case if his wife had earned a bigger share of any income from it. The arrangement would only make sense from a business point of view if the partnership was destined to make a loss so that the Applicant would benefit principally from it.

The case for the Applicant was not that he and his wife themselves traded in diamonds or antiques but that they retained Mobit to do that and were reassured that diamond trading was actually taking place by conversations with other doctors who said they had seen diamonds; by the invoices produced to the Applicant for signature; and because the partnership accounts were audited by a reputable firm of accountants.

Having considered the evidence and the arguments made in relation to it, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant has not succeeded in establishing that diamond trading actually took place. This view is not dependent on any argument relating to sham or to section 260 of the Act. It is more simply based on the Tribunal's view that the Applicant has failed to discharge the onus upon him of establishing that the loss was one which was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income. In finding that the Applicant has not established that there was diamond trading, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant has not established there was a business carried on although, at the time, he may have believed that it was. The consequence is that the loss cannot qualify for deduction under section 51. cf. The Birmingham and District Cattle By-Products Co. Ltd. v. Commissioner for Inland Revenue (1919) 12 TC 92.

Discount charges

The claims for deduction by the Applicant in respect of discount charges are for such charges paid on promissory notes taken out with Bill Discount Services Pty. Ltd., a company generally described as in the Moll group of companies. The funds raised by the notes were used for two purposes. The first bill was raised on 1 August 1975 and was for $7000. It was used to repay short term finance with the Bank of New South Wales which had financed the Applicant's payment to C.T. Moll & Co. of the first management fees pursuant to the Moll agreement. The second was for an amount of $90,000 raised on 23 February 1977 for Jason Trading Co. The funds were initially put on deposit with Mobit but were later journalised as a management fee.

The first bill was repaid in full in 1976. In relation to the second amount the Applicant's evidence was that he intended paying out the bills once Jason Trading Co. started making profits. When no profits emerged that did not happen and, in any event, the rate at which the bills inflated made such repayment impossible.

On behalf of the Applicant it is argued that if the $90,000 was not paid by way of management fee but rather by way of contribution to working capital, the discount charges paid on promissory notes issued for the purpose of raising that working capital would be a deduction. The finding of the Tribunal that the Applicant has failed to discharge the onus to establish the business was being carried on, however, forecloses that argument. There was no business in relation to which the outgoing can become ( a deduction on any count.

That, however, is not the case in relation to discount charges relating to the first bill and in respect of that it will be directed, consistently with the findings of the Tribunal in relation to management fees, that a deduction be allowed. This would appear to be in the sum of $401 (being 2750 x 802)

5500

Legal costs

The amount of $92 in issue is claimed as legal costs for the preparation of the Moll contract. Consistently with the Tribunal's findings in relation to management fees it considers that this sum is an outgoing of the Applicant in gaining or producing assessable income.

Additional tax

- 1978 year of income

Additional tax was imposed in this year pursuant to former sub-section 226(1) for failure to "duly furnish as and when required" pursuant to section 264 sufficient evidence and detail for the determination of his claims for management fees, discount charges and the loss from Jason Trading Co. As part of the record of interview of the Applicant on the 9th April 1977 he was asked to provide an itemised schedule showing the separate amounts of expenditure comprising his claims of $13,277 in 1975, $27,824 in 1976, $26,907 in 1977 and $32,316 in 1978. The hand-written notation reads:

"Mr. [investigator] advised that from past experience an itemised schedule would not be produced. [The Applicant] undertook to obtain the required info."

In his report the senior investigation officer noted:

"It was requested that [the Applicant] obtain and produce an itemised list of payments made by MANS. His solicitor has advised that he is still attempting to obtain this information but has been unsuccessful to date. In the absence of these details and in accordance with the A.D.C.'s instructions, 50% of the claim has been disallowed."

The recommendation was approved.

Cross-examination of the senior investigation officer concerning these matters was as follows:

"Reading through whilst standing before you now, the only question that I - or the requirement that I could see that [the Applicant] provide further information was question 22 concerning the list of payments made by MANS or made to MANS. Is that correct?---It would appear so.

And the question asked there was - in 22 was for amounts comprising the claim to $13,000, in other words the payments by [the Applicant] to MANS. Is that correct? Yes.

[The Applicant] in that question was not asked to explain the amounts made by MANS to anyone else. Is that correct? That is right.

And so your conclusion at folio 246 - sorry, your assertion, your statement that, "[the Applicant] obtain and produce an itemised list of payments made by MANS", is not correct? Just trying to recall whether we – I am positive that [the Applicant] was asked to obtain that information although it is not written there as a question and answer.

So these notes are not a full record?---Well, the notes are full as they go but he was requested to produce this information. But it does not form part of the question and answer schedule that is here.

Can you recall when you made that request? --- During this - well - - -

You are supposing though, are you? --- Well, I am, yes.

And is it fair to say that these events happened such a long time ago that you have no independent recollection of the interview with [the Applicant] conducted 9 April 1977?---I can recall the majority of it but I would not - to have a recall or not."

The interview took place on 10 April 1979. On 14 May 1979 the section 264 interview was formally terminated.

The relevant time at which the imposition of penalty is to be first decided is when there has been a failure to comply with the requirement to furnish information. The Tribunal is now re-exercising the power so that it is appropriate for it to consider facts emerging to the date of the hearing. A further interview was conducted with another senior investigation officer on 22 May 1980. In cross-examination that officer said the Applicant had provided him with all his practice records and as far as he was aware had provided him with the information in his possession. Although he was never satisfied that he had all the information, he was not aware of whether the Applicant had any more to give him and he believed the Applicant answered the questions put to him honestly.

The Tribunal is not satisfied that there was in fact a failure to disclose by the Applicant. Firstly, this is so because it is not clear even in retrospect with all the documents and evidence before the Tribunal, precisely what it was that the Applicant was asked to disclose. The record of cross-examination of the senior investigation officer bears this out.

Secondly, if the Applicant was asked to disclose information of expenditure by MANS then that information would have been available to the Respondent through the records and returns filed by members of the Moll group of companies. The Tribunal considers that the argument of the Applicant to the following effect, is applicable on this point: there is no failure on the part of a taxpayer if he does not tell the Respondent something which the Respondent already knows: Foster v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1951) 82 CLR 606 at 615, 619, and the Respondent had knowledge of all relevant details, from the books records and tax returns filed by MANS, C.T. Moll & Co., and other members of the Moll group. Alternatively, the lack of any detail by the Applicant at the time that he lodged his assessment as to the breakdown of the application of funds by. MANS was an omission to do something for which the Applicant was not responsible, and that therefore the Applicant had not "failed" to provide the information requested: Ganke v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4097 at 4101.

In the opinion of the Tribunal, the additional tax in the 1978 year of income should be fully remitted.

- 1975, 1976, 1977 and 1979 years of income

In these years of income additional tax has been imposed pursuant to former section 226(2) of the Act on the basis that the Applicant included in returns for each of those years, amounts by way of deductions for expenditure on management fees and discount charges "in excess of the expenditure actually incurred by him". The Respondent's contention is that even if the payments were actually made (which the Respondent denies) the outgoings were not "incurred" within the meanings of sections 51, 64A and 67 of the Act because the transactions giving rise to the outgoings were shams. The Respondent relies upon what was said in relation to shams in Snook v. London & West Riding Investments Ltd. (1967) 1 All ER 518 at 528 per Diplock L.J.; Albion Hotel Pty. Limited v. Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 4; (1965) 115 CLR 78 at 92 per Windeyer J.? Scott v. Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) (1966) 40 ALJR 265 at 279 per Windeyer J.; Mahony v. Commissioner of Taxation (1967) 41 ALJR 232 at 237 per Windeyer J. ; and Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1961) 8 AITR 328 at 347 per Windeyer J.

It should be added that it is not the Respondent's case that the additional tax in these years of income should be imposed because he considers the amount paid by the Applicant in respect of management fees or discount charges was excessive - cf. Magna Alloys and Research Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4542 at 4557 and 4549; and Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1948) 78 CLR 47 at 60; nor is the Respondent arguing for imposition of the tax on the ground that the taxpayer could be said to have wrongly categorised gain as not being income: North Coast Grazing Pty. Limited. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4553; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Rabinov 83 ATC 4437; Cyprus Mines Corporation v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 78 ATC 4468 at 4486.

The Tribunal is not satisfied on the evidence in this proceeding that it has been established that there was any mutual understanding between the parties to the contracts and agreements that they would not have any binding effect; that is, the evidence does not show that it was intended by all the parties that the documents concerned would give the appearance of certain rights and obligations different from those they intended to create. There is an entire absence of evidence on behalf of the parties with whom the Applicant and his wife contracted. Their evidence supports the view that they entered into transactions because they believed in and relied upon advice given to them by Mr. Moll. The necessary mutuality to support a finding of sham as submitted by the Respondent is not established.

It follows from what was said earlier in these reasons concerning management fees and discount charges that the Tribunal accepts that expenditure on such fees and charges was incurred by the Applicant. The deductions were claimed for the amounts so incurred. Those claims were therefore not in excess of the expenditure actually incurred so that there should be further remission of the remaining additional tax imposed under the provisions of sub-section 226(2) in the amended assessments for the 1975, 1976, 1977 and 1979 years of income.

In view of this conclusion it is not necessary for the Tribunal to consider arguments addressed to the power of the Respondent to amend the assessment under the provisions of sub-section 170(2).

Evidence in relation to other medical practitioners During the hearing counsel for the Respondent sought to call evidence from 6 medical practitioners who were said to have entered into contractual arrangements with Mr. Moll. Specifically, there was contention on behalf of the Respondent that it was a standard feature of the arrangements between Mr. Moll and medical practitioners that there would be some reimbursement of cash or other benefit in respect of management fees paid and that even if Mr. Moll had not honoured his commitments to the Applicant in this respect, the evidence should be called in order to establish the probability that such an arrangement existed in the case of the Applicant. The Respondent sought to call the evidence of the selected doctors because it was said to point to a systematic pattern of arrangements fitted into by a localised class of taxpayers - namely medical practitioners - for the same end and the Applicant was a member of that class: Martin v. Osborne [1936] HCA 23; (1936) 55 CLR 367 at 392 per Evatt J. relied on. ) The Respondent was unable to find an instance in reported Board of Review, Administrative Appeals Tribunal or other tax decisions in which the use of such evidence may. have been reviewed and referred to J.R.S. Forbes Similar Facts (1987) p. 169 (Ch. 8 - Similar

Facts in Civil Cases).

The Applicant, in reliance on Makin v. Attorney-General for N.S.W. C18943 AC 57; Martin v. Osborne (supra) at 367, particularly per Dixon J. at 375; and Peet & Co. Ltd. v. Rocci [1985] WAR 164 at 173-176 per Rowland J., submitted that the knowledge of the other 6 medical practitioners was not logically probative of the Applicant's arrangements with Mr. Moll. It was said that if the material was adduced it would not tend logically to show the existence or non-existence of facts relevant to the issue to be determined: cf. Re Pochi v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1979] AATA 64; (1979) 26 ALR 247 and 256.

Paragraph 33(1)(c) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 provides that the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence but may inform itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate. Having considered the above submissions the Tribunal does not consider it appropriate that it should hear the evidence from the 6 medical practitioners. Such evidence would not clearly be of logically probative value. It would attenuate these proceedings considerably. It would be evidence which would not disclose how many other medical practitioners have been asked to make similar statements but refused to do so or made statements that were inconsistent. If the evidence is called it could lead to the Applicant seeking leave to call all such other medical practitioners. In the opinion of the Tribunal neither the evidentiary requirements of this proceeding nor the proper management of it by the Tribunal require or justify the Tribunal in hearing such evidence.
