Re "R" and Commissioner of Superannuation [1983] AATA 386 (22 November 1983)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: "R"

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V82/434

Superannuation

COURT

IN THE ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISON

Mr I.R. Thompson (Senior Member)

Dr H.W. Garlick (Member)

Mr W.B. Tickle (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - applicant retired from Public Service on grounds of invalidity - whether health so restored as to enable her to perform duties of a kind suitable to be performed by her

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.74, 75, 154

Re Stoddart and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. A82/26: decided on 21 December 1982)

HEARING

CANBERRA

22:11:1983

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

1. The applicant is a married woman aged 36 years. She was born in Sri Lanka and is coloured. She entered the Commonwealth Public Service in August 1965 and became a permanent employee in 1966. From the start of her service until May 1979 she was employed in the Department of Supply, now the Department of Administrative Services. Over the years she was promoted to the grade of Clerk Class 2/3 but for long periods she was acting as a Clerk Class 4 and occasionally as a Clerk Class 5. On 11 May 1979 she was retired from the Public Service on the ground of invalidity and consequently became entitled to invalidity benefit under the provisions of Division 4 of Part V of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). Her retirement followed an examination of her by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr A. Kenneally, on 3 May 1979. He considered that she was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity. The condition from which she was suffering was stated by Dr Kenneally to be anxiety and depression, and the percentage which they contributed to the incapacity 100 per cent. Dr Kenneally, however, recommended that a review of her condition should be carried out after one year.

2. In June 1981 the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") exercised his powers under section 74 of the Act to require the applicant to submit herself for medical examination by a medical practitioner. On 29 June 1981 she was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr R. D. Wright, who considered that she was fit to resume her former duties. On 17 November 1981 a delegate of the Commissioner for the purposes of section 75(1) of the Act decided that her health had become so restored as to enable her to perform the duties of Clerk and he directed that the Public Service Board be informed of that with a view to finding her suitable employment. The reason given by the Commissioner's delegate for his decision was in the following terms:- "In arriving at the above decision I have had regard to the recommendation of the Commonwealth Medical Officer: That "R" is fit to resume the duties of Clerk."

3. The provisions of section 75 of the Act are as follows:-

"75. (1) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, after receiving the report or reports of a medical practitioner or medical practitioners with respect to the health of a person to whom invalidity pension is payable, that the health of the person has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are, in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant), the Commissioner shall so inform such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner.

(2) If -

(a) the pensioner is offered by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority employment (not being employment on a part-time basis) that involves the performance of duties that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, are suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant); and

(b) the person unreasonably refuses or fails to accept the offer within 14 days after the receipt by him of the offer, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows,

the Commissioner may cancel the person's entitlement to invalidity pension."

4. The applicant was notified of the Commissioner's delegate's decision by a letter of the same date; on 14 December 1981 her solicitors requested the Commissioner to reconsider the decision. The Commissioner did so, in pursuance of section 154(4) of the Act, and confirmed it on 1 November 1982. Application was then made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal; in the application the decision to be reviewed was stated to be "the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made on 1 November 1982 confirming a decision made on 17/11/81 that I am medically unfit (sic) to return to work". The reasons for the application were stated as being that "the decision of the Commissioner was wrong in that it was against the weight of the evidence in this matter. Section 154(6) of the Act, so far as relevant, provides:-

" (6) Applications may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or varied under sub-section (4) . . . . "

It is clear, therefore, that the decision which the Tribunal is empowered to review is that made by the Commissioner's delegate on 17 November 1981. We have no power to review the decision in respect of which the application was stated to be made but we have treated the application as being for review of the decision of the delegate.

5. The documentary evidence which has been placed before us includes medical reports by Dr Kenneally dated 1 May 1979, by Dr Wright dated 29 June 1981, by Dr M. W. Thompson dated 9 December 1981, by Dr L. R. Turecek dated 16 February 1982 and 18 February 1983, by Dr B. J. Taylor dated 30 April 1982 and 29 September 1982, by Dr M. B. Gayner dated 17 January 1979 and 17 January 1983, by Dr N. J. Sist dated 16 February 1983 and by Dr P. H. Cohen dated 20 May 1983. Included also are a report by a physiotherapist, Mr G. J. Reid, dated 8 July 1983, the applicant's sick leave record from October 1966 to May 1979, clinical notes by Dr Wright dated 29 June 1981 and job specifications for a number of Public Service posts occupied by Clerks of various grades. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, and by Dr Sist, Dr Gayner, Dr Thompson, Dr Turecek and Dr Cohen, all called by the applicant, and by Dr Taylor and Dr Wright, both called by the respondent.

6. Evidence of the circumstances in which the applicant was retired on the grounds of invalidity was given by the applicant herself, by Dr Gayner and by Dr Sist. The reports of Dr Gayner, Dr Sist and Dr Kenneally are also relevant to that matter, as are the contents of the applicant's sick leave record. The applicant gave evidence that between 1976 and 1979 she was doing the work of a Clerk Class 4 and sometimes of a Clerk Class 5. She said that she would get in to work at about 8.30 a.m. and finish at about 6.00 p.m. on some nights. About twice a week on average she would take work home with her and spend "a couple of hours" working at home each time. She said that the pressure of work and the apparent lack of appreciation of her efforts by her senior officers, which was manifested by the fact that she remained substantively a Clerk Class 2/3 although acting in higher grades, and by the fact that a senior officer told her that in her circumstances she should be at home with her family and not a member of the work force, played a significant part in causing her to suffer the mental condition which rendered her unfit for continued employment. She said that even before 1976 she was of a slightly nervous disposition. She had suffered slights on account of her colour since she came to Australia as a child. From 1976 onwards she had been badly affected by the fact that her husband, who had been employed in Medibank, had been retired on the grounds of invalidity. She said that during the three-year period before 11 May 1979 she suffered from headaches and insomnia and experienced feelings of depression and weeping. She was irritable with her children and chastized them even before she had found out whether they were doing anything wrong. She said that she lost her appetite, did not eat very much and as a result lost weight.

7. Her sick leave records show that in November 1974, January, June and August 1975 she was absent from work for periods of up to one day due to migraine. In August 1975 she was absent from work for one week for the same reason. In October 1975 she was absent for one day because of migraine, in November 1976 for two consecutive days because of tension headache and in September 1975 for two separate days because of migraine. The first reference to any possible psychiatric problem is to "nerve trouble" in March 1979. It appears from the sick leave record that, at least insofar as incapacitating headaches were concerned, her health was worse in 1974 and 1975 than during the three years immediately preceding her retirement.

8. Dr Sist gave evidence that he first saw the applicant on 9 March 1979. She had been seen by his partner once in 1975 when she was suffering from migraine, twice in 1976 when she was suffering from a pain in her chest, and once in 1978 when she had a muscular problem. He stated that, when he saw her on 9 March 1979, she told him that she was suffering from migraine, was "feeling uptight" and was not sleeping, and that she felt that she was "cracking up" and could not cope with work. He gave a medical certificate for absence from work for two weeks and saw her again after one week. As she told him that she was no better and could not cope with her work, he referred her to Dr Gayner, who is a specialist psychiatrist. He said that he had not seen her again after that although he had received a report on her from Dr Gayner. That report contained the statement that:-

"She made no secret of the fact that she was not really seeking psychiatric treatment but rather medical support in her objective of obtaining release from her job on the grounds of her health. If successful she would secure full pension rights".

Subsequently Dr Sist received a further report from Dr Gayner in which Dr Gayner commented "I had, as I remarked to you, considerable reservations about her bona fides in this matter".

9. Dr Gayner in his evidence before us confirmed that those were the opinions which he held at the time when he wrote those reports to Dr Sist. He said that the applicant complained to him that:-

"She had been depressed for five or six months and that she had suffered migraine headaches two or three times a week. She had been two weeks off work from the previous Thursday, which was about four days before I saw her. She told me that she'd been working for 14 years in a Commonwealth Government Department and that she had two children and that her husband had been sick on and off. Her husband had been superannuated out of his job on the grounds of bad nerves - these are her words - arthritis and blood pressure. She told me there had been many arguments and rows at home and he felt humiliated by being at home. He was very irritable. She told me at her place at work they had animosity for female clerks, the promotion was very slow to come and she could not face a return to work. She hoped to get superannuated out and she was seeking my support. She felt depressed, she told me, she could not sleep at night . . . she further talked about broken nights' sleep, her appetite was poor and a weight loss of half a stone in the last month had occurred. She also said that she did not have much energy."

10. Dr Gayner said that he noted that there was no overt evidence of an anxiety neurosis or a depressive illness. In his opinion she did not have what he would describe as a psychiatric illness per se. He accepted that she probably had headaches and she felt unhappy and tense; but that fell short of a classical depressive illness or any other major form of psychiatric illness. However, he felt "taking what she had told me at face value it was in the interests of her health to have a period of time off work" and he certified accordingly. He also prescribed Tryptanol tablets, an antidepressant medication. He said that he first saw her on 3 April 1979 and saw her twice thereafter, on 17 April and 8 May of that year. Since then he had had no further contact with her. He had been surprised to learn on 8 May 1979 that she had been "superannuated out of the service" on medical grounds. He had expected that such a decision would not be made before she had received a reasonable trial of psychiatric treatment and had been given some months of sick leave. Commenting on the reports of Dr Turecek and Dr Cohen he said that he did not find in them much convincing evidence of an underlying psychiatric condition. He expressed the opinion that, if she were to return to a more supportive work environment and could be reassured that she was not going to lose her superannuation entitlements, it was probably possible for her to function in the work force again.

11. Dr Kenneally recorded in his report on the applicant that:-

"Following retirement of her husband - a neurotic person with a chronic back pain and that with two children of school age - she developed symptoms of anxiety, anorexia nervosa and depression. She reacted predictably - inability to cope with allotted duties at work - and as 'defence mechanism' developed described conditions" (i.e. her anxiety and depression).

His prognosis was "probably pessimistic - what with domestic situation, inability to concentrate etc."

12. After she was retired on the grounds of invalidity the applicant did not seek medical advice or treatment, except in connection with a pregnancy which resulted in the birth of her third child on 6 August 1980, until after she had received a letter from the Commissioner at the end of May 1981 informing her that he wished to review her invalidity pension in accordance with his powers under section 74(1)(a) of the Act and asking her to arrange with a Commonwealth Medical Officer a suitable time for a medical examination. That medical examination was arranged for 29 June 1981. On 25 June 1981 she went to a general practitioner to whom she had never been before, Dr M. W. Thompson. She complained to him that she was having difficulty sleeping, that she was waking during the night, that she suffered from tension headaches and that her appetite was poor. The doctor regarded those problems as mainly symptoms of anxiety and depression and prescribed a mild sleeping tablet. He said that he was "a little bit concerned about her because of the severity of the symptoms she was complaining of and I had her ring me" (on 26 June) "just to say how she felt over that night having taken something to help her sleep". On 2 July 1981 he saw her again to reassess her condition. He recorded then that she was less depressed, sleeping better, obviously not coping with her mother-in-law, had had an examination by the Commonwealth Medical Officer the previous week "and, query, is being pressured to return to work". He felt that she had mild depression and started her then on Tryptanol. However, the dose which he prescribed, he said, 20 mg at night, was an extremely low dose and clearly indicates that, as he stated, he regarded the depression as mild. He did not thereafter see the applicant again until 7 December 1981.

13. On 29 June 1981 Dr Wright examined the applicant. Dr Wright is working now at the Royal Park Psychiatric Hospital and had worked there in 1975 and 1976. After that she had been a Psychiatric Registrar at the Royal Children's Hospital for a year and afterwards at the Alfred Hospital and Mont Park Psychiatric Hospital. However, from early 1980 until October 1982 she was a Commonwealth Medical Officer. In her report dated 29 June 1981 she recorded that the applicant was able to articulate clearly the specific problems concerning her work, and that there was no current objective evidence of depression. The doctor considered that the applicant's condition had improved and that she was fit to resume her former duties. At the hearing of the application in these proceedings Dr Wright gave evidence of the manner in which she conducted the examination and the reasons why she reached the conclusions which she did. She also accepted as accurate a typed transcript of clinical notes which she made at the time of the examination. Those notes read:-

"This 34 y.o. lass lives with her spouse - who has been superannuated from Public Service on the grounds of ill health (arthritis and hypertension) and their three children. Although she claims to continue insomnia plus weeping bouts, she has actually gained four kilo (on Tryptanol 50 mg nocte). There is no evidence of suicidal ideation nor of psychomotor retardation. She gives every evidence of coping well with the housework - cooking, shopping and cleaning."

(We note the fact that by 29 June 1981 Dr Thompson had not yet prescribed Tryptanol for the applicant. The last time it had been prescribed for her was in April 1979 by Dr Gayner.)

14. Dr Wright gave evidence that she based her assessment of the applicant's mental state on a modification of the Hamilton rating scales which she said was the scale most widely used for assessing depression and was one with which she was well acquainted as a result of her research study. She distinguished some things which were indicia of depression but also occurred in other everyday circumstances from things which were not normally encountered if there was no depression. For instance, she said, insomnia was one of the indicia of depression but many mothers of small children woke up in the middle night whether or not their children had woken up and that had nothing to do with depression. By contrast, suicidal ideation and psychomotor retardation, she said, were two indicia of depression which did not occur in healthy people. By psychomotor retardation she said that she meant a slowing of mental and bodily processes. A person who was not suffering psychomotor retardation might display signs of a slowing of mental and bodily processes either consciously or unconsciously but the reverse was not possible; if a person had psychomotor retardation, it was not possible for him to speed up his mental and bodily processes. She, therefore, considered it to be of considerable significance that the applicant showed no signs at all of psychomotor retardation. She said that the presence or absence of such signs was ascertainable by skilled observation and that as a result of her previous work she had had considerable experience of carrying out such observation. She said that the applicant showed no evidence at all of suicidal ideation. Under cross-examination she conceded that it was not necessarily of any significance that the applicant was four kilograms heavier than she had been when her weight was last recorded as a pregnancy had occurred in the intervening period.

15. The decision under review was then made on 17 November 1981. It was after the receipt of the letter informing her of it and of its consequences that the applicant returned to Dr Thompson. He saw her again on 7 December 1981 when she told him that she had to return to work in three weeks and was having symptoms of anxiety depression, i.e. insomnia, tossing and turning at night and had fainted on two or three occasions, which were only brief and without convulsions or incontinence. She told him also that she had injured her lower back and he examined her for that. At her request he wrote a letter which was headed "To whom it may concern" but which was intended to be presented by her to the Commonwealth Medical Officer. In that letter, after stating the complaints which she had made to him, he continued:-

"I have prescribed Tryptanol for her symptoms and these have improved but not disappeared. I have also suggested she resume attending the psychiatrist and do something positive to try and cure her problem permanently. (At times she appeared poorly motivated to recover.) The cause of her anxiety and depression is still not apparent to me. I feel it is unlikely to be due to stress from her previous work considering the time that has elapsed since she stopped work. At the same time I felt that given her present state of mind she is unfit to resume her previous job."

16. Dr Thompson gave evidence that because the applicant was not attending him regularly he felt that it was better to have the matter of her condition "sorted out once and for all at that stage by a psychiatrist". He, therefore, asked her on 7 December which psychiatrist had treated her previously. She told him that she was not sure. However, on 9 December, when she went to him to collect the report which he had written for the Commonwealth Medical Officer, she told him that it was Dr Gayner. However, he did not refer her to Dr Gayner but instead referred her to Dr Turecek. He gave evidence that he could not remember whether she was reluctant to go back to Dr Gayner but he had a feeling that she was, that he had certainly discussed with her the possibility of going back to Dr Gayner and that the choice of psychiatrist depended on a lot of factors including the way the patient felt about the previous treating doctor but also the geographical situation of the doctor and the convenience of travelling to see him. He said that he could recollect "a feeling at the time that she was reluctant to go back and see him" (Dr Gayner). In the letter of referral Dr Thompson wrote:-

"She has had superannuation status reviewed. Has now been told to return to work. Symptoms have improved on Tryptanol 25 to 50 milligrams nocte. She has asked to see a psychiatrist. I feel to support her claim for superannuation rather than try to permanently cure her symptoms."

The date on which Dr Thompson referred her to Dr Turecek was, he said, 22 December 1981. That was the last occasion on which he saw her himself.

17. As a result of Dr Thompson's referral Dr Turecek undertook the treatment of the applicant for her psychiatric condition. He saw her first on 4 January 1982. In his report dated 16 February 1982, addressed to the applicant's solicitors, he set out the applicant's history as she had told him of it and her complaints to him. He wrote that she had told him that she had been superannuated out of the Public Service, that "her review had come up in June 1981" and since then her symptoms of anxiety had been much worse. She told him that:-

"In November of 1981 she had received a letter asking her to return to work, but only as a temporary officer on a six month's (sic) trial and in a much junior position than when she was superannuated."

Of her complaints he wrote:-

"She told me that she was still complaining of headaches, that she was not able to sleep, that she felt 'all screwed up inside' and that at times she was feeling tearful and depressed and wondering if it would be 'just as easy to finish it off]' Her headaches were sometimes so severe that she found it necessary to take analgesic tablets although she found little relief from such medication. She also described being irritable with her husband and children, and was especially worried about her tendency to loose (sic) her temper with the children 'I don't care where my hand lands]' Her general practitioner had commenced her on antidepressant medication and she admitted to some improvement whilst on medication, but was considerably depressed about the future and threatened about what might happen to her."

He assessed her as follows:-

"At interview, she presented as a tense woman, rather boyish in hairstyle and personal attire. She was describing symptoms of anxiety and depression and the thought of her future was causing her a considerable degree of distress. She felt that her symptoms were so severe that she would not be able to tolerate the work situation again 'I know I can't face work'. She tended to perceive the work situation as being unsatisfactory and frightening to her. She felt that she was entitled to some type of security of contributing to the superannuation for so many years (sic) and felt that it was unfair to ask her to return as a casual temporary worker. The thought of returning to a lesser position, perhaps working under some of the girls that she was senior to in the past causes considerable distress. Her knowledge that the number of staff have been reduced in the Public Service and the workload increase (sic) at the same time is a further idea that induces considerable tension and distress. Furthermore, as mentioned previously, she finds it almost impossible to adequately meet her responsibility towards her young child. Overall, my opinion was that she was suffering from an anxiety/depression condition and was showing poor tolerance to stress and not coping adequately with her current responsibilities. Because of her symptoms and the specific nature of her situation, I formed the opinion that she would not be able to cope with returning to her previous work situation. I felt that she was in need of specific antidepressant medication and that she needed regular follow up interviews to help her with her current difficulties. In view of the above, I recommend that her superannuation be continued as I am able to confidently predict that her condition would exacerbate dramatically if she was (sic) asked to return to work."

Giving evidence he stated that the contents of that report were an accurate statement of what the applicant told him and of the opinion which he held at the time when he wrote it.

18. Dr Turecek gave evidence that since January 1982 he had been seeing the applicant in his professional capacity at intervals of approximately 4-6 weeks since he first saw her in January 1982. He said that he continued the antidepressant medications which she had been taking before she came to him, that is to say Tryptanol.

19. Cross-examined, Dr Turecek stated that the applicant did not have at present any underlying psychiatric or medical illness which actually prevented her from working. He agreed that she was prone to suffer symptoms, as she had described them to him, in pressure situations at home. He agreed also that a problem was faced by any woman who had to determine whether to go to work or to stay home and look after her children and that one solution to that problem was to resign from the work situation. He said that the applicant had not discussed that possibility with him "in any way to suggest a practical course of action on her part". She was concerned that, if she returned to work and was dismissed as a result of an inability to cope with the work, she would lose all her superannuation benefits. Asked whether her attitude to returning to work had changed following a clarification by the Commissioner of the information provided to her about returning to work, Dr Turecek said that she did not believe the official letters or the official intentions; she felt that, when translated to the actual situation at work, it did not work in that way. Consequently the question of her superannuation was complicating her situation in relation to the return to work.

20. He said that the prospect of going to work was an extra pressure for her apart from the other things that were causing pressure. Asked to comment on whether there was any significance in the fact that she had not been to a doctor on account of symptoms of anxiety or depression between May 1979 and June 1981 Dr Turecek said:-

"I did state that in terms of her psychiatric presentation I do not think we can say that she has got a severe disorder. So may be the nature of the disorder is such that in her mind it does not require specific approaches by the medical profession although we have heard that she does medicate herself: we have heard that she is not responding often to the analgesic tablets that she is taking: we have heard that she puts up with things like sleep disorders, irritability with the children etc."

When it was pointed out that that was a state in which many women would find themselves from time to time, particularly with a 10-year old son who, as the applicant told Dr Gayner was overactive, Dr Turecek commented:-

"It is a matter of degree. How many women would be frightened of hurting their son? How many women would be frightened of actually losing control and fearing that they may hurt their children? It is a question of degree."

He continued:-

"How many women would say it and how many would be at risk of actually doing it?"

Asked whether there was anything to indicate whether she was really at risk of doing it, he conceded that there was nothing to indicate that she was really out of control. He agreed also that the question of work refusal in the context of compensation was a very vexed question and he was not sure that it could be treated in isolation from the compensation case and the superannuation.

21. Dr Turecek admitted that, although the applicant had told him that some of her problems were within her family, he had not at any time spoken to her husband. He described her anxiety and depression as mild but considered that a return to work would exacerbate her condition dramatically. He said that what he was suggesting was that her attitude to going back to work was such that it would bring so much pressure as to cause exacerbation of symptoms. Her condition was not severe at present, when she was on antidepressants. Asked to state with more particularity what effect returning to work would be likely to have on her he said that:-

"If work is seen to be a subjective pressure, then we are going to have the same situation that we had, which caused the original doctor to agree on superannuation."

Asked what her incapacity was as a result of such pressure he replied:-

"Well, the sort of things I saw - and also Dr Gayner mentioned - was the way that she presented at interview which was that she was very miserable. I have seen her, sort of, visibly anxious. I have seen her, sort of, tearful. I have seen her depressed in terms of what Mr Gayner says and I have seen her much more, sort of talking about these things that we pointed just before about 'what is the use of going on' type of statements. So that is what happens to her. Whether you say that that is, you know, mild now but will be severe at the point when the work situation occurs, that is a matter of conjecture. I do not know: it is muddy."

He agreed that returning to a different work environment would be a source of less pressure than returning to her previous one.

22. Having applied to the Commissioner for reconsideration of his delegate's decision, the applicant's solicitors sent to him on 22 February 1982 a copy of Dr Turecek's first medical report, the one dated 16 February 1982. The Commissioner then wrote to the Senior Medical Officer at the Commonwealth Government Centre in Melbourne stating that the applicant was required to be examined by a psychiatrist, who should be asked to provide a report of the examination and to express an opinion as to whether her health had become so restored as to enable her to perform her previous duties or, if such was not the case, what duties might be performed. As a result, on 28 April 1982 she was examined by Dr Taylor, a specialist psychiatrist in private practice.

23. In her report dated 30 April 1982 Dr Taylor noted that the applicant told her that she was too ill to return to work and would not be able to cope with the stress of employment. She noted that the applicant had a rather paranoid attitude to the review of her case and claimed that the Government wanted her to return to work as a temporary employee and would dismiss her without benefits of superannuation after six months. She complained also that she might be employed at a lower classification and on a lesser rate of pay. Dr Taylor stated that, as she had not seen the applicant at the time she was retired, she could not say whether her health had improved or otherwise over the preceding three years but that at the time of the examination she appeared to be suffering from a depressive illness with some anxiety features. She said that she assumed from the medication which Dr Turecek had prescribed that he considered her to be suffering from depression. At that time she had not seen Dr Turecek's report.

24. She said that she "would assess the applicant's depression as being mild to moderate" but there was a likelihood that her symptoms would resolve with continuing treatment and that she should be fit to resume clerical duties similar to those she had previously undertaken. However, she thought that "the situation regarding the consequences of her return to work should be clarified and any mistaken apprehensions corrected". That was eventually done on 6 April 1983, when a letter was sent to the applicant on the Commissioner's behalf reassuring her that it was not intended that she should return to work without the protection of superannuation benefits and clarifying what that protection was. In the meantime in September 1982 the applicant's solicitors authorized the release to Dr Taylor of Dr Turecek's report dated 16 February 1982. Dr Taylor was then asked by the Commissioner to amplify her previous report in the light of the contents of Dr Turecek's report. She did so and commented:-

"I note that Dr Turecek considers that "R" suffers from an anxiety/depression condition, and that a return to work would exacerbate this condition. He bases his opinion partly on the fact that she now has the added responsibility of a young child and is no longer able to rely on the assistance of her mother-in-law. I am not sure that these factors would constitute a basis for being unable to work on grounds of invalidity."

She again expressed the belief that the applicant was medically fit to work. She said that it was possible that her condition might deteriorate if she returned to work but suggested that "this would be due to social pressures rather than a result of any illness".

25. Giving evidence at the hearing of the application Dr Taylor stated her opinion in substantially the same terms. By then she had seen the applicant again a week before the hearing. She said that the applicant was wearing a cervical collar and told her that she had been involved in a motor car accident and sustained a whiplash injury and sciatica and that she was having physiotherapy for those conditions three times a week and taking Naprosyn and Digesic tablets. Otherwise the symptoms she related were much the same as at the time of the examination in April 1982. Dr Taylor said that she asked the applicant whether she understood clearly what was happening now regarding her superannuation situation and the applicant told her "she was a bit unclear". The doctor said she asked her if she had received the letter and whether it was clear to her; and that the applicant replied that she did not take any notice of the information sent out because she intended to appeal; but then she said that apparently things had been sorted out. Dr Taylor took that to mean that the terms on which she could return to work had been clarified. She regarded the depression from which she considered the applicant to be suffering as a reaction to her situation and not an endogenous depression.

26. Asked about the conflict which women have between their work and their family, she said that probably the commonest reaction was that women decided that they would stay home after they had had their babies and look after them for some time until they felt that they were more able to leave them with somebody. But most women were torn between the idea of continuing on with their work or being at home with their children and they resolved it in one of two ways. Most decided to cease to work for a while and stay home with their child, but some seemed to be able to come to terms with continuing to work, provided that they had adequate social support either from their husbands or from some other persons responsible for looking after the children. She said that she saw that as a normal reaction to the situation.

27. Dr Taylor said that the applicant appeared to have two main concerns. One was the care of her children and how it could be managed and the other was the situation at work. She said that the applicant "did not get the same degree of problems at work". She said that, although the applicant told her that she was doing extra work at the time and needing to take work home, and that she was not getting promotion, when she was questioned further "about how she generally got on at work", she said that she got on well with people and there was not too much of a hassle. The applicant told Dr Taylor that some bosses were sometimes hard to get on with and she felt initially that she had trouble settling into the job because, she had a chip on her shoulder at the start; she had problems with being a woman and because of her colour. But that seemed to have settled down and she generally got on well with the other people towards the end of the time that she was working. However, she found that taking home the work and the reduction of staff were problems. Asked whether the applicant would have the capacity to perform clerical duties in a different department with slightly less responsibility and with safeguards in relation to the retention of her superannuation pension in case she had again to retire through ill health, Dr Taylor expressed the opinion that she would.

28. Dr Taylor's second report dated 29 September 1982 was made available to Dr Turecek and the applicant's solicitors invited him to comment on it. It was in response to that invitation that he wrote his second report dated 18 February 1983. He took issue with her suggestion that, if the applicant's condition deteriorated on her return to her employment, it would be due to social pressures rather than the result of any illness. He said that he agreed with Dr Taylor that her condition might very well deteriorate if she returned to her employment but had difficulty in agreeing that this would be due to social pressures rather than the result of any illness. He was of the opinion that all relevant factors should be considered including her previous state of health, the original reasons for superannuation, changed social circumstances, a need for antidepressant medication at the present stage and her subjective expectation that returning to work would definitely cause further deterioration. He, therefore, reiterated his original view that her condition might very well exacerbate dramatically if she were asked to return to work in the near future.

29. The applicant's solicitors then arranged for her to be examined by another specialist psychiatrist in private practice, Dr P. H. Cohen. He examined her once, on 16 May 1983. In his report dated 20 May 1983 he gave an account of her condition at the time of the examination as described to him by her. He stated it in the following terms:-

"She has been attending a private psychiatrist, Dr Turecek (every six weeks) for psychotherapy and also is constantly taking medication (Tryptanol 150 mg at night). She describes her appetite as poor and claims a weight loss of approximately one stone in the past eighteen months. She suffers from bad headaches twice a week for which she takes Veganin or other analgesics to get relief. She keeps occupied mostly doing housework and looking after her family, consisting of husband who was invalided out of work five years ago, and three children, all boys aged fourteen, eleven and two years. She says she has no social life and that she avoids crowds whenever possible. She describes her life at home as being 'alright' but says that she 'does her lolly' with the children at times. She says that her husband has improved considerably since she stopped work but that he suffers from arthritis affecting his back, also bad nerves and he also attends a psychiatrist. He does try to help around the home but is very restricted physically, but they do communicate with one another very well and enjoy each other's company and rarely entertain or go out. She says that before she was superannuated she was under a lot of pressure because she had to go and pick the children up from wherever they were and life was very hectic and disorganised and she had very little time to spend relaxing with her family."

30. He recorded that she described her relationship with her husband as being a mutually supportive one and said that she felt that she was much closer to her youngest child than she was to the older ones because she was now able to devote more time to caring for this infant and she enjoyed mothering. He recorded a complaint, which, it appears, she had not previously made to any of the doctors who had examined her, that she was unable to tolerate crowds and could not face travelling to the city regularly in peak hours by train. He continued:-

"She also says she feels that going back to the work situation would be very humiliating for her and she feels she would be unable to cope with such a mundane job as is being offered to her. She says that she has had a lot of hangups here about being dark skinned and found from the time that she arrived here that she was subjected to verbal abuse from strangers at times, and has had inferiority feelings about herself. She says she generally felt very let down about her public service job and at times has become quite depressed when she has dwelt on the past events. She feels that the head of the Contracts Branch in which she was employed was a prejudiced man and says that very few females ever go past Class 6 in that Branch. She claims that on the first day when reporting to work she was questioned about her religion, and was confronted with the opinion that she should not make a career in the Public Service as her children needed her to look after them. She believes that he held her back when she applied for higher positions several times. She is an intelligent, alert and rather proud but defensive young woman who expresses herself very well."

31. Dr Cohen expressed the opinion in his report that the applicant was suffering from a chronic anxiety state with masked depression, the symptoms of which were diminished by the continuous administration of an antidepressant drug. He said that he did not consider that her condition had changed very much from the condition for which she was superannuated about four years previously. She had been able to cope better because she had been relieved of the work pressures and also because she had been having psychiatric treatment of a supportive nature; but she had an invalid husband who was unable to cope with a large degree of responsibility and she had indicated that she had a very limited stress tolerance as evidenced by her irritability towards the children if they squabbled and the need to shut herself away from such hassles. She also had neurotic symptoms of phobia of travelling or being in crowded places and she gave clear evidence of a high level of anxiety during the interview which Dr Cohen conducted with her, evidenced by her constantly fidgeting and chewing her finger nails. He said that significant weight loss and a degree of insomnia had persisted in spite of medication; she was extremely insecure and told him that she believed that if she were to accept the temporary Class 2/3 position offered her, there would be no security of tenure and that she could find herself being dismissed without any rights to superannuation under new public service regulations. Consequently she did not consider that it would be reasonable to expect her to accept the work trial offered under those conditions. He said that he found himself in full agreement with her treating psychiatrist that:-

"In view of her current attitude and her present psychiatric condition I consider any return to her former work situation would be likely to exacerbate her psychiatric illness" (our emphasis).

32. Dr Cohen considered that the applicant would probably improve in her psychiatric condition provided that she could continue under reasonably favourable circumstances but that she would need to remain under psychiatric care and treatment for probably several more years. He agreed with Dr Turecek's comments in his report dated 18 February 1983 that it was impossible to separate out social factors and that one must consider the whole person and her socioeconomic circumstances, including circumstances which had arisen since she was retired. When he gave evidence at the hearing he repeated and amplified that comment. He said that he did not believe that one could separate out the socioeconomic circumstances, or social factors, and that one must consider the person as a whole in his or her environment.

33. He said that the system of psychiatric diagnosis and classification which was now accepted world-wide and was known as the DSM3 classification had what were called four "axes". That meant that one had to consider four separate aspects of the person in the diagnosis and the categories were first a psychiatric diagnosis, second a physical diagnosis if there was in existence any physical disability, third personality factors and fourth the social and environmental influence factors associated with those aspects of the person's situation. He said that psychiatrists now considered all those factors had to be taken into account to obtain an accurate psychiatric assessment. He agreed that the second, third and fourth axes were not any sort of illness and that the major axis was the psychiatric classification. He said that generally one would infer that the setting had an influence on a person's ability to cope but that it could be the reverse and that a person could manipulate his environment in such a way as to cause a disturbance in himself. He agreed with a suggestion put to him by the Tribunal that those four things were really just signposts to look to, in order to try and explain some sort of aberrant behaviour. He said that the concept had only been introduced in the last year or so and was still subject to some controversy, although it had been recognized internationally.

34. With regard to the contribution which the applicant's work situation made towards her condition, Dr Cohen said that it was not so much difficulty in coping with the actual duties which contributed but more her own attitudes towards superiors and the way she felt that they had treated her. He did not see it as being the nature of the work so much as the circumstances and the effect of interpersonal relationships and interaction with other personnel which was the main problem. Asked whether the solution to her illness was simply to retire from a working life he replied:-

"Well I suppose that would certainly be a great help if she were able to remain on a superannuation income . . . but clearly without the superannuation income which she is receiving the family would be in very difficult financial circumstances, I imagine."

When he made that comment he was not aware that the applicant's husband was receiving invalid benefits under the Act but he said that he got the impression from his contact with the applicant that the threat of losing her entitlement to superannuation was something which she felt very concerned about and she did not want to place it in any jeopardy by risking a work trial. He said that that had been a very significant factor in recent times since the question of her being required to have a work trial was put to her but "prior to that she had been superannuated and she had remained in need of some sort of psychiatric treatment . . . for some time before this question came up". We would comment here that it is clear that, when Dr Cohen examined the applicant and formed his opinion as to her condition, he was not fully informed of the facts of her family circumstances or of her medical history. When informed at the hearing that she was not receiving any psychiatric treatment until the matter of her returning to work had been raised, he stated:-

"I would see this as being a significant issue, certainly that there is an added stress then, that, if she should be incapable of coping with work, then she would see the future as being pretty disastrous, I imagine, from the family point of view, particularly as her husband, as she stated to me, was not really capable of coping with too much in the way of responsibility or support and she felt that there was a great deal of responsibility on her shoulders, I feel, as regards looking after the family is concerned."

35. Asked by the applicant's counsel to state the magnitude of her neurosis towards travelling in crowds, Dr Cohen said that he did not know just how severe her phobia was and he did not really establish that in the time he had available to examine her. He said that he gave the diagnosis as being a chronic anxiety state with masked depression and would say that the symptom of phobia was just part of that sort of overall picture. He had simply picked that one particular aspect of her symptomatology out because she mentioned it to him and it would be a situation which would be likely to produce a high level of anxiety state in that particular patient. Asked what he meant by the expression "masked depression", he said that in layman's terms it meant that the depression was not obvious when one had contact with the person even on closer examination. He said:-

"The patient will very often not mention that they are feeling sad, depressed, unhappy or melancholy or anything like that. They may not even be conscious of the existence of any depressive affect."

He said that when he referred to it being "masked" he meant that:-

"If one were to stop the antidepressant medication that she has been taking for many months I would expect that the depressive aspects of her illness would emerge much more clearly."

36. We find some difficulty in appreciating how Dr Cohen made the diagnosis of masked depression in view of the apparent inconsistencies between his description of the nature of the condition and the complaints made by the applicant. Asked whether he had considered the possibility that the applicant might be displaying her symptoms simply because of her desire to maintain her present status as a person receiving invalid benefits under the Act he said:-

"I appreciate . . . that there does appear to be some secondary gain of a financial nature that is being sought by "R" through continuing to use illness behaviour, I suppose, as a reason for not returning to work. I mean this is something that may or may not be a conscious thing on her part. I would see it as being at least to some extent unconscious, you know, not a sort of deliberate deception or pretensive illness. I think we are getting into a difficult area here." (our emphasis)

However, he said her remaining relatively symptom-free after being superannuated was indicative of the fact that she had been able to avoid stresses associated with the work situation but the threat of having to go back to a stressful work situation had exacerbated a condition of chronic anxiety and depression. Asked whether, if the circumstances of her previous employment which had caused her stress were removed, there was any underlying illness which was going to prevent her from doing clerical work, Dr Cohen replied:-

"I do see her illness as being a reaction to stress and primarily it would appear that the reaction has been associated with work stress, but one has also to bear in mind that there are other stresses related to her home situation, her family situation which will continue, I would presume, that is the disability of her husband, the fact that she has to assume a large degree of responsibility towards the raising of the children. So that stress will remain and whether she is capable of coping with that situation plus the additional stress which may result in having to go back to work is something that would have to be carefully weighed up, I think."

37. The opinions of the five doctors with psychiatric expertise whose reports are before us and who have given evidence in these proceedings vary considerably one from another. Their opinions relate to the applicant's condition at different times and the time with which we are particularly concerned is the date on which the decision was made under section 75(1) of the Act, 17 November 1981. Nevertheless, we consider that the inconsistencies are not explicable solely by reason of the different times in relation to which they are expressed. We think that consideration of the events which occurred and of the sequence in which they did so is of assistance in evaluating their evidence. First, the applicant's sick leave record indicates that, in spite of her evidence of a serious deterioration in her health over the three years before her retirement, she was not suffering any seriously incapacitating symptoms of anxiety or depression before March 1979. In that month she went to Dr Sist and for the first time complained to a doctor of the symptoms which have subsequently been diagnosed by a number of the medical witnesses as symptoms of anxiety and depression. She sought referral to a psychiatrist and she made it clear to that psychiatrist, Dr Gayner, that she was not really interested in obtaining treatment for her condition but was principally concerned with getting his support for an application which she intended to make that she should be retired from the public service on the grounds of invalidity. Having attained her objective, she again did not seek any treatment for a period of over two years. It was only when she had received notice that the Commissioner intended to review her condition that she went to Dr Thompson. Even then, after visiting him on two occasions in June and early July 1981, she sought no further treatment from him until December 1981 after she had received a further notice informing her that the Commissioner's delegate was satisfied that her health had been so restored as to enable her to resume work and that the Public Service Board had been asked to contact her when suitable employment became available. We consider it significant also that at that time, when at her request Dr Thompson referred her to a psychiatrist, she was reluctant to return to Dr Gayner.

38. We note that Dr Thompson in his letter of referral of the applicant to Dr Turecek felt impelled to comment "She has asked to see a psychiatrist. I feel to support her claim for superannuation rather than to try to permanently cure her symptoms." It is clear that at that time Dr Thompson had considerable doubts about her bona fides. Dr Gayner, in his letter to Dr Sist and also in his evidence to the Tribunal, expressed similar doubts. While recognizing that her conduct is not necessarily inconsistent with a genuine condition of anxiety and depression, we also have considerable doubt as to her bona fides. We note that, although when she was examined by Dr Wright in June 1981 she had not had Tryptanol prescribed for her since May 1979, Dr Wright noted, apparently as the result of information given to her by the applicant, that she was taking 50 milligrams of Tryptanol each night. We have, therefore, examined the evidence of all the medical witnesses to ascertain to what extent the opinions which they expressed were based either on information provided to them directly by the applicant or on information which, while not necessarily having come directly from her, had either come from her indirectly or was inconsistent with facts which are not in dispute.

39. Normally we should have given considerable weight to the evidence of the psychiatrist who had been treating the applicant since January 1972, in this case Dr Turecek. However, we are not able to do so because we are unfavourably impressed by his standards of practice. We note in particular that, although he had been treating the applicant for a period of nearly three years and he stated that she had had problems within her home during that time, he had never spoken to her husband. When asked to produce to the Tribunal his clinical notes of his consultations with her, all he had were jottings on scraps of paper, a most unsatisfactory system of recording vital information.

40. We have already noted that to some extent Dr Cohen based his original opinion on inaccurate information. In addition to the particular pieces of inaccurate information to which we have already referred, we observe that he based his opinion that the applicant's condition had not changed over a period of about four years in part on "the evidence that she was requiring continuous administration of a substantial dose of an antidepressant in order to be able to continue in a fairly limited way with running a home and looking after her family". In fact she was able to do that without the assistance of any drugs for a period of two years from May 1979 to June 1981. It was clear that Dr Cohen placed considerable reliance on reports of Dr Turecek. We have already expressed our view of the value which we accord to Dr Turecek's opinion. Having given very careful consideration to Dr Cohen's evidence, although we do not doubt his expertise we consider that the opinions which he has expressed regarding the applicant should not be given any great weight because of the unreliability of a considerable part of the data on which they were based.

41. Dr Taylor, who examined the applicant only once for the purpose of providing a report which the Commissioner could use in reconsidering his delegate's decision, appears to have accepted the applicant very much at her face value. We appreciate the difficulty which any doctor has in diagnosing in one short examination any but the clearest organic or pathological conditions. The task of a psychiatrist who has to make such an examination is most difficult. The information available to Dr Taylor was that the applicant had been retired on the grounds of invalidity suffering from anxiety and depression and that she was currently receiving treatment from a psychiatrist and on his advice was taking a substantial dose of Tryptanol. To a considerable extent, therefore, in making her own assessment of the applicant's complaints and of what she herself observed and forming the opinion that the applicant "would appear to be suffering from a depressive illness with some anxiety features", she was implicitly relying on the opinion of Dr Turecek as to the genuineness of those complaints. We do not seek to impugn the method adopted by Dr Taylor in the circumstances. She expressed her opinion with some caution. All we would say is that it does not really provide any substantial support to the applicant's case that she is now, and was in November 1981, suffering from a psychiatric condition of depression and anxiety.

42. Dr Wright was labouring under the same difficulties as Dr Taylor and Dr Cohen when she examined the applicant in 1981. In her case the only indication of another doctor's opinion which she had was that implicit in the information that the applicant was taking 50 milligrams of Tryptanol each night, information which was, in fact, incorrect but from which she might reasonably have inferred that the applicant's treating psychiatrist believed her to suffer from depression. Apart from that, she had to base her opinion on the applicant's account of her history and her complaints and on her own observations. It is probable that Dr Taylor and Dr Cohen also based their opinions to some extent on their observations but they gave no precise account in their evidence of the observations which they had made or of the significance of those observations in the process of the formation of their opinions. By contrast, Dr Wright gave an account of the observations which she had made and of the purpose which they served in enabling her to come to a opinion. Although she is not yet fully qualified as a specialist psychiatrist, she had in 1981 already had considerable experience of psychiatric work as a Psychiatric Registrar at a number of hospitals. We consider that weight should be given to her opinion insofar as it was based on her observations. In particular we accept her evidence that the applicant showed no indication of psychomotor retardation and that, because she did not do so, the likelihood that at the time of her examination by Dr Wright she was suffering from depression, in the proper psychiatric sense of that term, is reduced.

43. The specialist psychiatrist who impressed us most favourably was Dr Gayner. He stated in his report dated 17 January 1983 that, when he first examined the applicant, he considered that she was suffering from a reactive depression and that her job was one stress among others relevant to producing that psychiatric disorder and in consequence he prescribed Tryptanol for her. However, when giving evidence at the hearing, he said that "there was no overt evidence of anxiety or depression, meaning an anxiety neurosis or a depressive illness", and that it was on the basis of taking the applicant at her face value that he had considered it to be in the interests of her health for her to have a period of time off work. In fact he had considerable reservations about whether she should be taken at her face value. He said that it was apparent that Dr Sist also had had some reservations about the symptoms of which she complained. He stated convincing reasons for his own doubts about her bona fides. He said that in the course of his work advising the Victorian State Government he saw quite a lot of applicants for superannuation and in his private practice he saw people with nervous disability allegedly arising out of their jobs; but he could not recall anyone else who had come along quite as emphatically as the applicant and said to him "This is what I want from you, doctor. It is not really treatment that I am interested in; it's your support in this matter." He also found a discrepancy between the way the applicant presented to him, which was as a relatively emphatic and forceful person, and her complaints of inhibitions and anxieties which prevented her from doing her job. She was saying to him, he said, that she was fearful, inhibited and anxious; but his own observation of her was that she was a person who was very emphatically and forcefully telling him what she wanted. He considered that that was significant in evaluating the genuineness of her complaints.

44. Having considered all the evidence which is before us, we have come to the conclusion that the applicant is a person with a somewhat nervous disposition, that she is sensitive about her colour and any slight, real or imagined, which she suffers in respect of it, that she considers that her work was not appreciated and properly rewarded by promotion, that, although without real cause to be so, she has been concerned about the possible effects of a return to work on her superannuation rights, that her husband's invalidity and what she perceives as the overactivity of her second child places some pressure on her, and that she will have difficulties making arrangements for her youngest child to be cared for if her husband is able to obtain work (as he had done on a temporary basis at the time of the hearing) and she herself returns to work. We have no doubt that all those matters have made her unhappy and worried; but we are satisfied on a balance of probabilities that she has not, in May 1979, in November 1981 or at any time before, between or since those dates, suffered from any significant psychiatric condition of depression or anxiety, or any other illness or medical condition (using that term in its broadest sense to include mental condition), such as would incapacitate her for work as a Clerk in the public service. We are quite satisfied that, if she makes satisfactory arrangements for the care of her youngest child and is not placed in a work situation where she will be embarrassed and humiliated by having to work in subordination to officers who were junior to her when she was working previously, and provided that the work is not so stressful that it would cause normal mentally healthy persons to suffer psychiatric disorder, she is capable of returning to work as a Clerk Class 2/3 in the public service and of coping satisfactorily with that work without suffering any psychiatric disorder or other incapacitating illness or medical condition, and that was so on 17 November 1981 also.

45. In view of our findings of fact, it is not necessary for us to consider the submissions which were made to us by Mr Wilde and Mr Austin regarding the extent to which the condition of the applicant after 17 November 1981 may be taken into account in assessing her condition on that date.

46. It is clear from our findings that we have considerable misgivings about the opinion of Dr Kenneally in May 1979 which resulted in the applicant being retired on the grounds of invalidity. However, for the purpose of ascertaining whether or not her health has become so restored as to enable her to perform duties of a kind that are suitable to be performed by her, the criterion which is set by section 75(1) of the Act, we must proceed on the basis that the applicants health in May 1979 was as stated by Dr Kenneally, that is to say that she was suffering from depression and anxiety of such severity that they incapacitated her for work and warranted her being retired on the grounds of invalidity. Because of the findings of fact which we have made, it is not necessary to consider in any detail the meaning of the expression "restored". However, in so far as it is necessary for us to do so we accept fully the reasoning of the Tribunal in re Stoddart and Commissioner for Superannuation (A82/26: decided on 21 December 82) and adopt the meaning stated in the reasons for decision in that case.

47. Accordingly we are satisfied that the decision under review should be affirmed.
