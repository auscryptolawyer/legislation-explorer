ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - whether incurred in course of gaining assessable income - outgoings of a domestic nature rent paid for family home while undertaking postgraduate studies.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s51(1)

FC of T v Green [1950] HCA 20; (1950) 81 CLR 313, 9 ATD 142

Lodge v FC of T [1972] HCA 49; (1972) 128 CLR 171, 72 ATC 4174[1972] HCA 49; , 3 ATR 254

Lunney v FC of T (1958) 100 CLR 478

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

The question at issue in this application is whether the applicant is entitled to a deduction in his assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1983 in respect of rent paid for the temporary family home while the applicant was pursuing a course of study under a scholarship. The amount in question is $3,210.

  1. The only statutory provision raised by the notice of objection is sub-section 51(1).
  2. The applicant's income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1983 described him as a "veterinary surgeon/student". He is married with two children.
  3. In his income tax return for the 1983 year of income the applicant advised the respondent that he was employed by a government department as a veterinary surgeon posted in "A". His employer supplied him with accommodation (for himself and his family at A) for $35 oper week. Given that the return of income disclosed that he was residing at T which is over 200 km from A he went on to make the following statement (edited to maintain confidentiality):

"Applicant was supplied accommodation in A by his employer at $35 per week. He claims the difference between his rent in T and A should be deductible under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act as it was necessary expenses incurred in fulfilling his employers request. No reimbursement will be received by applicant from his employers.

1/7-1/4 2/4-13/4 14/4-30/6

Rent in T 90 100 120

Rent in A 35 35 35

Difference 55 65 85

Total 39 weeks at $55 per week = $2,145

Total 2 weeks at $65 per week = 130

Total 11 weeks at $85 per week = 935

Claim $3,210"

  1. There was no other explanation for the claim in the return. It was repeated word for word in the notice of objection without any reference to the relevant facts necessary to found the basis of the claim. No issue was before me as to whether the grounds of objection were adequate.
  2. The applicant presented his own case before the Tribunal, the respondent appearing through Mr McGill of counsel. The applicant tendered a number of documents and gave oral evidence which put flesh on the very bare bones in his return and notice

of objection.

7. Having resided at A for a number of years in a house supplied by his employer the applicant was invited to apply for a scholarship to study for his masters degree. The scholarship was subsequently granted to the applicant and he enrolled to study at the relevant university.

8. To facilitate the applicant undertaking the university course his employer arranged for him to be replaced at A by another veterinary surgeon and granted the applicant leave without pay from his employment for a period of up to 12 months from 22 February 1982. The leave without pay was granted to enable him to accept State Government Scholarship assistance to undertake the post graduate study leading to the grant of a Master of Science degree from the university at T. Conditions applicable to the approval were:-

(a) during the period of leave of absence the applicant was to be paid an allowance equivalent to his normal salary;

(b) the applicant was to be reimbursed for costs incurred in travelling to T at the commencement of his studies and returning to A at the completion of his studies as well as costs compulsorily incurred in order that the course requirements are met;

(c) the applicant was to enter into an agreement to return to duty in his employment for a period equal to 1-1/2 times the period of the study assistance; and

(d) the period of leave of absence would not be recognised by his employer as service for leave purposes except to a limited extent in respect of long service leave.

9. The applicant subsequently executed an agreement in writing and under seal whereby he bound himself to conditions (a) (b) and (c) outlined above. A manual setting out in some detail the rules relating to the study assistance scheme is in evidence before the Tribunal but it is not necessary for me to expand on the matters mentioned above except to say that it is clear from the document that the applicant was granted leave without pay from his employment but then paid an allowance under the scholarship equivalent to his salary in his employment plus expenses incurred. The expenses which would be reimbursed to him under the terms of the scholarship did not include additional living expenses.

  1. The quantum of the applicant's claim was proved before the Tribunal with documentary evidence and there is no doubt that the expenditure claimed as a deduction was incurred by the applicant. The applicant also established that the two houses he occupied at T with his family while undergoing his studies were, generally speaking, of a standard comparable with the house at A.
  2. Upon completion of the study leave the applicant did not return to A but resumed duty with his employer at T. This seems to have occurred because of 3 factors. The first was the need of his employer to fill his position at A while he was on study leave and therefore it was necessary to transfer the applicant to a position at T. The second reason was that the applicant had not completed the course at the end of the study leave period and he therefore elected to stay in T to complete the degree. The third reason was that an officer located at T had proceeded on study leave for the 1983 calendar year and the applicant took over his duties.

12. Although the applicant had been supplied with a house by his employer at A for a weekly rental of $32.50 (not $35 as stated in his tax return) no such housing was available to the applicant while he and his family lived at T. Furthermore he was required to vacate the house at A while he was absent from A.

  1. In fact the applicant has never returned to A in his employment. Subsequent to completing his master's degree and submitting his master's thesis to his employer he was promoted to a position of senior veterinary officer and since then has been acting as a supervising veterinary officer. Both promotions resulted in the applicant deriving additional income. The evidence is that the thesis was a significant supporting document for the applicant's promotion to senior veterinary officer which took effect from 1 January 1984.
  2. In essence the applicant submitted that the additional rent expense incurred while undertaking the course at T did not result in any private benefit to him. He incurred the additional expenses for the purpose of enabling himself to accept the grant of leave without pay and the study assistance so that he could pursue the course of study.

15. Counsel for the respondent submitted that the rent incurred by the applicant was not incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income of the applicant but in any event was expenditure of a private or domestic nature. In the alternative he submitted that 2/3rds of the expense related to the applicant's wife and children and therefore was not incurred within the terms of sub-section 51(1). Furthermore counsel for the Commissioner argued that section 82A operates to disallow $250 of the amount claimed. It seems strange that his last point was put to the Tribunal when the Commissioner allowed deductions amounting to $381 for self education expenses in his objection decision. Presumably the Commissioner takes the view that his objection decision was incorrect.

  1. In my view the rent paid by the applicant was not an expense necessarily incurred by the taxpayer for or in connection with a prescribed course of education. This is because the expense is too remote from the course of education and was not necessarily incurred for the purposes of the course. The Commissioner's argument as to the application of section 82A is therefore rejected.
  2. The principal argument for the respondent relied upon FC of T v Green [1950] HCA 20; (1950) 81 CLR 313 (9 ATD 142) where the High Court stated at pages 317-8:

"It has been held in the case of Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295, that the words in the initial part of s51 'All losses or outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' mean such losses and outgoings as are incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income.

Further, in the case of Ronpibon Tin No Liability and Toncrkah Compound No Liability v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1949) 78 CLR 48, at p56, it was said with reference to s51:- 'For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing an assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" mean in the course of gaining or producing such income' and 'In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or if none be produced would be expected to produce assessable income.' (1949) 78 CLR at p57.

It is not enough in order to establish a right to a deductions to show that it was proper or reasonable for the taxpayer to make the expenditure which he claims as a deduction. For example, it is perfectly reasonable and proper for a taxpayer to incur living expenses and many expenses of a private or domestic nature, but such expenditure is expressly excluded from deductibility by the final words of the first sub-section of s51. Thus, as has been stated in the course of argument, a taxpayer cannot deduct ordinary living expenses. It is true that such expenses are necessarily incurred if any income is to be earned or otherwise derived, but such expenses would be incurred whether income was earned or otherwise derived or not."

18. The question left unresolved by the High Court in Green's Case and which arises here is whether extraordinary living expenses are allowable deductions under sub-section 51(1).

19. The answer to that question and to this application is to be found in the judgment of Mason J (as he then was) in Lodge v. FC of T [1972] HCA 49; (1972) 128 CLR 171, 72 ATC 4174[1972] HCA 49; , 3 ATR 254. In that case the taxpayer incurred childminding expenses for the purpose of enabling her to carry out her work preparing solicitors' bills of costs and thereby earn assessable income. After referring to the Ronpibon Case and Lunney v FC of T (1958) 100 CLR 478 Mason J went on to say at page 175:

"The rejection in Lunney's Case of the claim that the expenses of travelling between home and work was an allowable deduction was based on the proposition that it is not enough to show that the expenditure was an essential prerequisite to the derivation of assessable income. The decision denied the notion that an expense was incidental and relevant to the derivation of income merely because it was necessary in that sense. The decision turned rather upon a view of the character of the expenditure incurred.

This approach to s51(1) is founded largely on the presence of the word 'in' in the principal parts of the sub-section.

....

In the light of the authoritative observations concerning s51(1) made by this Court in its earlier decisions I have no alternative but to arrive at the conclusion that the appellant's claim in this appeal cannot succeed. The expenditure was incurred for the purpose of earning assessable income and it was an essential prerequisite of the derivation of that income. Nevertheless its character as nursery fees for the appellant's child was neither relevant nor incidental to the preparation of bills of costs, the activities or operations by which the appellant gained or produced assessable income. The expenditure was not incurred in, or in the course of preparing bills of costs."

  1. On his Honour's reasoning it is clear that the applicant cannot succeed in respect of the rent on the family home in so far as it relates to his family. The cost of housing of his family at T simply cannot be said to have been incurred in or in the course of producing his assessable income.
  2. Can some proportion of the claim, say 1/3, as postulated in the alternative argument for the respondent, be allowed in so far as it applies to the applicant. The applicant says that the cost of accommodating his family is covered by the $35 per week excluded from his claim and the amount claimed was all incurred for the purpose of undertaking the course of study and therefore for the purpose of producing assessable income.

22. If the applicant had continued to maintain his home at A while he undertook the course of study at T the position may have been different but on the facts of this application I am unable to conclude that the rent paid was other than private or domestic expenditure. It was not incurred in the course of producing the assessable income because it was incurred for the purpose of providing the family home and the payments retained that characteristic irrespective of the income producing activities of the applicant.

  1. As the applicant relied upon the decision in FC of T v Highfield 82 ATC 4463 I should explain why that decision does not apply to the facts of this application.
  2. Dr Highfield, a dentist in general practice travelled to London to undertake and complete a Master of Science degree in periodontics. He was absent from Australia and claimed in his return of income expenses which included living away from home expenses and meals. Lee J found that Dr Highfield in setting off to acquire the degree considered that it would enable him to do more periodontic work in his general practice, which he would carry on and expand until he was able to take up practice as a specialist.
  3. Unlike the present applicant Dr Highfield went to London alone and maintained the family home in Australia. Also Dr Highfield leased his dental practice in Sydney on a locum tenens basis with the intention that he would resume the practice upon his return to Australia.
    1. His Honour found that the expenditure in question was incurred in gaining assessable income in his general practice and it was not disqualified from deduction because the course of study could also be construed as leading the applicant into specialist practice.
    2. Having found that the expenditure claimed fell within a positive limb of sub-section 51(1) his Honour then went on to find that there was no basis for finding that the expenditure was of a private or domestic nature. Although his Honour does not say so he was no doubt relying on the evidence that the taxpayer's family had remained in Australia at the family home so that the taxpayer's domestic premises were maintained in Australia (Case N69 81 ATC 358 at 366 per Mr Harrowell at paragraph 8).

28. The expenditure at issue in Hiqhfield's Case was not of the same character as the expenditure at issue here. The applicant paid the rent on the houses at T for the purpose of providing a house for his family to live in. The expenditure is therefore essentially expenditure of a domestic nature and it does not change its character simply because the applicant had to pay more to rent a house for his family in T than he had been used to paying for a house in A.

29. The objection decision will be affirmed.

The preceding eleven (11) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matter of QT86/1811.

Deputy Registrar
