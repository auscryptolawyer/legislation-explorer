Julian Rohan Hamilton and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 47; (1995) 37 ALD 511; (1995) 21 AAR 197 (20 February 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

JULIAN ROHAN HAMILTON v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A94/155

AAT No. 10026

Number of pages - 3

Superannuation

[1995] AATA 47; (1995) 37 ALD 511

[1995] AATA 47; (1995) 21 AAR 197

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

M.D. ALLEN (Senior Member), N.H. ATTWOOD (Member) AND H.G. JULIAN (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Application to make late election to transfer from Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme to Public Sector Superannuation Scheme. At time of election to remain in the CSS Applicant thought he had secure tenure. Now possibility of redundancy. Not a sufficient reason to permit late election.

Superannuation Act 1976 - S.157, S.244, S.245

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; 123 ALR 239

HEARING

CANBERRA, 31 January 1995

20:2:1995

The Applicant appeared in person.

Solicitor for the Respondent: Mr Anthony Harding appearing for:

Commonwealth Superannuation Administration

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

M.D. ALLEN, N.H. ATTWOOD AND H.G. JULIAN The Applicant in this matter seeks review of the decision of a Delegate of the Respondent made 19 August 1994 affirming a prior decision by another Delegate on 7 March 1994, not to direct pursuant to Ss.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 that a late election under Ss.244(1) of the said Act to transfer from the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme established by that Act to the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme established by the Superannuation Act 1990, be treated as if made within the period allowed by Ss.244(1) of the 1976 Act.

2. Ss.157(1) of the 1976 Act states:

"Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under

this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed

by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the

Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it

is desirable that the election should be recognized, the Commissioner

may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within

the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

And Ss.244(1) of the said 1976 Act states inter alia:

"(1) Subject to this section, an eligible employee who is not precluded

by or under the Superannuation Act 1990 (other than by paragraph 6 (2)

(a) of that Act) from being a member of the Superannuation (1990) Scheme

may, in writing addressed to the Commissioner:

(a) declare that he or she wishes to become a member of that scheme;

and

(b) elect to cease to be an eligible employee."

Paragraph 244(3)(e) goes on to provide that in the Applicant's case the last day upon which an election could be made was 30 June 1991.

3. Section 245 of the 1976 Act states:

"A person who makes a declaration and election under section 244 is

taken to have ceased to be an eligible employee at the end of the day on

which the declaration and election are made."

4. There are no provisions within either the 1976 Act nor the 1990 Act which permit any revocation of the election once made except for the special circumstances set out in S.247 of the 1976 Act. This can be compared with Ss.157(3) of the said 1976 Act which does permit the revocation of certain other elections under that Act.

5. As at 30 June 1991 the Applicant had not made any election to transfer to the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme (the PSS) established by the Superannuation Act 1990, consequently he remained as a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (the CSS) established by the Superannuation Act 1976.

6. The Applicant concedes that at the time he made his decision to stay in the CSS he was fully informed as to the advantages and disadvantages of both schemes. He had been in receipt of information disseminated by the Retirement Benefits Office and had attended seminars in which the competing schemes were compared and contrasted.

7. Exhibit 1 is an information kit disseminated to all members of the CSS prior to the time for election. That kit contained a Superannuation Election Form and notes, on the reverse of that form, quite clearly set out the irrevocable nature of the election to be made.

8. At the time he made his decision to remain in the CSS the Applicant considered that he had a secure job in the Department of Defence and that he would remain in the Commonwealth Public Service until he chose to retire. In particular, he contrasted what would be the benefits accruing to him under both schemes upon retirement at age 55.

9. Subsequent to making his decision the Applicant was on 13 August 1993 declared excess to Department of Defence requirements and informed of the intention to retrench him.

10. The Applicant initiated action in the Federal Court following that decision It is not germane to these reasons to canvass those proceedings except to state that whereas the Department of Defence settled the Applicant's claim and he is now employed in that department in a substantive and permanent position he feels, no doubt with good reason, that his future in that department is precarious.

11. Following the declaration of 13 August 1993, declaring him excess to requirements, the Applicant recalculated the benefits due to him under both the CSS and the PSS and determined that the PSS gave him the superior benefit should he be forced to relinquish his employment prior to attaining the age of 55. He therefore sought to make a late election to join the PSS.

12. That application for late election was not made until 16February 1994. The Applicant explained that delay by stating that upon initiating his action in the Federal Court he had waited until he had material from the Respondent in that action, corroborating his contention that at the time he elected to remain in the CSS, and indeed at no time prior to 13 August 1993, had the Department of Defence intended to declare him excess to requirements.

13. The material sought by the Applicant was contained in an Affidavit by David John Barritt-Eyles, the Respondent to his action, which Affidavit was sworn 10 February 1994. The Applicant then made his request for a late election supported by a Statutory Declaration and other material on 16 February 1994. It was his contention that he acted expeditiously upon the receipt by him of the corroborative material.

14. The Applicant's case is in reality a simple one. He seeks to make a late election to transfer from the CSS to the PSS following a change in his personal circumstances, namely that the security of tenure in employment he envisaged that he had, at the time he made his decision to continue in the CSS has been shown, on his perception, to be ephemeral.

15. There is no doubt that the Parliament set strict time limits in which the election to change from the CSS to the PSS was to be made. Although the 1976 Act contains a mechanism for late elections it is significant that there is no provision to revoke an election to become a member of the PSS, once made.

16. This finality of decision indicates to the Tribunal that whereas the legislature permits late elections under Ss.244(1) to transfer to the PSS, requests for the exercise of that discretion are to be scrutinised with care and not granted lightly.

17. In Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450 at 456 Davies J said:

"Many such provisions, including s 157, are not concerned with rights.

If an act is not performed within the prescribed time, there is no right

to have it performed. But there is a discretion to permit to be

extended time should it be appropriate in the circumstances to do so."

18. Prior to the passage quoted above, his Honour had quoted with approval the principles expounded by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 3 FCR 344. The first of these principles follows from the decision of Fitzgerald J (as he then was) in Lucic v Nolan 45 ALR 411 at 416, namely, that it is the prima facie rule that proceedings commenced outside prescribed time periods will not be entertained.

19. In Hunter Valley Developments supra Wilcox J went on to specify other relevant matters including the need for finality (in disputes), public considerations and considerations of fairness between the Applicant and other persons. A mere absence of prejudice is not enough for the exercise of the discretion.

20. The above factors are relevant here. The Applicant having made his election, no doubt actuarial calculations have been based on that decision and to alter those calculations now would affect both funds. The funds themselves are different, for example, the Applicant must contribute 5% of his salary to the CSS but can elect to contribute an amount of between 2% to 10% of salary to the PSS. There is also an element of unfairness to the interests of the members of both funds if persons having made their election are allowed, some years later, to reverse that election, with consequent actuarial variations. As von Doussa J pointed out in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; 123 ALR 239 at 246:

"On the other hand the orderly administration of the Superannuation

Scheme requires that time limits be applied, generally speaking, so that

decisions which impinge on investments and accounting procedures are

made soon after an event ... occurs."

21. Although in Chalk's case supra both Davies and Cooper JJ discussed the breadth of the discretion available in S.157, that case involved an election to preserve rights pursuant to the 1976 Act and not a transfer between schemes. Furthermore, they were concerned to give illustrations of matters which might affect a discretion and not to lay down binding principles of law.

22. As stated above, we consider that the Parliament in enacting Part XIV of the 1976 Act intended that in the normal course of events, once an election had been made as to which Superannuation Scheme to belong to, that election was irrevocable. There are a plethora of reasons why a member of the CSS might in retrospect wish to transfer to the PSS, these include matters relating to employment, family circumstances, health and all the other vicissitudes of life that might effect any person.

23. All these matters are matters which should have been within the contemplation of a member of the CSS when they elected. It is an unfortunate fact of life that even Public Servants in what was once regarded as a secure lifetime employment have for some years now been subject to a higher incidence of redundancy. The Applicant was, at the time he made his election, an unattached officer and by his own evidence revealed that he knew, or at the very least, suspected he was out of favour with sections of the Department of Defence. In these circumstances redundancy was a matter which should have entered into his contemplation. That his circumstances have now changed from the time when he made his decision so that his tenure is not altogether secure is not, so far as we are concerned, of sufficient moment to set aside the decision under review and direct that he be permitted to elect to transfer from the CSS to the PSS.

24. The decision under review is affirmed.
