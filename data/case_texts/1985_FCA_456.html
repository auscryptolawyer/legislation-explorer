Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v John Anthony Griffin; Michael William Duckett White [1985] FCA 456; (1985) 7 FCR 566 (6 December 1985)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: JOHN ANTHONY GRIFFIN; MICHAEL WILLIAM DUCKETT WHITE [1985] FCA 456; (1985) 7 FCR 566

No. G69 of 1985 and G70 of 1985

Income Tax

COURT

IN FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fox(1), Lockhart(2) and Neaves(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Exemptions - income derived in Papua New Guinea from court appearances by barristers resident in Australia - whether exempt from income tax in Australia on footing that derived from "any office or employment" in Papua New Guinea - s. 23(qa) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 6, 23(q), 23(qa), 160AF

Great Western Railway Co. v. Bater (1920) 3 K.B. 266 ref'd to

Edwards v. Clinch (1982) A.C. 845 ref'd to

Income Tax - Assessable income - Exempt income - Income from sources in Papua New Guinea from any "office or employment" - Barristers deriving fees from court appearances in Papua New Guinea - Meaning of "employment" - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), s 23(qa). Held: The exemption from tax afforded by s 23(qa) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) of income derived from sources in Papua New Guinea from "employment" is limited to salary, wages and similar remuneration. It does not extend to fees derived by barristers from carrying on their profession.

HEARING

Sydney, 1985, October 4; December 6. 6:12:1985

APPEAL

Appeals pursuant to s 200 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 from decisions of the Supreme Court of Queensland allowing the taxpayer's appeals from a decision of a Board of Review.

T R Hartigan QC and D J McGill, for the appellant.

I V Gzell QC and P M Wolfe, for the respondents.

Cur adv vult

Solicitor for the appellant: Australian Government Solicitor.

Solicitors for the respondents:Flower & Hart.

FPC

ORDER

The appeal be allowed.

The costs of the appeal to be paid by the appellant in accordance with the order granting leave to appeal.

The order of the Supreme Court of Queensland be set aside and in lieu thereof the appeal to it be dismissed with costs.

The amended assessment the subject of the proceedings be confirmed.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

Appeals allowed

DECISION

We are hearing appeals, by leave, from the Supreme Court of Queensland in two cases, which by consent were heard together. The cases raise the same question on the construction of s. 23(qa) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the "Act"), there being no dispute on the facts. In one case (G69 of 1985) the taxation year ended on 30 June 1978, in the other case (G70 of 1985), there were three assessments, for the taxation years ending on 30 June 1979, 1980, and 1981.

2. The respondents are both barristers, and they earned fees in Papua New Guinea for their professional services there. Section 23(qa) is as follows:

"23. The following income shall be exempt from income

tax: . . .

(qa) income consisting of earnings, salary,

wages, commission, bonuses or allowances derived

from sources in Papua New Guinea from any office

or employment (including employment by a

Government or an authority of a Government or as

a member of the Defence Force) where the income

is not exempt from income tax under the income

tax laws of Papua New Guinea and, if there is a

liability for payment of income tax under those

laws, the Commissioner is satisfied that the tax

has been or will be paid;"

3. The only question which arises is whether the earnings were derived from "employment". It is agreed that the barristers were retained by firms of solicitors comprising partners resident in Papua New Guinea and were briefed by those firms to appear on behalf of their clients in the courts of Papua New Guinea. It is also agreed that, being admitted both as barristers and solicitors in New Guinea, the appellants were entitled to sue for their fees (see s. 24 of the Legal Practitioners Ordinance 1954 (Papua New Guinea), and s. 20 of the Lawyers Act (Papua New Guinea)).

4. His Honour, taking a different view from a majority of Board of Review No. 3, held that their fees were derived from employment, within the meaning of the paragraph, and were therefore exempt.

5. The word "employment" is commonly used as an abstract noun, and it at first seemed to me to be wrong to regard it as confined in the present context to master and servant situations, or contracts of service. It has indeed been quite common to refer to a contractor as being "employed" (see, for example, Lecture League Limited v. London County Council (1913) 108 LT 924).

6. Prior to Act No. 80 of 1975, a resident of Australia (defined in s. 6(1) of the Act) who derived income in Papua New Guinea was liable to tax in Australia on the amount of that income, but was entitled under Division 18 of Part III of the Act to set off as a credit against the tax thus payable any amount of tax which he had paid under the Papua New Guinea Income Tax Ordinance ("the Income Tax Ordinance"), which was not a penalty or provisional tax (s. 160AF of the Act). The amount of the credit was calculated by reference to either:

(a) the amount of tax paid or payable in Papua New

Guinea (less any credits allowed by the Income

Tax Ordinance)

or

(b) the amount of Australian tax payable under the

Act

whichever was the less.

7. Act No. 80 of 1975 introduced a new Division 18 into Part III. That Act made a number of changes to accommodate the position resulting from the independence of Papua New Guinea.

8. The new Division 18 contained s. 160AF, which was in substantially similar terms to those of the repealed section. In particular, the manner of calculating the tax credit remained, although s. 160AF(3) provided for the situation where a taxpayer's income was derived partly from property, as a higher rate of tax was then payable on income derived from property. Sub-section 160AF(3) was repealed later in the same year by Act No. 117 of 1975. Sub-section 160AF(8) makes, and made, special provision for income from dividends.

9. The credit system still continues. Act No. 80 of 1975 inserted s. 23(qa) (which I have already set out), which exempts certain income of Australian residents which has been derived in Papua New Guinea.

10. Apart from the insertion of s. 23(qa) the following change, inter alia, was made by Act No. 80 of 1975:

Section 23AAA was inserted after s. 23.

Section 23AAA(3) relates to termination of employment and exemption from income tax in Australia of payments consequent upon such termination. The reference here is plainly to contracts of service.

11. The situation was formerly that an Australian resident did not get an exemption from tax on all income derived from Papua New Guinea, but obtained the credit to which I have referred, as a deduction against tax payable. He was still entitled to a credit during the taxation year in question, but it was thought appropriate to exempt in Australia certain income by the introduction of s. 23(qa). The sub-section is designed to avoid double taxation in relation to some receipts which would otherwise form part of the assessable income of a taxpayer resident in Australia. It has a counterpart, of more general application, in s. 23(q). The reason for not following that paragraph lies in the different relationship with Australia of Papua and New Guinea, and the credit system to which I have referred.

12. When, in 1975, the new Division 18 was substituted the Minister assisting the Treasurer, when moving that the Bill containing the amendment be read a second time in the House of Representatives had this to say:

"One purpose of this Bill is to amend the Australian

income tax law so that when Papua New Guinea becomes

independent it will be treated as a separate country for

Australian tax purposes. The Bill will also give effect

to other taxation proposals, some of which have been

earlier announced. Our income tax law contains a number

of special provisions relating to Papua New Guinea.

These are the product of a variety of circumstances,

including the longstanding policy of applying our tax

laws in a limited way to Australian Territories. Some

relate to an administrative position that came about in

1959 when a separate income tax was imposed in the

Territory of Papua New Guinea. The provisions

appropriate for a Territory are not of course

appropriate for an independent country. Moreover,

changes in both countries' laws since 1959 have removed

the justification for the provisions relating to the

1959 situation. Except in a few respects, the

amendments proposed by this Bill will end the operation

of these special provisions. In broad terms, those

provisions that provide special treatment for certain

categories of income or expenditure related to

activities conducted in either Australia or in Papua New

Guinea, but not in other countries, will be made

inapplicable by the Bill so far as concerns income and

expenditure related to activities conducted in an

independent Papua New Guinea. Subject to some

exceptions, income derived from a source in Papua New

Guinea by Australian residents will be treated in the

same way for Australian tax purposes as income from a

source in other places outside Australia. Residents of

Papua New Guinea will be taxed like other non-residents

on income from sources in Australia.

One of the more important exceptions is a form of double

tax relief to apply after independence. A tax credit

system at present applies in respect of Papua New Guinea

income of Australian residents and in the converse case.

It is proposed to retain this system in Australia after

independence but, as the Treasurer (Dr. J.F. Cairns)

announced recently, the salary or wages of Australians

working in Papua New Guinea will be exempted from

Australian tax where Papua New Guinea taxes the income."

13. The last sentence is of interest. (The emphasis is mine.)

14. I have not found it possible to discern a precise policy behind the particular exemption. There are however some pointers to its construction. There is the use of "employed" and "employment" in s. 23AAA, inserted at the same time, which gives an exemption in respect of receipts of some persons who were employed in Papua New Guinea by certain government authorities.

15. Assistance is to be derived also from a comparison of s. 23(qa) with the definition of "income from personal exertion" in s. 6(1) of the Act. It is, I think, appropriate to make the comparison for two reasons. One is that the paragraph plainly excepts income from property from its operation. The other is that in part it follows the language of the definition, and it is reasonable to conclude that omissions and changes were deliberate. The notable omission in the opening catalogue of terms is that of "fees", a term particularly apt to describe the remuneration of barristers, or, indeed, that of many consultants. Its omission is of significance. There is then the fact that while s. 23(qa) relates the income to "office or employment", many more words are required to comprehend all that is intended under the definition. It is I think of relevance that the use of "office" with "employment" suggests the use of "employment" in a more limited sense than the abstract meaning to which I was first attracted. If employment was intended in that sense, or to include contracts for services, the structure of the paragraph would in my opinion have been quite different.

16. In my opinion the term applies to cases where there is a contract of service, - what used under former terminology to be called a master and servant situation.

17. I am therefore of the view that the appeals should be allowed. The costs of the appeals should be paid by the appellant in accordance with the orders granting leave to appeal. The orders of the Supreme Court of Queensland should be set aside and the appeals to it dismissed with costs. The amended assessments the subject of the proceedings should be confirmed.

These appeals concern the construction of s. 23(qa) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") which was introduced into the Act by the Income Tax Assessment Act 1975 (Act No. 80 of 1975). Section 23 (qa) exempts from income tax:

"(qa) income consisting of earnings, salary,

wages, commission, bonuses or allowances derived

from sources in Papua New Guinea from any office or

employment (including employment by a Government or

an authority of a Government or as a member of the

Defence Force) where the income is not exempt from

income tax under the income tax laws of Papua New

Guinea and, if there is a liability for payment of

income tax under those laws, the Commissioner is

satisfied that the tax has been or will be paid;"

2. Both taxpayers are barristers who carry on practice in Australia. They are also admitted to practice as barristers and solicitors of the Supreme Court of Papua New Guinea. Each of them is an Australian resident who travelled from time to time from Australia to Papua New Guinea and earned fees from appearances in the courts of that country. The taxpayer, Michael William Duckett White, earned fees during the years ended 30 June 1979, 1980 and 1981. The taxpayer, John Anthony Griffin, earned fees during the year ended 30 June 1978. The fees were subject to income tax under the laws of Papua New Guinea which was paid by the taxpayers.

3. The Commissioner of Taxation included the fees as part of the assessable income of each taxpayer. The taxpayers objected to the assessments, the Commissioner disallowed the objections and the Commissioner's decisions were referred to Taxation Board of Review No. 3. The Board, by majority, upheld the Commissioner's decisions and disallowed the objections. The taxpayers appealed to the Supreme Court of Queensland from the Board's decision. The Supreme Court allowed the appeals with costs and directed that the matters be remitted to the Commissioner to amend his assessments in accordance with the Court's reasons for judgment. The Commissioner appealed to a Full Court of this Court from the Supreme Court's judgment pursuant to leave granted by a single Judge of the Court. As a term of obtaining that leave the Commissioner undertook to pay the reasonable costs of the taxpayers of the appeals to this Court, on a party and party basis, in any event. The Commissioner was ordered to pay the taxpayers' costs of and incidental to the application for leave. The appeals to the Supreme Court were heard together by consent as they were in this Court.

4. The Supreme Court (Macrossan J.) found that the word "employment" in s. 23(qa) was not confined to the relationship of master and servant, and that the association of "office" with "employment" tended to widen the scope which might otherwise have been conveyed by the use of the latter word, had it stood alone. His Honour attributed a wide meaning to "employment" in s. 23(qa) and found that the preferred construction required that earnings under a contract for services as well as earnings under a contract of service should be included within the exemption. His Honour held that the earnings of the taxpayers as barristers in Papua New Guinea should be regarded as covered by the words of s. 23(qa).

5. The word "employment" is not defined in the Act. It is a word of infinite and wide content. Its meaning must be taken from its context. Sometimes it may be used in the narrow sense of employment arising from the relation of master and servant. It may also mean a person's profession, occupation or vocation. It may encompass the business of a self employed person. Each of these meanings is recognised by the dictionaries. The word is no stranger to the law reports. Different meanings have been ascribed to it by courts over many years in a variety of contexts. The learned primary Judge referred to a number of the authorities where its meaning was considered and we were taken in argument by counsel to those and other cases including some where the word was considered by courts of the United Kingdom in the context of the Income Tax Acts of that country. Those last mentioned cases are not apposite as they were concerned with determining whether a taxpayer should be assessed to income tax under Schedule D or Schedule E of the Income Tax Act 1918 (or its predecessors) or more recently under the Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970 (U.K.). The meaning which the courts of the United Kingdom have ascribed to the word "employment" in that legislation takes its colour from its context and in particular from the association of the word and other words appearing in juxtaposition to it; for example "trade, profession, employment, or vocation" (Schedule D of the Income Tax Acts).

6. We were referred to other provisions of the Act to assist in the task of defining the meaning of "employment" in s. 23(qa). One such provision was the definition of the expression "income from personal exertion" in sub-s. 6(1) which, so far as presently relevant, provides:

"'income from personal exertion' or 'income derived

from personal exertion' means income consisting of

earnings, salaries, wages, commissions, fees,

bonuses, pensions, superannuation allowances,

retiring allowances and retiring gratuities,

allowances and gratuities received in the capacity

of employee or in relation to any services

rendered, the proceeds of any business carried on

by the taxpayer either alone or as a partner with

any other person, any amount received as a bounty

or subsidy in carrying on a business, any amount

that is included in the assessable income of the

taxpayer by reason of section 159GD, the income

from any property where that income forms part of

the emoluments of any office or employment of

profit held by the taxpayer, any profit arising

from the sale by the taxpayer of any property

acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by

sale or from the carrying on or carrying out of any

profit-making undertaking or scheme, and any profit

that is included in the assessable income of the

taxpayer by reason of section 26AAA, but does not

include -

(a) interest, unless the taxpayer's principal

business consists of the lending of money,

or unless the interest is received in

respect of a debt due to the taxpayer for

goods supplied or services rendered by him

in the course of his business; or

(b) rents or dividends."

Reference was also made to the use of the word "employment" in the definition of the term "business" in sub-s. 6(1) where it appears plainly in the context of the business of a self employed person.

7. The various authorities and statutory provisions to which we were referred, including sections of the Act itself, with the exception of the definition of the expression "income from personal exertion" in sub-s. 6(1), are to my mind of limited assistance in deciding the question before us except to show that in some contexts the word "employment" is used in a wider sense than a master and servant relationship and may be apt to encompass a person's profession, vocation, business or trade. But the meaning of the word "employment" in s. 23(qa) falls to be determined in its particular context and in the light of the history of the provision.

8. In order to determine the meaning of "employment" in s. 23(qa) counsel for all parties placed considerable reliance upon the distinction between contracts of service which govern the relationship of employer and employee on the one hand and contracts for services where a person's services are engaged or retained on the other hand. I have real misgivings about construing "employment" in s. 23(qa) with reference to these traditional distinctions as they have been drawn by courts over many years in contexts that have little in common with Australian income tax legislation. Also, the assumption is not infrequently made that these two kinds of contracts (contracts of service and contracts for services) cover the field of personal engagement. They do not, a point illustrated in the sphere of workmens' compensation legislation by Dare v. Dietrich [1979] FCA 47; (1979) 26 ALR 18 (Federal Court) and (1980) 30 ALR 407 (High Court).

9. What then is the meaning of s. 23(qa)? Its language is literally capable of bearing the meaning contended for by the taxpayers, namely, that it includes all income received in the capacity of an employee or for services rendered or being the proceeds of a business. But the only word in the section which could be relied on to support that construction is "earnings" in the collocation of "earnings, salary, wages, commissions, bonuses or allowances . . ." when used in association with "employment".

10. The narrower meaning for which the Commissioner contends is also capable of being supported. The word "employment" in the context of s. 23(qa) may mean employment in the sense of employment as an employee by an employer and bear no wider meaning.

11. The word "office" is, I think, less controversial. It has been considered by courts on various occasions. The Act does not provide a definition of the word. "Office" has been in income tax legislation of the United Kingdom for many years, at least as early as the Income Tax Act 1942 (Schedule E). In Great Western Railway Co. v. Bater (1920) 3 KB 266 Rowlatt J. said at p 274 that an "office" was something:

"which was a subsisting, permanent, substantive

position which had an existence independent of the

person who filled it, and which went on and was

filled in succession by successive holders."

12. In Edwards v. Clinch (1982) AC 845 Lord Wilberforce said of that statement by Rowlatt J. at 860 that it was:

"a definition or statement which was, I dare to

say, bred into the bones of every practitioner in

income tax matters and, more importantly, was known

to the legislature, and its drafting agents, on the

many occasions when revisions of the Schedules were

made or considered.

Because this was the origin of the income tax

meaning of, "office" I have doubts as to the value,

or indeed legitimacy, of now resorting to a

dictionary for a definition. Of course it would be

desirable in an ideal world for expressions in tax

legislation to bear ordinary meanings, such as the

citizen could find out by consulting the Oxford

English Dictionary. But it is a fact that many

words of ordinary meaning acquire a signification

coloured over the years by legal construction in a

technical context such that return to the pure

source of common parlance is no longer possible. I

think that "office" is such a word."

13. His Lordship was, of course, discussing "office" in the context of the English legislation; and his remarks cannot be treated as relating directly to the Australian income tax legislation, including s. 23(qa).

14. The word "office" is used in s. 23 (qa), in my opinion, in a sense akin to that in which it has been interpreted in the Income Tax Acts of the United Kingdom - that is a position with some degree of permanence. Its primary application would be to persons in the service of the Government of Papua New Guinea including those formerly in the service of the Commonwealth of Australia or the Administration of Papua and New Guinea before Papua New Guinea gained independence in 1975, but who remained thereafter in New Guinea in the service of that country's government or in the service of its agencies. Counsel for the taxpayers did not contend that the word "office" was appropriate to describe the position or activities of the taxpayers under the Act, although some formal submission to this effect was made but not argued.

15. If I were to construe s. 23(qa) standing alone I would prefer the narrower interpretation of "employment" for which the Commissioner contended. It seems to me to be more in accordance with the ordinary and natural meaning of the language used. Also, I derive some assistance from the definition of the expression "income from personal exertion" in sub-s. 6(1) of the Act. The draftsman of s. 23(qa) obviously would have been aware of that definition. The form of s. 23(qa) is consistent with the draftsman having transposed so much of the definition of "income from personal exertion" as is apt to describe income earned by an employee from his employment, yet omitted expressions such as "fees", "in relation to any services rendered", "the proceeds of any business carried on" and "the income from any property where that income forms part of the emoluments of any office or employment of profit held by the taxpayer" which are inapt to describe income earned by an employee. The contention of the taxpayers involves in my view squeezing the words of the provision to a literally permissible construction but one which does not accord with a fair reading of the provision as a whole.

16. The interpretation of s. 23(qa) is assisted by an examination of the history of the section and its relationship to related sections of the Act especially s. 23(q) and Division 18 - dealing with tax credits.

17. Section 23(q) exempts income derived by an Australian resident from sources outside Australia and Papua New Guinea which would be otherwise assessable under sub-s. 25(1) where that income is not exempt from income tax in the country where it is derived and tax imposed in that country has been or will be paid.

18. Prior to Act No. 80 of 1975 most income (there was an exception afforded by s. 7 in respect of certain income derived from sources in Papua New Guinea) derived by an Australian resident from sources in Papua New Guinea was subject to Australian income tax notwithstanding that it was also subject to income tax in Papua New Guinea. However, double taxation of such income was avoided by the system of allowing credits under Division 18 of Part III of the Act. This relief against double taxation was provided by way of allowance of a credit for Papua New Guinea tax against the Australian tax on that income.

19. Act No. 80 of 1975 amended the Act in a number of respects some of which are significant for present purposes. It took effect in all relevant respects from the date of Papua New Guinea independence day, namely, 16 September 1975. Act No. 80 of 1975 repealed Division 18 of the Act and substituted a revised Division 18 which made provision for granting a credit for Papua New Guinea tax against the Australian tax liability of an Australian resident who is in receipt of income from Papua New Guinea. But for the enactment of s. 23(qa) the income of Australian residents for which that provision affords exemption would be taxable by both countries subject to the tax credit provisions of Division 18 being provided by Australia for the Papua New Guinea tax.

20. If the legislature had intended to exempt from Australian tax income derived from Papua New Guinea sources by an Australian resident in the same way as the exemption applied to income from sources in other countries the obvious thing to do would have been to amend s. 23(q) by deleting the reference to Papua New Guinea. But plainly the general exemption which s. 23(q) would otherwise have afforded was not intended to apply in respect of income from Papua New Guinea sources except to some lesser degree than would otherwise have applied pursuant to s. 23(q). Thus the question arises as to what income of Australian residents derived from sources in Papua New Guinea is afforded the more limited exemption from Australian tax by s. 23(qa).

21. It was contended by counsel for the taxpayers that the exemption extended to "income from personal exertion" derived in Papua New Guinea but not to income derived there from property. But if this had been the draftsman's intention he could easily have said so rather than employ the narrower language which he did when casting the section. Nor can I discern any support for this contention from the language of any of the related sections.

22. In my opinion, upon its proper construction and when considered in the light of the matters to which I have referred, s. 23(qa) was designed to exempt salary, wages and other similiar remuneration derived by residents of Australia from sources in Papua New Guinea where the income is not exempt from Papua New Guinea tax and, if there is a liability for payment of that tax, the Commissioner is satisfied that the tax has been or will be paid. The section cannot extend to fees derived by barristers from carrying on their profession.

23. The present Divisions 18 and 19, as amended by Act No. 80 of 1975, preserve the tax credit method of double tax relief for income, other than income in the nature of salary or wages, derived from sources in Papua New Guinea by Australian residents.

24. My view of s. 23(qa) is confirmed by a perusal of the explanatory memorandum which accompanied the Bill that became Act No. 80 of 1975 and the second reading speech of the responsible Minister to the House of Representatives on 22 May 1975 notwithstanding some degree of ambiguity to be found there.

25. I would allow the appeals, set aside the judgment of the Supreme Court, confirm the amended assessments of the Commissioner and order the taxpayers to pay the Commissioner's costs of the appeals to the Supreme Court. The Commissioner must pay the taxpayers' costs of the appeals to this Court in accordance with the order granting leave to appeal.

I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment prepared by my brother Lockhart.

2. I agree that the appeals should be allowed for the reasons advanced by his Honour. I also agree in the orders proposed.
