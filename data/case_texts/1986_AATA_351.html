Last Updated: 6 March 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax: investments companies "taking up" Australian Savings Bonds issued in accordance with the Commonwealth Inscribed Stock Act 1911 - Bonds sold within twelve months of date of acquisition - whether Bonds purchased in terms of section 26 AAA - whether profit assessable in terms of action 26 AAA(2).

Income Tax; only part of "profit" claimed to be a non-assessable capital surplus in return of income - returned assessed including that part and objection to the inclusion of that part only issue raised as to whether, if profit as a whole non-assessable, Tribunal should order rejection to be enlarged to embrace whole amount.

Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) sections 26 AAA, 190 (a)

Re V.G.M. Holdings Ltd (1942) 1 All ER.244

Neville Reid and Company Ltd v C. of I.R. 12 TC 545

Barker v Lewis (1947) KB 186

Powell v Cleland (1948) KB 262

H.L. Bolton Engineering Co. v T.3. Graham and Sons (1951 1 QB 159

Applicants

And:

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr H.P. Stevens (Senior Member)

The question at issue in these applications relates to a profit made on the sale of Government bonds held for less than twelve months and is whether such profit has been correctly included in the applicants' assessable income for the year ended 30 June 1984 pursuant to the provisions of sub-section 26 AAA(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The applicants are all members of the same group of companies and there is no difference in the fundamental factual position of each. Accordingly in these reasons reference will be made basically to the first applicant only.

  1. Once active in the retailing area the group some years ago disposed of its retail interests and decided to invest in rental property. It was not interested in dealing but in obtaining something secure - buildings wholly or almost wholly occupied by substantial tenants. Two buildings were initially obtained both of twelve stories with one exclusively tenanted by Telecom and the other almost wholly by a legal association. In 1983 the group had further monies to invest and decided to invest in Australian Government Securities and a number of applications (not all same date) were made to "take up" (to use a neutral term) Australian Savings Bonds Series 22 and 23 "issued in accordance with the Commonwealth Inscribed Stock Act 1911" - more than one application was necessary because $200,000.00 face value was the maximum for any one holder.
  2. A copy of the prospectus and application form in relation to Series 22 "taken up" by the first applicant was not available but one for Series 23 "taken up" by another in the group was tendered and there is no reason to doubt that the Series 22 prospectus and application form was any different (apart from interest rate and dates) nor that the applicants filled in their respective forms in other than the same manner. The prospectus was for Australian Savings Bonds and Inscribed Stock Series 23, 13.75% p.a. maturing 1 March 1990 "on sale from a date to be announced on or after 19 October 1982 until further notice". Applicants could "elect to hold their investment in the form of Inscribed Stock, bonds to be held for safe custody by a bank, or bearer securities" and the application form made provision for such election (all elected for "Inscribed Stock and Sydney Registry"). The prospectus inter alia instructed "each subscription is to be paid in one amount in full when the application is made" and that "the Treasurer reserves the right to reject any application for the issue of Australian Savings Bonds in whole or in part and any unapplied subscription monies will be refunded" (without interest). The application form headed "application for Australian Savings Bonds and Inscribed Stock" stated, inter alia, that "in accordance with the prospectus (the applicant) apply for securities to the value of Two Hundred Thousand Dollars and wish to hold my investment in the form of Inscribed Stock."
  3. The application was apparently dated 30 September 1982 (same date as cheque for $200,000.00 drawn) and under cover of letter dated 26 November 1982 addressed to The Registrar, Registry of Inscribed Stock, Reserve Bank of Australia re Application No. 779268 documents comprising a Certificate of Incorporation, Copy of Memorandum and Articles of Association and Form Authentication of Corporate Seal were forwarded. Subsequently by advice of 12 January 1983 the Registrar advised the applicant "as requested in your application, stock as follows has been inscribed in" your name - the stock being Series ASB 22 maturing 1 December 1989, $200,000.00.
  4. After the bonds had been "taken up" an opportunity to acquire another property occurred and on 17 June 1983 the applicants lodged applications for the redemption of their holdings on 18 July 1983 and such were acknowledged by the Registrar by letter of 22 June advising cheques "covering the proceeds will be available for collection at this Registry following redemption on 18 July 1983." Soon after the applicants became aware that a higher amount than the redemption figure could be obtained by selling the bonds on the "market" and this took place. In respect of the applicant a contract note dated 30 June 1983 indicates a stock and share broker bought from it "as principal, not as agent." Government Series 22, 14.75%, 1.12.89, 200,000 units at 10.3.60, net proceeds $207,200.00. (3 of the others similarly sold to the same broker on 30 June with the fourth having a contract note with a different broker dated 26 July 1983).

6. Settlement of the 30 June sales took place early in July 1983 and letters were written to the Registrar (applicant 7 July 1983) advising "we wish to cancel the Application for Redemption". As settlement took place after 30 June 1983 the bonds were regarded as still being owned as at 30 June 1983 and the transaction as having taken place during the year ended 30 June 1984. In the preparation of the accounts for such year the accountants for the group did not regard the difference between the $200,000.00 cheque drawn and the $207,200.00 received from the broker as all "profit". The interest accrued to date of settlement was calculated as $3,546.00 and included in the return as part of the income amount "interest received $9,654.00" with the balance of the difference, i.e. $3,654.00 being shown in the accounts as an extraordinary item and transferred to Capital Reserve. The interest received amount of $9,654.00 was, in the statement of profit and loss, "offset" by an interest paid sum also of $9,654.00 to another member of the group as Trustee for the.... Management Trust" - details of basis of payment unknown but the Trust's entitlement might well have been less if the interest received had been less. The returned profit of $2,187.00 was reduced by a 1983 carry forward loss of $1,024.00 to yield a returned taxable income of $1,163.00.

7. In relation to the $3,654 figure this was described in the return of income as a non-assessable capital surplus on disposal of bonds and in support, it was stated:-

"The Government Bonds, as referred to in schedules 5 & 9 were acquired by way of subscription and accordingly the surplus on disposal is non assessable under the provisions of section 26 AAA. It is pointed out that section 26 AAA(i)(d) refers to the acquisition of 'shares' by way of subscription, and does not apply to deem a subscription for bonds to be a "purchase" for the purposes of the section.

In Income Tax Ruling 185 it is stated that a surplus on sale of rights would not be assessable under section 26 AAA because such rights would not have been 'purchased' and section 26 AAA only applies where property has been purchased (or is deemed by the section to have been purchased) and is sold within 12 months of purchase. For a similar reason the capital surplus on disposal of the bonds referred to above would not be assessable."

8. Upon assessment the returned taxable income of $1,163.00 was increased to $4,817.00 by the inclusion of the amount of $3,654.00. "Profit on disposal of Government Bonds....in the assessable income for the year ended 30 June 1984". An objection was lodged claiming the amount of $3,654 was not assessable in terms of either sections 25(1), 25A, 26(a), 26 AAA or any other provision of the Act. It is conceded by the Commissioner that sections other than 26 AAA are inapplicable and it is necessary to set out only that part of the objection relative thereto (all applicants' objections in same terms). These were:-

"5. THAT the said property was not been deemed to have purchased by the company, but was acquired by way of subscription.

  1. THAT section 26 AAA(i)(d) does not operate to deem the acquisition of Government Bonds by way of subscription to be a purchase of same. This section only refers to the acquisition of shares by way of subscription and makes no reference to the acquisition of bonds. Accordingly, the amount of $3,654.00 is not assessable under this section.
  2. THAT section 26 AAA(i)(f) does not operate to deem the acquisition of bonds to be a purchase as they were acquired by the company for cash consideration and were not exchanged for other property or 'without consideration'.
  3. THAT AS the bonds are not deemed to have been purchased, no part of section 26 AAA(i) or section 26 AAA (2) can operate to include in assessable income the surplus on sale of Government Bonds arising within a period of twelve months from the date of subscription."

9. Section 26 AAA deals with profits arising from some short term property transactions with subsection (2) providing that:

"(2) Where -

(a) a taxpayer has purchased property after 21 August 1973 and before the commencement of this section or purchases property after the commencement of this section; and

(b) the taxpayer has, whether before or after the commencement of this section, sold the property or an interest in the property before the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date on which he purchased the property, then, subject to this section, the assessable income of the taxpayer includes any profit arising from the sale of the property or interest."

and the "short" issue for consideration is whether bonds "taken up" are, in terms of the sub-section "purchased". The words "purchased" or "purchases" are not defined in the Act and subsection (1), inter alia, provides:

"(1) For the purposes of this section -

(a) a reference to property generally or to a particular kind of property includes a reference to an estate or interest in property or in that kind of property, as the case may be;

(b) a reference to an agreement or option includes a reference to an agreement or option that is not enforceable by legal proceedings whether or not it was intended to be so enforceable;

(c) an arrangement or understanding, whether formal or informal ,and whether express or implied, shall be deemed to be an agreement;

(d) the acquisition of a share by way of a subscription of capital, with or without the payment of any other consideration, shall be deemed to be the purchase of that share;

(e) the issue by a company to a person of shares in the company as, or as part of, the consideration for the sale of property by the person to the company shall be deemed to constitute the purchase by the person of the shares so issued;

(f) if property is transferred from a person or persons to another person or other persons in exchange for other property without consideration, the transfer shall be deemed to constitute the sale of property by the first-mentioned person or persons and the purchase of the property by the second-mentioned person or persons; "
  1. The section was intended to supplement existing provisions under which profits from the disposal of property might be included as assessable income. One such provision being section 26(a) involving "property acquired....for the purpose of profit-making by sale" (emphasis added). However, unlike section 26(a) and the subsequently enacted sections 25A and 160M(1), section 26 AAA(2) adopts the word "purchased" rather than the wider word "required" and in sub-section (1) provides, inter alia, that subscriptions of capital resulting in the issue of shares "shall be deemed to be the purchase of" those shares. Accordingly it was argued this was deliberate "in order to limit the scope of operation of the section" and "that the Commissioner cannot apply the section in a manner which ignores the clear meaning of the operative provision in an endeavour to avoid the consequences of the omission from the section of a provision to deem a subscription for such Government Bonds to constitute a purchase."
  2. Reference was made to the acquisition of shares by way of subscription of capital being deemed to constitute a purchase as a clear indication they would not otherwise fit the description of "purchased" and to the English case of Re V.G.M. Holdings Ltd (1942) 1 All ER 224 wherein the Court of Appeal held, that a subscription for shares was not a "purchase" within the meaning of that word for the purposes of the U.K. Company's Act 1929. In that case Lord Greene H.R. with whom the other Lord Justices simply agreed said at p.226:

"The sole question is whether or not the word 'purchase' in this section covers a case where the money which the company provides is used to assist a subscription for the company's own shares. There could he no doubt that, if that question were answered in favour of the respondent, the $15,980 was provided by the company by way of financial assistance, because whether a company provides the money by way of gift or by way of loan, or by buying assets from the person who is purchasing the shares at a fraudulent undervalue, all those transactions, it seems to me, would fall within the phrase 'financial assistance.' I, therefore, feel no difficulty about that; but, with all respect to BENNETT, J., I am unable to agree with the view that he took that subscription by these three directors for shares in V.G.M. was a purchase of those shares within the meaning of the section. In the first place, throughout the whole of the Companies Act, 1929, the language which is used with regard to the issue of shares to subscribers is invariably confined to words like 'issue,' 'subscription,' 'application,''allotment' and so forth. There is not a single passage in Act to which we were referred, nor to which my fairly complete recollection of the Act goes, in which the word 'purchase' is used in relation to the transaction of subscription. That being so, it seems to me that a very clear context would be required to enable a meaning to be put upon the word 'purchase' in this section which would extend it so as to cover the acquisition of shares by subscription.

Quite apart from those considerations relating merely to the language of the Act, it seems to me that the word 'purchase' cannot, with propriety, be applied to the legal transaction under which a person, by the machinery of application and allotment, becomes a shareholder in the company; he does not purchase anything when he does that. Counsel for the respondent endeavoured heroically to establish the proposition that a share before issue was an existing article of property, that it was an existing bundle of rights which a shareholder could properly be said to be purchasing when he acquired it by subscription in the usual way. I am quite unable to accept that view. A share is a chose in action. A chose in action implies the existence of some person entitled to the rights, which are rights in action as distinct from rights in possession, and, until the share is issued, no such person exists. Putting it in a nutshell, the difference between the issue of a share to a subscriber and the purchase of a share from an existing shareholder is the difference between the creation and the transfer of a chose in action. The two legal transactions of the creation of a chose in action and the purchase of a chose in action are quite different in conception and in result.

The result is, therefore, that I can find no context in this section which enables me to construe the word 'purchase' as bearing the extended meaning suggested, and I cannot agree with the view which BENNETT, J., took on that part of the case."

12. These observations were quoted with approval by Sankey J. in Neville Reid and Company Ltd v C. of I.R. 12 TC 545 at p.568 and in Powell v Cleland (1948) KB 262, Evershed L.J. referred to the decision of Morton L.J. in Barker v Lewis (1947) KB 186 where Morton L.J. referred to the decision in the first mentioned case above in support of his view that the word "purchaser" and the words "by purchase" for the purposes of the U.K. landlord and tenant legislation referred to a transaction of purchase or buying. The decision in Powell v Cleland was applied by the Court of Appeal in H.L. Bolton Engineering Co. v T.J. Graham and Sons (1951) 1 QB 159 - also a landlord and tenant case - Denning L.J. stating at p.169/170:

"In this contest I think that much help is to be gained from the fact that this Act of 1954 is dealing with a similar subject-matter to that of the Rent Restriction Act; and in the Rent Restriction Acts it is clearly established - by Baker v Lewis and Powell v Cleland -that 'purchase' there is not used in its technical legal sense but is used in its popular sense of buying for money. There are good grounds for thinking that 'purchase' is used in the Act of 1954 in the same sense as in the Rent Restriction Acts.

This provision corresponds to a similar provision in the Rent Acts. It is clear to my mind that the word 'purchased' in the schedule is used in the same sense as it is in the Rent Restriction Acts. So also, it must be used in the same sense in section 30 (2) which is in similar terms. It means, therefore, 'buy for money'."

13. On the basis of the above authorities it was said it was established that, if bonds were in the same category as shares, then the "taking up" thereof did not constitute a purchase. Accordingly the argument proceeded, in an endeavour to show they were in the same category, by stressing that the prospectus for the Australian Savings Bonds Series 29 currently on issue as stock only refers throughout to "subscription moneys" (same as for Series 23 - see para.3). In the same vein reference was made to The Principles of Modern Company Law, Gower, 3rd Edition at p.297 relating to the Prospectus productions of the U.K. Companys Act and to the definition of "prospectus" in section 5 of the Companies (New South Wales) Code.

14. It was further submitted that subsection (f) of 26 AAA(i) did not apply to deem a purchase and in this regard it was argued:

"The delivery of a cheque in payment of the subscription is not a 'transfer'. "The word 'transfer' [in relation to a promissory note] means endorsement and delivery." Bromage v Lloyd [1847] EngR 546; (1847), 1 Exch. 32, per Rolfe, B., at p. 35.

The word 'exchange' in paragraph (f) is apt to apply to preexisting property and not to something brought into existence by the transaction (as the bonds are created upon issue). The normal meaning of 'exchange' in this context is to give or receive a thing in place of another. The paragraph goes on to deem the property transferred to constitute a sale of the property and the property exchanged to constitute the purchase of the property. The paragraph clearly contemplates preexisting property, i.e., the context in which the word is used makes it clear that its ordinary meaning was intended."

If paragraph (f) had a wider meaning than that suggested above, it would not have been necessary to include paragraphs (d) and (e) of subsection (1).

15. As the cases relied upon show and the general rules of construction demand, the meaning of particular words must he gleaned from the context in which they appear. In Re Turcan (1888) 40 Ch D.5 the Court of Appeal unanimously held that insurance policies effected on a person's life had been acquired "by purchase" within the meaning of a covenant relating to property the deceased "should become possessed or entitled by devise, bequest, purchase, or otherwise, of." Cotton L.J., whom Lindley and Bowen L.J.J. concurred, said at p.10.

"But in my opinion we are not called on to decide that point, because the policies come within the class of property acquired 'by purchase' during his life. It was contended that they did not come within the meaning of this term; but in my opinion they may be properly considered as acquired by purchase during his life. They are contracts under which the policy-holder has a right to recover certain sums of money from the insurance office in certain events, and the premiums which he pays may be considered as money paid as an investment, so as to obtain for him the benefit of the policies."

16. The distinction between shares issued and life insurance policies issued is understandable having regard to the basic nature of a life assurance contract (see Baron Parke in Dolby v The India and London Life Assurance Co., [1854] EngR 920; (1854) 15 CB 365 at p387). Thus as stated in the Digest Volume 29 at p.400 "a life policy as ordinarily understood is the purchase of a reversionary sum payable at death in consideration of a present payment of money, or as is generally the case on the payment of an annuity to the insurer during the life of the person insured. Re Commonwealth Homes & Investments Co. Ltd. (1943) SASR 211."

However what is the position re securities issued as stock in accordance with the Commonwealth Inscribed Stock Act 1911? In this regard the applicant's representative who spoke to a written address, made no reference to such Act (nor did the written address) and, whilst it was canvassed by the Commissioner's representative, the general scheme of the Act and the actual procedures involved were neither the subject of evidence nor examination by either representative.

  1. An examination of the relevant Act and the regulations made thereunder as at the time of enactment of section 26 AAA (and indeed to date) indicates that unlike the Company's Codes their provisions do regularly use terms such as sale, sold, purchase and purchase money. The Act deals with two types of securities viz "Stock" and "Treasury Bonds" and, whilst the present case deals with stock, reference will be made to its provisions in relation to both types of securities.
  2. Section 3 is a definition provision and it defines "Stock" as meaning "Commonwealth Government Inscribed Stock or Australian Consolidated Inscribed Stock" and "Treasury Bond" as including "an Australian Consolidated Treasury Bond and any coupon issued in connection with a Treasury Bond or an Australian Consolidated Treasury Bond". Part II of the Act deals with the "Creation and Issue of Stock" and Part VA with Treasury Bonds. In relation to "Stock" section 4 provides inter alia that "the Governor-General may by order create capital stock called Commonwealth Government Inscribed Stock or Australian Inscribed Stock" and section 7 provides that such "stock may be issued and sold in such amounts and manner and at such prices and on such terms and conditions (including terms and conditions as to interest) as the Governor-General directs". Section 12 provides that "all moneys raised by the sale of stock shall be placed to the credit of the Loan Fund". In relation to "Treasury Bonds" section 51A provides, inter alia, "the Governor-General may authorise the Treasurer from time to time to make out and issue bonds called Treasury Bonds or Australian Consolidated Treasury Bonds, Debentures and such other securities as are prescribed" whilst section 51B provides such "may be issued and sold in such amounts and manner, and at such price, and on such terms and conditions, as the Governor-General directs".

19. The regulations in relation to stock were in the following terms:-

"7.--(1.) An application to purchase stock, except where the application is made on a form accompanying a prospectus calling for subscriptions by public tender, shall be in accordance with Form 1.

(2.) The application shall be accompanied by the purchase money, together with any premium and accrued interest to the date of purchase.

(3.) In the case of a joint account, the application shall set forth the names in the order in which they are to appear in the Stock Ledger.

8. Upon receipt of the application and purchase money, the Registrar shall issue a receipt in accordance with Form 2.

9.--(1.) Stock Ledgers shall be in accordance with Form 3 and may be kept in a loose-leaf system or in some other system approved by the Treasurer.

(2.) The Registrar or other officer authorized to make entries in Stock Ledgers shall initial all entries made by him in a Stock Ledger.

10.—(1.) The full name, address and description of each owner of stock shall be entered in the Stock Ledger.

(2.) Where the owner of any stock changes his address he shall give notice thereof to the Registrar in accordance with Form 4 or by letter to the like effect, and the Registrar in accordance with Form 4 or by letter to the like effect, and the Registrar shall record the change of address in the Stock Ledger, and shall thereupon forward a notice in accordance with Form 5 to the stock owner at the address previously recorded in the Stock Ledger."

  1. That the use of the words "purchaser" and "sale" in relation to Government securities is of longstanding is illustrated by the debentures issued in 1895 which were the subject of consideration by the Full High Court in Bonython v The Commonwealth [1948] HCA 2; (1948) 75 CLR 589 and on appeal by the Privy Council [1950] UKPCHCA 3; (1950) 81 CLR 486. Reference to the actual words of the debentures (81 CLR at p.489) and to the judgement of Dixon J. (75 CLR at p.615) indicates that The Government Loan Act of 1894 authorised the Governor in Council (Queensland) "to raise by way of loan such several sums not exceeding two million pounds as might be required" (giving also an authority "for the sale of the debenture or Inscribed Stock securing the amounts, in places beyond the limits of Queensland, and the employment of agents for the purpose.") whilst the formal debenture itself required "the Purchaser" to indicate where he wished "the interest first falling due to be paid".
  2. A "bond" is of course quite different to a "share" in that a bond represents a contractual situation where-under the obligee has rights against the obligor if he does not keep his part of the contract. A bond is normally an instrument under seal but, in relation to "Government Bonds" this may not necessarily be so - see Halsbury 4th Edition Volume 12 at para.1390. Whilst money changes hands when both a bond or a share is "taken up" there is no resulting contract in respect of a share whereby the issuing company is committed to the shareholder for any payments in the future. No doubt the shareholder hopes he will receive a return on his investment but there are no contractual rights brought into existence as there are in relation to bonds for the payment of principal and interest.
  3. Having regard to the differing legislation involved re shares and bonds and their inherently different characteristics it is considered that the argument put forward to show that bonds are in the same category as shares not been made out. Also the respective legislation clearly shows that whilst shares are issued bonds are purchased. This is something that has been "recognised" for a substantial period of time - present prospectus still stating on "sale" - and it is logical that when section 26 AAA was enacted a special provision was inserted to deem a share issue to be a purchase but no special mention was made in relation to bonds. Bonds were already within the category of property which is purchased.
  4. For the above reasons the Tribunal affirms the decisions on the applications under review.
  5. Being of this view it is strictly unnecessary to comment on another matter that was raised on the basis of a different result. However it should be briefly mentioned for it is the first time it has been raised in the Taxation Division.
  6. As paras 6, 7 and 8 above show only part of the difference of $7,200 viz $3,654 was claimed to be a non-assessable capital surplus but was nevertheless assessed whilst objection was taken only to the amount of $3,654. Despite this it was suggested that the objection should be regarded as covering the full amount of $7,200 or alternatively (and this is the aspect to be mentioned) that "the Tribunal should, in exercise of the power granted to it under section 190(a) order that the taxpayer in each case should not be limited to the grounds stated in the objection." In this regard it was submitted "that the making of such an order would not in any way prejudice the Commissioner....the argument for the Commissioner....would be the same regardless of the quantum of the profit which is contested."
  7. Under the provisions of section 190(a) as they stood prior to the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Bill 1986 a taxpayer was "limited to the grounds stated in his objection". As the provision now stands a "taxpayer shall, unless the Tribunal or Court otherwise orders, be limited to the grounds stated in his objection." The legislation does not set out any criteria which the Tribunal is to have regard to in exercising the discretion but the Explanatory Memorandum dealing with the change stated:

"It is expected that, in exercising the discretion, the general principles on which courts have permitted amendments of pleadings in other areas of the law will generally be applied. For example, the discretion is likely to be exercised where the need for an amendment of the grounds of objection arises as a result of the Commissioner relying on arguments in defence of an assessment where the particular basis was not adverted to in the adjustment sheet accompanying the notice of assessment."

27. There is no need to decide the matter in these applications but it might be noted that the reason for the amendment sought does not arise out the particular examples given above. The exclusion of only part of the overall difference was a deliberate act on the part of the group and it is far from clear that the Commissioner would not be prejudiced. As indicated in para. 6 the resultant "interest" figure was offset by a corresponding amount to a Trust and, if that Trust would have been entitled only to a lower figure if part of the difference had not been treated as "interest", the allowance could prejudice the Commissioner. Further this is not a case where the applicant has not had the services of a well recognised firm of chartered accountants and tax consultants nor a case where it is said his agents have been careless or negligent.
