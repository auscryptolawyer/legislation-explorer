The Tribunal decides to affirm the decision of the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation made on 3 April, 1930, confirming the decisions of his delegate made on 16 October 1979:

(i) to issue under sub-section 184 (2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions "history of anxiety state" and "history of myopia" in respect of Mr Blakemore; and

(ii) that the benefit entitlement of Mr Blakemore on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 29 August 1979 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age of 65 years and having completed less than 20 years contributory service should be in accordance with subsection 66 (2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

REASONS FOR DECISION

On 29 August, 1979, Mr GH Blakemore retired from his position as an Investigation Officer in the Department of Primary Industry on the ground of invalidity. At the time of his appointment to the Public Service in 1968, he had been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account established under Part V of the now superseded Superannuation Act 1922, ("the 1922 Act") because he failed to meet the medical standards for acceptance into the Superannuation Fund established under that Act. He was, therefore, a person to whom s184 of the 1976 Act applied. The relevant provision of that section as in force at the date of Mr Blakemore's retirement were as follows:-

"184.(1) This section applies to a person who is, or at any time has been, an existing contributor other than -

(a) an existing contributor who has previously ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity, being an existing contributor to whom, upon his so ceasing, invalidity benefit was payable in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) an existing contributor who has previously ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity.

(2) Subject to sub-section (4), where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 31 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to -
(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such conditions, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions,
the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 15(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement.

(3)

(4) The Commissioner shall not, under subsection (2) or (3), issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of a person if there is a benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the person at that time.

(5)

(6)

(7) In this section –

(a) a reference to a contributor to the Fund shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the existing Fund under Part III of the superseded Act;

(b) a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act; and

(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which -

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund; or

(ii) the person was not, by virtue of a provision of the superseded Act (other than sub-section 5(1) or 79 (2) of the superseded Act) , accepted as a contributor to the Fund,

other than –

(iii) a physical or mental condition that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, did not exist -

(A)at the time the person became a contributor to the Fund or a contributor to the Provident Account; or
(B)where the person so became a contributor on more than one occasion - at the time he last so became a contributor and immediately before which he was not a contributor to the Provident Account and was not in. receipt of pension in accordance with section 45 of the superseded Act; or

(iv) a physical or mental condition that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, the Superannuation Board was, as a result of a medical examination under sub-section 79(2) of the superseded Act, satisfied no longer existed unless the Commissioner is satisfied that, but for the failure of the person to furnish any information required to be furnished by him of the furnishing of false information at or in connexion with that medical examination, the Board would not have been so satisfied.",

The Commissioner for Superannuation, having formed the opinion that-there, was the requisite causal relationship between the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Blakemore's retirement and the conditions which were relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act (see s184(7) (c) (ii) ) issued a benefit classification certificate dated 15 November 1979 in respect of the applicant specifying the following conditions as the conditions by reason of which, in his opinion, the applicant was not accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under the 1922 Act:-

"History of Anxiety State History of Myopia."

As Mr Blakemore was only 50 years of age at the date of his retirement and as his contributory service was less than 20 years, it was not disputed that the provisions of s 184(2) (a) and (b) were satisfied. There is an important question as to the possible application of s184(4), to which we shall return later, because there was in existence at the date of Mr Blakemore's retirement a document dated 11 May 1977 which purported to be a benefit classification certificate in respect to the applicant specifying only "History of Anxiety State". The main thrust of the evidence and the argument before us was directed, however, to the question whether the Tribunal should form the opinion that the necessary causal relationship required by s184(2)(c) was established.

The consequence of the issuing of the benefit classification certificate dated 15 November 1979 was that the applicant's benefit entitlement on retirement was more limited than it would have been if the incapacity which was the ground of his retirement had not been causally related to the mental and physical conditions which had in 1968 led to his rejection from the Superannuation Fund. The Commissioner's decision was made originally by his delegate on 16 October, 1979, but was confirmed on review by the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation on 3 April 1980. The applicant has applied to this Tribunal pursuant to s154 of the 1976 Act for review of the Commissioner's decision as confirmed.

In essence, the applicant's case was that his retirement was caused entirely by the deterioration in his eyesight; that his eyesight condition - specifically myopia - had not been a reason for the Superannuation Board's decision in 1968 to refuse to accept him as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund; that his non-acceptance into the Fund was due entirely to his history of "anxiety state"; and that "anxiety state" had not contributed in any degree to his early retirement. Not suprisingly, the applicant relied particularly upon the benefit classification certificate dated 11 May 1S77 which, in his submission, was still "in force at the date of his retirement and which at the least was evidence that, at the date of its issue, the Commissioner had not considered the applicant's history of myopia to have been a reason for his rejection as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund in 1968. The applicant invited us, therefore, to find:-

(i) that myopia was not a medical condition that was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act (within the meaning of S184(7)(C) of the 1975 Act); and

(ii) that myopia was the only relevant ground for his retirement.

By this path, Mr Blakemore sought to avoid the otherwise inescapable effects of s66(2) of the 1975 Act upon the amount of his retirement benefit.

We had before us as Exhibits T1-T6 the decision under review, including the reasons therefor, the relevant findings of fact and the evidence and other material on which the decision was based; other documents relevant to the review lodged with the Tribunal by the Commissioner; the letter to the applicant dated 3 April 1960 advising him of the Commissioner's decision on reconsideration and the Application for Review. We also had before us as Exhibit T7 a letter from the Deputy Ombudsman to the Registrar of the Tribunal dated 24 April 1980 enclosing a letter dated 13 April 1980 from the applicant plus enclosures which the applicant tendered as part of the documents relevant to the review. On the hearing the applicant tendered one further exhibit (Exhibit A) whilst the respondent tendered further exhibits numbered 1 to 11. The applicant gave evidence on his own behalf. For the respondent, evidence was given by Dr B.J. Hackett, a Senior Medical Officer employed in the Commonwealth Department of Health, Dr CE Genn a Commonwealth Medical Officer also employed in that Department, Mr AF Craven, a Senior Investigation Officer in the Department of Primary Industry, Mr JWH Trescowthick, Executive Officer, Management Services, Department of Primary Industry and Mr RT Cargill, Regional Director of the Public Service Board in Victoria.

In order to clearly expose the rather difficult factual and legal questions which arise for our decision, we think that it will be convenient if we first refer to the evidence as to the physical and mental conditions which were relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act and which led to Mr Blakemore's non-acceptance into the Superannuation Fund.

THE APPLICANT'S NON-ACCEPTANCE INTO THE FUND

The applicant was appointed to the Public Service on 7 February 1968, the appointment apparently taking effect from 1 April 1963. In the light of the general medical examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 12 September 1967 and subsequent specialist examinations conducted by Dr Geoffrey Sutherland, a Consultant Opthalmolcgist on 27 September 1967, and by Dr AJM Sinclair, a Consultant Psychiatrist on 2 January 1968, the Public Service Board certified on 7 February 1968, for the purposes of s5(2)(c) of the 1922 Act, that the health and physical fitness of the applicant were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account established under Part V of that Act. The reasons for this decision were not set out in the certificate itself, but the documentary evidence before us (Exhibits T4, 2, 3, 4, 7, 9 and 13) gives us a clear picture of what those reasons were.

Of course, what is required for the purposes of sl84(7)(c)(ii) of the 1976 Act is that we must ascertain what were the physical or mental conditions by reason of which the person was not accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. By virtue of s5(2) of the 1922 Act the discretion as to acceptance of the applicant as a contributor to the Fund or the Provident Account was vested in the Superannuation Board, not the Public Service Board. We were informed, however, by Mr Wilde, who appeared on behalf of the Commissioner, that at the relevant time the Public- Service Board was acting as delegate of the Superannuation Board in exercising -the discretion under s5(2) of the 1922 Act. The case was presented by the Commissioner on the basis that the reasons which led the delegate of the Public Service Board to certify that the applicant was eligible for the Provident Account also constituted the reasons of the Superannuation Board for acting on that certificate. Having no information to the contrary, and the applicant having taken no objection to our so doing, we have proceeded accordingly (cf. AK Bailey and the Commissioner for Superannuation No. 123 of 1979).

Having considered the clinical notes and the report of the medical examination conducted by the Commonwealth Medical Officer on 12 September 1967 (Exhibit T4 and Exhibit 2); copies of the specialist reports of Dr Sutherland (Exhibit 3) and Dr Sinclair (Exhibit 4); extracts from the relevant medical standards applicable to entry into the Public Service in 1963 (Exhibits T4, 9 and 10) together with copies of documents extracted from the Public Service Board file covering the events leading up to Mr Blakemore's appointment (Exhibit 7) we are in no doubt, despite Mr Blakemore's submissions to the contrary, that there were two relevant conditions in the applicant's medical history, both of which precluded any chance of his acceptance into the Superannuation Fund and both of which obviously raised serious doubts as to the wisdom of appointing him to the Public Service at all. These conditions were, firstly, myopia from which the applicant had suffered since childhood and secondly, a psychiatric disorder, described in the Commonwealth Medical Officer's clinical notes, as an "anxiety state" from which the applicant had suffered in the United Kingdom in 1953.

VISUAL STANDARDS

Paragraph 63 of the Handbook for Guidance of Common-wealth Medical Officers provides that:-

"63. 'Normal' visual may be defined as 6/6 in each eye, measured separately, either unaided or corrected. In all cases in which vision in one or both eyes is 6/24 unaided, or worse, an Eye Specialist's report is required (at Commonwealth expense)."

According to the Commonwealth Medical Officer's Report, the applicant's unaided vision in both eyes was less than 6/6 0. As required by the Handbook, therefore, he arranged for a specialist eye examination by Dr G Sutherland on 27 September 1967, which disclosed that the uncorrected visual acuity of the applicant's right eye was 2/60 and that of the left eye was 2.5/60. Apart from the myopia, which was adequately corrected by the glasses which Mr Blakemore wore, no other abnormal condition was detected by Dr Sutherland.

It is clear that, in accordance with paragraph 72 of the Handbook, Dr Sutherland had been asked to indicate in his report whether or not the applicant was likely to retain useful vision either until the normal retiring age or, alternatively, for at least seven years. Dr Sutherland's comment in relation to these questions was as follows:-

"This applicant was (scil. has) a.fairly large myopic refractive error, and it will be preferable to reexamine him in twelve months' time, when a more accurate assessment of his visual prognosis could be made."

Dr BJ Hackett a Senior Medical Officer in the Department of Health was called to give evidence as to the way in which the medical standards were applied by Commonwealth Medical Officers. He said that if the specialist's report had indicated that the applicant was likely to retain normal vision until retiring age then if that had been the only relevant medical condition, the applicant could have been considered eligible for acceptance into the Superannuation Fund. If, however, the specialist had reported that the applicant's chance of continuing till retiring age was doubtful but that his visual disability would not prevent him from carrying out his duties for at least seven years, acceptance into the Provident Account could be considered provided there were no set standards of visual acuity for the particular position (see para. 75 of the Handbook).- Dr Hackett expressed the opinion that Dr Sutherland must have considered the applicant's myopia to be unstable and that by deferring his prognosis for twelve months there was a real question whether the applicant should even be accepted into the Provident Account.

Mr Blakemore, on the other hand, pointed to the fact that despite Dr Sutherland's recommendation, he was in fact accepted into the Provident Fund without any deferment to enable a further specialist eye examination to be conducted. He invited us to infer, therefore, that the condition of myopia was not seen as of any consequence.

In our view, however, it is evident from the papers which went to the Public Service Board that, having regard to the standards of visual acuity normally required, the applicant's unaided vision was seriously deficient. Based on one examination, Dr Sutherland was not prepared to formulate any prognosis in the terms required by paragraph 75 of the Handbook. Far from indicating that the myopia was not a cause for concern, we think that Dr Sutherland's report points to the likely seriousness of the condition in the future. There is also evidence before us that there were no other suitable applicants for the position for which the applicant had applied and that filling the position had become a matter of urgency. In these circumstances, we think the proper inference to be drawn is that, notwithstanding the absence of any evidence confirming that the applicant met even the minimum standards of visual acuity required by the medical guidelines, it was decided nevertheless to appoint the applicant to the position and to accept him into the Provident Account. If the matter rested there we would have no difficulty in finding as a fact that the applicant's history of myopia was, in terms of s184(7)(c)(ii) of the 1975 Act, a physical condition by reason of which the applicant was not accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund.

PSYCHIATRIC CONDITION

The other respect in which Mr Blakemore failed to meet the medical standards was equally serious. Paragraphs 105 and 106 of the Guidelines provided as follows:-

"105. A nervous condition of sufficient severity to require electro-convulsive therapy, sub-coma insulin, full coma insulin or similar therapy would disqualify. Prolonged therapy including psychoanalytical measures would also disqualify.

106. Experience with persons having histories of such illnesses has been most unfavourable. However, a single, minor episode of short duration followed by freedom from recurrence and absence of signs and symptoms for three years may not preclude acceptance under Provident Account conditions after a maximum period of probation. The full history of the case will be required."

Dr Hackett told us that on a strict interpretation of para 105, the applicant was unfit either for employment in the Public Service or for acceptance into the Provident Account. He noted that the Commonwealth Medical Officer had recorded the following comments in his clinical record of the applicant's medical examination on 12 September 1967:-

"PH (scil. "past history") 'anxiety state' 1958. Following accountancy exams in UK developed delusions of persecution. Was in hospital 3/12,. had insulin treatment. Following this never had any further symptoms, feels quite well. Gives impression of being a rather rigid type of person. Could come under provisions of para. 106."

The Commonwealth Medical Officer's clinical record concluded:-

"Defer 11/12, then consider for Provident. See also eye report to be sent for further exam in next examination."

In the light of these comments by the Commonwealth Medical Officer, Mr Blakemore was requested on 25 October 1967 to authorise the disclosure of the clinical notes regarding his treatment in the UK in 1958. This the applicant duly did and on 5 November 1957 Dr JA Hutchinson, Consultant Psychiatrist of St Edward's Hospital, Cheddleton, UK wrote to the Public Service Board advising that it was not the practice of the Hospital to release clinical notes. He then added the following information:-

"I am writing to let you know that this man was admitted to our hospital on the 11th November, 1958 and discharged on the 2ist February, 1959. His symptoms were very troublesome at the time but he made a very good response to treatment and I have not seen him since that time. I think the outlook must be reasonably good."

It will be noted that this letter contained no specific reference to the precise nature of the illness which had caused Mr Blakemore' s admission to hospital.

A note on the Public Service Board file dated 15 November 1967 indicates that Dr Thompson (presumably a Commonwealth Medical Officer) was inclined to accept Mr Blakemore under Provident Account conditions "despite his ECT treatment", but that in order to make up for the lack of information from the UK, Dr Thompson preferred to have Mr Blakemore examined by a Psychiatrist. This led to the examination by Dr Alex Sinclair on 2 January 196 8. His report, dated the following day, needs to be set out in full:-

"I examined the abovenamed at your request on 2nd January 1958. Mr Blakemore told me in review of his history that after an only fair education he decided, whilst still in employment, to do an accountancy course and did this in conjunction with a job, completing this task in 18 months when it usually takes 4-5 years. During this time he cut himself off from social activity, led a somewhat isolated life and was successful, but some time after this his father, to whom he was closely attached died, and he attended a doctor whom he thinks treated him for 3 months with "purple hearts" (Drinamyl) in the dosage of 4 per day. He then recounts a story which seems to indicate that at that time he developed paranoidal delusions. He entered a hospital as a voluntary patient for a period of about 3 months and was treated with a rather short course of insulin therapy in Stoke on Trent.

Almost immediately following discharge he sought a job and became a chartered accountant in a British firm between 1958-64 without further interruption. By this time he had reached the age where progress in Britain would be difficult so he emigrated. In Australia he has held responsible positions as an accountant in quite substantial firms but seeks security for the future in a job as a public servant. It is not surprising that this man should choose security having been rather "shaken" by his illness in 1958. He has made good social and emotional contacts in this country and appears to be quite a successfully adjusted migrant. He is now no longer isolated emotionally.

On interview he presents as an intelligent man with no evidence of schizoid or other psychotic thinking at all. He presents no evidence of any significant neurosis. He has full insight, his plans for the future are realistic and my view is that this man is absolutely no residual features of the short psychotic illness he had in Great Britain.

Concerning the genesis of the illness, in 1958 it may well have been due in part or in whole to the toxic effect of amphetamine which is well known as a factor in producing a paranoid psychosis.

Even if the psychosis were psychogenically determined, the personality and social factors which produced it now no longer exist.

My opinion is that Mr Blakemore is probably a well trained accountant, who is fit for permanent appointment for the post for which he is applying and that he stands no greater risk of recurrence of psychiatric illness than any average member of the population.

I have personally assured Mr Blakemore of his future in relation to likelihood of recurrence."

The favourable nature of Dr Sinclair's report apparently dispelled any doubts as to the wisdom of appointing Mr Blakemore to the Public Service, because the next event of any significance is an endorsement on the face page of Mr Blakemore's "Medical Examination for Permanent Appointment" (Exhibit 2) made by Mr A. Barritt-Eyles, the delegate of the Public Service Board, dated 25 January 1967 (scil. 25/1/63) saying "Accept P/A". The applicant became a contributor to the Provident Account on 1 April 1968.

Throughout the hearing of the case, it was accepted as common ground by both parties that Mr Blakemore's episode of psychiatric illness in 1953 was properly described as "anxiety state". In his evidence, Mr Blakemore referred to the episode as "nervous trouble". However, the history of the illness given by Mr Blakemore to the Commonwealth Medical Officer on 12 September 1967, and later to Dr Sinclair - in particular the reference to delusions of persecution - is more consistent in our view with a psychosis than a neurosis. Indeed Br Sinclair, whose report is the only psychiatric evidence presented in this matter, refers to the 1958 occurrence as a "short psychotic illness" and by inference as a "paranoid psychosis". The suggestion by Dr Sinclair that the psychosis may well have been due in part or in whole to the toxic effect of amphetamine, which we understand to have been an ingredient in Drinanyl, is certainly feasible. Thus, accepting Dr Sinclair's report, we would have thought that the proper finding was that the psychiatric condition from which the applicant suffered in 1958 was not an anxiety state, as he apparently believed, but was more probably a psychosis, possibly of toxic origin.

THE BENEFIT CLASSIFICATION CERTIFICATE DATED 11 MAY 1977

When the 1922 Act was superseded by the 1976 Act (Act No 31 of 1976), transitional provisions were necessary (inter alia) to bring existing contributors to the Provident Account under the new scheme. S184 was part of the mechanism for this transition. As originally enacted and as in force at the date on which the document dated 11 May 1977 was issued, s184 was considerably different from its present form. The section as set out at the commencement of these reasons was substituted by Act No 17 of 1978 which was in force at the date of the applicant's retirement. As originally enacted, the section provided (so far as relevant) as follows:- "

184(1) Subject to sub-section (2) of this section, section 16 does not apply to an existing contributor. (2) For the purposes of this Act (including sub-sections 16(5), (7) and (3)), there shall be deemed to be in force, on the commencing day, in respect of an eligible employee -

(a) who is an existing contributor and who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act;

(b) whose period of contributory service on the commencing day would have been less than 20 years if he had ceased to be an eligible employee on that day; and

(c) who at the commencement of this Act, had not attained his maximum retiring age,

a benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner under sub-section 15(4) specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act.

(3)

(4) "

Because of their relevance "to the subsequent developments in this matter, it is also desirable at this stage to set cut in their original form the provisions of s16(6), (7) and (3) which are referred to in the original sl84(2) set out above:-

"(6) Where a benefit classification certificate is in force in respect of an eligible employee, he may, on grounds specified in the request, request the Commissioner to revoke or vary the certificate and where an eligible employee makes such a request, he shall, within such period as the Commissioner requires, undergo such further medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner requires.

(7) A report or reports of the result or results of the further medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner.

(3) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports and such other matters (if any) as he considers relevant, and

(a) where there is not more than 1 physical or mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate and the Conmissioner is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with that condition, to cease to be an eligible employee before he attains his maximum retiring age - the Commissioner shall revoke the certificate;

(b) where there is more than 1 physical or mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate -

(i) if the Commissioner is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with those conditions, to cease to continue to be an eligible employee before the person attains his maximum retiring age - the Commissioner shall revoke the certificate; or

(ii) if the Commissioner is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with 1 or some only of the conditions specified in the certificate, to continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age - the Commissioner shall vary the Certificate by deleting the reference in the certificate to any other condition or conditions so specified; and

(c) in any other case - the Commissioner shall refuse the request to revoke or vary the benefit classification certificate."

On 30 September 1975 the applicant requested advice from the Commissioner for Superannuation as to the physical or mental conditions which had caused him to be accepted into the Provident Aceonnt. The Commissioner treated this request as an application pursuant to s16 (6) of the 1976 Act to revoke the benefit classification certificate deemed by s184 of that Act to be in force in respect of the applicant and required the applicant to undergo a medical examination. This the applicant did on 7 April 1977 before Dr CA Germ, a general practitioner employed as a Commonwealth Medical Officer.

Dr Genn said that he understood that the purpose of the examination was to enable him to recommend whether or not the applicant's benefit classification certificate should be changed. Unfortunately, however, he appears to have become preoccupied with consideration of the applicant's psychiatric condition and to have ignored the clear evidence from his own examination and from the original medical records of the respects in which the applicant failed to meet the required medical standards. He conceded in evidence that this was an oversight on his part.

Dr Genn's clinical notes disclose that the applicant's unaided vision in both eyes was still less than 6/50 and he recorded having noted the specialist's eye report. He also noted the psychiatric report and added the comment "brief psychosis". At the foot of the Medical Report he made the following, observations, which are consistent with his clinical notes:-

" He is rigid, obsessive, introverted and rather paranoid about his non-promotion." A trifle grandiose. Possibly delusional. Thinks Dairy Board have tried to silence him".

The only other relevant details from Dr Genn's clinical notes are that he records the applicant's previous history as "1957 nervous breakdown - in hospital 3/12"; he records the opinion "Personality difficulties" and concludes "BCC - 'anxiety state'".

Because the parties were both apparently satisfied that 'anxiety state' correctly described the psychiatric episode in 1958, no attention was directed during the hearing to the apparent contradiction between Dr Genn's reference in his Medical Report to the U.K. episode as a "brief psychosis" and his recommendation that the benefit classification certificate should specify "anxiety state", which we understand to be a form of neuroris. Indeed, with due respect to Dr Genn, the symptoms which he observed in 1977 would appear more consistent with a possible psychosis than with a neurosis. Be that as it may, insofar as his report provides evidence as to the applicant's psychiatric history at the time he joined the Public Service, it confirms our view that "history of psychosis" is a more accurate description of the applicant's illness than is "history of anxiety state".

On the front of the Medical Report Form (Exhibit T4), Dr Genn completed two printed sections of the report. The first of those read as follows:-

"On the basis of my examination, I am of the opinion that the applicant's Benefit Classification Certificate should not be varied."

The second statement was in the following form:-

"After clinical examination, medical tests and specialist advice, as appropriate, I consider the applicant does not meet general medical standard due to the following mental/physical condition(s).

"Mental/Physical Condition (s) suffered Anxiety State"

As will be seen, no reference was made to myopia.

In his evidence, Dr Genn attempted to explain that when seating the opinion that the certificate should not be varied, what he meant in effect was that all relevant conditions be retained on the certificate. However, the printed word carried the day because following receipt of Dr Genn's report and in apparent reliance upon its accuracy in identifying all the conditions "by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept (the applicant) as a contributor under Part III of the superseded Act" (see s184(2)), the Commissioner wrote to the applicant on 11 May 1977 advising as follows (omitting formal parts):-

"I refer to your request to revoke your Benefit Classification Certificate.

2.The Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation has directed that a Benefit Classification Certificate remain in force in your case.
3.A. copy of the certificate is enclosed for your information."

The enclosed certificate, which purported to be a benefit classification certificate dated 11 May, 1977, relating to the applicant and which was signed by a Delegate of the Commissioner specified "History of Anxiety State" beneath the following printed certification::-

"After considering the report(s) of the medical examination undertaken by the abovenamed in relation to

initial benefit entitlement under the Superannuation Act; or

a request made to the Commissioner for Superannuation to revoke or vary a Benefit Classification Certificate application to that person:

(appropriate box::)

I am of the opinion that he/she is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with the undermentioned physical or mental condition(s), to continue to be an eligible employee until he/she attains his/her maximum retiring age."

Although the appropriate box was not ticked, the Certificate must obviously be read as referring to the applicant's request for revocation. Whilst Mr Blakemore may have been surprised or even relieved to find no mention of his myopia, there was no way in which he could have known of the series of mistakes which had preceded the issue of this apparently valid certificate.

THE INCAPACITY WHICH WAS THE GROUND FOR APPLICANT'S RETIREMENT

In August, 1973, having noted a deterioration in his eyesight which he associated with the onset of middle age, Mr Blakemore consulted an eye specialist, Dr B Coote who gave him a proscription for new glasses. The correction, according to Mr Blakemore, was 9.25 in one eye and 10.25 in the other. He said that Dr DJ Burne, the general practitioner who had referred him to Dr Coote, had been rather amazed at the extent of this correction. In January 1979 Mr Blakemore went to see Dr Burne with thoughts of retiring because of the increased problems with his vision. On 24 January 1979 Dr Burne wrote the following letter to the Commonwealth Medical Officer:-

"Dear Doctor,

Re: Mr J. Blakemore

This man suffers from a gross degree of myopia, which requires him to have very thick lenses in his glasses. He also suffers from quite bad presbyopia. He has attended Dr B Coote who confirms this.

He suffers from severe eyestrain due to his clerical work, and also on driving a car. In view of this, I feel it would be justified that he be retired, on medical grounds."

On 2 February 1979 Mr Blakemore was referred for examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer so that an assessment could be made whether he should be retired on the grounds of incapacity for further service. This examination was conducted by Dr de Laine on 5 February 1979 and whilst we had his clinical notes and report before us, Dr de Laine was unfortunately not able to give evidence due to his absence on long service leave. His clinical notes record the following comments:-

"Apparently considerable difficulty with his vision while attending to clerical duties in office, as well as driving car. (Examined by Dr Sutherland in 1967). Personality aberration (paranoid). Disputatious, argumentative.

To be retired."

It is apparent that following the last mentioned entry "to be retired", Dr de Laine originally wrote "Myopia and Presbyopia 100%". For reasons which, in his absence, could not be explained, he later altered his clinical notes and his medical report by changing "100%" to "50% and by adding "Personality aberration (paranoid features) 50%". By way of side note Dr de Laine added "Intends to find employment as manager of a motel. Obviously Carlisle's dictum applies - The best of all possible worlds". In the Medical Report sent by Dr de Laine to the Department, he described the applicant's condition as follows:-

"Severe myopia and presbyopia. Allegedly suffers from severe eye strain due to his clerical work and also when driving a car. Personality aberration considered to exist. Stated will take on 'managing a motel'.

Prognosis: Condition stationary it seems."

Dr de Laine considered that Mr Blakemore was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity but that he may, in due course, partially or completely recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment.

Mr JWH Trescowthick, the Executive Officer, Management Services, Department of Primary Industry told us that following receipt of Dr de Laine's report (which he recalled seeing in its altered form), Mr Blakemore went on sick leave for approximately 6 months. On 21 August 1979 on instructions from the Public Service Board, he directed that a further medical examination be arranged to ensure that a comprehensive assessment of the applicant's condition was made.

As a consequence of this direction Mr Blakemore was again medically examined, as it happened, by Dr Genn on 23 August 1979. Dr Genn's clinical notes of that examination are as follows:-

"Attending CAE; meeting people. Doesn't anticipate employment. He feels his notion of motel management was bravado, no figure work now. Sleeps well. Mo medication.Seldom drives a car and never at night.

Only people I didn't get on with. Perhaps some professional jealosy. They were a bit amateurish.

Doesn't feel he could sit at a desk and do figure work. He feels his previous history (psychiatrically) was stationary.

O/E Psychiatrically presents well. Myopia Presbyopia 100%"

Dr Genn's medical report described the applicant's condition and prognosis as follows:-

"For many years has suffered from myopia and presbyopia R < 6/60 L < 6/60 converts to 6/5 and 6/6. He works as an accountant and has great difficulty with figures and suffers from severe eye strain. The presbyopia is worsening. .Report from Dr D Burne - noted.Diagnosis: Poor "

Or Genn certified Mr Blakemore as unfit for continued employment but without the qualification which Dr de Laine had added as to his possible future fitness for work.

In his evidence,- Dr Genn said that he was aware when he examined Mr Blakemore of Dr de Laine's opinion regarding personality aberration but that there was no evidence of any personality disorder in .August 1979. Mr Blakemore's problem was entirely with his eyes in Dr Genn's opinion.

Mr R.T. Cargill, -the Regional Director of the Public Service Board in Victoria who had the sole responsibility in Victoria for the retirement of staff under the Public Service Act was called to give evidence as to the circumstances leading up to the applicant's retirement and as to his reasons for approving that retirement.

Being concerned at the reference in Dr de Laine's report of 5 February 1979 to the possibility of Mr Blakemore recovering and becoming fit to resume employment, Mr Cargill's deputy gave instructions for a further medical examination to be carried out. On receipt of Dr Genn's report dated 29 August 1979, however, Mr Cargill said that he was satisfied that Mr Blakemore should be retired on the ground of invalidity and gave his approval accordingly on 7 September 1979. He said that he had based his decision entirely on Dr Genn's report and not on the earlier report of Dr de Laine.

Notwithstanding the evidence of Dr Genn and Mr Cargill as to the reason for Mr Blakemore's retirement, Mr Wilde also led evidence from Mr A.F. Craven, a Senior Investigation Officer in the Department of Primary Industry, who had acted as Mr Blakemore's supervisor for most of the applicant's eleven years in the Department, as to his observation of the applicant in the discharge of his duties and in his conduct and attitude to his fellow employees. Mr Craven said that the applicant's work had been good right up to the time that he left the Department in February 1979. There had been no indication of any problems with his work due to his eyesight, although he was aware that Mr Blakemore's glasses had been upgraded within the twelve months before his retirement, suggesting some natural deterioration.

Mr Craven said, however, that from about the middle of 1974 considerable tension developed between Mr Blakemore and some of his fellow employees. Whilst no reasons were given, the officers informed Mr Craven that they did not wish to work with the applicant. Most of the problems arose when an officer was working in the field with Mr Blakemore. When the officers were together working on reports in the office, "there was always tension". Mr Blakemore, he said, would be the only officer talking and he would be passing provocative remarks. Mr Craven was aware, however, that in February 1979 the applicant went to the Commonwealth Medical Officer to see whether he could be retired on account of his eyesight. There was no suggestion at the time that Mr Blakemore was to see the Commonwealth Medical Officer for any psychiatric reason. Mr Craven said that he was surprised when he heard that the applicant was to be retired on account of his eyesight.

Mr Blakemore was cross-examined on his relationships with his fellow employees. His assessment was that other people "hated my guts". He confirmed that there had been personality clashes with other employees, the first of which occurred shortly after he joined the Department. He said that he preferred to work alone because he was very quick and a very well trained accountant. His fellow officers were not professionals.

Mr Blakemore said that in his eleven years with the Department he had not been promoted. Although he had acted for short periods as a Senior Investigation Officer, it had been made clear to him in a letter dated 2 5 March 19 74 from the Acting Assistant Secretary, Management Services Branch of the Department (Exhibit A) that whilst his work was good, his relationships with his supervisors, subordinates and management were such that the Department could not strongly support his temporary transfer to the Section head position for other than short periods.

Whilst it is fairly clear from this evidence, and from our impressions of Mr Blakemore's evidence as a whole, that he experienced considerable difficulties in relating satisfactorily to his fellow employees, and whilst both Dr Genn's report dated -7 April 1977 and Dr de Laine's report dated 5 February 1979 provide confirmation that the applicant exhibited clinically observable symptoms of personality difficulties, the evidence does not, in our view, establish that these personality difficulties constituted the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. The applicant's evidence was that he had not sought or received any psychiatric treatment since 1953. There is no suggestion that he was recommended by his Department for retirement on psychiatric grounds. Furthermore, because of what appears to us to be the quite misdirected emphasis upon "anxiety state", there is no satisfactory psychiatric evidence before us to establish any causal link or connection between whatever personality disorder from which the applicant may have been suffering prior to his retirement and the psychotic episode in 1953.

Thus, assuming as Mr Wilde submitted that the Tribunal can go behind the medical report on which the recommendation for retirement was based (cf. Re Keith and Acting Commissioner for Suparannuation (1979) 2 ALN 165), we do not consider that our conclusion on the evidence in this case would be altered. That evidence establishes that the applicant sought and was ultimately granted retirement due to incapacity flowing from the deterioration in his eyesight during 1978. We find that the incapacity was caused by myopia and presbyopia and, subject to our consideration of the events of 1977 culminating in the issue of the certificate dated 11 May, 1977, we would have no hesitation in finding that the incapacity was caused or was substantially contributed to by a physical condition, namely myopia, that was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act.

THE BENEFIT CLASSIFICATION CERTIFICATE DATED 11 MAY 1977

This brings us back to the most difficult aspect of this case, namely the legal force, if any, to be attributed to the document purporting to be a benefit classification certificate dated 11 May 1977 specifying "history of anxiety state". During his submissions, Mr Blakemore, who was unrepresented, advanced two propositions with regard to this document:.-

(i) he submitted that the certificate was still in force and that it had never been cancelled; and

(ii) he submitted that the certificate was evidence that the reason for his acceptance into the Provident Account was his history of anxiety state.

Neither proposition was developed beyond its mere assertion and, whilst it is no criticism of the applicant that he was unable to appreciate the difficulties which these submissions raised, the Tribunal has had no assistance on these questions other than that provided on behalf of the Commissioner. As a result we have no confidence that all relevant issues have been fully explored.

In our view, however, a decision on the first submission is essential to the resolution of this application having regard to the opening words of s134 (2) of the 1975 Act as amended which make the application of that subsection subject to the provisions of s184(4). That subsection, it will be recalled, provided as follows:-

"(4) The Commissioner shall not, under subsection (2) or (3), issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of a person if there is a benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the person at that time."

If the certificate dated 11 May, 1977, which on its face certainly purports to be a benefit classification certificate, is a certificate to which s184(4) applies, the Commissioner had no power to issue the later certificate dated 15 November 1979. In the light of our finding that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was the deterioration in his eyesight, the consequence of such a conclusion would be that s66(2) would not apply and the applicant would be entitled to the invalidity benefits payable in accordance with s66(1) of the Act.

In order to complete the relevant legislative background, it is desirable to set out the provisions of subsoctiens (2) (3) and (4) of s77 of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1973 (Act No 17 of 1973), noting that it was by subsection (1) of s77 that the original s184 of the 1976 Act was repealed and substituted in its present form:-

"(2) Subject to sub-sections (3) and (4) of this section, sub-section 134(2) of the Principal Act shall be deemed never to have applied to or in relation to existing contributors who did not cease to be eligible employees before the commencement of this section.

(3) Where the benefit classification certificate deemed, by sub-section 134 (2) of the Principal Act, to be in force, on the commencing day, in respect of a person has been revoked under sub-section 15(3) of the Principal Act before the commencement of this section, the Commissioner for Superannuation shall not include the condition or conditions that was or were specified in that benefit classification certificate in any benefit classification certificate issued in respect of the person under section 134 of the Principal Act as amended by sub-section (1) of this section unless the Commissioner is satisfied that, but for the failure of the person to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or the furnishing of false information at or in connexion with a medical examination which the person was required to undergo under. sub-section 16(6) of the Principal Act, the certificate would not have been revoked.

(4) Where the benefit classification certificate deemed, by sub-section 184(2) of the Principal Act, to be in force, on the commencing day, in respect of a person has been varied under sub-section 16(3) of the Principal Act before the commencement of this section by deleting from the certificate a reference to a physical or mental condition or conditions, the Commissioner for Superannuation shall not include that condition or those conditions in any benefit classification certificate issued in respect of the person under section 134 of the Principal Act as amended by sub-section (1) of this section unless the Commissioner is satisfied that, but for the failure of the person to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or the furnishing of false information at or in connextion with a medical examination which the person was required to undergo under sub-section 15(6) of the Principal Act, the certificate would not have been so varied."

Mr Wilde submitted that the certificate dated 11 May 1977 carried no legal consequences at all. It was swept aside by s77(2) of the 1973 Act and was not affected by either subsection (3) or (4) of s77. At the date of the applicant's retirement, therefore, namely 29 August 1979, Mr Wilde submitted that there was no benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the applicant.

Having regard to the fact that under S134(2) of the 1975 Act as originally enacted the benefit classification certificate referred to in that section was, "deemed to be in force" by virtue of that subsection, we think it is clear firstly, that the operation of the section did not depend upon the Commissioner issuing a physical document. The certificate was brought into notional existence by force of the section itself. Furthermore, on the findings we have made as to the physical and mental conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor to the Fund, that certificate as deemed to be in force by virtue of s184 must be taken, at the date of commencement of the 1975 Act, as having specified:

(1)History of psychosis
(2)History- of myopia

Had nothing occurred in relation to the "deemed" certificate prior to the repeal and substitution of s134 by Act No 17 of 1978, it is equally clear that the "deemed" certificate would be taken never to have existed (see s77(2) of the 1978 Act) . But what is the position when the Commissioner has professedly considered an application to vary or revoke the certificate, has stated that the certificate must "remain in force" (see letter to Mr Blakemore dated 11 May, 1977 - Exhibit T4) , and has issued a document in the form of a benefit classification certificate stating that after considering the report of the medical examination in relation to the applicant's request for revocation or variation of the certificate, he is of the opinion that the applicant is unlikely by reason of or for a reason connected with his history of anxiety state to continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring ago?

Mr Wilde submitted that -

(i) there was no authority under s18 4 or s16 of the Act as it then stood for the Commissioner to issue the document dated 11 May 1977; and

(ii) in any event, the evidence before the Tribunal established that, due to the mistakes in Dr Genn's medical report dated 7 April 1977, the Commissioner had never applied his mind to the question whether the "deemed" certificate should be varied by deleting the reference to myopia. The Commissioner could not be found, therefore, to have varied the certificate within the meaning of s77(4) of the 1978 Act.

Dealing firstly with this latter submission,-we agree as we earlier indicated, that the "deemed" certificate brought into • force at the date of commencement of the 1976 by the original s184(2) must be taken to have specified history of myopia. It is significant in our view that neither the certificate itself nor the covering letter dated 11 May 1977 referred to any variation of the deemed certificate by deleting the reference to myopia. We have considered the possibility that, having regard to the provisions of s16(8) of the 1976 Act, the Commissioner must be taken, as a matter of necessary implication, to have varied the deemed certificate by deleting myopia, but in the light of the evidence which satisfies us that the Commissioner did net apply his mind to that condition, we agree with Mr Wilde's submission that such an inference is not open to us. We therefore find as a fact that the "deemed" certificate was not varied by the Commissioner on 11 May 1977 and that, accordingly, s77(4) of the 1973 Act did not have any application to the applicant.

As to the certificate itself, we can find no provision in the 1975 Act as it stood on 11 May, 1977, which either required or authorised the issue of a benefit classification certificate consequent upon the Commissioner's consideration of an application to vary a "deemed" certificate. We note, of course, that the document was in fact served upon the applicant and that s16(12) of the 1976 Act provides as follows:-

"(12) A copy of a benefit classification certificate, and a copy of any revocation or variation of such a certificate, and notice of refusal by the, Commissioner of a request to revoke or vary such a certificate, shall be served on the person to whom the certificate relates or, if the person has died, on the personal representatives of the person or such other person or persons as the Commissioner, in his discretion, determines."

This subsection, however, is not one of the subsections of s16 which, by force of the original S184(2) of the 1976 Act, applied to an existing contributor such as Mr Blakemore. As a consequence, there was no specific obligation under the Act as it then stood, for the Commissioner to notify the applicant as to the result of his consideration of the application for revocation or variation, nor was there any special requirement as to the form of any notification that the Commissioner might see fit to give.

In the circumstances, we have concluded that the document dated 11 May, 1977, despite its form, does not have any legal effect as a benefit classification certificate for the purposes of the Act. As neither the saving provision in s77(4) of the 1976 Act nor the provisions of s184(4) of the 1976 Act as amended applies, we are of the opinion that the Commissioner was entitled to exercise the powers conferred by S184(2) of the 1976 Act as it stood when the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of his retirement on the ground of invalidity.

ESTOPPEL

It only remains to consider Mr Blakemore's second submission that the certificate dated 11 May 1977 should be seen as evidence that the reason for his acceptance into the Provident Account was his history of anxiety state. Viewed simply as part of the totality of the evidence before us on that question, we would have no difficulty in rejecting the certificate as of any assistance to us in determining what medical conditions were in fact relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act. It was clearly vitiated by mistake.

Hr Wilde, however, quite properly dealt with the submission on the wider basis that the certificate might be seen as creating an estoppel. Relying on the decision of Dunn J in Jauncey and the State Service Superannuation Board (Supreme Court of Queensland - Appeal No. 42 of 1977 - unreported) Mr Wilde submitted that Mr Blakemore was precluded from setting up an estoppel which would prevent the proper operation of the Act. That case concerned misleading advice given by an officer of the State Service Superannuation Board to the appellant to the effect that he would be eligible for a pension if he retired after attaining the age of 50 years. In fact, on the proper consideration of the appellant's terms of employment and. the circumstances of his retirement, there was no power under the State Service Superannuation Act 1972 for the Board to grant him a pension. His Honour held that estoppel could not release the Board from its obligation to obey the statute and the appeal therefore failed.

It is clear of course that the powers of a statutory authority cannot be extended by the doctrine of estoppel (see the authorities cited . in Re Callaghan and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1978) 1 ALD .227). Furthermore, in respect of a statute enacted for the benefit of a section of the public estoppel cannot be raised to release either the statutory authority administering the Act or any member of the public from the obligation to obey duties of a positive kind imposed by the statute (see Maritime Electric Co Ltd v. General Dairies Ltd . (1937) AC 610. The principle in the Maritime Electric Co Ltd case was expressly limited to statutory provisions falling within the same category as the Public Utilities Act of New Brunswick then under consideration by the Privy Council (see at 520). The relevant sections imposed a positive duty upon a public utility to charge and to receive standard non-discriminatory rates for electricity. The principle of that case has been either applied or distinguished in a series of subsequent cases presenting almost identical factual circumstances (see Roma Electric Light and power Co Ltd v Hair (1955) St.R.Qld 311; cf. Taranaki Electric-power Board v. Proprietors of Puketapu 3A Block Inc (1958) NZLR 297; and Morth West County District Council v J.I. Case (Aust) Pty Ltd (1974) 2 NSWLR 511).

The application of the principle in a slightly different context arose recently in State of SA v Atsa Pty Ltd (1980) 29 ALR 367. In relation to a section of the Business Franchise (Tobacco) Act 1974 (S.A.) imposing a licence fee for a tobacco licence payable by instalments Wells AJ at 397 said:

"In my opinion, the Act in question here, being a statute designed to raise revenue, must be regarded as enacted for the benefit of the public, for the purpose for which a government raises revenue is to enable it to carry on government and semi-government activities in a way which it believes will be for the public good and to make- expenditure of various kinds with the same object. I do not see that in this regard it matters that there will always be some members of the public who will not agree that some or all of those activities or expenditure are for the public benefit. The law as to estoppel against a statute enacted for the benefit of a section of the public is authoritatively laid down in Maritime Electric Co Ltd v. General Dairies Ltd (1937) AC 610, a decision of the Privy Council."

In the contest of powers conferred upon the Crown by statute or by common law Lord Denning M.R. in Laker Airways Led v. Department of Trade [1976] EWCA Civ 10; (1977) 1 QB 643 commented upon the concept of "public benefit" as follows at 707:

"The Attorney-General concedes that estoppel could in suitable circumstances be raised against the Crown: but he contends this was not a case for it. The lav; on this subject has developed a good deal lately. The underlying principle is that the Crown cannot be estopped from exercising its powers, whether given in a statute or by common law, when it is doing so in the proper exercise of its duty to act for the.public good, even though this may work some injustice or unfairness to a private individual: see Maritime Electric Co Ltd v. General Dairies Ltd (1937) AC 610, where the Privy Council, unfortunately, I think, reversed the Supreme Court of Canada (1935) SCR 519. It can, however, be estopped when it is not properly exercising its powers, but is misusing them; and it does misuse them if it exercises them in circumstances which work injustice or unfairness to the individual without any countervailing benefit for the public: see Robertson v. Minister of Pensions "(1949) 1 KB 227; Reg, v. Liverpool Corporation, Ex parte Liverpool Taxi Fleet Operators ' Association (1972) 2 QB 299 and HTV Ltd v Price Commission (1976) ICR 170, 185-186."

For a recent discussion of the relevant authorities with respect to estoppel and the exercise of statutory powers, as opposed to statutory duties, see Re Bundy and the Secretary. Department of Housing and Construction (No. 12030 of 1979).

Despite the express reservation by the Privy Council of its opinion with respect to statutes not falling within the same category as the Public Utilities Act of New Brunswick, the Maritime Electric Co Ltd case appears to have become accepted as authority for the unqualified proposition that estoppel cannot be set up if its establishment will result in preventing the performance of a statutory duty (see, for example, Spencer Bower and Turner on Estoppel by Representation, 3rd edn. p.149 of. De Smith, Judicial Review of Administrative Action, 3rd edn. p.273). Indeed there are other decisions in the United Kingdom which would appear to support this conclusion (see Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Brooks (1915) AC 473 at 491; and Society of Medical Officers of Health v Hope (1960) AC 551 at 568 per Lord Keith . On the present state of the authorities, therefore, we accept that to be the law.

Turning then to the relevant sections of the Superannuation Act 1976, what is the nature of the Commissioner's function? Both in its original form and as it now stands s184 (2) of the 1976 Act proceeds on the premise that the physical or mental condition or conditions in respect of an existing contributor which were relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act will be capable of objective ascertainment. The only element of discretion conferred on the Commissioner by s184(2)(c) (cf. s66(2)(c)) is in the power to form an opinion on the critical question whether the death of a person or the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement had the necessary causal relationship with the relevant physical or mental condition or conditions.

Whilst the Commissioner is vested with the general administration both of the 1922 Act and of the 1976 Act (see s18), he is not acting in the, exercise of any discretionary power when informing his mind as to the relevant medical conditions for the purposes of the 1922 Act. Although it is true that, in a practical sense, the Commissioner must evaluate all the available evidence in order to ascertain what medical conditions were relevant, he has no discretionary power to disregard any condition which, on a proper consideration of the evidence, he ought to have found to have been relevant. Otherwise the scheme of the Act with respect to existing contributors would be distorted and claims upon the Superannuation Fund might be admitted which, on proper consideration of the evidence, were not entitled to be admitted. As benefits under the Act can, in certain circumstances, be a charge, in part at least, upon the Consolidated Revenue Fund (see s112), there is a public interest in the Act being properly administered. In our view, therefore, s184 imposes a duty on those responsible for the administration of the Act to ascertain correctly what the relevant conditions were for the purposes of the 1922 Act.

The question is - can the Commissioner (or, on appeal, this Tribunal) be precluded from the proper discharge of this statutory duty by estoppel? From the authorities we have cited the answer to that question must, in our opinion, be in the negative. We would add, however, that if we were wrong in that conclusion we do not think that there is any evidence before us to show that the applicant acted to his detriment in reliance upon the correctness of any representations contained in the letter and certificate dated 11 May 1977 (see Norfolk County Council v. Secretary of State for the Environment (1973) 1 WLR 1400 at 1405). If, as we have found, the applicant's eyesight deteriorated during 1973 to the point where he could not continue working, his retirement on that ground was inevitable. The certificate could not, on that promise, have been the cause of his decision to retire although its existence may have given him the impetus to seek retirement earlier than he might otherwise have done. Accordingly, as we do not think that the applicant can rely on the letter and certificate dated 11 May 1977 so as to create an estoppel against the proper application of s184 of the 1976 Act as amended, we have concluded that Mr Blakemore's application for review cannot succeed.

Although, in our view, the evidence as to the applicant's psychiatric treatment in 1958 more probably establishes a history of psychosis rather than a history of anxiety state, we do not see any point in varying the benefit classification certificate as nothing turns on the distinction, whatever the 1958 condition may have been the evidence, as we have said, does not establish that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement had any causal relationship with the isolated psychiatric episode in the United Kingdom some twenty years previously. But we are satisfied that the applicant's myopia was a physical condition that was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act and that his retirement was caused or. substantially contributed to by that condition. On that ground, therefore, we affirm the Acting Commissioner's decision made on 3 April 1980 confirming the decision of his delegate made on 16 October 1979:

(i) to issue under sub-section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions "history of anxiety state" and "history of myopia" in respect of Mr Blakemore;

(ii) that the benefit entitlement of Mr Blakemore on his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 29 August 1979 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age of 65 years and having completed less than 20 years contributory service should be in accordance with sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.
