Last Updated: 25 February 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - allowable deductions - member of armed forces - overseas travelling expenses.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s.51(1).

Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1935] HCA 81; (1935-1936) 54 CLR 295

] No. M.59/1985 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ]

And:

REASONS FOR DECISION MR. K.P. BRADY

  1. The question for decision in this reference is whether any par of the amount of $1,439 claimed in respect of overseas travelling expenses in the year of income ended 30th June, 1980, is an allowable deduction under the provisions of s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.
  2. The Commissioner of Taxation (the Commissioner) disallowed the claim and the objection against the disallowance. The decision on the objection was, at the request of the taxpayer/applicant (herein called the applicant) referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board of Review concluded its hearing of the matter before 1st July, 1986, and reserved its decision. Under the provisions of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, the functions of the Taxation Boards of Review were transferred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal with effect from 1st July, 1986. As a consequence, the decision in the instant case, together with reasons in writing for the decision, are given pursuant to the provisions of s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.
  3. At the hearing of the reference before the Board of Review, the applicant represented himself and gave evidence under oath. He did not call any witnesses. The Commissioner was represented by one of his officers. No witnesses were called to give evidence on behalf of the Commissioner.
  4. The applicant is of European origin. He commenced to take an active interest in the sport of skiing at about the age of six years, and he pursues this interest to the present time. In 1967, at about the age of 20 years, he came to live in Australia. In 1974 he joined a branch of the Australian Armed Services as a trainee physical training instructor. While still a trainee, he was invited by senior officers to coach a State inter- service ski team, and in each year since then he has, in an official capacity, personally competed in, and has coached a team to compete in, all inter-service ski-championships held in Australia. During the times at which those activities were engaged in he was in receipt of his normal service pay and allowances and was, it seems, fully protected in the matter of compensation for any injuries that might have been sustained. Travelling, accommodation and incidental expenses, other than entertainment expenses, incurred by him were paid by the service authorities. However, it seems that the applicant has held no formal qualifications or certificates in the areas of physical training or skiing, although he has acquired several service trade certificates.
  5. Because of a back injury, the applicant was only able to pursue the occupation of physical training instructor for a very short time. However, by the commencement of the year in issue, that ended 30th June, 1980, he had attained the rank of corporal and worked in a cataloguing section in the area of defence for the whole of that year. It seems that some time subsequent to that year he was promoted to the next senior rank, which was that of sergeant, which rank he still holds. We were given to understand that that rank, relative to a corporal, entailed greater responsibilities and attracted a higher rate of pay and allowances. The duties performed by the applicant as sergeant have concerned the provisioning of spare parts for military equipment of some complexity and in some instances, it seems, of considerable value.
  6. In about September 1979, the Australian Defence Authorities received an invitation from their British counterparts to participate in an international ski-championship to be conducted by them at a well known ski resort in mainland Europe. It was to be, and was in fact, a purely military competition in which only members of the defence forces could, and did, participate. The competition commenced late in January 1980, and lasted one week. However, the applicant, along with other members of the Australian ski team, proceeded to Europe several weeks in advance of the competition in order to acclimatise themselves to local conditions and to train for the various events entered into. The applicant has not claimed a deduction in respect of expenses incurred by him during the training period or during the short period spent in Europe after the competition and before his return to Australia.
  7. It is necessary to refer now in some detail to a document tendered in evidence as Exhibit B. The document is headed "Defence Force Policy on Sport" and was published in 1983. On the basis of assurances given by the applicant, I accept that, in all material respects, that document faithfully records the Defence Force policy on sport which was applicable during the year of income ended 30th June, 1980. The Commissioner's representative did not raise any objections to the tendering of the document or to any of the statements made therein.
  8. For present purposes, the relevant parts of the document are considered to be:

"INTRODUCTION

1. Physical fitness is an essential element of the operational efficiency of the Defence Force. Properly planned and executed physical activity is a necessary factor in maintaining the health and fitness of each member. Sport encompasses physical activity and is therefore an integral part of Service training.

  1. inter-Service sport is a valuable opportunity for both individuals and teams to associate with other Services;
  2. Service involvement in civilian sport may improve community relations and encourage recruitment;
  1. some sports provide inexpensive training mediums in the form of skills essential to the Defence Force, e.g. rifle shooting, orienteering, sailing or gliding; and
  1. sport encourages such personal attributes as the development of qualities of individual courage, initiative, resourcefulness, leadership and the ability to co-operate in a team.

4. The aim of this instruction is to state Defence Force policy on sport.

5. For the purposes of this instruction, the following definitions apply:

c. Combined Services Sport. Combined Services sport is sporting competition between teams or individuals from two or more of the Services. It may be conducted at local, state or national level.

e. Service Representatives. Service representatives are Service personnel who are members of an approved Service team or who have gained representative status through a Service selection process.

  1. National combined service and international competitions - up to one week for training and a maximum of two weeks for competition.

9. In lengthy or costly sporting activities, approval to participate and the provision of support and assistance may be conditional upon a contribution from the member. A reasonable balance between the contribution from welfare, public and personal funds should be maintained.

a. Members will receive Service pay and normal allowances where applicable; the payment of supplementary allowances (overseas travelling, etc.) will be subject to separate consideration, with each instance being considered on its individual merits.

The ADFSC consists of one-star representatives from each of the Services and a representative from Defence Central. It is based at the Department of Defence, Canberra, and meets as required in accordance with the ADFSC Charter, as shown at Annex E. Although the ADFSC formulates and promulgates policies on matters such as management procedures and standardization of rules of competition, it is not involved in the day-to-day running of unit, inter-unit or single Service sport.

  1. National tri-Service sporting organizations are responsible for the co-ordination and administration of all matters relating to national inter-Service and national combined Services sport. These organizations are directly responsible to the ADFSC for the implementation of applicable sporting policies and act as advisers to the ADFSC on individual sports.
  2. The ADFSC is to exercise the role of the presiding board for approved national tri-Service management committees. The ADFSC may also undertake control and co-ordination tasks when sporting activities are conducted at international level and when appropriate, at the national level.

  2. Bids for Service air transport to support national and international sporting competitions may be submitted in accordance with Service procedures. If approved, flying hours will be allotted from within the normal allocation of flying hours. When suitable training flights are available, they may be utilized without debit to Service allocations.
  3. Approved Service sporting teams may travel as passengers on scheduled Service air transport. Sporting teams have priority of travel over non-duty personnel but not over freight or other duty passengers.
  4. Service transport, other than Service air transport, may be authorized for use at public expense by Service representatives, subject to the availability of funds.

9. Whilst the applicant's participation in the military ski-championships referred to earlier appears to have been duly authorised and to have fallen squarely within the ambit of the "Defence Force Policy on Sport", it appears to have raised problems concerning financial assistance which could not be provided from authorised public funds or from welfare and amenities funds (see paragraphs 32 and 34 cited above). It also appears that assistance in the matter of overseas air travel was not possible either because "exceptional circumstances" did not exist which might have warranted the granting of sufficient funds for that purpose, or because funds for that purpose were not available (see paragraph 36 cited above). The possibility of the applicant being provided with service air transport overseas was not mentioned to the Board, and I draw the inference that it was not available to him at the relevant times.

  1. In the matter of finance, the applicant explained to the Board that the invitation by the British Defence Authorities to participate in the ski-championships was received by the Australian Authorities in September 1979. It seems that, by that time, all funds from all available sources for the relevant accounting period had been either spent or allocated in such a way that no funds could properly be allocated for payment of expenses to be incurred by participants in the ski-championships. This position was made clear to all potential participants (including the applicant), who apparently were advised that they would be required to pay their own overseas expenses in the event that they were selected as part of the ski-team and then proceeded to Europe to participate in the championships already mentioned. The applicant was aware of this position at the time of making application for selection as a team member and at the time when his selection was advised to him. It also seems that the applicant was not under any legal compulsion to undertake the tour, nor did he perceive at the relevant times any expectation by the Defence Authorities that he should undertake the tour at his own expense. In fact, it seems, if he had not been able to finance the trip himself he could not have undertaken it. The inference I draw from the evidence is that, in those circumstances, his career prospects would not have been disadvantaged in any way. In this respect, the applicant advised the Board that his future promotional prospects were not conditional upon his participating in the ski-championships.
  2. In order to undertake the trip, the applicant was required to take annual leave for the period of his absence from Australia. This he did. During his absence, he was paid his normal service pay and allowances and also, I understand, he was fully covered during that period in the event of injury for compensation in terms of the Defence Force Policy on Sport (see paragraphs 2 and 14(b) of Exhibit B cited in paragraph 8 above).
  3. The applicant gave the Board to understand that he was "on duty'1 during his absence overseas, notwithstanding the requirement to take annual leave. The Board was also given to understand that the phrase "on duty" means, in terms of the "Defence Force Policy on Sport", that a member of the armed services is "under control of an officer of the defence forces and you must do whatever he says" (p.17a of transcript). Whilst I accept that the applicant was "on duty" during the week in which the ski-championships were held, I have some reservations as to whether that was the precise position during the several weeks prior to that time when he was in training while living with relatives. I do not know what the position was during the time that elapsed subsequent to the championships before the applicant's return to Australia. However, I do not think that the question of whether the applicant was, strictly speaking, "on duty" during the whole of the period of his absence from Australia is decisive in the matter requiring a decision and I therefore proceed to arrive at my conclusions on the basis that he was "on duty" (see paragraphs 2 and 14 of Exhibit B, paragraph 8 above).
  4. For completeness I would mention that, of the 28 defence personnel who applied for selection as a team member to participate in the ski-championships, only 12 were selected. One of those selected could not proceed overseas for personal reasons.
  5. The component parts of the applicant's claim for deduction may be summarised as follows:

Air fares $ 937

Passport 25

Departure tax 10

Rail fares in Europe 174

Bus fares in Europe 40

Accommodation for seven days

during ski-championships 193

Cost of ski tows, etc. 60

$1,439

In the light of the evidence, the Commissioner's representative accepted that those expenses were incurred by the applicant during the year of income in issue, i.e. that ended 30th June, 1980, and that they related solely to the applicant. With respect, I consider on the evidence that it is proper to conclude that the expenses were incurred in the period mentioned and for the purposes as stated.

  1. I turn now to a consideration of the law. To succeed in his claim the applicant, as an employee, must show that the expenses in issue were "incurred [by him] in gaining or producing the assessable income" (of the year of income ended 30th June, 1980, or of a subsequent year(s)), and that they were not outgoings "of a capital, private or domestic nature" (s51(1)).
  2. The meaning of the phrase "in gaining or producing" has been examined by the Courts on a number of occasions and still retains the meaning attributed to it by the High Court in Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd v FC of T [1935] HCA 81; (1935-1936) 54 CLR 295. Dixon J. (as he then was), at pp.303-309, stated:

"The expression 'in gaining or producing' has the force of 'in the course of gaining or producing' and looks rather to the scope of the operations or activities and the relevance thereto of the expenditure than to purpose in itself."

To be deductible, the expenditure must also be "incidental and relevant" to the gaining or producing of assessable income (Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1948-1949) 78 C.L.R. 47) and have the "essential character" of a business or income producing expense (Lunney v. F.C. of T. (1958-1959) 100 C.L.R. 478).

  1. For expenditure to be deductible there must be, in the words of Menzies J., a "perceived connection" between the expenditure and the gaining or producing of income (F.C. of T. v. Hatchett, 71 ATC 4184). However, having regard to the views of David Hunt J. at pp.4724-4725 in Martin v. F.C. of T. 83 ATC 4722, which were endorsed by the Full Federal Court on appeal in that case (84 ATC 4513), the finding of a "perceived connection" between an outgoing and assessable income is not sufficient by itself to justify a deduction. While, as pointed out by Ormiston J. at p.4065 in the case of F.C. of T. v. Klan 85 ATC 4060, the "perceived connection" test may sometimes be an appropriate means of testing whether a loss or outgoing was incurred in the gaining or producing of assessable income, it is not a substitute for the wording of the statute itself (s.51(1)), which requires that, to be deductible, expenditure must be shown to have been incurred "in or in the course of" gaining assessable income.
  2. Turning to an examination of the facts in the instant case in the light of the principles established in the cases cited (and in many other cases which need not concern me here), I find that there was a direct connection between the expenses incurred by the applicant and his actions in travelling to Europe and participating in the ski-championships held there. However, those actions of themselves, and the ski-championships as such, were not productive of assessable income in any year of income, and consequently the expenses incurred in respect of those actions in that context could not on any view, in my opinion, be regarded as incurred "in or in the course of" gaining or producing the applicant's assessable income.
  3. It is undoubtedly true that the applicant's long experience and skill as a skier and as a coach of ski teams, including, it seems, the Australian team which participated in the championships, helped to maintain or improve the applicant's health and fitness and provided an avenue for the development of attributes such as individual courage, initiative, resourcefulness, leadership and the ability to co-operate in a team, which were at the heart of the "Defence Force Policy on Sport" (see paragraphs 1 and 3(d) of Exhibit B cited in paragraph 8 above). These conclusions appear to be supported by the fact that skiing was determined to be an accredited sport by the Defence authorities in terms of its policy on sport, which, in turn, enabled the applicant to be a participant in the European championships. However, from an income tax viewpoint, it is apparent on the evidence that the applicant's income was not derived as a skier or from being healthy and fit (subject to his back ailment) or because he had acquired some or all of the various personal attributes that may have had their origin in his skiing activities. In the circumstances, it could not be said, in my opinion, that the nexus between the outgoings in issue and the applicant's personal skills as a skier, and other personal attributes, was sufficiently proximate to the derivation of his income so as to enable me to find that the expenses were incurred "in or in the course of" gaining or producing his assessable income.
  4. Similarly, it could not be said, in my opinion, that the expenses in issue were related in a real or direct sense to the derivation of his income on the simple basis that it accrued to him by virtue of his being a member of the defence forces. It was certainly a necessary prerequisite to the derivation of his service income that he be a member of the defence forces but, in fact, during the whole of the year in issue, the level of his income and its derivation was dependent upon his status as a corporal and the performance of his duties in a cataloguing section. Therefore, while it is possible that his performance of those duties might have been assisted by the various personal attributes already referred to, I can find no evidence that would support the proposition that the expenses in issue were related in any meaningful way for tax purposes to the derivation of his income in the year in issue, or of any other year, from that source. In the words of Menzies J. in Hatchett's case (supra), as judicially explained, there was not, in my opinion, a "perceived connection" between the outgoings and the applicant's assessable income.
  5. As already indicated, the applicant was promoted to the rank of sergeant some time after the end of the year in issue with responsibilities concerned with the provisioning of spare parts. It was said in evidence that this promotion, relative to that of other personnel who had received a similar promotion before that time, was an accelerated one. As previously mentioned, the rank of sergeant carried with it a higher rate of pay and allowances than those received by the applicant as a corporal. Again, however, I fail to detect any relevant nexus for tax purposes between the ski-championships and the outgoings connected with it and the applicant's service income. While, as suggested by the applicant, his qualities of initiative and leadership no doubt played a role in his promotion, the evidence does not enable me to conclude that those qualities were significantly enhanced by his participation in the ski-championships or, if they were, that they were the critical factors which were taken into account in his selection for promotion over other potential promotees who probably demonstrated similar qualities that had been developed by them in the field of skiing or in other sporting activities. The evidence does not enable me to conclude that the expenses were incurred to make it more certain that the applicant would earn more at some future time and that his activities giving rise to those outgoings went in any discernible way beyond improving mind and body.
  6. Finally, I turn more specifically to the question of whether the expenses in issue were of a private nature. As already indicated, the applicant was not under any legal obligation to participate in the ski-championships and, on the evidence, his future service career would not have been prejudiced by his non-participation. It seems to me that the underlying reasons for the applicant's decision to undertake the overseas trip at his own volition and at his own expense are to be found in his love of skiing, his long association with the sport and, perhaps, a feeling of personal loyalty to be with a team which he had coached and of which he wished to be a part. A further reason may well have been his desire, in any event, to return to Europe and visit his relatives there. Therefore, having regard to the evidence overall, I am of the opinion that the character of all of the expenses in issue was essentially of a private nature, with the consequence that they are precluded as a deduction by the exclusory clause in s.51(1).

23. For the above reasons, I propose to affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection under review.

REASONS FOR DECISION

MESSRS. J.E. STEWART and D.J. TROWSE

  1. We adopt Mr. Brady's recital of the facts; however, our decision on the evidence differs from his.
  2. In our view, the outgoings incurred by the applicant in participating in the overseas skiing championship were incurred in gaining his assessable income. Also, they were not of a capital, private or domestic nature. Accordingly, we consider that they are properly deductible under the first limb of s.51(1).
  3. It is clear, we believe, from the evidence that the applicant was on duty and his salary paid by the Government for the whole period that he was away overseas. He told us that he had been involved in competitive skiing in the air force over the past 10 years, and he saw the overseas championship as furthering his career by providing him with the opportunity of continuing to compete in R.A.A.F. ski-championships in Australia. That matter, it seems, had some relationship with his income because his demonstrable ability to act as a captain or coach of an inter-service skiing team denoted leadership qualities, which was a factor in securing promotion.
  4. It is perhaps unfortunate for the applicant that involvement in an overseas skiing contest has connotations of pursuing a hobby or pastime, certainly a non income-earning activity. However, on the evidence, we believe that the skiing championship, at least so far as the applicant was concerned, was no different to a more pedestrian-type physical education exercise such as a bivouac on Mount Buller. The glamour attaching to the former activity can be misleading. In our view (and here we paraphrase what Dixon C.J. said in F.C. of T. v. Finn (1961-1962) 106 C.L.R. 6C), the applicant's participation in the ski championship was all done while he was in the employment of the Government, earning his salary and acting in accordance with the conditions of his service.

5. The question requiring an answer in this reference is whether the applicant, in making the outlays on the trip to Europe, was incurring expenditures in the course of deriving his assessable income. Judicial pronouncements on the interpretation of s.51(1) are voluminous, but the most authoritative statements are those of the Full High Court. One of the most recent is to be found in the joint judgment of Gibbs, Stephen, Mason and Walsh JJ. in F.C. of T v. P.P. Smith 81 ATC 4114 at p.4117, where it was said:

"The section does not require that the purpose of the expenditure shall be the gaining of the income of that year, so long as it was made in the given year and is incidental and relevant to the operations or activities regularly carried on for the production of income. What is incidental and relevant in the sense mentioned falls to be determined not by reference to the certainty or likelihood of the outgoing resulting in the generation of income but to its nature and character and generally to its connection with the operations which more directly gain or produce the assessable income."

6. By the use of the words "more directly" in the second last line of the above excerpt, the High Court has recognised that relevance of expenditures to income generating activities can be a matter of degree. Confirmation of that aspect is contained in F.C. of T. v. Forsyth 81 ATC 4157, where Wilson J. states at p.4163:

"In every case it is clearly a question of fact and degree whether the outgoing has the necessary relation to the gaining of assessable income."

7. In our view, the outlays made in order to participate in the skiing championship were incidental and relevant to the applicant's income-producing occupation as a member of the armed forces, stemming from the use made by his superiors of his skiing prowess in the course of his employment as a physical training instructor. It is true that he was not engaged in p.t. instruction at the time that he made the overseas trip because of sustaining a slight back injury; instead he was engaged in office duties. However, in our view, that is a peripheral matter. Essentially he was paid in his capacity as a member of the armed forces; he was not paid, except in a very narrow sense, for performing a specific task such as cataloguing.

8. For the above reasons, we believe that there existed a relationship between the outlays occasioned by the applicant's participation in the skiing championship and his assessable income, although we concede that the degree of relationship is not a very strong one. We would allow the deduction.
