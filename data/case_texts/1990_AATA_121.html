VT88/514 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 121; (1990) 21 ATR 3420; 90 ATC 402 (16 May 1990)

Last Updated: 1 June 2009

INCOME TAX - Pastoral company - Pastoral property leased to and

farmed by two employees of that company in partnership -

Employees entitled to lump sum termination payments - Deeds of

Indemnity - Policies of Insurance - Assignment of obligations

under deeds of indemnity - Assignment of benefits of policies of

insurance - Whether insurance premium deductible as cost of

indemnity - Whether arrangement a "sham" - Whether premiums of a

capital character - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1985, secs

51(1), 177F.



Case W62, 89 ATC 570

Fletcher & Ors v FC of T 88 ATC 4834

Ransburg Australia Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4114



AAT No 5895



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. VT88/514

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



APPLICANT



And:



RESPONDENT



DECISION



Before: Dr P. Gerber (Deputy President)



Date: 16 May 1990



The Tribunal affirms the objection decision in relation to the

assessments for the years ended 30 June 1982 to 1985 inclusive.



(Sgd. P. Gerber)

DEPUTY PRESIDENT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. VT88/514

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



APPLICANT



And:



RESPONDENT



REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Dr P. Gerber (Deputy President)



Date: 16 May 1990



1. By deed dated 24 June 1982 between Bert Pty Ltd

("Bert") and Ernie Pty Ltd ("Ernie"), the R & S Grover Unit Trust

was created.



2. The applicant, Bigbird Pastoral Co Pty Ltd ("Bigbird")

owns a pastoral property ("Blackacre") which was leased to Mr R

and Mrs S Grover, who farmed the property in partnership. On 24

June 1982, Bigbird executed "Employment Agreements" with Mr and

Mrs Grover.



3. By Clause 4 of the agreement between Bigbird (the

employer) and Mr Grover:



"the sum of $800,000 shall become due and payable on

14 April 1997, or if the employee ceases to be

employed by the employer before that date, such

proportion of that sum as the period of employment

calculated from the date hereof to the date on

which the payment would otherwise have become due

which sum shall be due and payable on the date on

which the employee so ceases to be employed."



4. Clause 4 of the agreement between Bigbird and Mrs Grover

differed only in that her employment was to continue until 30

November 2001, at which time Bigbird undertook to pay her the sum

of $950,000.



5. On 18 June 1982, Bigbird signed a proposal form with

Greater Pacific Life Assurance Company (GPL), seeking life

assurance over the lives of Mr and Mrs Grover. These policies

were issued on 8 July 1982, the "Date of Entry" in each case

being 1 July 1982. At an earlier point of time, viz. 25 June,

1982, Bigbird, by deeds of indemnity, assigned all its rights,

title and interests in the insurance policies to Ernie.

Additionally, Bigbird guaranteed to meet payment of the insurance

premiums, in consideration of which, Ernie undertook to indemnify

Bigbird in respect of the termination payments to Mr and Mrs

Grover. It should be noted that on that date, Bigbird had nothing

at law capable of assignment, having merely signed a proposal

form with GPL which, until fructified into two life policies,

conferred a mere contract to assign, which, although capable of

assignment in equity, conferred no legal interest. Nevertheless,

this contract to assign was treated by the parties as a valid

assignment and registered pursuant to schedule 5 of the Life

Insurance Act 1945 on 17 August 1982, by which time Ernie had

assigned the policies to Oscar Pty Ltd ("Oscar").



6. Mr Komesaroff, who appeared for Bigbird, submitted that

the two policies were taken out to assist Bigbird in satisfying

the debts of $800,000 and $950,000 which may become owing to Mr

and Mrs Grover respectively.



7. Bigbird claimed deductions for the cost of the insurance

premiums pursuant to sec 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act

(the "Tax Act"). The Commissioner disallowed the claimed

deductions for the tax years 1982 to 1985 on the following

alternative bases:



(a) that the cost of the insurance premiums were of a capital

nature and not deductible under sec 51;



(b) that the arrangement was a sham;



(c) that the arrangement attracted Part IV A of the Tax Act.



8. The evidence disclosed that the only business activity of

Bigbird consisted of employing the Grovers to collect their own

rental payments and bank them.



9. The tax returns of Bigbird disclosed rental income of

$1000 in 1981 which increased substantially during the following

four years:



1982 $61,946

1983 $79,501

1984 $71,714

1985 $69,517



The company's accountant, testified that the rent increase was

needed to fund Bigbird's commitments under the employment

agreements and insurance policies.



10. It is not without significance that as soon as the

Commissioner disallowed the taxpayer's objection, payments of

insurance premiums ceased.



Are the insurance premiums deductible under sec 51(1)?



11. Mr Komesaroff submitted that the sum of $49,160, paid to

GPL and disallowed in each of the income tax years 1982 to 1985,

was incurred in earning the taxpayer's assessable income. He

further submitted that the payments represented the consideration

granted to secure the indemnity from Oscar (nee Ernie) of its

obligations pursuant to the two employment agreements.



12. Mr Graham, of senior counsel for the Crown, submitted that

the case is governed by the decision in Ransburg Australia Pty

Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4114 and invited the Tribunal to conclude

that a true indemnity for the employment agreement obligations

did not exist, and that the insurance premiums were therefore not

deductible.



13. The critical issue to be resolved is: what is the source

of the indemnity, if any, granted to Bigbird?



14. Mr Komesaroff, in a forceful submission, contended that

"the insurance policies were not the subject of the indemnity

given to the taxpayer company. The indemnity was the promise to

pay contained in the deeds of indemnity". Mr Graham, on the other

hand, urged upon me that Mr Komesaroff's submission is not borne

out by the evidence.



15. On the whole of the evidence, I am satisfied that the

deeds of indemnity of themselves were not intended to indemnify

Bigbird in respect of its obligations to Mr and Mrs Grover under

the employment contracts. Indeed, Mr Grover accepted in

cross-examination that the amounts he (and his wife) would

ultimately receive in satisfaction of the entitlements under the

employment agreements were the sums there and then standing to

the credit of the life policies.



16. At p 18 of the Transcript the following appears:



Mr Graham: Mr Grover, the obligation under the employment

agreement is to pay - in your case - $800,000?

--- No, as I said, that will not be paid. A

proportion of it will be, but not that amount.



Well, whatever is available under the insurance

policies, if anything, will be paid? Is that right?

--- Correct.



17. In other words, it is conceded, properly so in my opinion,

that neither Grover nor his wife would receive their full

employment entitlements, but rather the amounts standing to the

credit of the insurance policies, in full satisfaction of their

entitlements under their respective contractual agreements, and

that no regard would be paid to the rights, expressed in clause 4

of their agreement, to recover the balance owing "as a debt due

and payable on demand".



18. I am therefore satisfied on the evidence that the deeds of

indemnity do not represent a true indemnity of Bigbird's

obligations under the employment contracts. It follows that the

insurance premiums are not deductible qua consideration for

securing the "indemnity" from Oscar under sec 51(1).



19. The Commissioner conceded (albeit grudgingly) that the

insurance premiums may be deductible under sec 51(1) if the

proceeds can be shown to indemnify Bigbird in respect of its

financial obligations to Mr and Mrs Grover, as was the case in

Case W62 89 ATC 570. However, Mr Graham submitted forcefully that

those facts were absent in the case now before me.



20. Thus, Mr Grover conceded that, contemporaneous with the

payment of the initial premiums (totalling $49,160), he had

intended making withdrawals shortly thereafter, and in fact

$35,000 was withdrawn only a fortnight after the payment of the

premiums. Monies withdrawn over the next four years were put to

the following uses: loans to Mr and Mrs Grover for their farming

pursuits, loans to the taxpayer to fund the salaries payable to

Mr and Mrs Grover, and an investment in a unit trust was

effected.



21. This is a far cry from the facts in Case W62. In that

case, a company - Noah Pty Ltd - was the sole trustee of the Noah

Unit Trust, the beneficiaries of which were Dick, his wife Dora

and their children. By the terms of his contract of employment

with Noah Pty Ltd, Dick was to be paid an annual salary of

$16,000, together with a lump sum of $263,000, payable at the end

of 20 years service with the company. A further term provided

that, in the event of earlier termination of employment, Dick was

to be paid "an amount calculated by a competent actuary that at

that date shall approximate in monetary value the value of the

lump sum". To indemnify itself for the amount it would be liable

to pay Dick after 20 years or on earlier retirement, the trustee

took out service contract insurance with a life insurance

company. The trustee claimed that the insurance premium paid in

the 1985 income year was deductible under sec 51(1). The claim

was disallowed and Dora, as a beneficiary, objected.



22. I allowed the objection in that case because I was

satisfied that there was a sufficient nexus between the

obligation to make a future payment to the employee and the

premiums paid to the insurance company under the service contract

insurance (which obliged the insurer to pay a like amount to the

employer) so as to constitute a true indemnity. In other words,

the premium was coterminous with the liability it intended to

cover and, not being a loss or outgoing of capital or of a

capital nature, was thus an allowable deduction under sec. 51(1).



23. This is a far cry from the facts now before me. Thus, an

actuary, called on behalf of the Crown, gave evidence of the

future benefits payable under the insurance policies assuming

varying rates of interest and the presence and absence of

withdrawals of cash by the policy holders. His evidence

disclosed that the obligations under the employment agreement

could only be satisfied (even assuming no cash withdrawals) by

the policies of Mr and Mrs Grover earning interest at 9.21% and

9.28% respectively from 1 July 1989. Such rate of interest was

said to be highly optimistic when compared with the rate applied

by other Life Assurance companies on equivalent policies. (The

rate applied by the ANZ Life Assuance Co Ltd was said to be

7.55%).



24. Again, the employment agreements provided for a pro rata

payment to the two employees in the event of early termination of

employment; (cl 4). Exhibit 6 provided a schedule of the sums

payable at the end of each financial year. Taking the case of

the husband, and assuming a 7.55% interest rate and no

withdrawals, the evidence is clear that at no stage does the

surrender value of the policy come even close to the

proportionate liability under the contract of employment. The

final sum payable of $800,000 could only be obtained if the

policy matures on 1 July 1996, and then, in the remaining 288

days to 14 April 1988, earns a rate of interest of 10.6% (net of

tax).



25. The "Policyowners's Additional Rights and Privileges"

stipulate:



"The Policyowner may draw upon the Available Value

in the Thriftmaster account (reduced by any debts

secured to the Company under the Policy) without

discontinuing the policy and without altering the

premium, provided that he leaves in the account

enough value to cover at least that part of one

year's premium which relates to term insurance life

cover."



26. As noted before, upon disallowance of the tax deduction

for the premiums in 1985, Bigbird discontinued its premium

payments. The cessation of these payments, when viewed against

the whole background of this case, satisfies me that these

policies were never intended to meet Bigbird's contractual

obligations under the employment agreements.



27. I find that these policies of insurance are not so much

indemnity agreements than straightforward savings investment

policies. Thus, the policies of insurance contain no provision to

indemnify either the taxpayer or the trustee, nor is the risk

disclosed in respect of which indemnity is said to be provided;

the obligation under the policies is that GPL will pay "the

Savings Sum Insured" as it exists under the policies; the payment

of this sum is not conditional upon the life insured becoming

entitled to be paid his or her termination lump sum under their

respective employment agreement.



28. Furthermore, the entitlement under the employment

contracts arise by reason of continuous employment to the date

specified in clause 4 (or cessation of employment before that

date). The obligation upon the insurer is thus merely to pay the

sum insured, either upon the event of death or survival to the

relevant date. For good measure, there is no provision in the

policy for an amount to be payable by the insurer in the event of

early cessation of employment. The course that Bigbird would

probably adopt is that the "Savings Sum Insured" would be

withdrawn at that time. Clearly, employment with Bigbird may

have ceased prior to those two specified events under the

insurance policies.



29. To summarize, I find on the evidence, contrary to the

evidence in Case W62 that:



(i) there is inadequate correspondence between the sums

realizable under the insurance policies and the

employment agreements;



(ii) there is no necessary correspondence between the

events specified in the insurance policies with the

event expressed in clause 4 of the employment

contracts;



(iii) there is no nexus between the insurance policy and

the employment contract in that the policy fails to

offer indemnity for a specific liability nominated

in the policy document.



30. The evidence satisfies me that this case is indeed wholly

governed by the decision in Ransburg Australia Pty Ltd v FC of T

(supra) and that there is insufficient congruence between the

obligation of Bigbird to indemnify Mr and Mrs Grover and the

premiums paid to GPL so as to constitute a genuine indemnity. The

outgoings constitute payments made on savings investment

policies, so that the premiums are of a capital nature and thus

excluded from deduction under sec 51(1) of the Tax Act.



31. If the outgoings cannot pass the test of sec 51(1), it is

unnecessary to consider the Commissioner's alternative

submissions of "sham" and/or Part IV A ("If the payments are not

deductible under sec 51, that is the end of the matter. The

taxpayer's appeal must fail."; per Lockhart, Wilcox and Burchett

JJ, Fletcher & Ors v FC of T 88 ATC 4834).



32. I affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection.



This is to certify that this and the preceding 6 pages are a true

and correct copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein.
