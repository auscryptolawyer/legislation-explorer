CATCHWORDS

Whether decision should be remitted to decision-maker for reconsideration before review by the Tribunal - Consideration of powers to remit under section 42D of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act - Order refused - Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, s. 42D - Taxation Administration Act, s. 14ZY.

Re Lavery and Registrar, Supreme Court of Queensland and Others (No. 2) (1996) 23 AAR 52

REASONS FOR DECISION

7 May 1998 J Block, Senior Member



  1. The matters referred to by their case numbers at the commencement of these Reasons are linked; in this regard:

(a) Case NT98/41 in which a certain private company (referred to as the "Company") is the Applicant, relates to the year ending 30 June 1995; the relevant objection relates to the disallowance of deductions amounting in aggregate to $3,301.

(b) Cases NT98/43, 98/47 and 98/48 relate to objections against fringe benefits tax assessments referable to the Company for the years ended 30 June 1994, 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996 (collectively the "relevant years" and each a "relevant year").

(c) Case NT98/42 relates to an objection by W against an assessment to tax of an amount of $7,140 in respect of the year ending 30 June 1995.

(d) The objections referred to in subclause (b) are collectively referred to as the "FBT Objection", while the objections referred to in subclauses (a) and (c) are collectively referred to as the "IT Objections".

  1. The Applicants were represented by Mr John Eager, a consultant solicitor, while the Respondent was represented by Mr Ian Marriott, an officer of the Respondent.
  2. (a) At a directions hearing convened before the Tribunal on 20 April 1998, each Applicant applied for an order under section 42D(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, which reads as follows:
"At any stage of a proceeding for review of a decision, the Tribunal may remit the decision to the person who made it for reconsideration of the decision by the person."


(b) The Tribunal was referred in respect of the applications set out in subclause (a), to the documents lodged under section 37(1A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act. Mr Eager in addition furnished the Tribunal with a document entitled "Applicants' Contentions in Support of Directions Being Sought at Directions Hearing on 20 April 1998" (the "Contentions Document") and which sets out, in some detail, the basis for the applications referred to in subclause (a).

(c) The application referable to the FBT Objection was argued at some length before the Tribunal; it should be noted at the outset that the arguments before the Tribunal were confined to that objection. Mr Eager submitted (as appears from clauses 1.1 and 1.3 of the Contentions Document) that the IT Objections could conveniently "travel" with the FBT Objection, more particularly having regard to the fact that the amounts involved are comparatively small, and also taking into account the fact that the IT Objection referred to in clause 1(c) may be materially affected by the manner in which the FBT Objection is resolved; put more particularly if the FBT Objection is resolved against the Company, the Company may not, so Mr Eager argued, have sufficient profits for the purpose of the assessment referred to in the IT Objection in question. Mr Marriott did not object to this method of dealing with these matters; see also in this context clause 3.1 of the Contentions Document which reads, edited for purposes of anonymity, as follows:

"These contentions are specifically based on the facts of the objections against the Company's 1994, 1995 and 1996 FBT Assessments. They can easily be adapted to the facts of the other 2 reviews."
  1. The term "property" refers to a 33 acre property in New South Wales of which W and his partner L are the joint owners. In respect of the property:

(a) Mr Eager informed the Tribunal that although it was originally contemplated that the property would be acquired by the Company, and although it was the Company which applied for and obtained a bank loan for the purpose of funding a part of the acquisition price, it was finally decided that W and L (who control the Company) would become the registered owners.

(b) Mr Eager also informed the Tribunal that the security documents in respect of the bank loan were prepared and executed on the basis that the Company was the borrower while W and L furnished guarantees and granted a mortgage by way of third party security over the property in favour of the bank.

(c) Clause 3.8 of the Contentions Document contains a statement to the effect that the dwelling on the property (which appears to be a two storey building) occupies about one acre only and that more than 31 acres are utilised by the Company in respect of its farming and business activities.

(d) Because the Company was the borrower, it on-lent certain moneys to W and L on an interest free basis and to enable them to acquire the property. In addition, the Company made certain payment referable to the property and being in particular council rates, telephone, electricity, and certain interest expenses. Fringe benefits tax was assessed in respect of the relevant years, and it is against those assessments (and also additional tax imposed) that the Company objected pursuant to the FBT Objection.

  1. (a) It would seem that in respect of the relevant years, and although the Company utilised the main part of the land involved, the Company "paid no formally identified rent/licence fee for its use of more than 31 acres of farm and business premises ...." (see clause 3.8 of the Contentions Document).

(b) Mr Eager asserted that the value of the Company of the use of the major part of the land would have been at least equivalent or perhaps greater than the value of any of the alleged fringe benefits.

  1. The Company, in respect of the FBT Objection, filed detailed objections. The Respondent was obliged in respect of such objections, and under section 14ZY of the Taxation Administration Act either to allow or disallow them and elected to disallow them. The Respondent, although not obliged to do so under the provisions of section 14ZY of the Taxation Administration Act did in fact furnish reasons for its decision; (see in this context T52 to T75 of the FBT Objection T Documents.
  2. (a) Pursuant to section 37(1A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, the Respondent was obliged to furnish a Statement of Reasons; (see T1 and T2 of the FBT Objection T Documents which in turn refer to T52 to T75 of the FBT Objection T Documents).

(b) A considerable part of the Contentions Document can aptly be characterised as a forceful attack on the reasons provided by the Respondent. The reasons were criticised on various grounds set out in particular in clauses 3.11 and 3.12 of the Contentions Document, which asserts inter alia:

(1) that almost one half of the 24 page document (T52 to T75 of the FBT Objection T Documents) is copied from the FBT Objection.

(2) that attachment 1 to the Objection was omitted from the T Documents, thus having the effect (so it was asserted) that the FBT Objection was "relatively unintelligible". (see clause 3.12.1 of the Contentions Document).

(3) that the Respondent did not deal or did not deal adequately with grounds of objection advanced by the Company.

(4) that the Respondent’s reasons are such that the Company is entitled to assert that the Respondent did not comprehend or consider certain grounds of objection.

(5) that in general terms the Company is entitled to assert that the reasons were a "sham".

  1. (a) The Tribunal does not consider it necessary to deal in any detail with the Company’s complaints in respect of the Respondent’s reasons. The Tribunal considers in particular that allegations of "sham" or allegations that the Respondent did not read or comprehend aspects of the Company’s Objection, are entirely without foundation. The Objection and the reasons furnished must make it clear that there are very real issues between the parties and including:

(1) the extent to which the "otherwise deductible" rule might apply, having regard to all relevant factors and including the fact that there was no specified rent or licence fee payable by the Company to W and L for the use of the relevant land during the relevant years.

(2) whether the benefits constituted fringe benefits under the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act, having regard to the capacity or capacities in which W and L receive some or more or all of the relevant benefits.

(3) the extent to which additional taxes were properly imposed.

(b) The Tribunal does not think it necessary or even desirable for it to attempt to a consideration of the issues; these are aspects which must of necessity be left for consideration by the Tribunal which hears the substantive objections.

  1. The Tribunal was referred in particular to the decision of Deputy President Forgie of this Tribunal in Re Lavery and Registrar, Supreme Court of Queensland and Others (No. 2) (1996) 23 AAR 52, a decision with which this Tribunal respectfully (and entirely) agrees. Clauses 15 to 23 inclusive of that decision are of such relevance and assistance that they are included in this decision as follows:
"15. Even in cases in which a decision maker has given reasons for the decision, he, she or it must give them again when a person lodges an application for review of a decision. Within 28 days of receiving a notice that an application has been lodged with the Tribunal, the decision maker must lodge a statement under section 37 of the AAT Act together with documents which are in his, her or its possession or control and which he, she or it considers to be relevant to the review of the decision by the Tribunal (sub-section 37(1)).
  1. The statement must set out the decision maker’s findings on material questions of fact referring to the evidence on which the findings were based and giving reasons for the decision (paragraph 37(1)(a)). Where the Tribunal considers that a statement lodged under section 37 does not contain adequate findings of fact, particulars of the evidence relied on or particulars of the reasons for decision, it may order that the decision maker lodge a further statement setting out further and better particulars relating to those findings of fact, the evidence or the reasons (section 38).
  2. Section 33 is concerned with the production of material relevant to the review of the application. It provides that, subject to the provision of the AAT Act and of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Regulations, the Tribunal’s procedure is within its discretion. The section goes on to provide for those by whom directions may be given (sub-section 33(1A) and (2)) and for the variation and revocation of directions (sub-section 33(3)). The directions may relate to a particular proceeding or to a class of proceedings (sub-section 33(4)). Without limiting the nature of the directions which may be given, sub-section 33(2A) provides that
“a direction as to the procedure to be followed at or in connection with the hearing of a proceeding before the Tribunal may:

(a) require any person who is a party to the proceeding to provide further information in relation to the proceeding; or

(b) require the person who made the decision to provide a statement of the grounds on which the application will be resisted at the hearing; or

(c) require any person who is a party to the proceeding to provide a statement of matters or contentions upon which reliance is intended to be placed at the hearing.”
  1. It is clear from the structure and framework of the AAT Act that a decision maker is not limited to, or constrained by, the reasons he, she or it initially gave for making the decision. If he, she or it were so limited or constrained, there would be no point to the provisions of section 28 and section 37 which enable the Tribunal to direct that further statements of findings of fact, evidence and reasons be given by the decision maker. Nor would there be any point in the provisions of sub-section 33(2A) clarifying the Tribunal’s power to give directions to the decision maker to provide a statement of the grounds on which the application is to be resisted. The point of these provisions is to ensure that the other parties are aware of the decision maker’s position in the Tribunal and, equally, that the decision maker is aware of the applicant’s position.
  2. These provisions are consistent with the nature of the review that occurs in the Tribunal. It is a de novo review on the merits. Its nature was more fully expanded in Drake v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60 when it was said by Smithers J:
“It might be thought that it would be open to the Administrative appeals Tribunal not to decide for itself whether a decision made by an administrator was the right decision which ought to have been made in the circumstances but rather to satisfy itself that the decision of the administrator was one which an administrator acting reasonably might have made. But to do this would be to review the reasons for the decision rather than the decision itself. It is the actual decision which by virtue of s25(1) and (4) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal is to satisfy itself whether a decision in respect of which an application for review is duly instituted is a decision which in its view, was objectively, the right one to be made. Merely to examine whether the administrator acted reasonably in relation to the fact, either as accepted by him or as found by the Tribunal may not reveal this. In this connection the observations of Sheppard J in Home v Locke [1978] 2 NSWLR 88 at 98-100 are in point. It is to permit implementation of the function of the Tribunal, as so understood, that there has been conferred upon the Tribunal extensive powers of investigation. Those powers are conferred so that the Tribunal may equip itself to make an appropriate recommendation or affirm the decision: see S43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act and Pt XXII of the Schedule thereto.” (pages 77-78)
  1. It follows from the purpose of administrative review, as well as from the specific provisions of the AAT Act to which I have referred, that a decision maker may seek to support the decision on a basis completely different from that upon which it was originally made. Equally, a person applying for review of that decision may seek to have it set aside on a basis completely different from that which he, she or it originally put to the decision maker. As the purpose of the proceedings in the Tribunal is to reach the correct and preferable decision and not to decide any application by stealth or entrapment, all parties are, subject only to exceptions not relevant in this case, required to put their whole case on the table before the proceedings commence.
  2. It would follow from the framework of the AAT Act and the nature of administrative merits review that the power given by section 42D to remit a decision to a decision maker should not be exercised simply because inadequate findings of fact, references to evidence or reasons have been given. Nor should it be exercised simply because a decision-maker supports his, her or its decision on a basis different from that originally put forward. The AAT Act provides other means to remedy those situations.
  3. Determining the occasions on which the power given by section 42D may be exercised is more difficult. It is not clear from the AAT Act itself. I have, therefore, looked to the Explanatory Memorandum which accompanied the Law and Justice Legislation Amendment Bill (No.3) (1994). Section 42D was inserted by Item 21 of Schedule 2 to that Bill. The notes in relation to that Item state:
“99. The Tribunal does not have the power to order that a matter be remitted (sic) the decision-maker for further consideration unless it sets aside the decision under subparagraph 43(c)(ii) of the Act. This can result in matters proceeding to a hearing even where the parties agree that the decision-maker should review the decision.
  1. Item 21 of Schedule 2 inserts new section 42D which will provide that the Tribunal has the power to order that a matter may be remitted to the decision-maker for further consideration at any stage of the proceedings. Where a decision is remitted the decision-maker may affirm, vary, or set aside the decision and make a new decision in substitution for the decision set aside.
  2. New subsections 42D(3) and (4) will provide that where a decision is varied, or a new decision is substituted, the applicant may proceed with the application for review in respect of the varied or new decision or withdraw the application.”
  3. The notes do not provide any assistance as to when the power should be exercised. There are, however, two things that can be gleaned from the notes. the first is that there seems to be an underlying assumption that case management procedures are at the heart of the new section 42D. The second is that the power is needed in cases in which the decision maker should review the decision but does not give any indication of when such cases might arise."
  4. The Tribunal was referred to the practice directions of this Tribunal. Those directions are relevant even though as Mr Eager pointed out, they may not have the force of law. The practice directions provide inter alia for the filing of Statements of Facts and Contentions by each of the parties and also for the holding (in certain circumstances) of a second conference hearing after those Statements have been filed. The FBT Objection is at a stage where Statements of Facts and Contentions have not as yet been filed.
  5. The Tribunal was referred to certain other authorities and in particular Dalton v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) 85 ATC 4476 and also Case 25/95 95 ATC 263 - but does not consider that it needs to refer to either of those decisions in specific terms.
  6. (a) As Deputy President Forgie said in Lavery’s case, the explanatory memorandum does not furnish any real assistance as to when the Tribunal should exercise its power under section 42D(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act. It may be that the section confers a power which will be exercised rarely; the absence of case authority (other than Lavery’s case) in respect of section 42D, even though it was introduced into the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act some three years ago, suggests that this may be so.

(b) It may perhaps be correct to say that the power should be exercised where the reasons are so unsatisfactory that it is fair to infer that the decision maker has not applied his or her mind, or where the reasons are indeed aptly categorised as a "sham", but that is very far from being the case in this instance. It may be that the Respondent’s reasons could perhaps be refined but this is not an aspect in respect of which this Tribunal need (or indeed should) attempt to be specific.

  1. During the course of argument and having regard to clause 3.10 of the Contentions Document, it became clear that the Company would prefer to avoid the cost of a full hearing. This is understandable enough. But the application by the Company is in the opinion of the Tribunal, misconceived. There is nothing in the documents which would lead this Tribunal to consider that any good purpose would be served by remitting the objections to the decision maker. Indeed, Mr Marriott’s presence and the arguments presented at the directions hearing indicate in the clearest possible terms that this is so. I again refer to the decision of Deputy President Forgie in Lavery’s case, and in particular clauses 26, 27 and 28 which read as follows:
"26. In this case, Mr Lavery has submitted that he has been given inadequate details as to the basis of the Registrar’s decision. Any such inadequacy in those details can, and has, been addressed by requiring the Registrar, and the other parties, to lodge statements setting out the issues in the case together with the facts and contentions upon which they are relying. Those statements were directed to be given by virtue of the power given by section 33 of the AAT Act.
  1. As I have already found, the framework of the AAT Act and the purpose of administrative merits review has led me to conclude that the power under section 42D should not be used simply because a decision maker has initially given a statement of reasons which was inadequate or which could be improved. Any inadequacies can be addressed by using powers under sections 28, 33 and 37 as I have already described.
  2. Is there any other reason why I should remit the decision to the Registrar in this case? While Mr Lavery clearly disagrees with the decision, that is the subject of the review proceedings itself. Nothing has been raised in either the submissions or in the statements of issues, facts and contentions which indicate that there is any merit in the decision maker’s reconsidering the decision. Indeed, the Registrar has given no indication that he wishes to reconsider his decision. None of the material that Mr Lavery has lodged has persuaded him that he should do so. Given that, there would be no practical point in remitting the decision to him for reconsideration. Every indication is that he would make precisely the same decision he has already made and further time would be wasted."

  3. In summary, this Tribunal does not consider that the reasons furnished are indicative of the fact that the Respondent did not understand the relevant objections or that he failed to apply his mind or that they amount to a "sham". The Tribunal agrees with the reasoning of Deputy President Forgie in Lavery’s case that the framework of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act is such that the Tribunal has sufficient powers under the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act with which to remedy any inadequacies. The Tribunal does not consider that any good purpose would be served by remitting the objection decisions in question to the decision maker for reconsideration. In the circumstances, the application by the applicant in the FBT Objection, and also, to the extent relevant, by the Applicants in respect of the IT Objections, under section 42D(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, are refused.
