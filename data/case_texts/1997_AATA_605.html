Wallbridge-Yost and Commissioner for Superannuation [1997] AATA 605 (14 January 1997)

Last Updated: 2 January 2009

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re WILLIAM JAMES WALLBRIDGE-YOST

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



DECISION



AAT Decision No 11533



Tribunal Mr C P Webster (Senior Member)

Mr J T C Brassil AM (Member)

Date 14 January 1997

Place Hobart

Decision The decision under review is affirmed.     



[Sgd C P Webster]

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Application to have an election made cancelled - whether “special circumstances” that warrant an extension of the period within which such an application can be made exist.     



Superannuation Act 1976 - ss.62, 157(3)

Superannuation (Cancellation of Elections) Regulations - r.5

REASONS FOR DECISION

14 January 1997 Mr C P Webster (Senior Member)

Mr J T C Brassil AM (Member)

Ms A Cunningham (Member)



1. The decision under review is the decision of the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation of 12 November 1991 which was affirmed upon reconsideration on 4 March 1994 which refused the applicant’s application under s.157(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) to have his election made under s.62 of the Act cancelled.

The Relevant Issues and Law

2. The relevant law is contained in s.62 and s.157(3) of the Act.

3. Section 62 of the Act enables an eligible person to elect within three months of retirement either a pension or lump sum benefit under ss.55 or 59 of the Act or to receive a benefit under s.62 of the Act.

4. Section 157(3) of the Act enables an election s.62 of the Act to be cancelled within three months of the making of that election upon application by the person making the election.

5. Section 157(3)(b) allows for the three months period in which the cancellation of an election can be made to be extended by the respondent, if the respondent is of the opinion that “special circumstances” warrant extension of the period.

6. Section 157(3) of the Act states:-

“(3) Where:

(a) a person makes an election (whether before or after the commencement of this subsection) under sections 62, 64, 68, 69, 71, 72, 76A, 83, 84, 86, 87, 92, 128, 137 or 139A.

(b) the person who made the election, or another person (being a person who is prescribed or included in a prescribed class of persons), makes an application to the Commissioner not later than 3 months after the day on which the election is made or the day on which this subsection comes into operation, whichever is the later, or within such further period as the Commissioner, in special circumstances, allows, requesting that the Commissioner cancel the election; and

(c) the Commissioner, having regard to such matters (if any) as are prescribed and such other matters as he considers relevant, is satisfied that the election should be cancelled;

the Commissioner may direct that the election shall be cancelled and, if he so directs, this Act has effect as if the election had not been made.”


  1. The issues to be decided in this review are whether special circumstances exist in which the Commissioner may allow an extension of the period of three months to allow the applicant to make application for the cancellation of an election by the applicant and whether the election made by the applicant should be cancelled.

The Applicant’s Case

  1. The `T’ documents were tendered into evidence. These documents show (amongst other things):-

(a) That on 5 March 1971 the applicant was accepted as a contributor of the superannuation fund established by the Superannuation Act 1922.

(b) On 8 February 1991, a delegate of the Secretary of the Department of Administrative Services

 retired the applicant from the public service, retirement having effect from 13 February 1991. (c) On 12 February 1991 the applicant completed a superannuation election form, and elected to remain a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. (d) On 13 February 1991 the applicant completed a benefit application form and elected to take a lump sum in lieu of pension (and refund of his productivity component) and a lump sum was subsequently paid for the applicant’s benefit. (e) On 10 October 1991 the applicant requested that his election for a lump sum benefit be cancelled. 9. The applicant gave evidence in person as follows:- (a) That he was a single man and was aged 66 (having been born on 4 June 1929.) (b) He was employed by the Commonwealth of Australia for two periods namely 4 February 1948 to 3 February 1954, and 9 March 1967 to 13 February 1991. Whilst employed with the Australian Construction Services the applicant was responsible for structural drafting. (c) In late 1990 he considered a redundancy offer by his employer. He attended a presentation seminar conducted by his employer concerning the redundancy, and approximately fourteen days later after attending the seminar he expressed interest in accepting the redundancy.  

(d) The seminars consisted of four presentations of approximately one hour each in duration, each spread out over four days. The applicant was also given two booklets giving examples of superannuation options.

(e) The seminars were attended by approximately 70 employees. The applicant was confused after attending the seminars as to his superannuation options.

(f) Following the seminars, each attendee was given a “floppy” disc, which could be used by each attendee to compare the various superannuation options. A colleague assisted the applicant to use the “floppy” disc to compare the options.

(g) After attending the seminars, the applicant decided he would take an indexed pension, but after receiving advice from a financial adviser, a Mr. Begaud, he changed his mind and elected to take a lump sum.

(h) Prior to consulting Mr. Begaud, the applicant consulted another financial planner, namely Commonwealth Life on two occasions, on 15 and 29 January 1991.

(i) The applicant’s change of mind to take a lump sum was due to three factors, namely:-

(i) the fact that he was confused;

(ii) incorrect advice given by Mr. Begaud; and

(iii) the applicant’s poor health.

(j) Mr. Begaud presented the applicant with massive documentation which was directed to convincing the applicant to take a lump sum instead of an indexed pension. Mr. Begaud advised the applicant, in answer to the applicant’s queries, that the applicant could live off the interest from his investments, from the lump sum paid whilst preserving his capital.

(k) The applicant agreed that he did not specifically request Mr. Begaud’s advice as to which option, an indexed pension or a lump sum, he should take. He also agreed that he did not read Mr. Begaud’s written report to him following his first consultation with Mr. Begaud.

(l) At the time the applicant sought advice from Mr. Begaud, the applicant considered that he was in poor health. He was fearful of dying and he thought a lump sum was a better option than an indexed pension as the former could be left to his relatives upon his death whilst the indexed pension ceased upon his death.

(m) The applicant was an enthusiastic inventor who during his working career had, in his spare time, worked on various inventions. It was the applicant’s intention upon his retirement to spend more time on his inventions. He intended to buy equipment, such as a milling machine, upon his retirement, to enable him to continue working on his inventions.

The Respondent’s Case

  1. Mr. Kevin Begaud gave evidence that:-

(a) He was a certified practising accountant who was employed as a senior investment consultant with Beakleys Investment Planners in 1991.

(b) On 1 February 1991 he was consulted by the applicant who advised him that he had elected to take a lump sum upon his redundancy and requested advice as to how to invest the lump sum.

(c) As a result of an initial interview Mr. Begaud prepared a written report to the applicant which contained his recommendations. Following the provisions of that report, the applicant made a complaint about Mr. Begaud who then saw the applicant again and explained his recommendations to the applicant.

Mr. Begaud was adamant that at all times the applicant expressed a wish to take a lump sum. Mr. Begaud stated that the applicant had expressed a view that “he didn’t want his money to die with him”, and that he needed capital to enable him to continue with his inventions and that the report of Mr. Begaud to the applicant accurately set out the applicant’s instructions to Mr. Begaud.

Discussion of Evidence and Findings of Fact

  1. The Tribunal did not consider that there were any material discrepancies between the evidence of the applicant and Mr. Begaud, however where there were any differences between the two, the Tribunal preferred the evidence of Mr. Begaud to that of the applicant where there were any differences in their evidence.
  2. The oral evidence of Mr. Begaud is supported by his report to the applicant, written shortly after the initial interview in which Mr. Begaud sets out the instructions of the applicant upon which he had proceeded to make his recommendations.
  3. The evidence of the applicant was confused and contradictory.
  4. The applicant’s evidence to the Tribunal was that he made the decision to elect to take a lump sum as the result of a “massive presentation” by Mr. Begaud, which convinced him to take a lump sum, despite his earlier decision to take an indexed pension.
  5. At the same time, the applicant’s evidence was that he did not raise with Mr. Begaud the question of which was the best option for him to take. The applicant contended that the principal reason for taking the lump sum was the advice of Mr. Begaud. His contention that he placed great reliance on the advice of Mr. Begaud is not consistent with his actions in not reading Mr. Begaud’s opinion.
  6. If the applicant did not read Mr. Begaud’s report, it is difficult to accept his contention that he relied upon the advice of Mr. Begaud.
  7. The Tribunal makes the following findings of fact namely:

(a) The applicant was born on 4 June 1929 and was at all times a single man.

(b) That the applicant was employed by the Commonwealth of Australia in the Australian Construction Services and was responsible for construction drafting, between the periods 4 February 1948 to 3 February 1954 and 9 March 1967 to 13 February 1991.

(c) That the applicant was at all relevant times a contributor to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme.

(d) That on 3 February 1991, the applicant elected to take a lump in lieu of a pension and productivity component and subsequently a lump sum was paid to this benefit.

(e) That on 10 October 1991, the applicant sought cancellation of his election for a lump sum benefit, but that the delegate refused to direct that the applicant’s election be cancelled.

(f) That prior to the applicant making an election on 13 February 1991 to take a lump sum in lieu of a pension, the applicant had:-

(i) attended seminars as to his superannuation options;

(ii) had been provided with details as what he would receive if he accepted the various options; and

(iii) had consulted financial advisers.

(g) That the applicant had a history as an inventor and intended, upon retirement, to continue his interest in inventions and to that end intended to buy the necessary manufacturing equipment.

(h) That at the time of making his election, the applicant thought he was in extremely bad health and that he might soon die, and that he was anxious, in the case of his death, to have money to leave to his remaining relatives.

(i) That prior to consulting with Mr. Begaud, the applicant had decided that he would elect to take a lump sum and that he sought advice from Mr. Begaud as to how he should invest the lump sum.

(j) That the applicant’s decision to take a lump sum was not based on the advice received from Mr. Begaud (which he did not read in any event), but on his belief that he could die shortly after retirement and because he wished to have money which he could spend on the manufacturing and development of his inventions.

Application of the Law

  1. The first question to be determined is whether the Commissioner should allow an extension of the period of three months to allow the applicant to make an application for the cancellation of the election by the applicant. The respondent has conceded that an extension of time should be allowed. The concession is made after consideration of the principles enunciated by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in Re Eastman and Commissioner for Superannuation, 4 ALN N110. The respondent concedes (a) that the delay between the making of the original election and s.157(3) application was influenced by the fact that the applicant did not know of the existence of s.157(3), (b) that the applicant would if he had known of s.157(3) earlier have sought cancellation of his election at an earlier stage; and (c) the applicant moved with reasonable diligence to use the provision once its existence came to his notice.
  2. The Tribunal agrees with the concession made by the respondent for the reasons stated by the respondent and extends the period to enable the applicant to seek a cancellation of his election.
  3. The next issue for the Tribunal to consider is whether the applicant’s election should be cancelled.
  4. The matters which are to be considered in determining whether an election should be cancelled are specified in regulation 5 of the Superannuation (Cancellation of Elections) Regulations. Regulation 5 states:-
“For the purposes of paragraph 157(3)(c) of the Act, the following matters are matters to which the Commissioner shall have regard in determining whether an election referred to in paragraph 157(3)(a) of the Act should be cancelled:

(a) the availability of all information relevant to the making of the election to the person who made the election (including a person, who, by virtue of sub-section 157(2) of the Act, is deemed to have made the election) and, if the election was made under sub-section 157(2) of the Act, also to the person whom the Commissioner, in pursuant of that sub-section, allowed to make the election; and

(b) the understanding that the person who made the election (including a person who, by virtue of sub-section 157(2) of the Act, is deemed to have made the election) and, if the election was made under sub-section 157(2) of the Act, also the person whom the Commissioner, in pursuant of that sub-section, allowed to make the election, had of the nature of the election and the consequences of making the election.”


  1. The regulation requires consideration of two questions. The first question is whether the applicant had available to him all information relevant to the making of the election.
  2. The Tribunal considers that the applicant did have all necessary information available to him to make an election.
  3. The evidence indicates that the applicant had available information which indicated to him the amount that he would receive under each of the available options; that he attended seminars held by the respondent prior to his retirement; and he had consulted investment advisers as to how a lump sum would be invested.
  4. The next question is whether the applicant understood the nature of the election and the consequence of the election.
  5. This test is subjective, that is, it is the respondent’s understanding which is to be considered not that of some hypothetical reasonable person.
  6. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant understood the nature and consequences of his election.
  7. The applicant, on his own evidence, thought he would die shortly after retirement. He was a single man who wished to ensure that his superannuation entitlements did not “die” with him. He also wanted capital to enable him to purchase equipment to enable him to continue development of his inventions. The choice of superannuation schemes that the applicant made was not an inappropriate choice given the applicant’s personal situation and his intentions after retirement. His actual election is consistent with him understanding the election he made.
  8. The applicant had made a definite and clear decision to elect a lump sum prior to seeing Mr. Begaud and was advised by Mr. Begaud of the investment possibilities that were available to him having elected to take a lump sum.
  9. The Tribunal does not consider that the applicant’s health and mental condition was such at the date of his retirement to render the applicant of being incapable of understanding the nature of his election. There is no evidence that the applicant suffered any medical condition which affected his ability to comprehend. The medical conditions from which the applicant suffered were relatively minor physical ailments.
  10. The applicant was not a person substantially ignorant of mathematical concepts. He was a structural draftsman and inventor for many years and as such would have had a greater than rudimentary understanding and familiarity with mathematical concepts. There is nothing to suggest to the Tribunal that his lack of mathematical knowledge or lack of education or experience contributed or caused him to misunderstand the nature and consequences of the election made.
  11. On the evidence available to the Tribunal, the applicant had all the information relevant to making the decision available to him, and in making the decision understood the nature and consequence of the election. There are no relevant matters which persuade the Tribunal to allow the election to be cancelled.
  12. The decision under review is affirmed.
