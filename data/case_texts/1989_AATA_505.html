Income Tax - Allowable deductions - overseas trips by ancient history teacher to historic sites - trips undertaken in vacation periods at own expense - whether deductible.

The question to be decided in these applications is whether certain travel expenses incurred by the applicant are allowable deductions within the terms of sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). The applications relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1977, 30 June 1978, 30 June 1980 and 30 June 1981 and 30 June 1983. The taxation returns in question were lodged with the respondent in 1983.

2. Insofar as it is relevant sub-section 51(1) provides for a deduction in respect of outgoings to the extent in which they are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are outgoings of a private nature.

  1. The applicant is a high school teacher and was so employed by the Department of Education at all relevant times. Her specialisation as a teacher was in ancient history. She also taught modern history and English. The applicant claimed, and I accept, that she was a leader in her field so far as teaching of ancient history is concerned. I should say at the outset that the applicant impressed me as a person with an enthusiasm for her profession bordering upon a consuming passion. She is forthright, articulate and a most impressive witness. I have no reason to doubt her evidence which I found to be refreshingly honest and straightforward.
  2. Details of the claims made are as follows:-

Year ended 30 June 1977 $

Overseas travel expenses 2,218

(USSR and UK)

Year ended 30 June 1978

(Ireland, S.W. England and Greece)

(prelim, expenses re China)

Year ended 30 June 1981

(China)

Year ended 30 June 1982

(U.S. of A. and Central America)

In asserting the deductibility of these amounts to the respondent it fair to say that the applicant failed to give the respondent details of the amounts claimed and the specific nature of the travel. Whether the respondent subsequently became aware of these details is not apparent to the Tribunal.

  1. Those details were proved by oral testimony of the applicant. It was established that during the year ended 30 June 1977 she travelled on a pre-arranged tour of USSR. Of necessity she planned the tour through Intourist (Soviet Tourist Agency) in Australia. That was necessary because she wanted to go to specific historical sites which raised problems as to whether language interpreters would be available in those areas. The various sites were described by the applicant as important source material centres. The Intourist official was able to get the necessary visas to allow her to visit the particular sites. He was also able to design a personal itinerary which took the applicant to the particular historical sites which she nominated.
  2. Exhibit A sets out the itinerary designed by Intourist for the applicant. It may be summarised as follows:

At Samarkand the applicant went to see the tombs of Tamerlane and also the tomb of his grandfather and the remains of the grandfather's observatory, an offshoot of the Helenistic culture left behind in Russia by Alexander. That, she said, was relevant to the teaching of the Greek unit in ancient history because it evidenced the influence of the Helenistic culture right across to China. That is but an example of the types of enquiry and sightseeing undertaken by the applicant while in the USSR. The trip to Bukhara did not take place because of adverse weather conditions. At Erevan, the capital of Armenia, the applicant investigated excavations of the ancient site of Uratu, a former Assyrian kingdom near the border of Armenia ana Turkey. Photographs for use in classwork were taken at these sites. The UK leg of the trip was concerned with visits to the British Museum, Ethnographical Museum, Museum of London, Oxford, Cambridge, Avebury, Bath and Stonehenge.

  1. In the course of the evidence counsel for the respondent objected to the Tribunal hearing evidence as to outgoings incurred by the applicant in excess of the amount in dispute. The basis for the objection was that it was not relevant for the applicant to prove the outgoings had been apportioned before making the claim in the return and the objection. In other words that the applicant should not be allowed to establish outgoings incurred in excess of the amount claimed because the applicant had reduced the outgoings to the amount claimed so as to apportion between allowable and non-allowable amounts. This submission by counsel for the respondent that the applicant should have claimed a deduction for the full amount of the outgoings is without justification. The Tribunal ruled that it must first find the quantum of the outgoings as a fact and then determine what proportion, if any, of those outgoings is allowable - such amount not to exceed the amount claimed as a deduction in the notice of objection.
  2. In respect of the year ended 30 June 1978 the applicant travelled to South West England, Ireland and Greece. Exhibit C sets out the itinerary and the outgoings. The outgoings total $2,139. The outgoings set out in the exhibit do not include any amounts for meals or accommodation. In South West England the applicant spent her time photographing and looking at iron age, bronze age, pre-Roman and Roman remains including stone circles, monuments, single standing stones and remains etc. She related these activities to the teaching of year 11 ancient history. In locating these historical sites she relied upon a guide to prehistoric and Roman monuments.
  3. After England the applicant travelled to Ireland for a short time and then to London for visits to the British and London museums and then visits to Oxford and Cambridge. From there she went to Greece where she visited Delphi, Athens and Angora, the latter involving visits to ancient history museums and archaeological sites. The applicant's oral evidence made it clear beyond doubt that she travelled to each of these places for the purpose of researching and collecting materials of ancient history topics. There was no evidence of recreational pursuit, nor could such pursuit be inferred from the evidence before the Tribunal.
  4. During the year ended 30 June 1981 the applicant travelled to China. A claim for a deduction of $125 in the year ended 30 June 1980 is made up of $100 paid as a deposit for the China trip and $25 for a passport. The applicant's representative conceded the claim regarding the passport.
  5. The applicant gave evidence to the effect that the school at which she taught was proposing the introduction of an ancient history course on India and China. Resource materials were apparently hard to come by in Australia and the applicant found it necessary to travel to China for the purpose of collecting materials. Exhibit E is a copy of a 3 page submission by the applicant dated 20 April 1979 seeking approval for the grant of professional development leave for the period 6 August 1979 to 14 September 1979 embracing four working weeks and two weeks term vacation. The four working weeks were to be used as follows:
    • (a) 6-10 August - Griffith University School of Asian Studies;
(b) 27-31 August - China following previous two weeks of holidays period also in China; and

(c) 3-7 September - Griffith University School of Asian Studies.

The applicant proposed to enter China on 13 August and return to Australia on 1 September 1979.

Suffice it to say that she approached the trip as a professional teacher of ancient history rather than as a tourist. The ticket for the trip cost $2,190 of which $100 was paid in the year ended 30 June 1980 and $2,090 was paid in the year ended 30 June 1981. The cost of the ticket included accommodation in Hong Kong and all costs in China for travel accommodation and meals. Those are the only amounts claimed in respect of those years although it is clear from the evidence that the applicant incurred outgoings other than the cost of the air ticket in the year ended 30 June 1981 during the course of the trip.

15. In the year ended 30 June 1983 the applicant went on a tour to parts of the U.S. of A, Mexico and South America arriving in Los Angeles on 9 December 1981 and returning to Australia on 20 January 1983. The first ten days were spent visiting New York, Cleveland, Washington and New Orleans to visit the museums best equipped with Amerindian materials back to 30,000 BC. Detailed evidence of these visits was given by the applicant it is sufficient to say that I am satisfied that each of the cities was visited for the purpose of investigating and collecting materials relating to ancient history topics except in respect of New Orleans where the emphasis appears to have been on modern history topics.

16. The visit to Mexico concentrated on historical sites and the anthropology museum. The applicant then travelled to Lima, Peru in order to visit the Amerindian gold museum and two other archaeological museums in the city. She visited Inca and pre Inca sites and also museums dealing with the early Spanish occupation, Renaissance history and the conquest of the New World

  1. All of this clearly reflected, in my view, a determination on the part of the applicant that to be an effective ancient history teacher it was necessary for her to research at first hand and to obtain relevant materials of her subject. She wanted to be able to give her pupils first hand accounts of the sites, museums, etc rather than rely upon what lawyers sometimes refer to as "hearsay upon hearsay".
  2. There was no dispute that the applicant had incurred outgoings amounting to $7,689. These were summarised in the income tax return as follows:

As will be apparent there is a discrepancy of $20 between the amount claimed and the amount said to be incurred. No issue was made of this, the applicant being content to maintain the claim at $7,689. No apportionment of outgoings was made by the applicant in making the claim.

19. Each of these trips was taken by the applicant in the course of paid leave from her employment during the school vacations and at her own volition. She was not required by the Education Department to undertake the trips but, on her evidence, she was encouraged to undertake the trips.

  1. During the years in dispute the applicant held a bachelor of arts degree completed in 1959, a bachelor of education degree completed in 1968 and a further bachelor of arts degree completed in 1978. The last degree included honours in ancient history. At all relevant times she was employed as a teacher subject to a senior teacher in charge of the department in the school. No evidence was lead as to the applicant's prospects of promotion to a position of senior teacher.
  2. Counsel for the respondent Commissioner submitted that although the evidence established that the applicant undertook the travel to improve her ability and capacity as a teacher, there was not a clear nexus between the outgoings and her assessable income. In essence he referred to the essential character test to be applied to the outgoings which, so it was submitted, thereby did not fall within the positive limbs of section 51. Reliance was placed upon dicta of the High Court in various cases to the forefront of which were FCT v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60, 12 AID 348, and FCT v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 CLR 494, 71 ATC 4184.

22. The difficulites which have arisen in countless first instance cases similar to the present case are amply illustrated by two Taxation Board of Review decisions. The first is Case N24 81 ATC 131 involving, in the words of the headnote, an experienced history teacher employed by a private school who went on a three week trip to China which was predominately the usual tourist package save that she was also shown some places thought to be of particular interest to historians. It was the taxpayer's case that she believed that the trip was relevant to her occupation and made her a better teacher but she was not seeking promotion to a higher paid classification of teacher. Taxation Board of Review No 3 held that the outgoings were not incurred in gaining or producing the teacher's assessable income. Dr Gerber reviewed the authorities at some length and concluded that the taxpayer could not succeed since the "taxpayer cannot take her case beyond the point of increased professional competence simpliciter" (p.137).

23. In Case 0104 83 ATC 522 the same Board of Review had to consider overseas travel expenses incurred by an ancient history teacher. Once again adopting the headnote, the taxpayer, a high school ancient history teacher, went on a four week historical and archaeological tour to Greece and Italy. The tour was led by classic lecturers from two universities. The taxpayer's purpose in making the trip was to make herself more credible as a teacher by seeing some of the sites she was lecturing about, to show to her superiors her attitude of involvement in her teaching subject and to help secure her advancement to teaching at the tertiary level. Ten months after the trip she successfully applied for a job at a tertiary institution at a higher salary. She believed that her overseas experience played a part in her selection for that position. The majority held that the overseas trip was reasonably calculated to lead to advancement and increased earnings.

24. In his reasons at page 523 the Chairman quoted the taxpayer's evidence to the general effect that she felt the travel would influence her superiors as to future promotions and that she had an eye on a lectureship and went on to say:

"Once that uncontested evidence is accepted, and regard is had to the subsequent advancement in her teaching career, a real connection is established between the expenditure and the taxpayer's assessable income in the sense that the tour went some way to establishing for her such pre-eminence as a teacher with considerable practical experience who yet preserved a professional and ambitious approach to her area of expertise, as to secure her advancement to teaching at the tertiary level; her evidence is clear that, from the beginning, she saw this particular tour as an instrument to that end. In the words of Waddell J. in F.C. of T. v. Smith 78 ATC 4157 at p.4162, ... in reliance upon the prospects of promotion which appeared reasonably to be present, (she) spent money to earn more in the future'."

25. At page 525 Dr Gerber (Member) examined the decision of the High Court in Finn and concluded with a somewhat pragmatic conclusion in these words at pages 525-6:

"I see no reason to doubt that she looked on the expenditure as reasonably calculated to earn increased income.

In the circumstances, it may be gratuitous to add that the so-called 'perceived connection' with the derivation of income is invariably assumed where self-employed professionals attend conferences in remote corners of the earth. If senior partners of legal firms can rummage tax-free through the bazaars of Hong Kong in search of colourful bolts of silk, only to learn that, as Australian graduates, they have little contract, less equity and tort nothing ('striving to better, oft we marre what's well': King Lear), I see no good reason why pedagogues trying to improve their teaching should not be granted a similar fiscal indulgence. The next legal convention, to be held in Italy, is already widely advertised. If self-employed lawyers can claim the cost of a professional visit to Rome, I can find no plausible reason why ancient history teachers should not be likewise tax free to roam. After all, Italy is at least as renowned for its antiquities as for its ancient common law tradition. It is part and parcel of the job. It has the nexus, and for good measure, the perceived connection."

26. It would, of course, not be correct for this Tribunal to merely align the present applications with one or other of these decisions. Although the facts are similar to the point where they are hardly distinguishable there are differences. Furthermore, there is no rule of "stare decisus" in this Tribunal albeit that there is a general policy of maintaining consistency in decisions. In the minefield of cases involving teachers claiming outgoings in respect of overseas travel the Tribunal must resort to basic principles rather than attempt to reconcile the previous decisions. I did that in Case VL 88 ATC 101, a case involving overseas travel expenses incurred by a music teacher, as follows at pages 104-106:-

"It is strange that the parties did not direct the Tribunal's attention to the decision of the High Court in F.C. of T. v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60; (1961) 12 A.T.D. 348. In the words of Dixon C.J. that case involved a claim for a deduction (C.L.R. p.63: A.T.D. p.348) :

"... by a professional officer in the service of the Government of Western Australia. His claim is that he incurred the expenses of travelling in order the better to fit himself to perform the work which the Western Australian Government required of him ...’

The claimed deduction before the High Court in Finn's case was an amount of £352 which related to part of a period of leave in Britain and Europe devoted to the study of architecture. It was admitted by the Commissioner that Mr Finn's activities abroad were confined to architecture and its study. Dixon C.J. was satisfied that Finn had devoted his available time to the advancement ... of his architectural equipment, outlook and skill' (C.L.R. p.65; A.T.D. p.349).

Mr Finn was a senior design architect in the Public Works Department and paid on the maximum of the salary range prescribed for his position. It would be by promotion only that he could go into a higher salary range. His evidence before the Board of Review, and used in the High court, in response to a question as to whether he believed his period abroad would better fit him for promotion in the future was (C.L.R. p.66; A.T.D. p.350):

'"I hope so. That was the whole point of the trip. I mean, this is purely from my side of it. The reason why I did it was that the Principal Architect was due to retire at the end of the year and there were two people senior to me, in seniority, and so the purpose of my trip was to gain the extra knowledge to fit myself to perhaps get possible promotion over them."'

Before the Board of Review (Case K70) (1960) 10 T.B.R.D. 370 - decision dated 19 February 1960) it was established that up to the time of the hearing before the Board there had been no variation in the taxpayer's duties or his salary as a result of his trip (per Chairman at p.373). Mr Finn travelled to Europe and Britain from May to September 1957 and then proceeded to South America. The assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1957 was the assessment before the Board and the High Court. Considerable time had elapsed, it may be assumed, before the matter came on for hearing before the Board of Review.

Dixon C.J. elucidated the law relating to subsec. 51(1) at C.L.R. pp.66-67; A.T.D. p.350 as follows:

' The words of s.51 which are most material to the primary question on which the taxpayer's claim to a deduction depends are "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income". With respect to this phrase, before the consolidation of 1936 was made it was remarked that "the expression 'in gaining or producing' has the force of 'in the course of gaining' and looks rather to the scope of the operations or activities and the relevance thereto of the expenditure than to the purpose itself: Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v. F.C. of T. [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 C.L.R. 295 at p.309. In Ronpibon Tin N.L. and Toncrkah Compound N.L. v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 47, at p.56 the judgment of the Court contained two or three comments that may be relevant. It was said "No doubt the expression 'in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing' lays down a test that is different from that implied by the words 'in gaining or producing'. But these latter words have a very wide operation and will cover almost all the ground occupied by the alternative." (1949) 78 C.L.R., at p.56. Again, "For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an out-going incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end." (1949) 78 C.L.R., at p.56. There is a third comment which may be added though perhaps it does not go so far, "In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income." (1949) 78 C.L.R. at p.57'."

Dixon C.J. concluded that the expenditure claimed was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and that sec.51 does not require that the assessable income be assessable income of the current year of income.

His Honour then went on to consider whether the expenditure should be held to be outgoings of capital or of a capital, private of domestic nature. That question he answered in the negative with the following dicta at C.L.R. pp.68-69: A.T.D. pp.351-352:

"The money was laid out by the taxpayer in the acquisition of better knowledge of a skilled profession. The pursuit of information concerning the modernization or improvements in an art is part of the constant process of keeping up to date which skilled professions call upon those who practise them to pursue, though sometimes in vain. Had he dwelt nearer to the sources of such knowledge and information he doubtless would have visited them from time to time in his career. As it was he had been able to do so only once before and in the meantime had depended on literature. It is simply a false analogy to treat him in his visit abroad as engaged in the equivalent of the acquisition of something of an enduring nature and therefore capital. You cannot treat an improvement of knowledge in a professional man as the equivalent of the extension of plant in a factory. Unfortunately, skill and knowledge in most arts and sciences are not permanent possessions: they fade and become useless unless the art or the science is constantly pursued or, to change the metaphor, nourished and revised.'

Kitto J. was of the same opinion as the Chief Justice but gave reasons which tended to concentrate on whether the taxpayer was acting within the scope of his office of senior design architect in the Public Works Department. At C.L.R. p.69; A.T.D. p.352 his Honour had this to say:

' the respondent incurred the expenditure during a period of leave, and in carrying out activities beyond any which had been or could lawfully have been specifically required of him by the Government. But it was nevertheless in my opinion incidental to the proper execution of his office and not otherwise that he engaged in those activities. For the office was of a kind which by its nature made incumbent on the occupant much more than the performance of set duties at set times. Its professional status implied an obligation of progressive acquaintance with a living and developing art.'

Kitto J. was satisfied that the taxpayer undertook the travel within the scope of the office and therefore in the gaining of his salary. It should be noted that Kitto J. was not concerned as to whether the overseas travel would lead to an increase in that salary.

Windeyer J. also agreed with the Chief Justice emphasising as had the Chief Justice that each case of this kind must depend on its own facts. His Honour was prepared to state a broader proposition than the other members of the Court at C.L.R. p.70; A.T.D. p.352 as follows:

'Generally speaking, it seems to me, a taxpayer who gains income by the exercise of his skill in some profession or calling and who incurs expenses in maintaining or increasing his learning, knowledge, experience and ability in that profession or calling necessarily incurs those expenses in carrying on his profession or calling. Whether he be paid fees by different persons seeking his skilled services from time to time, or be paid a regular salary by one person employing him to exercise his skill, matters not in my opinion.'

In F.C. of T. v. Hatchett 71 ATC 4184, Menzies J held that there was a plain connection between the obtaining of a Teacher's Higher Certificate and the gaining of assessable income because the certificate resulted in additional income being derived in the following year of income. However his Honour held that fees paid to a university for a Bachelor of Arts course had a problematical and remote relationship with assessable income having regard to the taxpayer's lack of success in passing the university examinations.

At p.4187 his Honour stated the test as follows:

'The test to be applied is a more stringent one, namely were the outgoings incurred in gaining assessable income?

Here I am not dealing with the general question whether the payment of university fees can ever afford a deduction from assessable income; I am dealing with the particular question whether the fees paid by the taxpayer in the circumstances already stated are deductible ... there must be a perceived connection between the outgoing and assessable income. Had the taxayer paid fees for subjects in the Faculty of Law, it would, I think, have been obvious that the fees were not allowable deductions. In my view the payment of such fees would have as much connection with the taxpayer's assessable income as the fees in fact paid.'

His Honour then went on to discuss the decision of the High Court in Finn's case drawing attention to the fact that Windeyer J adopted the wording of the second positive limb of sec.41(2) in his judgment (quoted above). Menzies J concluded that there must be a connection between the expenditure and assessable income and while he was able to find that connection in relation to the Higher Teacher's Certificate he could not find it in relation to the university fees. Clearly Hatchett's case does not stand for the proposition that university fees cannot be deductible. Rather, it illustrates that each case must be determined on its own facts, the decision depending on whether there is sufficient connection between the outgoings and the taxpayer's assessable income."

27. The decision of Helsham J in FCT v. White (1975) 1 NSWLR 1 concerned a different factual matter but analysed the decisions in Finn and Hatchett. After careful analysis his Honour concluded at page 7:

"As the result of the decision in the two cases, it seems to me possible to say that expenses incurred in pursuing studies associated with employment will qualify as allowable deductions under s.51 when it can be said that those studies are part and parcel of the employment, which means that the expenditure is incurred in the process of carrying out the employee's duties, or, even if they are not such, they can be seen to have a direct effect on income. Where that cannot be seen, I think it will be difficult to establish the necessary connexion between the study activity and the employment, so as to give study expenses the character of outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the income derived from the employment."

  1. It was suggested that because the applicant incurred the outgoings of her own volition they were somehow not deductible on that ground. The relevant circumstances of the present case are no different in any material respect to the facts in Finn. In that case the outgoings were in respect of overseas travel while the taxpayer was on leave. The voluntariness of the outgoings does not alter the essential character of outgoings incurred in the course of gaining or producing a taxpayer's assessable income.
  2. Applying the test enunciated by Helsham J in White I find that the research and gathering of materials by the applicant was undertaken in the course of the applicant's duties as a teacher of ancient history. That finding is based on what I consider to be an incontrovertible fact i.e. that a teacher must engage in research and preparation to perform the functions of a teacher. That this applicant, being a dedicated teacher, chose to engage in more research and preparation than may be undertaken by other teachers does not in any sense detract from the fact that the research and collection of materials was performed in the course of her employment as a teacher.
  3. There is no evidence before the Tribunal to support a finding that the outgoings would have any direct effect on the applicant's future assessable income even in the sense that Mr Finn hoped that his period abroad would better fit him for promotion in the future.
  4. However, the applicant has satisfied the first of the tests enunciated by Helsham J and that is sufficient to bring her within the authority of Finn's Case. This is because her activities overseas were part and parcel of her duties as a teacher of ancient history. That she undertook the research activities while on paid leave from her employment is, as shown by the decision in Finn, irrelevant.
  5. I turn now to the quantum of the outgoings. By way of a general finding I am satisfied that each of the trips was undertaken by the applicant primarily and principally for the purpose of research into areas of learning directly related to her duties as a teacher. While there was some evidence of private activities those amounted to no more than would occur during any other working week and are de minimus for the purposes of these applications.
  6. In respect of the year ended 30 June 1977 an amount in excess of the amount of $2,218 claimed was incurred. The amount claimed will be allowed.
  7. For the year ended 30 June 1978 the amount claimed is $2,788. During examination-in-chief the applicant swore that the amounts set out in Exhibit C were the amounts incurred which had been claimed as deductions. The amounts set out in the exhibit are allowable deductions. They total $2,149. There is no other evidence of amounts incurred and although the Tribunal queried the parties as to whether the exhibit proved the amount claimed neither representative took the matter any further. The amount proved before the Tribunal is $2,149 and that amount will be allowed.
    1. The amounts relating to the years ended 30 June 1980 and 30 June 1981 were proved as to $100 for the year ended 30 June 1980 and $2,090 for the year ended 30 June 1981 and will be allowed accordingly.
    2. In respect of the year ended 30 June 1982 the applicant and respondent agreed before the Tribunal that an amount of $7,709 had been incurred. The applicant claims $7,689 and that amount will be allowed. The difference appears to represent nothing more than a clerical error but was specifically not pursued by the applicant's representative. The claimed expenditure does not include amounts of a private nature. The claim will be allowed in full.
    3. The Tribunal will set aside the objection decisions under review and allow the claims to the extent indicated.

This and the preceding twenty (20) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matter of QT86/3731-3734 and QT87/736.

Secretary
