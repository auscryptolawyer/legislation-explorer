DECISION

SHEPPARD J In these matters I have had the opportunity of reading the judgments to be delivered by the other judges of the Court. In relation to the question of Mr. McAusland's retainer, I agree with the conclusions of Gummow J and with his reasons therefor. In relation to the other matters which were the subject of argument before us, I agree with French J.

GUMMOW J The complex procedural history of this litigation is described in the judgment of French J. I agree with the orders proposed by his Honour as to the disposal of what he identifies as the second and third appeals.

2. The conclusion I have reached as to the substantive question on the first appeal reinforces the conclusions expressed by French J on the second and third appeals.

3. As to the first appeal, I agree with what French J says upon the issues of standing and jurisdiction, with particular reference to Darling Downs Investments Pty Ltd v Ellwood (1988) 18 FCR 510. However, I have reached a different conclusion upon the substantive question in the first appeal. This turns upon the operation of the order of the Supreme Court of New South Wales made 23 November 1987, and the proper construction of s. 383 of the Companies (N.S.W.) Code 1981 ("the Code").

4. It is necessary to recapitulate some basic facts.

5. Antlers Pty Limited ("Antlers") was incorporated on 26 May 1971 under the Companies Act 1961 (N.S.W.) ("the 1961 Act"). Its memorandum and articles of association adopted, with modifications, Table A.

6. On 2 November 1971, Mrs Kavich and her brother, Mr Portelli, were appointed directors of Antlers. Regulation 64 of Table A dealt with the retirement from office of directors at annual general meetings. Regulation 64 did not apply to Antlers and the result was that Mrs Kavich and Mr Portelli were not required by effluxion of time to retire and face re-election.

7. On 28 February 1978, Mrs Kavich became a bankrupt. Her office as director thereupon was vacated by operation of reg. 72 (b) of Table A. As the remaining director, Mr Portelli was authorised by reg. 84 of Table A to act for two purposes. The first was to increase the number of directors to the necessary quorum; this appears to have been two (reg. 83). The second purpose was the summoning of a general meeting of Antlers.

8. There is no evidence that either of these steps had been taken before 21 August 1978. On that day, the Supreme Court of New South Wales ordered that Antlers be wound up.

9. For the purposes of the present appeal, I am prepared to assume that the winding up order did not have the effect of discharging Mr Portelli from his office as director; see the discussion of the authorities by Cohen J in Lord Corporation Pty Ltd v Green (1991) 22 NSWLR 532 at 541-543. But what is fairly clear from the authorities is that even if Mr Portelli retained his office during the currency of the winding up, he was in that period unable to exercise the already limited powers under reg. 84 as sole remaining director.

10. By motion filed in the Supreme Court on 3 November 1987, orders were sought that the appointment of the liquidator be terminated or, in the alternative, there be an order "permanently staying any further proceedings on the winding up of the company". The motion was supported by affidavits including one by the liquidator in which he stated that he consented to the termination of his appointment and to a stay of the winding up of Antlers.

11. On 23 November 1987, a Judge of the Supreme Court (Kearney J) made orders pursuant to s. 383 of the Code. They were entered on 25 November 1987. In their form as settled, the orders are as follows:

"THE COURT ORDERS that -

1. The winding up of the company be terminated on 1/12/87.

2. Costs of the motion to form part of the costs, charges

and
THE COURT DIRECTS that -

of the winding up."

12. The order shows Mr Laurence Brian Hunter as "plaintiff" although other materials in the Supreme Court file show that Mr Peter Bernard Allen had been appointed official liquidator by order made 14 July 1986, after the retirement of Mr Hunter. It was Mr Allen who swore the liquidator's affidavit in support of the application.

13. No meeting of members as provided in para. 3 of the orders was held. On 13 April 1993, Mr Portelli appointed Mrs Kavich as an additional director. The primary Judge upheld a challenge to the retainer of a solicitor engaged by a Board of Antlers which had been constituted in this fashion.

15. A provision of like nature to s. 243 had first appeared in Britain in the Companies Act 1862 (U.K.), s. 89. Similar provisions were introduced into the companies legislation of all the Australian colonies; see the list in Rich "Australian and New Zealand Company Law being the supplement to Stiebel's Company Law", 1913, pp 237-238.

16. The concluding words of s. 89 "on such Terms and subject to such Conditions as it deems fit", indicated that the order of the Court might be made conditional upon the happening of certain events. An example is the order of Swinfen Eady J made on 11 February 1911 in Re W. Griggs and Sons (1906) Limited, which is set out in Stiebel "Company Law and Precedents", 3rd Ed., Vol. 2, 1929, pp 1349-1350. The material part of the order stated:

"THIS COURT DOTH ORDER that the Liquidator the said H.A.K.

do forthwith summon a General Meeting of the Shareholders of

the above-named Company for the purpose of appointing

Directors thereof and that thereupon all further proceedings

in the voluntary winding-up of the above-named Company be

stayed."

17. Section 383 of the Code, under which Kearney J acted, went further than s. 243 of the 1961 Act. It specifically provided that the Court might give directions for the resumption of the management and control of the company by its officers, including directions for the convening of a general meeting of members to elect directors to take office upon the termination of the winding up.

18. Section 383 stated:

"383 (1)

At any time during the winding up of a company, the Court

may, on the application of the liquidator or of a creditor

or contributory, make an order staying the winding up either

indefinitely or for a limited time or terminating the

winding up on a date specified in the order.

(2) On such an application, the Court may, before making an

order, direct the liquidator to furnish a report with
to a relevant fact or matter.

(3) Where the Court has made an order terminating the winding

up, the Court may give such directions as it thinks fit for

the resumption of the management and control of the company

by its officers, including directions for the convening of a

19. Section 383 (as it appeared in the Victorian Code) was discussed by Tadgell J in Re Skay Fashions Pty Ltd (in liq) (1986) 10 ACLR 743 at 746. In the course of refusing an application, his Honour said:

"The considerations which ought to move the court when

assessing the merits of an application for the termination

of a winding up are in general those that are applied, in

the bankruptcy law, to an application to rescind a

sequestration order. I refer to the well-known decision of

Buckley J in Re Telescriptor Syndicate Ltd (1903) 3 Ch 174

and particularly at 180. The court will refuse to act

simply upon the assent of creditors. Here some of the

creditors of the company have said they do not oppose the

application; others have expressed no point of view and some

of them, as I have indicated, have opposed the application.

The Court also has to consider whether the termination of

the winding up will be conducive or detrimental to

commercial morality and the interests of the public at

large. It is a clear axiom that insolvent companies should

be wound up and that they should stay in liquidation unless

solvency can be demonstrated. If solvency could be

demonstrated here, it would be no more than bare solvency.

That is about as much as could ever be expected if an

adjournment were allowed. The court would, if making an

order to terminate a liquidation, probably, and in the

ordinary course should, give directions for the resumption

of management and control of the company by its officers.

As I say, here I am given no information at all as to who

might conduct the company's affairs or how they might be

conducted. For all one knows, they would be conducted in

the same sloppy fashion as they have been conducted

heretofore."

20. In an appropriate case, the Court may grant a temporary stay of the winding up conditional upon certain steps being taken in the period of the stay. An example is provided by the decision of McLelland J Collins v G. Collins and Sons Pty Ltd (1984) 2 ACLC 595. The plaintiffs in that case were the sole shareholders and directors of the company. His Honour accepted the undertaking by their counsel that during the period of the temporary stay of the winding up, the company would not trade and that the plaintiffs would immediately upon the expiration of the temporary stay notify the liquidator whether they were proceeding with the application. The proposal of the plaintiffs was that in this period of some six weeks there would be a reorganisation of the company's affairs which would provide funds to pay all creditors of the company other than the plaintiffs.

21. Section 383 of the Code, like its predecessors, is a provision plainly remedial in nature. As such it should be given a generous construction so as to permit the fullest relief which will be allowed on a fair reading of its language: Devenish v Jewel Food Stores Pty Limited [1991] HCA 7; (1991) 172 CLR 32 at 44.

22. Indeed, before us there was no submission that any of the various constructions sought to be placed upon the orders of 23 November 1987 was beyond the scope of s. 383.

23. In my view, the terms of s. 383 of the Code are wide enough to authorise the Court to give directions which require the convening of a general meeting of members to elect directors to take office upon the termination of the winding up. This is so even where there is during the currency of the winding up, as I have assumed to be so in this case, a remaining director who might otherwise seek, upon termination of the winding up, to enjoy a revived power himself to summon a general meeting. If that path were pursued, then plainly there might be an hiatus because the power of the remaining director to summon the general meeting might first be exercisable only upon the termination of the winding up. Any practical difficulties are obviated by construction of the legislation to give the Court the power to order meetings to be held in advance of the coming into operation of the order terminating the winding up. A situation may then be achieved where the affairs of the company can come back fully to life immediately upon the termination of the winding up.

24. In my view, the three paragraphs of the orders as entered on 25 November 1987 are to be read together as an exercise of the power given the Supreme Court by s. 383. The purpose sought to be achieved by the orders which, in my view, is plain upon their face, is that the winding up of Antlers was to terminate on 1 December 1987, whereupon directors were to take office, being those elected at the meeting to be held on the day before, 30 November.

25. The orders are not so expressed as to provide for the election on 30 November as an alternative or optional procedure to any which might otherwise concurrently have been available. In fact, as I have indicated, none was available on 30 November, the powers of the remaining director, Mr Portelli, being at best then suspended by reason of the winding up of Antlers.

26. The Supreme Court did not make an order absolute in terms, for the winding up of the company to come to an end, followed by a direction to hold a meeting at some later date. Plainly, 1 December 1987 is selected as the date of the cessation of the winding up because that is one day after the date appointed for the meeting to elect directors. A requirement for the election of new directors may be seen as something the Court regards as necessary before it is satisfied that a stay or termination of the winding up is appropriate. In the present case, para. 3 of the orders should be seen as part of and, in that sense, a condition of, the coming into operation of the termination order.

27. It follows, in my view, that the winding up of Antlers has not been terminated pursuant to the orders made 23 November 1987. Accordingly, Antlers remains in winding up. For this reason there was in office no Board of directors of the company which could resolve to give the retainer to which there has been a challenge.

28. The challenge was correctly upheld, and the principal proceeding therefore was properly dismissed. This state of affairs should have been plainly reflected in the orders made by the primary Judge. Subject to a reformulation of the orders, the first appeal should be dismissed with costs.

FRENCH J

Introduction

A solicitor was instructed by persons associated with a company to bring an application to set aside consent orders and a compromise agreement in earlier proceedings in which the company was a party. The solicitor's retainer was challenged by motion on the basis that the company was without any validly appointed directors and that the solicitor had no authority to act on its behalf. The challenge was made good and it was held that the company had no authority to institute the proceedings because it lacked directors. The solicitor was ordered to pay the costs of the action. He appeals against that decision and the company is joined as an appellant in the same appeal. A subsequent order in later proceedings appointing a receiver to the company, in part by reason of its lack of directors, is also the subject of an appeal, as is a subsequent order refusing to rescind the appointment of the receiver. The case is complicated by the fact that one of the company's former directors became bankrupt while holding office, thereby vacating that office. Further, the company was the subject of a winding up order which was later terminated with a direction for the holding of a general meeting to elect directors. The meeting was not held. The case raises a short but important point about the power of a court to give directions relating to the resumption of the management and control of a company when a winding up order is terminated.

Background History

2. Antlers Pty Limited (Antlers) is a company which was incorporated on 26 May 1971. Its Memorandum and Articles of Association adopted Table A of the Companies Act 1961 (NSW), subject to certain modifications. The company appears to have been incorporated as a shelf company. On 2 November 1971, the two subscribers' shares, which constituted its issued capital, were transferred to Vera Mary Kavich and her brother, Frank Portelli. Both were appointed directors of the company on the same day. By virtue of modifications in the company's Articles of Association to the provisions of Table A of the Fourth Schedule of the Companies Act 1961 (NSW), the directors were not required to retire and face re-election by effluxion of time. On 28 February 1978, Mrs Kavich became a bankrupt. Her office as director was vacated by operation of Regulation 72(b) of the company's Articles:

"72. The office of director shall become vacant if the

director-

.

.

.

(b) becomes bankrupt or makes an arrangement or composition

with his creditors generally."

3. On 21 August 1978, Antlers was wound up by order of the Supreme Court of New South Wales. On 28 February 1981, Mrs Kavich was discharged from bankruptcy by operation of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth). On 23 November 1987, the Supreme Court of New South Wales made the following order:

"1. The winding up of the company be terminated on 1/12/87.

2. Costs of the motion to form part of the costs, charges and

expenses of the winding
THE COURT DIRECTS that -

3. A meeting of the members of the company be convened by one

day's notice in writing by the applicant to be held

on 30/11/87 to elect directors to take office upon

termination of the winding up."

The applicant was Mrs Kavich. The direction referred to in the order was made pursuant to sub-s.383(3) of the Companies (NSW) Code 1981. The meeting directed by the Court was not then convened. No meeting of members to appoint directors was convened until July 1993.

4. Mrs Kavich has two sons, Anthony Francis Kavich and Paul Kavich. Anthony Francis Kavich became a bankrupt on 1 June 1990 upon the acceptance of his debtors petition. On 23 July 1992, the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, claiming to be a creditor of Anthony Kavich, filed an application in NB976 of 1990. The Deputy Commissioner sought a declaration that funds in a bank account in the name of Paul Kavich were the property of Anthony Kavich. An order was sought that Anthony and Paul Kavich and the bank transfer the money to the Official Trustee. On the day the application was filed, the Deputy Commissioner obtained an ex parte order from Einfeld J restraining the Kavich brothers and the bank from dealing with the funds. A further ex parte order was obtained from Einfeld J on 4 August 1992 restraining the Kavichs from dealing with certain shares registered in the name of Paul Kavich. The application was amended on 11 August 1992 to add a claim for final relief in respect of the shares, similar to the relief claimed in respect of the funds. On 18 August 1992, further ex parte orders were obtained restraining Anthony and Paul Kavich, their grandmother, Carmen Portelli, and a relative, Paul Baiada, from dealing with funds up to specified amounts in nominated accounts in two other banks. A further amended application was filed on 19 August 1992, adding Mrs Portelli and Mr Baiada as respondents and seeking declarations and orders in respect of the funds in the additional bank accounts.

5. On 21 August 1992, the application was further amended and the Official Trustee, who had been named as a respondent, was joined as an applicant. Vera Kavich, Antlers and Mrs Kavich's brother, Frank Portelli, were also added as respondents. Relief was claimed in the alternative against these three respondents. The alternative relief in effect sought declarations that the funds and shares in question belonged to Antlers, the shares in which were vested in the Official Trustee by virtue of their beneficial ownership by Mrs Kavich at the time of her bankruptcy, it being contended that the other shareholder, Mr Portelli, was a mere nominee for Mrs Kavich. The alternative relief was in truth a proceeding in Mrs Kavich's bankruptcy.

6. Subsequently, a settlement agreement was arrived at and, on 27 November 1992, Einfeld J made orders disposing of the application. The orders reflected an agreement between the parties that a specified sum of money be withdrawn from Mr Paul Kavich's bank account and paid to the Official Trustee to be dealt with as property of the bankrupt estate of Mrs Kavich. The Mareva orders were varied to allow the withdrawal of the agreed sum and were to be discharged on payment being made. On the same day, however, the Deputy Commissioner issued a notice of assessment under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) to Mrs Kavich and on 30 November issued a notice of assessment to Antlers and notices under s.218 of the Act to the three banks in respect of the accounts of Paul Kavich, Mrs Portelli and Mr Baiada. The s.218 notices related to the tax due by Antlers.

7. On 3 December 1992, the Deputy Commissioner commenced proceeding NG892 of 1992 in which Antlers, the three banks, Mr Baiada, Mrs Portelli and Paul Kavich were named as respondents. The relief sought was in substance declarations and orders in respect of the s. 218 notices issued to the banks. On the day the application was issued, Einfeld J made ex parte orders restraining the respondents from dealing with the funds. These were the same funds covered by the Mareva orders in matter number NB976 of 1990. John Atkinson McAusland, a solicitor, filed an appearance on behalf of Antlers. On 10 December 1992, Antlers and Paul Kavich conceded that the funds in Paul Kavich's bank account were beneficially owned by Antlers. At the request of the Deputy Commissioner, his Honour made a declaration to that effect. On 15 December 1992, in matter number NB976 of 1990, Antlers filed a notice of motion seeking to vary or set aside the orders previously made in that matter. The notice was signed by Mr McAusland although he was not solicitor on the record. However, no objection was taken to his retainer in that proceeding. On 7 January 1993, Burchett J varied the orders made in both NB976 of 1990 and NG892 of 1992 to allow Paul Kavich to withdraw a specified sum from his bank account for the purpose of paying it to the Deputy Commissioner on account of the tax assessed against Antlers. In February 1993, following a directions hearing in these matters, three further proceedings were commenced. In application NG105 of 1993, orders were sought between the same parties as in NB976 of 1990 to enforce the agreement noted by the Court on 27 November 1992 and claiming interest on the sum payable under the agreement. In application number NG118 of 1993, Paul Kavich, Vera Kavich and Mr Portelli sought orders against the Deputy Commissioner and Official Trustee setting aside the orders made on 27 November 1992.

8. Application number NG117 of 1993 was also filed about the same time as NG118 and is the proceeding in which the first of the present appeals arises. In NG117 of 1993, Antlers is the applicant and the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy are the respondents. Antlers alleges by its statement of claim in those proceedings that the orders made by Einfeld J on 27 November 1992 and the agreement made between the parties and noted by the Court on that day in NB976 of 1990 were vitiated by mutual mistake on the part of the respondents and, alternatively, unilateral mistake on the part of Antlers. It also makes a restitutionary claim based on alleged unjust enrichment on the part of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy. Mr McAusland was the solicitor on the record in that proceeding.

9. On 8 March 1993, all five proceedings came before Einfeld J for directions which were given together with an order that the matters "be consolidated and heard together and that evidence in each of the proceedings be evidence in each of the other proceedings". This direction should not be read as other than a direction that the applications be heard together and that evidence in each be evidence in each of the other proceedings. At that time, the validity of Mr McAusland's retainer was raised. An attempt to pre-empt further challenge to the retainer was made on 13 April 1993. Mr Portelli convened a meeting of directors of Antlers comprising himself alone. He purported to appoint Mrs Kavich to fill a casual vacancy. They then met, purportedly as directors, and ratified Mr McAusland's authority to act for Antlers in the pending proceedings and generally. Mrs Kavich's appointment was purportedly made pursuant to Regulation 84 of the Articles which provides:

"84. The continuing directors may act notwithstanding

any vacancy in their body, but if and so long as their

number is reduced below the number fixed by or

pursuant to the regulations of the company as the

necessary quorum of directors, the continuing

directors or director may act for the purpose of

increasing the number of directors to that number or

of summoning a general meeting of the company, but for

no other purpose."

10. On 14 April 1993, a notice of motion was filed in Court by leave seeking a declaration that Mr McAusland was not and had not at any time been properly retained by Antlers, an order that he cease purporting to act for Antlers and an order that the application (NG 117 of 1993) be struck out. Costs were claimed against him and also an order in the alternative that the proceedings be stayed. The motion came on for hearing before Whitlam J on 24 May 1993 and on 29 June 1993 his Honour made orders that the proceedings be dismissed and that Mr McAusland pay the costs of the respondents. The first of these three appeals by Mr McAusland is against those orders.

11. On 6 May 1993, the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy, as trustee of the bankrupt estate of Vera Mary Kavich, filed an application under s.461 of the Corporations Law naming Antlers as first respondent and George Kavich as second respondent. He sought, inter alia, a declaration that he was the owner of one share in Antlers and an order that he be entered as shareholder on the share register of the company. He also sought an order removing George Kavich as shareholder of Antlers to the extent that he claimed to be such or that the share register showed him to be such. There was an entry in the transfer journal purporting to show a transfer of her share from Vera Kavich to George Kavich on 9 May 1977. The bona fides of that entry were not unquestioned. That is, however, a matter on which there is not evidence on the record sufficient to allow a finding one way or the other. The Official Trustee also sought an order for the winding up of Antlers on the grounds of oppression against the shareholders, the just and equitable ground and on the basis that the directors had acted in the affairs of the company in their own interests rather than in the interests of the members as a whole. The matter came on before Whitlam J on 2 July 1993 for directions. However, following extensive argument, his Honour appointed James Morrison Millar as Receiver and Manager of Antlers for the purpose of ascertaining and preserving its assets until further order of the Court. His Honour gave no separate statement of reasons for the orders and counsel expressly declined his offer to provide reasons. Nevertheless, the basis upon which the order was made emerges broadly from the transcript of his Honour's exchanges with counsel. That is the second order appealed against pursuant to leave granted by Burchett J on 12 July 1993.

12. On 5 July 1993, Mr Frank Portelli and Mr George Kavich purported to hold a meeting of directors of Antlers at which they resolved to appoint Mr Frank Portelli and Mr Keith Dickson as directors. Mr Dickson executed a consent on the same day. Appearing before Burchett J on 12 July 1993 in proceedings NG3118 of 1993 and NG117 of 1993, Antlers, George Kavich and Vera Kavich sought an order rescinding the appointment of the receiver on the basis that there had been a significant change of circumstances. His Honour declined to make such an order on the basis that the application for rescission amounted to an appeal against the decision of Whitlam J. It directly raised the correctness of the receiver's appointment. Burchett J granted leave to appeal not only against the interlocutory order appointing a receiver but also against his own refusal to rescind that order. It is an appeal pursuant to that leave that is the third appeal in this group.

The Decisions Under Appeal

13. The first appeal raises the correctness of the decision of Whitlam J on 29 June 1993 in which he dismissed the action in NG117 of 1993 and ordered Mr McAusland to pay the costs. His Honour held, contrary to submissions made on behalf of the Commissioner of Taxation and the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy, that Mr Portelli did not cease to be a director upon the making of the winding up order on 21 August 1978. He relied upon Austral Brick Co. Pty Ltd v. Falgat Constructions Pty Ltd (1990) 21 NSWLR 389 for the proposition that on a winding up directors remain in office although their powers cease. That proposition was not disputed before the Court. Under Regulation 64 of the Articles, Mr Portelli continued in office as a director with no fixed term. It was argued before his Honour, however, that he had ceased to be a director of Antlers after 30 November 1987 by virtue of the order made by the Supreme Court on 23 November 1987. Failure to hold the meeting of members on 30 November 1987, as directed by the Supreme Court in its order of 23 November 1987, it was said, had the same effect as a failure to hold an annual general meeting for the re-election of directors. The directors vacate their office. His Honour was referred to In re Consolidated Nickel Mines Ltd (1914) 1 Ch 883 and Club Flotilla (Pacific Palms) Ltd v. Isherwood (1987) 12 ACLR 387. On this basis, his Honour concluded that Mr Portelli's office as a director was vacated after 30 November 1987 for failure to convene the meeting directed by the Supreme Court. There were therefore, in his view, no directors of Antlers in office who could authorise the institution of these proceedings. Nor, in his Honour's view, was it likely that a meeting of the members of the company could be held. There was therefore no point in adjourning or staying the proceedings. He considered that the appropriate order was that the proceedings be dismissed and that Mr McAusland, as solicitor on the record for the applicant, pay the respondents' costs.

14. In the application for the appointment of a receiver, his Honour was referred, inter alia, to a decision of a purported general meeting of Antlers held on 19 March 1993 at which it was resolved to increase the share capital of the company through the issue of 50,000 one cent cumulative redeemable preference shares at a premium of 99 cents each. The Notification of Resolution filed with the Australian Securities Commission was signed by Frank Portelli on the same day. The various shares were allotted on the same day, being 20,000 to George Kavich, 10,000 to Christine Gilan, 5,000 to Carmen Portelli, 10,000 to Paul Kavich and 5,000 to Carmel Law. On 18 April 1993, a further resolution was allegedly passed to increase the share capital of the company to $100,000 through the issue of 99,998 ordinary shares at $1 each. These were then allotted as follows:

Paul Kavich 15,000

Christine Gilan 15,000

Carmen Portelli 10,000

Carmel Law 10,000

Frank Portelli 49,998

It appears not to have been in dispute that these share issues were intended to ensure that the Official Receiver in Bankruptcy who claimed to hold Mrs Kavich's interest as a shareholder as part of her bankrupt estate would be unable to affect the operation of the company.

15. As appears from the transcript, his Honour had regard to the effect of orders freezing company funds and the issue and disposition of the company's shares. He directed his mind to the appointment of a receiver or provisional liquidator pending an appeal from the effect of his decision dismissing the earlier proceedings on the grounds that there were no directors of the company. He said:

"... it seems to me that if there is an appeal in

respect of what is said about their being directors of

Antlers, as long as everybody is protected by a

provisional liquidator or a receiver being there, it

is best to have that issue tidied up."

Counsel for Mr McAusland, Mrs Kavich and Mr Portelli said that if a receiver were simply to take possession of the assets with no other steps to be taken in the other proceedings pending a resolution of the appeal, he could not see any immediate detriment. Counsel opposed the appointment of a provisional liquidator because of the impact that would have on the company's reputation and its ability to trade. His Honour considered it sensible to appoint a receiver or a provisional liquidator. He noted that the parties would probably be content with a receiver, subject to an undertaking as to damages by the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy who was the applicant in that case.

"..it seems to me from what I know of the circumstances

that it is sensible to either appoint a provisional

liquidator or a receiver. I think from the

applicant's point of view they would be just as

content with a receiver and Mr Bevan would probably

accept that on my giving an undertaking."

His Honour indicated that on the affidavit evidence alone he would be entitled to appoint a receiver. That was apparently a reference to evidence of the alterations to the share capital and the allotment of the additional ordinary shares in the company. Counsel for Mrs Kavich, Mr Portelli and Mr McAusland then said he would not oppose the appointment but asked that it be on terms that the company or the members would have the opportunity to approach the court at short notice if they could rectify the defects in the appointment of the directors. The orders made by his Honour appointing the receiver and manager included liberty to relist the proceedings on 48 hours notice. Burchett J subsequently took the view that the application for rescission of the appointment of the receiver constituted an appeal against Whitlam J's order. He refused to rescind, but granted leave to appeal against his own decision to that effect.

The Standing of the Appellants

16. The appeal against the judgment of Whitlam J of 29 June 1993 is brought pursuant to leave granted by Burchett J on 12 July 1993. The order, however, was dispositive of the proceedings and, in the circumstances, leave was not necessary. The notice of appeal sets out some seven grounds which challenge his Honour's decision that there were no directors holding office in Antlers for the purpose of authorising the institution of the proceedings and the finding that the members of Antlers were unable to hold a meeting for that purpose. His Honour was said to have erred in determining that the failure of the directors to hold a meeting on or before 30 November 1987, pursuant to the order of the Supreme Court of New South Wales of 23 November 1987, had the effect of vacating the office of the directors as from 30 November 1987. In this connection, it was asserted that his Honour erred in the construction of sub-s.383(3) of the Companies (NSW) Code 1981 and in determining the true construction of Regulation 84 of Table A of the Companies Act 1961 (NSW) as it stood at the date of incorporation of the appellant. The notice of appeal seeks orders that the judgment given on 29 June 1993 be set aside and that the respondents' notice of motion be dismissed with costs.

17. The respondents take the preliminary point that the notice of appeal challenges the judgment but not the orders and that regardless of the conclusion reached by the Court as to the correctness of his Honour's reasonings, the order dismissing the proceedings should stand. It is apparent, however, that the orders sought in the notice of appeal involve the setting aside of the orders given by his Honour and their substitution with an order that the motion be dismissed. The second preliminary point taken is that although both McAusland and Antlers are named as appellants, the only appellant is McAusland. It is said that McAusland has no standing to challenge the order that the proceedings be dismissed. But that order stands upon the foundation of the asserted lack of authority in the directors to retain him and is necessarily the foundation of the costs order made against him which was for the costs of the action and not merely of the motion. Mr McAusland does have standing, in my opinion, to challenge the whole of the order made on 29 June 1993.

18. A question of the utility of the appeal was raised by the respondents on the basis that this Court has no general jurisdiction to set aside contracts on the grounds of unilateral mistake except, arguably, by cross-vesting which has not been invoked. The proper course, it is submitted, is to move to set aside the agreement in the proceedings compromised by it. Reference was made to Darling Downs Investments Pty Ltd v. Ellwood (1988) 18 FCR 510 where it was held by Pincus and Einfeld JJ (Fisher J dissenting) that this Court has power, under s.22 of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth), to make orders enforcing settlements in Federal Court proceedings. The position adopted by the majority was summarised at p 526 of the report:

1. Section 22 of the Federal Court of Australia Act must be construed

as having an effect analogous to s.24(7) of the English Judicature

Act.

2. Section 24(7) and its Australian counterparts have been held to

include, amongst other remedies, orders enforcing compromises of

suits.

3. Section 22 should also be read as including such orders within its

ambit.

While that authority finds support, under s.22, for orders enforcing compromises of proceedings pending in the Court, it does not directly address the question whether the section will support an order setting aside such a compromise. It is the case, however, that an order setting aside a compromise would reinstate the entitlement to claim the relief sought in the pending proceedings and on that basis fall within the power conferred by s.22. It follows that the jurisdiction which supports the power is the primary jurisdiction to entertain the compromised action. Otherwise, the contention being that the controversy before the Court was not in fact properly resolved, the matter appears to fall within its accrued jurisdiction. If that be right, then the question arises whether the setting aside of the contract must be done within the framework of the existing proceedings or can be done by a separate application. That is in essence a procedural matter. Provided the separate application is properly characterised as raising a claim within the framework of a matter pending in the Court, albeit dependent upon the exercise of accrued jurisdiction or the same primary jurisdiction, the question whether or not the separate application should be allowed to proceed as such is one of case management. If the proper course is to bring that application in the pending proceedings then no doubt the fresh application can be consolidated with the pending application.

Directions Upon Termination of a Winding Up

19. The substantive question in the case turns upon the operation of the order of the Supreme Court of New South Wales and the proper construction of sub-s.383(3) of the Companies (NSW) Code 1981. Section 383 provides:

"383(1) At any time during the winding up of a

company, the Court may, on the application of the

liquidator or of a creditor or contributory, make an

order staying the winding up either indefinitely or

for a limited time or terminating the winding up on a

date specified in the order.

383(2) On such an application, the Court may, before

making an order, direct the liquidator to furnish a

report with respect to a relevant fact or matter.

383(3) Where the Court has made an order terminating

the winding up, the Court may give such directions as it

thinks fit for the resumption of the management and

control of the company by its officers, including

directions for the convening of a general meeting of

members of the company to elect directors of the company

to take office upon the termination of the winding up.

383(4) The cost of proceedings before the Court under

this section and the costs incurred in convening a

meeting of members of the company in accordance with an

order of the Court under this section shall, if the

Court so directs, form part of the costs, charges and

expenses of the winding up.

383(5) Where an order is made under this section, the

company shall lodge an office copy of the order with the

Commission within 14 days after the making of the

order."

The section is reproduced in virtually identical language in s.482 of the Corporations Law.

20. It is contended for the respondents that a power to direct the election of persons "to take office" is predicated upon the premise that the persons so elected will thereupon comprise the Board of Directors. The necessary implication of sub-s.383(3) is said to be that, upon the convening of the directed meeting, the prior directors cease to hold office and, if they are to continue, must be re-elected. Alternatively, and on the assumption that Mr Portelli continued in office as a director of Antlers beyond 30 November 1987, the direction of the Supreme Court was said to displace the power he would otherwise have had under Regulation 84 of the Articles to appoint a second director to constitute a quorum. The appellants' submission, however, is that the Supreme Court's direction that a meeting be held on 30 November 1987 did not have the effect of terminating the office of any of the directors. Paragraph 3 of the Supreme Court's orders, being a direction that a meeting be held, was simply a procedural or mechanical direction to facilitate resumption of the management and control of the affairs of the company by its directors and did not affect the appointment of directors who would at all times have remained in office until the dissolution of the company by the liquidator. The language of sub-s.383(3) was said to make it clear that the directions which the Supreme Court is empowered to make are purely procedural or mechanical and do not affect the status of the directors as officers of the company, as distinct from affecting their control of the company's affairs.

21. The power to terminate the winding up of a company was not to be found expressed as such before the enactment of s.383 of the Companies Code in 1981. An analogous power to make an order after a winding up order had been made staying all proceedings in relation to the winding up was to be found in s.243 of the Companies Act 1961 (NSW). This followed the language of s.256 of the Companies Act 1948 (UK) which in turn was based on s.89 of the Companies Act 1862 (UK). That provision conferred a power to be exercised on principles similar to those governing the power to annul a bankruptcy by reference to the interests of creditors and considerations of commercial morality - In re Telescriptor Syndicate Ltd (1903) 2 Ch 174 at 181 (Buckley J); Re Calgary and Edmonton Land Co. Ltd (1975) 1 All ER 1046 at 1050-1051 (Megarry J). Under s.243 and its predecessors, a stay order could be made for the purposes of the reconstruction of a company after a winding up order had been made - Re Stephen Walters and Sons Ltd (1926) 70 Sol Jo 953. In the latter case, which is only briefly reported, no direction was made by Romer J for reinstatment or election of directors.

22. In the fourteenth edition of Buckley on the Companies Acts (1981) at p 619, it is said in relation to s.256:

"The section may be used for reconstruction purposes,

e.g. where a company wants to raise further capital to

pay its debts and resume business. An order can be

made under this section staying all proceedings in the

winding-up, except for the necessary purposes (e.g.

ascertaining and satisfying the debts), and directing

meetings of the members which can vote the new

capital, elect a new board of directors, and so on."

There was no express power to be found in the Companies Act 1961 (NSW) or its statutory ancestors authorising a court to give directions, in the context of a stay order, for the election of directors. It was said not to be the practice of the Supreme Court of New South Wales to do so - Re Country Traders Distributors Ltd and the Companies Act (1974) 2 NSWLR 135 at 139 (Mahoney J). That is not to say, however, that such a power is not an incident of the power to order a stay. It seems to have been contemplated by the author of the fourteenth edition of Buckley on the Companies Acts (supra). The nature of an indefinite stay of a winding up order is, in effect, to place the control of the company back in the hands of its shareholders. It brings the winding up process to an end, permitting the company to carry on business in accordance with its memorandum and articles of association. This is so despite the fact that when such an order is made, the winding up order remains unrevoked - Krextile Holdings Pty Ltd v. Widdows [1974] VicRp 83; (1974) VR 689 at pp 693-694 (Gillard J).

23. In Austral Brick Co. Pty Ltd v. Falgat Constructions Pty Ltd (1990) 21 NSWLR 389, Young J briefly considered the difference between a permanent stay and a termination order under s.383, which authorises both types of order. His Honour concluded at p 393:

"It seems to me that the word "termination" is only

included in the 1981 Act to make it a little more

intelligible to the ordinary person that when the

court makes an order for termination, the whole effect

of the winding-up order ceases."

It may well be the case that the termination of a winding up order has, in most cases, little in the way of practical operation that differs significantly from a permanent stay. The same general considerations of the interests of relevant parties and commercial morality as have been held to apply to the making of a stay order also apply where a termination order is sought - Re Skay Fashions Pty Ltd (in liq) (1986) 10 ACLR 743 at 746 (Tadgell J). But the concept conveyed by the language of termination differs from that conveyed by the notion of a permanent stay. The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary defines the transitive verb "stay" in the relevant sense as "to stop, arrest, delay, prevent (an action or process, something which is begun or intended)." The verb "terminate" means, inter alia, "to bring to an end, put an end to, cause to cease; to end (an action, condition, etc)". In the case of a permanent stay, the winding up order, as Gillard J observed, remains unrevoked. A termination order prospectively extinguishes the winding up order. The distinction is a semantic one which may have no practical significance. Whether it does or not need not be decided here. It does not impinge on the question whether the former directors of the company are restored to their former powers. That depends upon whether the winding up order vacates the offices of the directors or merely suspends their powers.

24. The question whether a winding up order vacates the offices of the directors or suspends their powers until the extinction of their offices by dissolution of the company has been the subject of conflicting authorities and opinions. Support for the dismissal theory comes from Measures Bros. Ltd v. Measures (1910) 2 Ch 248; McPherson, The Law of Company Liquidation 3rd Edition (1987) pp 171-172; Gower, Principles of Modern Company Law 4th Edition (1979) at 727; Palmer's Company Law 23rd Edition (1982) at 1138 and in the South African decision of Attorney-General v. Blumenthal (1961) (4) SA 313 (TPD). In Re Country Traders Distributors Ltd (supra) at 139 Mahoney J was inclined to accept that a winding up order suspended directors' powers although he did not express a concluded view on the question. As his Honour observed, the power conferred by s.243 is to stay a winding up order rather than to order an annulment. In such a stay, control would be expected to revest in the "pre-winding up" directors, a consideration supportive of the suspension theory. The residual power that directors have to bring an appeal in the name of the company against the winding up order is a further indication in that direction - Re Rick Wilson and Co. Ltd (1982) 1 ACLC 566 at 569 (McLelland J); Arafura Finance Corporation Pty Ltd v. Kooba Pty Ltd (No. 2) (1987) 12 ACLR 331 at 332 (Muirhead AJ), cf Anfrank Nominees Pty Ltd v. Connell (1989) 1 ACSR 365 at 383 (Kennedy J).

25. The conflicting authorities and opinions were reviewed by Young J in Austral Brick Co. Ltd (supra). His Honour preferred the suspension of powers approach. In a more recent review of the cases in Lord Corporation Pty Ltd v. Green (1991) 22 NSWLR 532, Cohen J found it unnecessary to come to a concluded view. His Honour's judgment, as that of Young J, seems, however, to demonstrate a predominance of authority in support of the suspension of powers approach.

26. The conclusion that directors' powers are merely suspended by a winding up until dissolution or termination or stay of the winding up is supported by the general statutory scheme. Upon the appointment of a liquidator the property of the company vests in the liquidator (Companies Code s.374) who is given wide powers, including the power to carry on the business of the company (Companies Code s.377). The liquidator is to realise the property of the company, distribute dividends (if any) to the creditors and adjust the rights of contributories (Companies Code s.381). Thereafter, the court may release the liquidator and order that the company be dissolved (Companies Code s.382). There is nothing in the statutory scheme prior to the dissolution of the company that requires more than the cessation of the powers of the directors until dissolution. The fact that it leaves open the possibility that a permanent stay or termination of the winding up may be ordered militates against the unnecessary inconvenience of automatic vacation of the directors' offices upon the making of the order.

27. The suspension of the directors' powers does not cause time to stop running so far as the continuance of their terms is concerned. If, while a winding up order is in effect, a director's term of office comes to its end by force of the Articles of the company, there is nothing about the winding up process or the relevant statutory provisions which would prevent that event. So it may happen that prior to a termination order, all offices may have been vacated by effluxion of time. But as Young J observed in Austral Brick (supra) at 393:

"This last problem is covered by s. 383(3) of the Code

in the case of an order terminating a winding up..."

28. The further question arises as to what is the operation of s.383(3). On its face, the sub-section is purely facultative. It confers the power to give directions for the resumption of the management and control of the company by its officers in the case of a termination order, although curiously not in the case of a permanent stay. The Explanatory Memorandum is of virtually no assistance. It states that, so far as it relates to stay orders, s.383 is based on s.243 of the uniform Companies Acts. The power to give directions is said to be a new provision comparable to s.352(2) relating to the termination of an official management. In my opinion, the power conferred on the Court by s.383(3) is no less than the power which the Court necessarily has incidental to its power to order a permanent stay of the winding up order. The express conferral of the power in the case of a termination order was perhaps done out of an abundance of caution.

29. Against this background, the question to be asked is whether, when a direction is given for the convening of a general meeting of members of the company to elect directors, it has the effect of vacating the office of any directors whose office would otherwise continue. There may be a variety of circumstances in which directions for the resumption of the management and control of the company might be given. Where there are no directors still in office at the time of the termination order, it will, no doubt, be desirable to ensure that a meeting takes place so that persons are elected to take responsibility for the management of the company's office from the moment that the termination order comes into effect. It may be that there are directors still in office, albeit their powers are suspended, in circumstances in which the Court considers that there should, in any event, be a spill of all Board positions. The power conferred by s.383(3) is a wide auxiliary power. Directions "for the resumption of the management and control of the company by its officers" could arguably extend to a direction vacating the office of an existing director and requiring a meeting to be convened to elect directors to take office on the termination of the winding up. But such an order may not be necessary. It would be a dramatic and important direction having a more powerful effect in this respect than the winding up order itself. It is not to be found by mere implication from a direction to convene a meeting.

30. A direction to convene a meeting of members to elect directors of the company was made in the present case. But there is nothing in the material before the Court, and apparently nothing on the record of the Supreme Court proceedings, to indicate the basis upon which the order was sought or given. It might have been based upon an assumption that the winding up order had vacated the offices of the directors.

31. The direction might have been intended to facilitate the management and control of the company by providing a mechanism for the election of directors. That is not to say that the existence of a continuing director was contemplated. There is no basis upon which it is to be implied that the direction was intended to vacate the office of any existing director. Nor is it any easier to find grounds for reading into the direction an implied exclusion of the mechanism whereby a continuing director may appoint an additional director for the purpose of completing a quorum pursuant to the Articles. The consequences of non-compliance with the order in the event that there were no directors would have been clear. There would have been no person validly appointed to control the affairs of the company. It does not follow that, where there is an existing director, non-compliance with the order had or would have been intended to have any effect upon the director's capacity to proceed, under the Articles and after the termination took effect, to constitute a Board.

32. Mr Portelli did not cease to be a director on 30 November 1987. He continued as a director thereafter and was able to act pursuant to Regulation 84 to create a quorum by appointing Mrs Vera Kavich as a director. It follows, in my respectful opinion, that his Honour was in error in taking the view that he did that Mr Portelli ceased to be a director of the company on 30 November 1987 and that there were no directors thereafter. For those reasons, in my opinion, the appeal against his Honour's decision on the motion challenging the retainer of Mr McAusland should be allowed. The basis upon which his Honour found that Mr McAusland had no retainer was the foundation of his order dismissing the proceedings in their entirety. For he held that there were "no directors of Antlers who could authorise the institution of the proceedings".

The Second and Third Appeals

33. The remaining appeals can be shortly dealt with. It is not at all clear that the decision by his Honour to appoint a receiver was critically dependent on the finding that there were no validly appointed directors of the company. His Honour expressed concern in the course of the argument before him about other aspects of the control and management of the company. The decision on the first appeal may be a factual circumstance in support of a motion to rescind or terminate the appointment of the receiver, but it is not exhaustive of the issues which affect that appointment. The appeal against the appointment of the receiver and the appeal against the refusal by Burchett J to rescind that appointment should be dismissed.
