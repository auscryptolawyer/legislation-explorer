Johnson and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 200 (18 December 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007

Superannuation - refusal to pay benefits to spouse and step-daughter of deceased pensioner - application for review - whether applicant "spouse" of deceased -whether step-daughter an "eligible child" - meaning of "wholly or substantially dependent" - whether applicant and daughter dependent upon deceased -exercise of discretion

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ss 3, 9, 110

Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth) ss 47, 51

LILY IVY JOHNSON

THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No.79/10052

AAT Decision No 334

Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member)

Mr R.B. Hutchison (Member)

Mr P.C. Wickens (Member)

18 December 1979

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION'

No.: 79/10052

Re: LILY IVY JOHNSON

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION The Tribunal sets aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation and in substitution for that decision decides that the applicant Lily Ivy Johnson and her daughter Frances Theresa McDonald (known as Johnson) were wholly or substantially dependent upon the late John James Arthur Johnson at the time of his death. The applicant falls, therefore, within paragraph (b) of the definition of "spouse" and her daughter falls within the definition of "eligible child" in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DATED this 18th day of December 1979

Sgd. Allan N. Hall

(Allan N. Hall)

Senior Member

Sgd. R.B. Hutchison

(R.B. Hutchison)

Member

sgd..P.C. Wickens

(P.C. Wickens)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. 79/10052 GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: LILY IVY JOHNSON

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

This is an application by Mrs Lily Ivy Johnson for a review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made on the 6th August, 1979 confirming an earlier decision on 2nd January, 1979 by the Commissioner's delegate, the effect of which was to deny Mrs Johnson payment of benefits under the Superannuation Act 1976 either for herself or her youngest daughter, Frances Theresa McDonald (known as Johnson), consequent upon the death of her husband John James Arthur Johnson on 9 June 1978. The Commissioner confirmed the decision made by his delegate that Mrs Johnson is not a "spouse" and her daughter Frances is not an "eligible child" within the meaning of section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

So far as relevant to this application, "spouse" and "eligible child" are defined in that section as follows:-

"Spouse" in relation to a person who has died and was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee or a retirement pensioner, means -

(a)a person who was legally married to the deceased person at the time of the person's death and who, at that time, was living with the person on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis; (and)
(b)a person who was legally married to the deceased person at the time of the person's death but who was not living with the person on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at that time, and who in the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased person at that time.

(c)....

(d) ..."

"Eligible child", in relation to a person who has died and was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee or a * retirement pensioner, means a person who -

(a)is a child of the deceased person other than (where the deceased person was a retirement pensioner at the time of his death) a child who, by virtue of section 9, is deemed not to be an eligible child for the purposes of this Act;
(b)is a person the 16th anniversary of whose birth has not occurred or -

(i) the 16th anniversary of whose birth has occurred but the 25th anniversary of whose birth has not occurred;

(ii) is receiving full-time education at a school, college or university; and

(iii) is not ordinarily in employment or engaged in work on his own account; and

(c) (i) was (except where the person is a child of a spouse of the deceased person, but not of the deceased person) living with the deceased person; (or)

(ii) was, in the opinion of the Commissioner, wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased person; or

(iii) "

The applicant and the deceased were married on 10th March, 1967 following the dissolution in the previous year of the applicant's first marriage to Maurice McDonald. There were nine children of the applicant's first marriage of whom only the youngest, Frances, is claimed to be an eligible child for the purposes of the Act. There were no children by the applicant's second marriage. The applicant informed us that due to her husband's excessive drinking habits and his refusal to seek any help for the problem she had found it impossible to continue to live with him. After two trial periods of separation - one in 1974 and one in 1975 - she left him finally on the 15th September, 1975. It was agreed that the house which stood in their joint names would be sold; the proceeds of sale divided; and the furniture shared between them. In the final distribution the applicant told us that she received $1,000 more than her husband because she at that stage had the care of her two youngest children. Her husband kept the car.

At the time of the separation the applicant said that her husband had agreed to send her money every week or every fortnight when he got paid. This was an oral agreement and nothing was reduced to writing. After the separation and after she had received her share of the proceeds of sale of the house her husband made spasmodic payments over a period of approximately four months. When asked how regular were the payments Mrs Johnson replied:"they were not regular, they were once every three weeks or once a month". As to the amounts involved she said:"about $50 and sometimes $30. It was not much". Asked whether she had replied to her husband's letters, Mrs Johnson replied: "I just wrote and said that I was not going to come back because I had - you know, the marriage before was not happy and my daughter needed me up there and being sick and I really had enough problems without him making my life a misery and drinking all the time."

Some three weeks after the applicant left her husband she had received a telegram from her son-in-law in Brisbane saying that her eldest daughter was in hospital with cancer. Mrs Johnson flew to Brisbane immediately but as her daughter was not to be operated on until the end of November she returned to Sydney. She finally moved to Brisbane in December 1975. During the time when she was in Sydney she said that she had received a number of threatening telephone calls and letters from her husband whilst he was in a drunken state, which was very upsetting to her. When she had gone to Queensland there was no telephone but she still received threatening letters. Her last personal contact with her husband was just before she went to live in Queensland on a permanent basis, (i.e. in December 1975).

Mrs Johnson said that at about the end of February or the beginning of March 1976 she had written to her husband telling him that she had not been able to obtain a job; that she was living off the proceeds of the sale of the house; and that she needed him to support her. She said that she had written a couple of times. However she received no reply to these letters. Mrs Johnson said she was able to obtain a part-time job working as a cleaner in a hospital in Brisbane but after 4 months she had to discontinue that work on doctor's orders and was then obliged to seek welfare payments from the Department of Social Security. Initially she was placed on unemployment benefits but as there was no suitable work available for her she was offered the alternative of seeking a Widow's Pension or a Supporting Mother's Benefit. She was told that before she could obtain the Widow's Pension (which carried slightly greater benefits than the Supporting Mother's Benefit) she would need to take out a maintenance order against her husband. Mrs Johnson informed us that she had said "I do not want to do that because when he was sending money and letters he always kept saying that this would be the last if you do not come back". She accordingly opted for the Supporting Mother's Benefit and continued to receive that benefit until her husband's death, since when she has been in receipt of a Widow's Pension.

At no stage since the applicant left her husband and in particular at no stage since he ceased to make spasmodic payments to her has the applicant sought to obtain a maintenance order against her husband. The applicant did suggest at the conclusion of the hearing that just prior to her husband's death she had been intending to take out a maintenance order because she knew she could not manage on the pension. However, she had not in fact consulted a solicitor or taken any other positive steps in this direction. The applicant had, in our view, made it very clear by her actions that the marriage had broken down so far as she was concerned due to her husband's drinking. The extent to which their lives had drifted apart is borne out we think by the fact that the first news she received of her husband's death was through her daughter Maureen who told the applicant that "I have got a letter saying that John Johnson is dead. You have to see a solicitor".

It appears from the documents lodged with the Tribunal that the deceased, who had been employed as a Clerical Assistant, Grade 3, in the Department of Defence, was retired from the Public Service on the grounds of invalidity on 29 March 1978. Mr Lorenz, for the Commissioner, informed us that in an application for invalidity retirement pension which the deceased had signed on 17 April, 1978 he had shown his marital state as "separated". In the space provided to nominate the person who should receive spouse's benefit if eligibility should arise, he had written "NIL". The deceased had been in receipt of invalidity benefit for only a short time prior to his death on 19 June 1978.

In explanation of her failure to pursue a claim for maintenance the applicant said (inter alia) in a letter to the Commissioner dated 8 February 1979:-

"As I have now enrolled my daughter in Sydney Tech. College, I find it very hard to manage on my low income. My husband knew that I would not take any legal action against him in regard to maintenance as I had a lot of trouble in this area with my former husband, who, after bringing myself and family out from the U.K. in 1959 deserted us all to return there by himself. Then after an absence of four years and sending no support money all the time he was away, arrived back in Australia unexpectedly, and finding he wasn't welcome, attempted to take our lives for which he received nine months in Boggo Road Gaol, Brisbane 1966. My divorce followed, and my subsequent marriage to Mr Johnson. I hope you can understand my reluctance for not wanting to take legal action against Mr Johnson. I did not want to put myself and my family through another ordeal."'

In a written statement which she presented at the hearing (Exhibit A), she added to this explanation in the following paragraphs:-

"Ever since I left him, I had received threatening phone calls and letters, executed whilst he was in a drunken state, which were very disturbing to me at this time. It was for this reason that I declined to take out a maintenance order on him thinking that this would at least give me some peace and quiet. Also, I did not wish to embarrass him in his job by chasing money as I knew his job was important to him. I also thought that perhaps later he might redeem himself enough to effect another reconciliation.

Let me also point out to you that my decision to leave my husband was a hard one to make due to my utter dependance on him as well as my reluctance to see the marriage fail. I knew that if I left I would have to burden myself and my two teenage daughters onto my married children, something which I did not want to do but which was necessary due to the high cost of living .... By doing so (ie. by living on welfare benefits) I am now informed that I was not dependant on my husband, which seems grossly unfair given my reasons for not seeking a continuous system of maintenance. One feels that this is a "blame the victim" philosophy - where else could I turn?

I tried hard to make a go of my marriage but because of my husband's drinking I have lost my home and health and any financial standing that might offer any hope of a better life for myself and my daughter. Because of this I feel that the Superannuation Board has made not only an unfair decision but a wrong decision, based on refutable evidence, with regards to myself and my daughter, who being a minor, is in no way responsible for her parent's actions."

We are satisfied that the applicant, although legally married to the deceased at the time of his death, was not living with him on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at that time. So far as her daughter Frances is concerned we are satisfied that, in accordance with the definition of "eligible child", she is a child of the deceased person within the meaning of sub-paragraph (a) of that definition ("child" is defined to mean (inter alia) a stepchild of a deceased person); and that she is a person between the ages of 16 and 25 who is receiving full time education at a college and is not ordinarily in employment or engaged in work on her own account within the meaning of sub-paragraph (b) of that definition. We are further satisfied that Frances was not living with the deceased at the time of his death. In each case therefore, the only question is whether in our opinion they or either of them were wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death. We note in this regard that both the applicant and Frances would have been entitled to bring proceedings for maintenance against the deceased during his lifetime under the Maintenance Act 1964 (N.S.W.) (see, in particular, so far as Frances is concerned sections 12, 27A and the definition of "child of the family").

Were the Applicant and Frances dependent on the Deceased?

The Commissioner contended that before a finding of dependency can be made there must be evidence of some financial or material support flowing from the deceased person to the applicant at the time of his death. The mere fact that there may have been some legal entitlement on the part of the applicant to bring proceedings for maintenance against her husband on behalf of herself and her daughter was not in itself a relevant consideration so far as the Commissioner was concerned. Mr Lorenz, who appeared on behalf of the Commissioner, said that he was not aware of any case which had arisen for consideration where a wife had obtained a maintenance order against her husband but had been unsuccessful in enforcing it. He thought that in such circumstances the Commissioner might be justified in exercising the discretion conferred upon him in relation to the question of dependency. But where no proceedings had been instituted, the position was otherwise.

Mr Lorenz informed us that, if the Superannuation Act 1922 had still been in force, the applicant as the widow of the deceased pensioner would have been entitled to payment of a pension regardless of the fact that she and her husband were separated and that her husband was making no payments of maintenance for her or her daughter. Under the 1922 Act, the fact that the widow of a deceased pensioner may have lived separate and apart from her husband for a substantial period or that she may have established a defacto relationship outside the marriage were not relevant considerations. A defacto wife, on the other hand, had no entitlements under the 1922 Act.

One of the significant changes effected by the 1976 Act was the recognition given by that Act to the claims of a defacto spouse (whether a husband or wife) following the death of a deceased eligible employee or retirement pensioner. So far as the legal spouse of a deceased employee or pensioner was concerned, if the spouse and the deceased employee or pensioner were not living together on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of the pensioner's death, the test of dependency had to be met. Indeed the 1976 Act envisaged the possibility that there may be two "spouses" for the purpose of the Act -firstly, a legal wife wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased person, and, secondly, a defacto wife who for a continuous period of not less than three years immediately preceding the person's death had ordinarily lived with that person as husband or wife on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis. Section 110 of the Act provided the criteria for apportionment of the benefit payable in such circumstances.

Mr Lorenz submitted that the changes effected by the 1976 Act were quite deliberately intended as a matter of policy to exclude a claim by a legal wife who at the time of a person's death was not living with him on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis unless that person at the time of his death was providing some financial or material support to his spouse. Notwithstanding that neither party to the marriage had established any defacto relationship with any third party, the consequence necessarily followed, in Mr Lorenz' submission, that the legal wife was left without any benefits under the Act unless she had pursued and enforced any entitlement to maintenance which by law may have accrued in her favour.

The question whether a wife or child is dependent upon a husband or father, as the case may be, is, in our view, a question of fact: see New Monckton Collieries Ltd v Keeling (1911) AC 648. In that case, a wife who for more than 20 years had not lived with or been supported by her husband and had in no sense relied upon him for financial help endeavoured unsuccessfully to establish that she was his "dependant" for the purposes of a claim under the Workmen's Compensation Act 1906. In the course of his decision, Lord Atkinson made the following pertinent observations at p. 653:-

"It by no means follows, however, that though there is no presumption of law that a wife is dependent upon her husband's earnings merely because of his legal obligation to maintain her, this legal obligation is to be ignored in deciding on the fact of her dependency. On the contrary, the existence of the obligation, the probability that it will be discharged, either voluntarily or under compulsion, the probability that the wife will ever enforce her right if the obligation be not discharged voluntarily, are all matters proper to be considered by the arbitrator in determining the question of fact whether, or not, the wife, at the time of her husband's injury, looked to his earnings for her maintenance and support in whole or in part. It is one of the many elements to be taken into account."

In the same case, Lord Robson said (at p.662-3), referring to the role of an arbitrator under the Workmen's Compensation Act:-

"All he has to do is to find whether or not there is, in fact, dependence on the husband in whole or in part at the time of his death. That single question of fact is all that concerns him, though the circumstances to be considered in arriving at the answer may be of infinite variety. The wife does not necessarily cease to be dependent on the husband simply because the latter refuses to recognise or perform his obligation and succeeds in throwing the burden of her maintenance for the time being on the wife's parents or friends, or on the State. They may fulfil the husband's duty for him, but the wife's.legal dependence is still on him and not on them, and his death deprives her of the proper stay and support on which alone she is entitled to rely. The circumstances, however, are wholly different where the wife herself has for years clearly asserted and definitely maintained her complete independence of her husband. That is what the applicant did in this case, but her doing so seems to have counted for nothing in deciding either the existence or the degree of her dependency."

In the following year, Reeling's Case was explained by Fletcher Moulton LJ in Lee v Owner of Ship Bessie (1912) 1 KB 83 at p 88 as -follows: -

"In my opinion the effect of this decision is that legal obligations to support must not be taken at their theoretic value, but at their practical value. For instance, the mere fact that a husband is bound to support his wife does not establish that she is totally or at all dependent upon him within the meaning of the Act if the circumstances are such that there is no reasonable probability that her rights would have been practically and effectually asserted. But if on the evidence there is any fair probability that the legal rights would at any future time have been actually and effectually asserted by the- wife, then there is evidence of dependency, and the compensation must be regulated by an estimate of her practical loss subject to the provisions of the Act."

The question of dependency was further explored in Potts v Niddrie and Benhar Coal Company Ltd. (1913) AC 531. In that case, a workman had deserted his wife and children in March 1907. During the next two years he made occasional voluntary payments of small amounts for the support of his wife and two younger children. In September 1909, the wife obtained a maintenance order for the support of the two younger children and recovered an amount from her husband's employers by "arrestment". The workman then disappeared and was not traced until his death in 1911. From the date of desertion, the wife and family were supported entirely, except as stated above, from the earnings of the two elder children. The House of Lords held that there was sufficient evidence to justify a finding that the two younger children were wholly dependent on the earnings of their father.

Referring to Reeling's Case (supra), Viscount Haldane LC agreed that a mere legal right to maintenance may not, in certain circumstances, be sufficient to establish dependency. At p 537 his Lordship said:-

"The true question in the present case is, in my opinion, whether there was, as one of the facts to be taken into account, an effective and valuable legal right. If there was such a right, and there was no legal difficulty in the way of enforcing it, then the mere fact that a want of opportunity to resort to it, which might have proved only temporary, had reduced the mother and children for the time to living on charity, cannot affect the conclusion that by the father's death they lost something on which they could depend."

In the same case, Lord Shaw cited with approval the following passage from the judgment of Lord Moncrief in Sneddon v Addie & Sons Collieries Ltd. (1904) 6 F. 992 at p.996:-

"There may be cases in which a husband's legal obligation to support his wife may be held to be sopited or suspended. But when that legal obligation, not discharged by the husband, concurs with total destitution on the part of the wife and inability to support herself, the bare fact that at the date of his death the husband was not implementing his obligation is not sufficient to prevent us from holding that the wife was wholly dependent on him. Neither, in my opinion, is the question affected by the fact that during the husband's absence and neglect the wife was kept from starvation by the casual charity of strangers, or even of relatives who might have been bound to support her if the husband had not been alive."

And at p 541, Lord Shaw summed up the problem as follows:-

"With regard to the facts of a case of desertion, or refusal or omission to support dependants, these facts may be within a wide range. On the one hand, there may be a temporary absence of a husband or a father, with the expectancy of immediate aid on the part of those left behind. That is the one extreme. On the other hand, there may be a long absence entirely acquiesced in, and those left behind may live a separate and completely independent life, having no reliance whatsoever either upon support actually obtained or possible through the agency of law. Between those two extremes there are many gradations, leaving room for the arbitrator to pronounce upon dependency - whether it is total or partial, or whether it exists. In the present case there was a resort to the law and a legal affirmation through the decree of dependency. Furthermore, there was evidence of a determination to vindicate the children's right to support. It was, as it were, proved that the wife was determined with regard to her children to shew her and their dependence upon him. There were circumstances of occasional support prior to the decree, and by reason of it."

Finally, in Jeffrey v Commissioner for Government Transport (1957) SR NSW 634 the question arose whether a child was at the date of her father's death dependent upon the father. The facts were that the parents had separated in 1949, the child going to live with her mother. Having paid maintenance and provided other support for the child for a number of years the father suspended payments for the child in February 1955. He died in June 1955. In the period immediately preceding the father's death the child had in fact been maintained by her mother. In the view of the trial judge, the question of dependency was to be determined by ascertaining who was maintaining the child at the time of her father's death. The trial judge directed that, as the child was in fact being maintained by the mother, the child was dependent on the mother for its support and not on its father. Relying on the House of Lords decisions to which we have referred, however, the Full Court held that the trial judge had misdirected himself by deciding the question of dependency in this way. As was said by Street CJ at p 637:-

"The law is clear that the question is one of fact, as the section so states expressly. Past happenings and future probabilities are all involved, and there was in the present case, it seems to me, ample, if not overwhelming, evidence to go to the jury of a dependence in fact of the plaintiff upon the deceased officer."

See also Kauri Timber Co (Tas) Pty Ltd v Reeman [1973] HCA 8; (1973) 47 ALJR 184. Whilst we would not place undue reliance upon the point there is, we think, some assistance to be drawn from a consideration of the 1922 Act as to the meaning of the expression "wholly or substantially dependent". Section 47(2) of the 1922 Act provided (in part) that:-

"(2) Pension shall not be paid to the widower of a female pensioner ... unless, in the opinion of the Board, he was wholly or substantially dependent upon her immediately before her death ....."

Section 51(1A) then provided that:-"(1A) Where -

(a) the husband of a female pensioner

is wholly or substantially dependent upon her; and

(b) his wife leaves him without means of support, the husband may, from time to time, apply to a court of summary jurisdiction constituted by a Police, Stipendiary or Special Magistrate, and, on proof that the husband is wholly or substantially dependent on his wife and that his wife has left him without means of support, the court may order the payment, during such period as it thinks desirable, of pension in accordance with section forty-seven of this Act as if the pensioner were dead."

It seems obvious that under the 1922 Act, the question whether a husband was "dependent" upon his pensioner wife did not fall to be resolved by considering only the evidence as to payments by way of financial support in fact being made. We see no reason why the same expression used in the 1976 Act should be given any different meaning.

Of course, in the cases under the workmen's compensation legislation to which we have referred, the question under consideration was whether a person was "dependent wholly or in part upon the earnings of the worker at the time of his death". Under the Superannuation Act 1976 we are concerned with the question whether a spouse was, in the opinion of the Commissioner or, on review, of this Tribunal, "wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased person" at the time of his death. The differences in language point in our view to a greater breadth of discretion under the Superannuation Act 1976 in determining, consistently with the scope and object of that legislation (see Shrimpton v Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 CLR 613 at 620 per Latham C.J.) whether, in all the circumstances, dependency can fairly be said to be established.

Whilst the receipt of maintenance or of other material support may he the best evidence of dependency it is not, in our view, the only evidence to which regard should he had. On the other hand, the mere existence of an entitlement to claim support does not necessarily establish dependency see Keeling's Case (supra). Neither does a husband's refusal to acknowledge or satisfy a claim for support legitimately made upon him by his wife and children preclude a finding of dependency: Potts' Case (supra).

The Tribunal's Findings

We do not doubt the applicant's evidence that her husband was an excessive drinker. We believe that there is corroboration for her evidence in a letter dated 3 March 1976 from her late husband's sister to the deceased which was amongst his possessions at the time of his death (Exhibit B). In that letter there is a reference to the deceased's acknowledgement that he had a drinking problem. However, the most compelling evidence is the copy of the death certificate which was before us as part of Exhibit T4 which disclosed the cause of death as (inter alia) "cirrhosis of the liver - years".

That the deceased accepted some responsibility for his wife and step-daughter is, we think, confirmed by the evidence of spasmodic payments sent to the applicant by her husband following their separation and division of assets. The absence of any payments of maintenance since approximately January, 1976, and the absence of any positive steps on the part of the applicant to enforce her entitlement to maintenance since that date, is we think, adequately explained by the applicant's evidence as to her unfortunate experience in relation to her first husband, by her desire not to cause any embarrassment to her husband in his employment, and, most importantly, by her ability to secure a supporting mother benefit from the Commonwealth, without the necessity to pursue a claim for maintenance, which provided her with temporary sustenance.

However, the supporting mother benefit would only have been payable to the applicant until her youngest daughter Frances completed her education (see Part IV AAA of the Social Services Act 1947). Thereafter, as a deserted wife without dependent children, the applicant would not have been entitled to a widow's pension unless she had taken "such action as the Director-General considers reasonable to obtain maintenance from her husband ..." (see section 62 (3) of the Act). Having regard to the age of the applicant and to the difficulties which she had previously experienced in obtaining and keeping suitable employment, we have concluded (to adopt the words of Fletcher Moulton LJ in Lee's Case (supra)) that there was a fair probability that the applicant's legal rights to maintenance would have been "actually and effectually asserted". It is not without significance, in our view, that neither party had instituted any proceedings for divorce. The separation from her husband had not been so long nor were the circumstances in which the applicant had been living since separation such as to demonstrate that either she or any other person had accepted the responsibility for the support of herself and her daughter. As we see the evidence, the applicant is a deserted wife who quite properly looked to her husband for support for herself and Frances following their separation; who received some support for a short time; who was then denied that support; and who, because of the availability for the time being of a supporting mother benefit, had been able to defer what she quite understandably regarded as the unpalatable prospect of seeking to recover maintenance from her husband.

We can see nothing in the changes effected by the Superannuation Act 1976 to support the conclusion that a legal wife constructively deserted in circumstances such as the present should not be said to be "dependent" upon her husband. In the exercise of the discretion which Parliament has conferred upon the Commissioner and, on review, upon this Tribunal, we think that this is a proper case in which to form the opinion that the applicant and her daughter Frances were wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased, notwithstanding that at the time of his death the deceased had refused to acknowledge that dependence and that, as a consequence, the responsibility for the support of the applicant and Frances had fallen upon the Commonwealth.

We therefore set aside the decision of the Commissioner and in substitution for that decision we decide that the applicant and her daughter Frances were wholly or substantially dependent upon the late John James Arthur Johnson at the time of his death. The applicant, therefore, falls within paragraph (b) of the definition of "spouse" and her daughter Frances falls within the definition of "eligible child" in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.
