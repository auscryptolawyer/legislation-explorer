The decision under review is set aside and the matter is remitted to the Respondent with a direction that Ms Yan qualifies for registration as a tax agent.

CATCHWORDS

TAX AGENT – application for registration refused – engaged in relevant employment – substantial involvement in income tax matters – qualities of a tax agent – decision set aside and remitted to Respondent with a direction that the Applicant qualifies for registration as a tax agent.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – ss. 251JA, 251BC

Income Tax Regulations 1936 – reg. 156



Mula v Tax Agents’ Board of NSW (1996) 97 ATC 2001

Civiti v Tax Agents’ Board of VIC (1990) 90 ATC 2039

Su v Tax Agents’ Board of SA (1982) 61 FLR 1



REASONS FOR DECISION



5 December 2005
Miss N Isenberg, Member


DECISION UNDER REVIEW

  1. This is an application by Ms Yan for review of a decision of the Tax Agents’ Board of New South Wales (“the Board”) dated 7 July 2005 which refused Ms Yan’s application for registration as a tax agent in New South Wales.

BACKGROUND

  1. Ms Yan applied for registration as a tax agent and submitted to the Board an application for original registration as a tax agent, dated 28 March 2005 and filed 31 March 2005 (T5).
  2. The application identified one employer for whom Ms Yan had worked in fulfilling the requirement that she demonstrate “relevant employment”. Ms Yan provided a number of attachments including a Statement of Relevant Employment (“SORE”) (T17). The SORE was not signed by her employer and the application was notated in the following terms at Question 18 (at T5/17):

But unfortunately my employer, Mr. Peter Tsang didn’t sign statement of relevant employment for me. The reason he said to me “I’m sorry. I can’t sign this for you because you are working there, unless you leave. I can sign it.” You are welcome to discuss with Peter about my application before let me know. So I choice leave and looking for other job. Thanks.

  1. By letter dated 29 April 2005 the Board requested Mr Tsang to complete a SORE or provide reasons as to why he could not do so (T12/33). On 20 May 2005 the Board again wrote to Mr Tsang requesting a response to its earlier correspondence (T17/40).
  2. By letter dated 31 May 2005 (T18/42) Mr Tsang responded to the Board’s correspondence. He claimed that he could not sign the SORE for the following reasons:
    • Ms Yan only joined his firm in December 2001 and therefore to his knowledge had not prepared any tax returns for the 2000 tax year.
    • The number of returns for the years ended 2001, 2002 and 2003 were not correct.
    • Errors allegedly found in her preparation of tax returns. In this regard he cited a tax return which had been prepared by Ms Yan and which contained numerous errors.

Mr Tsang stated he believed Ms Yan required a further twelve months of training before being considered for registration.

  1. On 7 July 2005 the Board wrote to Ms Yan advising that the Board had met and considered her application on 29 June 2005. It further advised that the Board had decided to refuse registration at the time pursuant to section 251JA(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). The refusal was based upon a perceived lack of breadth and depth in Ms Yan’s preparation of a sufficient quantity of income tax returns.

THE HEARING

  1. At the hearing conducted on 16 and 17 November 2005, Ms Yan was self represented and was assisted by an interpreter in the Mandarin language. The Board was represented by Mr M Allatt of the Australian Government Solicitor’s office. Ms Yan gave evidence, as did Mr Tsang. I had before me the documents (T documents) lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and the documents provided by the parties during the hearing.

LEGISLATION

  1. The Act relevantly provides as follows:

SECT 251JA

Original registration of tax agents



(1) The Board shall register the applicant as a tax agent if the applicant satisfies the Board that:

(a) if the applicant is a natural person:

(i) the applicant is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters; and

...

(2) The Board shall refuse to register the applicant in any other case.

...



SECT 251BC

Fit and proper persons to prepare income tax returns



(1) Without limiting the generality of an expression used in this Part, but subject to this section, a person is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters, as at a particular time, if:

...

(b) both of the following conditions are satisfied:

(i) the person was not registered as a tax agent, or as a nominee, for the purposes of this Part immediately before the commencement of section 39 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No. 2) 1988;

(ii) the person does not hold such qualifications (whether academic, by way of experience or otherwise) as are prescribed;



  1. The Income Tax Regulations 1936 (“the Regulations”) relevantly provides as follows:

REG 156

Prescribed qualifications for the purposes of subparagraph 251BC

(1) (b) (ii) of the Act

(1) For the purposes of subparagraph 251BC (1) (b) (ii) of the Act, the following qualifications are prescribed:

...

(c) the person:

...

(ii) shall have:

(A) been engaged in relevant employment on a full-time basis for not less than a total of 2 years in the preceding 5 years;



...



(2) In this Regulation relevant employment means employment by a person or a partnership or as a member of a partnership in the course of which there has been substantial involvement in income tax matters including:



(a) the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax returns;

(b) the preparation or examination of objections to assessments issued in respect of such returns; and

(c) the provision of advice in relation to income tax returns, assessments or objections.



(Tribunal’s emphasis added)



ISSUE BEFORE THE TRIBUNAL

  1. There was no dispute that Ms Yan met the academic and character criteria for registration as a tax agent.
  2. What remained in issue was whether she had been engaged in relevant employment on a full-time basis for not less than a total of 2 years in the preceding 5 years, as required by the Regulations.

CONSIDERATION OF THE EVIDENCE

  1. In coming to the correct and preferable decision, I took into account all of the evidence, submissions, case law and relevant legislation.
  2. The identified issues are the matters to be tested in regulation 156 of the Regulations. It should be noted at the outset that the questions asked of an employer in the SORE do not precisely reflect these questions.

Ms Yan gave evidence to that effect.

  1. It was further agreed by Mr Tsang that Ms Yan’s time was approximately devoted as follows:

“APPROXIMATE PERCENTAGE OF WORKING TIME SPENT IN DEALING WITH INCOME TAX & ACCOUNTING MATTERS:

a. income tax matters.............. 65%

b. accounting matters.............. 25%

c. auditing & other duties...... 10%”

  1. Ms Yan provided an additional SORE dated 22 July 2005 from a former employer, David Kutcher & Co, for whom she had worked from 14 February 2000 to 31 August 2001. At that firm she had prepared 50 individual and 4 partnership returns in 2000 and 5 individual returns in 2001. That document also indicated that 95% of her time was spent in accounting matters, with only 5% spent dealing with income tax matters.
  2. Mr Tsang wrote in his letter to the Board dated 31 May 2005:

“... Another reason I am not confident about Ms Maoqian Yan for her application for tax agent is that there are still doubts about her being able to handle all tax returns independently, as there are still errors found in her preparation of tax returns.

I just quote a case of individual tax return which she prepared last month for Ms Kwan. I sent a copy of the written correspondence from Ms Kwan for your reference. This is just to show Mrs Maoqian Yan needs a bit more time to be a tax agent.

  1. Ms Yan countered Mr Tsang’s claim that she had made errors in compiling tax returns. As to Ms Kwan’s return, Ms Yan said that she did not prepare that return and it was unfair to consider that matter against her.
  2. Ms Yan provided to the Tribunal a statement dated 20 September 2005 by Ms Cindy Chen, a casual, who has advised that it was she, and not Ms Yan, who had compiled Ms Kwan’s tax return (Exhibit A, Attachment 4 to Ms Yan’s correspondence to Tribunal dated 2 October 2005).
  3. At the hearing there was some dispute between Mr Tsang and Ms Yan about whether Ms Yan was in a supervisory role in respect of Ms Chen. The firm’s policy is that no casual employee may speak with a client, and it is clear that, at least to that extent, Ms Yan had some involvement in the matter.
  4. Ms Yan also addressed the issues raised by Ms Kwan, which apparently were ultimately dealt with to the client’s satisfaction.
  5. Irrespective of the extent of Ms Yan’s actual involvement in the preparation of Ms Kwan’s return, the relevance of that matter must be kept in perspective, having regard to the total number of tax returns which have been prepared by Ms Yan.
    • Did Ms Yan prepare or examine objections to assessments issued in respect of such returns?
  6. The question in the agreed SORE:

“GIVE BRIEF DETAILS OF THE EXTENT OF INVOLVEMENT IN:

...

g. preparing objections or private rulings”

was left unanswered. Mr Tsang said in his evidence that the question was not applicable as his firm did not do those matters.

  1. Ms Yan gave evidence that she had negotiated with the Australian Tax Office (“ATO”) in relation to assessments and had assessments amended following her representations.
    • Did Ms Yan provide advice in relation to income tax returns, assessments or objections?
  2. The question in the agreed SORE which addresses the provision of advice:

“GIVE BRIEF DETAILS OF THE EXTENT OF INVOLVEMENT IN:...

f. provision of advice”

was also left unanswered.

  1. Mr Tsang agreed in evidence that Ms Yan had provided advice to clients in relation to income tax returns and assessments. She had informed them as to items which were deductible and explained when items they wished to claim were not.
  2. As noted above, Ms Yan liaised with the ATO in relation to queries on assessments.

MR TSANG’S POSITION

  1. Mr Tsang expressed his own views about the qualities of a tax agent. He expected a tax agent to be able to provide advice in relation to financial planning, tax minimisation, structures and the like, and that Ms Yan did not have that capability.
  2. While Mr Tsang may have his own views as to the qualities of a tax agent, these do not reflect the requirements of the Regulations.
  3. Mr Tsang’s major concern, albeit not expressed at all in his letter to the Board, was as to Ms Yan’s communication skills. By way of example, an email was tendered in relation to communications with someone on behalf of one of the firm’s clients. The email response sent by Ms Yan was unintelligible to the client, judging by the client’s response. Ms Yan gave evidence in relation to the substance of the dispute, but did not, in my view, adequately address the communication issue. She was specifically asked to comment as to the clarity of the email, and she said only that it had been because she had ‘cut and pasted’ her response. She did add, that when she subsequently discussed the matter with the client, the client had apologised.
  4. In evidence, Mr Tsang was asked as to his comment in relation to his remarks in his letter to the Board dated 31 May 2005:

“In my opinion, Mrs Maoqian Yan should be given at least another 12 months before she can have the ability to fully develop her skills to be a tax agent...”

In particular, he was asked as to what he thought would change in “another 12 months”. He said that he would introduce a training program for his staff to improve communication skills so that they can write so that “[I] and a layman” can understand. He said he would arrange an English test and if Ms Yan passes, “then [I] would sign”.

  1. I observe that Ms Yan is part way through her Masters in Professional Accounting. The course includes assignments and written examinations in essay form, and I therefore assume some reasonable English skills are required in order to be able to pass. In any event, the evidence was that the vast majority of the firm’s clientele are of Asian origin.
  2. Again, while Mr Tsang may have his own views as to the requisite English language skills to be a tax agent, there was no evidence that Ms Yan’s English had inhibited her communications with the ATO. In fact, Mr Tsang’s evidence was that she had satisfactorily done so. Competence in English beyond that is not required by the Regulations.
  3. I asked Mr Tsang if he was aware of what his clients thought of Ms Yan and having regard to the following extracts of references from Winho Trading Pty Ltd, Jader Pty Ltd as trustees of Chung Family Trust, Mr Alex Isin, and Wing Ying Pty Ltd (respectively):

“... Mary displayed a high professional work ethics with particular and as a person high principle and integrity. I still prefer Mary preparing any group income tax returns for the 30 June 2005 financial year. Because her provided business services and tax advise to us are excellent.” (sic)

“We found her to be efficient, conscientious and honest in working for companies’ account.”

“It was nice to here (sic) that you would be doing my tax return this year too...”

“... Mary had prepared my group accounts and income tax returns from the financial years of 2001 to 2004. She possesses a high professional work ethic, and shows great integrity. She has provided me with exceptional tax advice and other related business services over the years...”

He said that he looked at all her work, from which I infer that he was suggesting the accolades were because of his vigilance in relation to her end product. He queried if it were enough that a tax agent make 90% of clients happy. In his view, all clients of a tax agent need to be happy.

  1. Mr Tsang also expressed a concern that if Ms Yan were to practise on her own account as a tax agent her limited language skills may get her into difficulty. He said if a client complained to the Board about her she may find herself suspended and unable to conduct a practice.
  2. As to complaints to the Board about Ms Yan if she were a tax agent but employed at Mr Tsang’s firm, he said that this would not be an issue because, as principal of the firm, he would continue to sign off on all her work, because he carried the responsibility for it.
  3. Mr Tsang also said that he did not care if Ms Yan left his firm and she could “leave tomorrow”, which I find somewhat out of step with his view of her as “a treasure” and that he relied on her and “absolutely trusted her” as a “key staff” (sic).
  4. While it is very noble of Mr Tsang to be concerned about Ms Yan’s welfare in the event she commenced to practice on her own account, it is, essentially, none of his concern.
  5. Mr Tsang denied that he was judging Ms Yan’s readiness to be a tax agent by his own standards after 30 years of practice as an accountant.

SUMMARY

  1. While aspects of Mr Tsang’s concerns are valid, particularly in respect of communication skills, the legislation sets out the requirements that must be satisfied in order to be a tax agent. Perceived skill deficiencies in other areas, is, rightly or wrongly, not relevant, providing the legislative requirements are met.
  2. I was referred to Su v Tax Agents’ Board of SA (1982) 61 FLR 1, in relation to the qualities of a tax agent, where Davies J noted (at 4):

“The function of a tax agent is to prepare and lodge income tax returns for other persons. A person is a fit and proper person to handle the affairs of a client if he is a person of good reputation, has a proper knowledge of taxation laws, is able to prepare income tax returns competently and is able to deal competently with any queries which may be raised by officers of the Taxation Department. He should be a person of such competence and integrity that others may entrust their taxation affairs to his care. He should be a person of such reputation and ability that officers of the Taxation Department may proceed upon the footing that the taxation returns lodged by the agent have been prepared by him honestly and competently.”

  1. In submissions it was conceded on behalf of the Board that had Mr Tsang’s information as to Ms Yan’s relevant experience been available at the time the Board considered her application it would probably have permitted her registration as a tax agent.
  2. I referred to Mula v Tax Agents’ Board of NSW (1996) 97 ATC 2001 at paragraphs 19 to 21 wherein there was discussion of the meaning of “substantial involvement” and “broad range”. I also considered Civiti v Tax Agents’ Board of VIC (1990) 90 ATC 2039 in relation to those terms. I agree that, on the available evidence Ms Yan has a substantial involvement in income tax matters and meets the criteria specified in regulation 156.

DECISION

  1. The decision under review is set aside and the matter is remitted to the Respondent with a direction that Ms Yan qualifies for registration as a tax agent.
