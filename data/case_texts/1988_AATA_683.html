REASONS FOR DECISION

THE APPLICATION AND THE ISSUE

This is a- application for review, made by a Corporate taxpayer whereby the decision of the Respondent not to accede to an objection raised by the company to an assessment issued in respect of the year ended 30 June 1983, is to be considered.

The assessment sought to bring to tax under Division 7 of Part III of the Income Tax Assessment Act an amount said to have been derived by the company (a private company within the meaning of the ACA during the year of income. The taxpayer claimed that pursuant to section 105A of the Act it had made or was deemed to have made a sufficient distribution with the result that -here was not an undistributed amount liable to additional tax under section 104 of the Act this on the basis of its assessable income being other than "income derived from property" and indeed not "income".

The assessment in the matter issued on 3 April 1934, the Notice of Objection being dated 14 April 1986. The objection was disallowed by letter post noted 27 March 1937 but forwarded to the taxpayer care of a firm of chartered accountants and not care of the address for service shown on the objection. A further letter referable to this disallowance was forwarded on 26 June 1987. The request of the taxpayer to refer the disallowance to the Tribunal under section 187 of the Act was made on 24 June 1987. In any event on 24 November 1987 the Tribunal ordered that an application made by the taxpayer for an extension of time within which to request the Respondent to refer to the Tribunal its decision upon the objection made by the Applicant to assessment of income tax for the relevant year be granted.

The Tribunal is vested with jurisdiction.

THE FACTUAL STUATION

The taxpayer was incorporated in February 1983. The only assets that had been acquired by it as at 30 June 1983 were comprised of moneys owing to it in an amount of s55 by a finance and investment trust and in an amount of $8,271 by a trust, the F. Discretionary Trust No. 1. The liabilities of the company as at that date were comprised of a provision for income tax of $40,145. Paid up share capital and reserves of unappropriated profits totalled $47,126.

The company in its profit and loss statement for the period ended 30 June 1983 showed a receipt of income by way of a present entitlement to receive a distribution from the F. Discretionary Trust No. 1 in the amount of $87,271. It is apparent that as at 30 June 1983 the "present entitlement" had not as yet been paid to the taxpayer. The distribution had been declared by the corporate trustee of the trust on the 30 June 1983 and to the following effect:

"IT IS HEREBY IRREVOCABLY RESOLVED that the net income of the F. Discretionary Trust No. l for the current Accounting Period ending on 30 June, 1983 be distributed and paid as soon as possible to the undermentioned General Beneficiaries in the undermentioned amounts and/or proportions -

To G.F.B. as trustee of the B. Family Trust No. 1, such being an "eligible trust" for the purposes of this Trust Estate - as to the sum of $10,000.00;

To P.K.M., the wife of J.E.M. - as to tie sum of $19,500.00; and

To [the taxpayer] an "eligible corporation" by reason of the Specified Beneficiary of this Trust Estate holding shares in it - as to 100% of the balance of such net income after allowing for the distributions referred to above."

There not being any operating expenses, the net operating profit was shown as a light figure to the "present entitlement".

In its income tax return for the subject year, the "business or income producing activity" of the taxpayer was shown as "investment company" and the net profit and thence taxable income at $87,271. No other transactions of any relevant significance occurred in relation to the taxpayer during the subject year.

On 30 April 1984 the taxpayer company declared and "paid" a dividend of $27,700 out of the previously unappropriated profits of $47,126. No income of any nature was derived by the company during the year ended 30 June 1984.

The company received the amount of $87,271 during the 1983 financial year consequent upon it being an "eligible corporation" and thence a "general beneficiary" within the meaning of a deed of settlement made in September 1981, the subject trust carrying on its activities under the name of the F. Discretionary Trust No. 1. The F. Discretionary Trust No. 1 had itself received or become entitled to receive, income of $116,771, this from only one source namely as a beneficiary of the MEB Families Unit Trust. After distribution or entitlement to distribution had been made in respect of two other beneficiaries, the balance remaining of the $116,771 that is, $87,271 was distributed and paid to the taxpayer.

By a deed made 1 October 1981 a solicitor had assigned a proportion of his interest from time to time as a partner in a partnership of solicitors to F. Pty Limited as Trustee of the MEB Families Unit Trust. At all relevant times the partnership was carrying on business as solicitors. The interest so assigned was equivalent to 49/100ths of the interest of the solicitor as such partner, that is, that proportion "of the share of -he profits to which the [solicitor] otherwise would be entitled".

The moneys so received (in fact or by entitlement) by the unit trust were derived from the said interest of the solicitor in the legal practice. [There was evidence of the unit trust also receiving an entitlement to income from a P. Discretionary Trust No. 1. There was not any evidence as to quantum. ] The income "received" by the F. Discretionary Trust likewise was derived, but once removed, from the said interest of the solicitor in the legal practice: the "income" "received" by the taxpayer likewise was derived but twice removed from the said interest of the solicitor in the legal practice. The said solicitor was a. all relevant times public officer of the taxpayer, whilst a member of the "excluded class" so far as beneficiaries of the F. Discretionary Trust No. 1 was concerned, public officer of its corporate trustee, public officer of the corporate trustee of MEB Families Unit Trust and the assignor of a proportion of his interest in the solicitors partnership to the Unit Trust. The income received by the firm of solicitors from which the distribution pursuant to the assignment was made was, on the evidence, substantially derived from the carrying on of the practice of solicitors.

It may here be noted that irrespective of the above nexus the taxpayer contends that the amount received by it, the $87,271, is to be looked at in its hands, and determination made as to whether it is of an income nature irrespective of what character it might have had if received or receivable from some other recipient. It is necessary it is said to look at the circumstances surrounding the taxpayer to determine the nature of the receipt. This "receipt" is said not to be income within the meaning of Division 7.

The contention of the Respondent Commissioner is that the "income" derived by the taxpayer was income from property or income derived from property. It was on this basis that the assessment was raised seeking recovery of the undistributed profits tax, the taxpayer having made a distribution by way of dividend insufficient to reach the threshold of the appropriate retention allowance. The Respondent thus says that,

the taxable income was $87,271

the primary tax payable was $40,145

$47,126

from which should be deducted the property income retention

allowance (section 105B 10% x $47,127) $ 4,713

sufficient distribution is thus $42,413

from which a dividend was paid of only $27,700

tax is then payable under Division 7 on the undistributed

amount of $14,713

THE EENEFICIAL ENTITLEMENT AND ITS ASSESSABILITY

The Respondent sought to assess the Applicant for Division 7 tax on the basis of the beneficial entitlement of the taxpayer derived from the Families Discretionary Trust No. 1 in the relevant year being "income from property" or "income derived from property" within the meaning of the

The Applicant contended that the entitlement was not income and not "income from or derived from property".

Section 105AC1) of the Act provides for what amounts to a sufficient distribution namely,

" For the purposes of this division, a private company shall be deemed to have made a sufficient distribution in relation to a year of income if it has, during the prescribed period, paid in dividends (other than special fund dividends and prescribed dividends) an amount not less than the excess of the distributable income of that year of income over the retention allowance in respect of that distributable income."

The "distributable income" in relation to a private company is defined in section 103(1) of tie Act to mean:

"....The amount ascertained by deducting from the taxable income of the company-

(a) the tax payable under this Act..... in respect of the income of the year of income;

(b) taxes which are pail in the year of income being -

(c) the amount of any re- loss...... incurred by the company in the year of income in carrying on business cut of Australia."

The "retention allowance" in respect of the distributable income of a private company of a year of income is

"....The aggregate of - 80% of the reduced distributable income and 10% of so much of any income of the company (other than dividends received from other private companies) derived from property as is included in the distributable income."

If the entitlement of the taxpayer is income derived from property then only 10% of such income - that is, 10% of the whole of the entitlement there not being any "reduced distributable income or a balance remaining after the distributable income had had deducted from it tie income derived from property included in it - could be retained and not distributed, without the company becoming liable to Division 7 tax. The assessment issued accordingly.

Thus the scheme contained in the Act for the taxation of private companies incorporates features whereby the company as an entity distinct from its shareholders is subject to tax on its taxable income and if the private company fails to make a sufficient distribution of its income, an additional tax is imposed on what is termed the "undistributed amount". Section 104(1) of the Act provides that a private company which is not by section 105A to have been deemed to have made a sufficient distribution in relation to the year of income, is liable to pay additional tax upon the undistributed amount. The additional tax imposed on the undistributed amount, that is, on the amount remaining after deducting from the distributable income the sum of the retention allowance (etc.) is calculated by reference to what is termed the reduced distributable income which itself is defined in sub-section 103(1) (supra). The retention allowance is then calculated in accordance with the terms of section 105B".

The term "income derived from property" or "income from property" is itself defined in section 6 of the Act as is the term "income from personal exertion" or "income derived from personal exertion". The latter concept relating to personal exertion is mutually exclusive of the concept of income from property and is defined as meaning, "Income consisting of earnings, salaries,

wages, commissions, fees, bonuses, pensions, superannuation allowances, retiring allowances and retiring gratuities, allowances and gratuities received in the capacity of employee or in relation to any services rendered, the proceeds of any business carried on by the taxpayer either alone or as a partner with any other person, any amount received as a bounty or subsidy in carrying on a business, any amount that is included in the assessable income of the taxpayer by reason of section 159(G)(D), the income from any property where that income forms part of the emoluments of any office or employment of profit held by the taxpayer, any profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit making by sale, or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit making undertaking or scheme, and any profit that is included in the assessable income of the taxpayer by reason of section 26AAA, but does not include -

(a) interest ; or

(b) rents or dividends.

"Income from property* or "income derived from property" is defined by section £ as meaning,

" All income not being income from personal exertion."

Was the beneficial entitlement of the taxpayer income or income derived from property? "Income" is not itself defined in the Income Tax Assessment Act. Income from personal exertion is defined as above and is given a specific meaning, the concept having included within it items some of which are income as the term is generally understood and some of which are not. It was submitted on behalf of the Applicant, that it does not necessarily follow that receipts included within assessable income, by reason of express statutory provision, but which are not specifically included as "income" by reason of the definition of "income from personal exertion" are thereby "income from property". This situation is illustrated by operation of the provisions of section 26(i) which sub-section causes there to be included in assessable income, inter alia, a bonus on an insurance policy, and reimbursement of a section 71 embezzlement and section 26(e) which causes there to be included an allowance in relation to employment. The point here being made is that by statute items are included in assessable income which do not fall into either of the concepts of income that is, from personal exertion or from property. It was sought by the taxpayer to equate the "beneficial entitlement" to a receipt which can be included within assessable income for primary tax but is not income and income from property within the meaning of Division 7.

The question is thus, whether the assessable income, that is to say the taxpayer's share of the net income of F. Discretionary Trust No. 1, is an amount derived by applying the net income definition in section 95(1) and including it in assessable income by virtue of the application of section 97(1) as income derived from property. If so, it is subject to the 10% retention allowance. If not, it will, subject to primary tax, be included in both the distributable income, being the taxable income less the tax referable to it, and the reduced distributable income in respect of which an 80% retention allowance is provided.

The Applicant sought to show that the receipt by a discretionary object of a discretionary power of a trustee, and its being a gain derived from property is negatived in the circumstances of the present case. Assistance in this regard was said to be derived from Re: Goldsworthy [1969] VicRp 107; 1969 VR 843 where it was said that under a discretionary trust where it could not he known whether anything would ever be receivable by a beneficiary until there had been an exercise of discretion in favour of the objects of the trust, that the rights of objects of such a trust are not to be classified as proprietary interests and under a discretionary trust of the kind here in question, it could not be known whether anything would ever be receivable by any one under the trust until there had been an exercise of discretion in favour of applying moneys thereunder. When assessing the amount of duty under the Probate Duty Act 1962 applicable to the interest the subject of the trust, the Commissioner, it was held, should disregard the objects of the discretion and have regard only to the person taking proprietary interest. I do not myself see the situation in Goldsworthy as being relevant in the present matter. Here there was an exercise of the discretion in the relevant year, in favour of applying the moneys. There was an existent proprietary interest.

In seeking to identify receipts which were not of an income character the taxpayer referred to ParsonS "Income Taxation in Australlia" page 34 where the following proposition appears:" The character of an item as income must be judged in the circumstances of its derivation by the taxpayer and without regard to the character it would have had if it had been derived by another person."

The aspect of a receipt having one character for one person and another character for another person was referred to by Bowen CJ in FC of T V Harris (1980) 80 ATC 4238 where at 4243 His Honour said referring to the particular case before him that the then case was not one "where the motives of the donor threw much light on the character of the receipt in the hands of the donee." The case there being considered was one where a bank had made an ex gratia lump sum payment to a retired employee who at the time was receiving a pension from the bank. The payment was unsolicited and the matter at issue was as to the nature of the receipt in the hands of the pensioner. It was held that the receipt did not constitute income according to ordinary concepts. This was not the position in the present case where the motive of the payer, and the expectation of the beneficiary taxpayer, was clearly apparent from the relevant trust deed and the wording of the distribution, combined with the facts that the taxpayer was a discretionary object of a discretionary power of the trustee of F. Discretionary Trust No. 1 which latter trust was a unit holder in the Family Unit Trust which unit trust had had assigned to its trustee a proportion of the interest of the solicitor in the income of the solicitor's practice.

Again at page 60, pare 2.132 of "Income Tax in Australia " it is said that "a gain which is a mere gift does not have the character of income". The use here of the word "mere" was meant it was said to indicate a gain not having the character of income and which was not the product of services, employment, or business, not a periodical receipt, not a compensation receipt, not a mere windfall gain and not a gain derived from property. The evidence in the subject case thus established that the receipt of the beneficial interest was not a product of services, employment or business or one of the periodical receipts (see Harris (supra) 4243) a compensation receipt, or a gain derived from property. I do not consider that the extracts from Parsons are particularly helpful in a resolution of the question arising in the present matter. It might equally be said that the proposition illustrates no more than the receipts the of gifts or windfall gains are items which would not be comprised in assessable income.

In the present case the taxpayer became presently entitled to the amount of $87,271 as a beneficiary of the F. Discretionary Trust No. 1 being an object of the trust the trustee exercising its discretion in its favour. The amount was subsequent to its receipt the subject of an interest free loan from the applicant to another entity. The loan on the evidence was subsequently repaid. The sum that the applicant received as a beneficiary in the F. Discretionary Trust No. 1 was in turn derived by t entity pursuant to its entitlement under tie units that held in the MEB Families Unit Trust. The NEB Families Un. Trust in turn derived its income from the income of the partnership of solicitors by virtue of "Everett" [Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Everett [1980] HCA 6; 143 CLR 440; 78 ATC 4595] type deeds of assignment and income from the practice discretionary trust. The Applicant company was not a partner in the solicitors partnership, nor did it carry on a business either alone or with any other person or entity during the subject year. It did not receive any gifts or windfall gains of a capital nature. It derived its income solely as a beneficiary of a trust being an object of the trust in its favour. The trustee of the trust exercised its discretion. It was submitted or behalf of the Respondent Commissioner that the taxpayer was irrevocably entitled to the beneficial interest once the trustee exercised its discretion which it had done, on the evidence, on 30 June 1983. I agree with this submission. Sections 95(A) and 97 of the Income Tax Assessment Act insured this to be so. (See also FCT V Whiting (1943 ) 58 CLR 199; FC of T V Bolford [1952] HCA 73; (1953) 88 CLR 589; Garthside v IRC [1967] UKHL 6; (1968) 1 All ER 121).

Section 50 of the Income Tax Assessment Act where it is provided that "where the assessable income is derived from more than one of the following classes of income, that is to say income from personal exertion, income from property other than dividends and income from dividends, the following provisions shall apply to all allowable deductions". If there was a further category of income there would not be any means of allocating deductions to any such category. There is not a further or third category of assessable income that is not one of those specified in section 50. Section 1053 of the Act referable to the retention allowance also proceeds on the assumption of there not being another category of income.

The taxable income of the Applicant consisted of an amount included in its assessable income by virtue of Section 97 of the Act. It was not suggested that the taxable income of the Applicant included any capital amounts. It was a discretionary beneficiaries interest in a trust's distribution of income. To say that it is not "income" or not "its income" because the distribution was made pursuant to the exercise of a discretion by a trustee has, in my opinion, no basis at law (Garthside (supra) at p. 127).

The definitions contained in section 6 of the Income Tax Assessment Act of "income derived from property" and "income from personal exertion" are mutually exclusive. The application of these mutually exclusive concepts in circumstances relating to a trust distribution was considered in Tindal v FC of T [1946] HCA 26; (1946) 72 CLR 608. In that case the income of a trust estate had come from the trustee's ownership of a pastoral property on which it carried on business and a share in a grazing business carried on with another person in partnership. A beneficiary was entitled no an annuity charged on both capital and income and a question arose as to whether a payment of arrears being income derived from the trust estate of the beneficiary was income from personal exertion or income derived from property. Although using slightly different phraseology, the judges of the High Court were unanimous in holding than the relevant income was income derived from property. It is worthwhile considering the way in which each of the judges expressed their opinion. At 622 Lathan CJ said:

The other questions in the case enquire whether either the sum of.... or the sum of...... if included in assessable income is income derived from personal exertion or from property. The income is income derived by a beneficiary of a trust estate. It does not fall within the definition of income from personal exertion contained in section 6 of the Act and it is therefore income derived from property which is defined as meaning "all income not being income from personal exertion."

At 623 Rich J said:

" It cannot be brought within what in the Bohemian's Club v Acting Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1918] HCA 16; (1918) 24 CLR 334 at 338, I called the artificial definition of section 6 as income from personal exertion and therefore is income from property.

At 625 Stark J said:

" Another question is whether the income of the taxpayer from the trust estate was income derived from personal exertion or from property. Definition of these terms in the Act requires that the question be answered that the sum of.... is income derived from property. Syme v Commissioner of Taxes (Vic) (1914) AC 1013 is distinguishable on the words of the Act."

At 629 said:

" The second question presents no difficulty. What is income from personal exertion is defined in section 6. All other income is income from property. The income derived by the taxpayer, as stated in the reference, does not fall within the definition from personal exertion. Syme v Commissioner of Taxes (VIC) [supra] is inapplicable because no doubt with that decision in mind the draftsman of the definition introduced the words "carried on by the taxpayer" after the words "proceeds of any business". The taxpayer did not carry on the business. She was a beneficiary, not a partner. The trustee did carry on the business, that is as a partner.

The second question should therefore be answered that it is all income from property."

In Syme v Commissioner of Taxation (Vic) (supra) the Privy Council had held that the income of the beneficiaries of a trust estate which carried on the business of publishing a newspaper was income from personal exertion within the meaning of section 2 of the Victorian Income Tax Act (1896). The Privy Council observed that [unlike the definition in the present section 6(1)] the definition of personal exertion income included the words "any trade carried on...." but did not specify "by whom such trade is carried on".

Finally, Williams J in his reasons for decision said at 633:

" The next question is whether this sum is income derived from personal exertion or from property. The definitions in section 6 divide assessable income into income from personal exertion and income from property. Income from personal exertion includes the proceeds of any business carried on by the taxpayer either alone or as a partner with any other person. Income from property is defined to mean all income not being income from personal exertion.

The income derived by the trustees from the carrying on of the partnership business was income derived from personal exertion. But the Appellant and not the trustee is the taxpayer and she is not a partner. When she receives her share of the net income under section 97 it is not income which falls within the definition of income from personal exertion and is therefore income from property."

It is noted that by section 6 of the Act "assessable income" is defined as meaning all the amounts which under the provisions of this Act are included in the assessable income. It is clear by reason of section 97 of the Act that the beneficial entitlement of the taxpayer in the present master is "assessable income". Further there is not any room for a category other than income from personal exertion and income from property emanating from assessable income unless so specifically provided for in the legislation. The view expressed in "Taxation of Trusts", B. Marks, at 256 namely,

" Although as will be pointed out...... there is much merit in the argument that a trustee is a mere conduit and income does not alter its nature as it passes through a trustee to a beneficiary, it must be now well settled that in view of the peculiar definition of income from personal exertion and especially the inclusion of the words "carried on by the taxpayer", trust income derived by a trustee from trading or business activities which is distributed to (and attributable to) a private company beneficiary is property income and thus subject to only a 10% retention allowance."

is in accord with authority and, with respect, a true statement of the law. This conclusion nay be dependent on a categorisation of the receipt as income. It is my view that the inclusion of the item in assessable income by reason of the provisions of section 97 of the Act precludes it being regarded as other than income for the purposes of Division 7. It is not a question of locking at the chain of entitlement to de-ermine the nature of the receipt. The circumstances in which it was received by the taxpayer are sufficient to this end.

DECISION

It is my view that the beneficial entitlement of the taxpayer in the subject year being part of the assessable income of the taxpayer, was income within the meaning of the Income Tax Act and that in the context of Division 7 of that Act was income derived from property.

Accordingly, the objection decision of the Respondent is confirmed.
