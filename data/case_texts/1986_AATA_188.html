Re Janice Beverly Neal and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 188 (7 July 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: JANICE BEVERLY NEAL

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A85/90

AAT No. 2753

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Justice J.D. Davies (President)

E. Smith (Senior Member)

Dr D.B. Travers (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - benefit Classification Certificate - substantial delay in issue of certificate - death of employee prior to issue of certificate - retrospective issue of certificate - whether certificate must be issued within a reasonable time - duty of Commissioner to issue certificate - account taken of staff limitations in Commissioner's office

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ss.16, 66

Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (Cth)

Public Service Act 1922 (Cth) ss.34, 47

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) s.42

Re Homsy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALN N.18

Re Amess and Commissioner for Superannuation (1984) 7 ALD 290

Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237

HEARING

CANBERRA

7:7:1986

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

The following reasons refer to the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Act") as amended by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (Cth). However, the amendments made do not affect the reasoning in any material way.

2. This is a review of a benefit classification certificate issued on 30 May 1984 in respect of the late Robert Claude Neal. The certificate was issued under s.16(10) of the Act and specified the conditions "migraine" and "history of anterior myocardial infarction". On subsequent re-consideration, a delegate of the Commissioner on 26 June 1985 varied the certificate by adding the additional conditions "triple vessel coronary artery disease" and "impaired left ventricular function".

3. Sub-sections 16(2) and (3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) provide that the Commissioner may require a person who proposes to become or becomes an eligible employee to undergo within such period as the Commissioner specifies such medical examination or examinations as the Commissioner determines and that a report or reports of the result thereof should be furnished to the Commissioner. Section 16(4) provides that the Commissioner shall consider the report or reports and determine whether or not to issue a benefit classification certificate. Section 16(4) reads :

"16.(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report

or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of

the opinion that there is a real risk that the

person, by reason of or for a reason connected

with a physical or mental condition or conditions

referred to in the report or reports, will not

continue to be an eligible employee until the

person attains his maximum retiring age, the

Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification

certificate to that effect, being a certificate in

which the relevant condition or conditions is or

are specified."

Section 16(10) provides for the circumstance where an eligible employee dies or ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before a benefit classification certificate has issued. Section 16(10) reads :

"16.(10) Where -

(a) a person who is an eligible employee dies or

ceases to be an eligible employee by reason

of retirement on the ground of invalidity -

(i) before he has undergone a medical

examination or examinations as

required under sub-section (2);

(ii) if he has undergone such an

examination or examinations - before

the report or reports of the result

or results of the examination or

examinations has or have been

considered by the Commissioner;

(iii) if the report or reports has or have

been considered by the Commissioner -

before the Commissioner has reached a

decision as to whether he should

issue a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the person

under sub-section (4); or

(iv) if the Commissioner has decided to

issue a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the person

under sub-section (4) - before the

certificate has been issued; and

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied that his

death or the incapacity which was the ground

for his retirement, was not caused, and was

not substantially contributed to, by a

physical or mental condition or conditions

of the person that existed at the time the

person became an eligible employee, or by a

physical or mental condition or conditions

connected with such a condition or such

conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the

person a benefit classification certificate in

which there is or are specified the physical or

mental condition or conditions of the person

which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, existed

at the time the person became an eligible employee

and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate

shall be deemed to have been in force in respect

of the person immediately before his death or

retirement."

4. Section 66 provides for invalidity benefits and s.66(2) provides for reduced benefits in the case where -

"66.(2) .....

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee

by reason of retirement on the ground of

invalidity before attaining his maximum

retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person,

immediately before the person's retirement,

a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the

incapacity which was the ground for his

retirement was caused, or was substantially

contributed to, by a physical or mental

condition or conditions specified in the

certificate or by a physical or mental

condition or conditions connected with such

a condition or such conditions,

.....".

5. In the present case, the late Mr R.C. Neal, who was born on 17 June 1947, was required by a delegate of the Commissioner to submit himself for examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer. Mr Neal was seeking engagement as a driver with the Department of the Capital Territory. Mr Neal was examined by Dr J.J. McCarthy, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, on 7 December 1981. Dr McCarthy reported to the Commissioner that Mr Neal was fit for appointment but did not meet the general medical standard due to the condition "migraine". On 11 December 1981, Dr H.S. Turner, Senior Medical Officer, reported to the Commissioner that she concurred in that view.

6. These reports would have justified the issue of the benefit classification certificate specifying the condition "migraine". Before any certificate was issued, however, Mr Neal died on 16 December 1983 from a myocardial infarction. The infarction was in no way related to his migraines.

7. On 21 December 1983, a delegate of the Commissioner considered the reports of Dr McCarthy and Dr Turner and decided to issue a benefit classification certificate specifying the condition "migraine". That certificate did not in fact issue as advice was subsequently received of Mr Neal's death on 16 December 1983. On 30 May 1984, the subject benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions "migraine" and "history of anterior myocardial infarction" was issued and that certificate was subsequently varied on re-consideration on 26 June 1985 by the addition of the condition "triple vessel coronary artery disease" and "impaired left ventricular function".

8. The decision of the delegate on 26 June 1985 was in evidence before the Tribunal. That decision set out the reasons for the decision and included a statement of the history of the matter, including the advice that "... due to substantial arrears of work at the time the delegate did not consider the report (of Drs McCarthy and Turner) until December 1983". Before the Tribunal that explanation was not challenged or expanded save that, in his address to the Tribunal, Mr A. Anforth, who appeared for the respondent, said :

"... One of the Commissioner's major problems is

this : that there are many tens of thousands of

people enter the Commonwealth employment each

year; very, very few of those, in relative

numbers, retire. If the Commissioner were to have

to direct his resources into ensuring that every

medical report which was tendered was looked at,

and further investigations carried out to his

satisfaction with respect to the people coming in,

that would cause an enormous research resource

problem and, indeed, most of it would be totally

wasted because so very few of them, percentage

wise, retire on invalidity; and if they do retire

within 20 years, up to which point a bcc is

effective, that all the work invested in that

number of persons who do not retire, which is the

vast majority, would be wasted time.

Where the Commissioner has the choice between

sinking his resources in that way or in attempting

to allocate more of his resources to those that do

actually retire, and where it is known that that

allocation of resources is, in fact, useful.

.....

... I am putting it to you in terms of the

capacity of the Commissioner to manage this scheme

- and I am not suggesting that he uses 16(10)

instead of 16(4). I am suggesting that the delays

in looking at 16(4) are because he has to allocate

his resources investigating the 66(2) situation

where the connections exist. That it is not a

case of putting aside 16(4) decisions and taking

16(10); it is a case that there are not enough

resources to deal with the 16(4) so delays become

inevitable.

.....

... it is a practical matter of administration

which the office has been unable to overcome at

this point in time. I do not think I can put it

on any other basis than that. And that, indeed,

is the explanation offered in the section 37

statements : that it was due to the workload

existing within the office at the time that the

Commissioner, simply, did not get to look at the

report."

9. Before the Tribunal the only ground argued was -

"That by 21 December, 1983 the Commissioner for

Superannuation had no jurisdiction to issue a

benefit classification certificate by reason of

delay."

It was submitted that the scheme of the Act requires a benefit classification certificate to be issued, if at all, within a reasonable time after the Commissioner has received the medical reports which he is, by s.16(4), required to consider.

10. Like submissions have been rejected by the Tribunal in Re Homsy and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALN N18 and Re Amess and Commissioner for Superannuation in (1984) 7 ALD 290. In Re Homsy, at page N.28, the Tribunal said,

"The only time limit the Act appears to impose on

the issue of a benefit classification certificate

(under s.16(10)) is that the employee shall not

have completed 20 years' contributory service, and

the specific provisions included in s.16 seem to

the Tribunal to prevent any construction of the

section that would read into it a requirement that

the Commissioner's powers must be exercised within

a reasonable time."

Likewise, in Re Amess at p.293, the Tribunal said,

"52. However, in our view, s.16 of the

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) stands in a

different position. It imposes a duty upon a

public official to be exercised for the purposes

of the Superannuation Act 1976. It is, in our

view, a statutory function to be exercised in the

circumstances prescribed by Parliament for the

protection of the superannuation fund against

claims under the Superannuation Act 1976 by reason

of early invalidity retirement. If Parliament had

intended that the functions directed to be

performed must be performed within a limited

period or that the power to issue a BCC was to

lapse if not exercised within a 'reasonable' or a

specified time, nothing would have been easier

than to say so."

11. However, in Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237, a case in which there was a substantial delay in the issue of a certificate, Beaumont J said, at 244-5,

"... There remains the question, raised by Davies J

in argument, whether, in any event, the

Commissioner was empowered by s 16(10) to issue a

benefit classification certificate as late as 30

September 1980.

The Commissioner contends that the provisions of s

16(10)(a)(ii) apply in the present case. The

suggestion is that, although the report of the

Commonwealth medical officer was received, by the

Commissioner by about 29 September 1977, the

backlog was so great that the Commissioner had not

considered the report until after the respondent's

retirement some three years later. This raises a

question of fact not previously addressed in the

proceedings and it is not appropriate that we now

attempt to express any view on it. The proper

course is for the matter to be remitted to the

tribunal for further consideration (cf Drake v

Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979)

24 ALR 577 at 607).

Moreover, apart from the exploration of that issue

of fact, a question of law arises as to the

application here of s 16(10) in any event, having

regard to the lapse of time between the medical

examination and the issue of the certificate. As

has been mentioned, as a result of the amendment

of s 16 in 1978, a medical examination is no

longer mandatory. Once the Commissioner has

decided to require a medical examination, there is

every reason to suppose that the statutory

functions provided for by s 16(4) must be

exercised, if at all, within a reasonable time.

That is to say, the Commissioner must consider the

report of the examination within a reasonable

time; and, if he forms the requisite opinion, the

Commissioner must also issue an appropriate

certificate within a reasonable time (cf R v

Fairford Justices; Ex parte Brewster (1976) QB

600; see also de Smith, Judicial Review of

Administrative Action 4th ed, pp 145-6). It is

suggested on behalf of the Commissioner that,

notwithstanding that s 16(4) was not complied with

in the case of the respondent, the Commissioner

could none the less avail himself of the

retrospective power to issue a certificate by

calling in aid s 16(10). Presumably that power is

claimed to subsist for as long as 20 years (see s

16(5) and Re Homsy and Commissioner for

Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD Note 18) Such an

arbitrary result of the use of a transitory

provision such as s 16(10) is unlikely to have

been the legislative intention (see Cooper Brookes

(Wollongong) Pty Ltd v FC of T [1981] HCA 26; (1980) 35 ALR 151;

[1981] HCA 26; 147 CLR 297). But, in the absence of full

argument on the point, it is not appropriate to

pursue it now. I would, however, indicate that,

for the reasons mentioned, I am not presently

persuaded of the correctness of the decision in

Homsy, supra."

In Miller's case, Davies J concurred with the views expressed by Beaumont J.

12. Mr R. Crowe, who appeared for the applicant, submitted that an inference as to time ought to be drawn from the provisions with respect to probationary and permanent employees in the Public Service Act 1922 (Cth). Mr Crowe referred to the fact that a person may not be appointed to the Public Service unless he is a fit and proper person (s.34(1)(c)) and that he is not eligible for appointment as a permanent officer unless "the Board is satisfied, after he has undergone a medical examination approved by the Board, as to his health and physical fitness" (s.34(2)). Mr Crowe referred to s.47(7) which provides :

"47.(7) As soon as practicable after the expiration

of the period of 6 months from the date on which a

probationer commenced duties in pursuance of his

appointment, the Board shall either -

(a) confirm the appointment of the probationer;

(b) annul his appointment; or

(c) direct that he continue on probation for

such further period as the Board thinks fit,

but so that the total period of his

probation does not exceed 1 year."

We do not, however, see any correlation between those provisions and the functions which the Commissioner has to perform under s.16 of the Act. We have found no useful guide as to time in those provisions.

13. The task which the Commissioner performs under the Act is different from that performed by the Public Service Board under ss.34 and 47 of the Public Service Act 1922 (Cth). The Commissioner is not required to make a decision as to a benefit classification certificate before a probationary employee is appointed as a permanent officer. Section 16(2) of the Act makes it clear that the Commissioner may issue a benefit classification certificate both to a person who proposes to become an eligible employee and also to a person who has become one.

14. Notwithstanding Mr Crowe's argument and the persuasive case put tentatively by Beaumont J in Miller's case, we do not think that a condition as to reasonable time should be implied. Parliament has specified a procedure to be followed but has not specified any time within which the steps should be taken. "Reasonable time" is an amorphous concept and scarcely a workable test in relation to routine decision making. In the Act, there is no guide as to time against which to judge reasonableness. We do not think Parliament intended that a court or tribunal should specify a period of 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years or 3 years as a reasonable period for the ordinary case which involves no difficulty. Yet, that is what would have to be done if Mr Crowe's submissions were to be accepted.

15. The functions which s.16(4) repose upon the Commissioner are mandatory. They are not discretionary. The Commissioner is under a statutory duty to consider the medical reports. In our opinion, it ought not to be implied that Parliament intended that the medical reports be considered within a reasonable time or not at all. Section 16(4) not only confers power upon the Commissioner to consider the medical reports and, in an appropriate case, to issue a benefit classification certificate, it imposes a duty upon him to do so. No time limit for carrying out that duty is specified and, in our opinion, none ought to be implied.

16. Notwithstanding the above, we do not read s.16 as intending that the position of an eligible employee should be prejudiced should the Commissioner for any reason fail to carry out one of the steps that the Act prescribes shall be carried out, as it would be if the Commissioner could ignore the s.16(4) requirements and act at will under s.16(10). The criteria for a benefit classification certificate adumbrated by s.16(4) at the relevant time, namely, that "... there is a real risk that the person, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, will not continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age ..." is different from the test adumbrated by s.16(10), namely, that "... the Commissioner is not satisfied that his death or the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement, was not caused, and was not substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions that existed at the time the person became an eligible employee or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected therewith."

17. In our opinion, when s.16(10) refers to the separate stages which must occur in the consideration of the issue of a benefit classification certificate and in the issue thereof, it does not refer to a situation which has arisen because the Commissioner has failed to perform a duty which s.16(4) imposes upon him, whether the failure has resulted from a deliberate decision not to perform it or because performance of the duty was overlooked due to loss or misplacement of documents or simply from inattention to the performance of the duty. Rather these provisions refer to the case where the Commissioner has not had time to consider the report or reports or has not had time, after such consideration, to issue a benefit classification certificate. The opening reference to the death or early retirement and the use of the word "before" in each of the sub-paragraphs is an indication of this. If Parliament had simply intended that the s.16(10) power should be exercised whenever the Commissioner had not previously made a decision as to the issue of a benefit classification certificate or, having made a decision to issue a certificate had not done so, Parliament would have said so.

18. The present is not a case where the medical report or reports were either deliberately or by oversight not considered by the Commissioner. The crux of the case is whether the lapse of time that occurred between the Commissioner's receipt of the medical report or reports in December 1981 and Mr Neal's death in December 1983 was so great that it can be said that the Commissioner did not attend to or abandoned compliance with the obligations which s.16(4) imposed on him. This is not the same question as whether the Commissioner acted under s.16(4) within a reasonable time. The reasonableness of the time that expired is a relevant consideration but not determinative of the issue.

19. The material before the Tribunal does not show any more than that the Commissioner was dealing with the medical reports which he received as expeditiously as he could, having regard to the staff resources available to him. Some of Mr Anforth's remarks do, indeed, suggest that the Commissioner preferred not to fulfil his duty under s.16(4) as that provision intended, for to do so "would cause an enormous research resource problem". However, we think that a fair reading of the whole of Mr Anforth's remarks and the fact that the reports in this case were considered on 21 December 1983, lead to the conclusion that the delay which occurred was due to the limitation in the staff resources available to the Commissioner and that there was no deliberate policy not to carry out the s.16(4) function as Parliament intended or any inattention to the performance thereof.

20. The delay which occurred was two years. This was a very great delay but staff limitations in the Commissioner's office is a matter which must be taken into account. In other areas of the bureaucracy involved in large volume decision-making, delays of this magnitude may also be encountered. On the whole, and taking into account that the reports were in fact considered on 21 December 1983 in the due course of administration, we are of the view that the Commissioner did not in this case abandon or otherwise neglect to fulfil the duties which s.16(4) imposed on him, although he was slow in fulfilling them.

21. In these circumstances, the provisions of s.16(10) apply, particularly sub-paragraph (a)(ii) in that Mr Neal died before the reports had been considered by the Commissioner.

22. For the above reasons, therefore, we are of the view that the grounds upon which the decision under review was challenged have not been supported. It was not put that there was any other aspect of the decision which ought to be considered. In our opinion, therefore, the decision under review should be affirmed.

As the determination of questions of law are, by virtue of s.42 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, to be decided in accordance with the opinion of the Presidential Member presiding, and as the question in this case is primarily one of law, I make only the following observations.

2. First, I am entirely in agreement with the decision in this matter and the views expressed by the President in respect of the submission that the Superannuation Act requires a benefit classification certificate to be issued within a reasonable time after the Commissioner has received the medical reports which he is required by s.16(4) of that Act to consider.

3. On the further test stated by the President, I also agree that, on the facts of this case, there is no evidence of deliberate policy of the Commissioner not to carry out the s.16(4) function or any negligence on the part of the Commissioner or his officers in fulfilling that function.
