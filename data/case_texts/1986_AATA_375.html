Re Heather Lynett Miller and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 375 (24 November 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: HEATHER LYNETT MILLER

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. N84/122

AAT No. 3039

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

C.J. Bannon QC (Deputy President)

G.P. Nicholls (Member)

Dr D.B. Travers (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - benefits classification certificate - 3 year delay in considering C.M.O. report - whether Commissioner obliged under section 16(4) to issue certificate within a reasonable time - whether section 16(10) gives Commissioner independent power to issue certificate despite failure to comply with section 16(4).

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) ss. 16, 66

Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth.)

Re Miller and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 7 ALN N89

The Commissioner for Superannuation v Heather Lynette Miller No. G39 of 1985 (unreported decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court, 28 November 1985).

Re Neal and Commissioner for Superannuation A85/90 (No. 2753, delivered 7 July 1986)

Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman [1985] HCA 41; (1985) 60 ALR 1

Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Ganke (1975) 1 NSWLR 252

Re O'Reilly; Ex parte Australena Investments Pty. Limited (1984) 58 ALJR 36

Cessnock Municipal Council v Attorney-General (NSW) (1956) 1 LGRA 287

Donovan v City of Sale [1979] VicRp 47; (1979) VR 461

Dallikavak v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1985) 61 ALR 471

Koon Wing Lau v Calwell [1949] HCA 65; (1949) 80 CLR 533

Ward v Williams (1954 - [1955] HCA 4; 1955) 92 CLR 496

Leon Fink Holdings Pty. Limited v Australian Film Commission (1979) 141 CLR 672

HEARING

SYDNEY

24:11:1986

ORDER

That the decision under review is set aside and in substitution thereof the Tribunal decides that the benefit classification certificate dated 30 September 1980 is of no effect and that it be set aside as invalid.

DECISION

The applicant, Heather Lynett Miller, seeks a review of a decision made by a delegate of the respondent on 30 September 1980 to issue a benefit classification certificate pursuant to section 16(10) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) ("the Act").

2. The applicant suffered a hormonal disorder of the breast which was diagnosed in 1973. Discomfort was noted upon medical treatment in 1975. In February 1977 the applicant was treated with drugs for hormonal mastopathy of the breasts. On 6 September 1977 the applicant became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Act, working as a switchboard operator for Telecom. On 29 September 1977 the applicant, at the request of the respondent, was medically examined pursuant to section 16(2) of the Act. The examining medical officer submitted his report (T4 p 19) on the same day, noting the removal of a breast lump and hormonal mastopathy and reporting that, in his opinion, the applicant met the general medical standard.

3. In December 1977 the applicant underwent radical surgery involving the removal of the tissue of both breasts and plastic reconstruction. On 27 August 1980 the applicant was retired on the ground of invalidity before attaining her maximum retiring age as a result of incapacity caused by a fibrous reaction leading to adhesions and pain following the operation.

4. On 30 September 1980 a delegate of the respondent issued a benefit classification certificate, purportedly under section 16(10) of the Act specifying the condition "removal of breast lump". The delegate decided, pursuant to section 66(2)(c) of the Act that the applicant's incapacity, which was the ground of her retirement, was caused or was substantially contributed to, by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate.

5. On review by The Administrative Appeals Tribunal (Re Miller and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 7 ALN N89), the latter decision was set aside, but on appeal to the Federal Court of Australia, it was remitted back to this Tribunal. The Federal Court decided that the fibrous reaction from which the applicant suffered was connected with her earlier hormonal disorder, which was the subject of the benefit classification certificate, and was so connected for the purposes of both section 16(10)(b) and section 66(2)(c) of the Act (see The Commissioner for Superannuation v Heather Lynette Miller No G39 of 1985, unreported decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court, 28 November 1985).

6. In remitting the matter to this Tribunal for rehearing the Federal Court directed the Tribunal to consider a matter raised by Davies J. in argument, namely, whether the Commissioner was empowered under section 16(10) of the Act to issue a benefit classification certificate as late as 30 September 1980.

7. This Tribunal was directed to consider:-

(1) A question of fact relating to the reasonability of

the time lag between the receipt of the

Commonwealth medical officer's report by the

Commissioner on about 29 September 1977 and the

lack of consideration of that report by the

Commissioner until after the applicant's retirement

some three years later.

(2) A question of law as to whether or not the

Commissioner had power to issue a benefit

classification certificate pursuant to section

16(10) of the Act when he did, and whether or not

the statutory power to issue such a certificate

under section 16(4) of the Act has to be exercised,

if at all, within a reasonable time.

8. In the course of his judgment, Beaumont J. with whom Davies J. agreed, said:-

"Moreover, apart from the exploration of that issue of

fact, a question of law arises as to the application

here of s. 16(10) in any event, having regard to the

lapse of time between the medical examination and the

issue of the certificate. As has been mentioned, as a

result of the amendment of s. 16 in 1978, a medical

examination is no longer mandatory. Once the

Commissioner has decided to require a medical

examination, there is every reason to suppose that the

statutory functions provided for by s. 16(4) must be

exercised, if at all, within a reasonable time. That

is to say, the Commissioner must consider the report of

the examination within a reasonable time; and, if he

forms the requisite opinion, the Commissioner must also

issue an appropriate certificate within a reasonable

time (cf. R v. Fairford Justices; Ex parte Brewster

(1973) QB 600; see also de Smith Judicial Review of

Administrative Action 4th ed. pp 145-146). It is

suggested on behalf of the Commissioner that

notwithstanding that s. 16(4) was not complied with in

the case of the respondent, the Commissioner could

nonetheless avail himself of the retrospective power to

issue a certificate by calling in aid s. 16(10).

Presumably, that power is claimed to subsist for as

long as 20 years (see s. 16(5)): Re Homsy and

Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD N.18).

Such an arbitrary result of the use of a transitory

provision such as s. 16(10) is unlikely to have been the

legislative intention (see Cooper Brookes (Wollongong)

Proprietary Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of

Australia [1981] HCA 26; (1980) 147 CLR 297). But, in the absence

of full argument on the point, it is not appropriate to

pursue it now. I would, however, indicate that, for

the reasons mentioned, I am not presently persuaded of

the correctness of the decision in Homsy, supra."

9. In the rehearing of this application, the Tribunal received further evidence in relation to these above issues.

10. The Act in its original form provided in section 16(2):

"16. . . .

(2) A person who proposes to become, or becomes,

an eligible employee shall undergo such

medical examination or examinations by an

approved medical practitioner or

practitioners as the Commissioner requires."

11. The Act was amended in 1978 and, in particular, section 16(2) was amended to its present form and section 16(10) was amended by firstly inserting the following words in section 16(10)(b):

". . . or by a physical or mental condition or conditions

connected with such a condition or such conditions.";

and secondly by adding the words after section 16(10)(b) providing for the retrospective effect of the issue of a benefit classification certificate.

12. Sub-sections 16(5) and (11) envisage the issue of a benefit classification certificate in certain circumstances where an employee's contributory service is less than 20 years. Sub-sections 16(2), (3), (4) and (10) of the Act, as it now stands, are as follows:-

"16 . . .

(2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes of this

section, require a person (other than a person

to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to

become or becomes an eligible employee to

undergo, within such period as the Commissioner

specifies, such medical examination or

examinations by an approved medical

practitioner or practitioners as the

Commissioner determines.

(3) A report or reports of the result or results of

the medical examination or examinations shall

be furnished to the Commissioner.

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or

reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is

of the opinion that the person is not likely,

by reason of or for a reason connected with a

physical or mental condition or conditions

referred to in the report or reports, to

continue to be an eligible employee until the

person attains his maximum retiring age, the

Commissioner shall issue a benefit

classification certificate to that effect,

being a certificate in which the relevant

condition or conditions is or are specified."

. . .

(10) Where -

(a) a person who is an eligible employee dies

or ceases to be an eligible employee by

reason of retirement on the ground of

invalidity -

(i) before he has undergone a medical

examination or examinations as

required under sub-section (2);

(ii) if he has undergone such an

examination or examinations - before

the report or reports of the result

or results of the examination or

examinations has or have been

considered by the Commissioner;

(iii) if the report or reports has or have

been considered by the Commissioner -

before the Commissioner has reached a

decision as to whether he should

issue a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the person

under sub-section (4); or

(iv) if the Commissioner has decided to

issue a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the person

under sub-section (4) - before the

certificate has been issued; and

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied that his

death or the incapacity which was the

ground for his retirement, was not caused,

and was not substantially contributed to,

by a physical or mental condition or

conditions of the person that existed at

the time the person became an eligible

employee, or by a physical or mental

condition or conditions connected with such

a condition or such conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the

person a benefit classification certificate in which

there is or are specified the physical or mental

condition or conditions of the person which, in the

opinion of the Commissioner, existed at the time the

person became an eligible employee and, for the

purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be

deemed to have been in force in respect of the

person immediately before his death or retirement."

13. Before dealing with the factual and legal issues, the Tribunal sets out the extra facts elicited at the rehearing. They were set out in affidavits and as a result of cross examination of the deponent George Frederick Hayes, the Assistant Commissioner, Superannuation and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Administration Branch. It appears from Mr Hayes' evidence that prior to September 1979, medical reports on eligible employees were sorted into two categories by a junior officer. In cases where the Senior Medical Officer recommended that the eligible employee did not meet the General Medical Standard, the medical reports were passed to a delegate of the Commissioner for consideration and a decision on whether or not a benefit classification certificate should be issued. In cases where the Senior Medical Officer recommended that the eligible employee met the General Medical Standard, the reports were put to one side pending the availability of sufficient staff resources being made available at a later date to enable consideration of the reports by a delegate of the Commissioner. It is apparent that the applicant's medical report fell into the second category of reports.

14. It further transpired that the medical reports in the second category were not transmitted to the Commissioner, but remained deposited at Telecom, with a delegate of the Commissioner appointed for that purpose, being an officer of Telecom. Evidence from Mr Hayes, corroborated by Messrs Kevin Lawrence Husselt and Alan Ernest Manning testified to the large backlog of medical reports on eligible employees and the insufficiency of staff available to the Commissioner to examine medical reports and issue benefit classification certificates. However, the inference is inescapable that in the case of the applicant, the medical report did not alert the officer engaged in sorting reports to a medical problem and on an organisational basis, there was no intention on the part of the Commissioner to consider the medical report on the applicant unless and until staff was made available to him to overcome an enormous backload of work. It is not necessary for us to determine to what extent the "sorting" of reports constitutes a delegated consideration of them but it seems to us likely that it does.

15. The applicant made an affidavit and was cross examined. In evidence, she stated that on 19 September 1977 she received confirmation of her acceptance as an "eligible employee". Further, she said that from 15 September 1977 to the date of her retirement, fortnightly deductions were made from her wages for superannuation. The applicant stated that for the whole period of her employment she believed she was eligible for full superannuation and that she was not made aware of any qualification on her entitlement, or of any delays in processing consideration of her medical report. Although there is a notation on the application form dated 21 June 1977 for becoming an eligible employee (T4 p. 72) drawing the applicant's attention to the possibility of the issue of a benefit classification certificate, the Tribunal accepts that the applicant was not made aware of any possible bar sinister affecting her eligibility in the future.

16. The Tribunal must be satisfied that the answer to the question of fact posed by the Federal Court is that the delay in consideration of the medical officer's report on the applicant was unreasonable if the matter falling to be determined under section 16(4) of the Act requires consideration within a "reasonable time".

17. The Commission, in our opinion, does not escape responsibility for the decision of the junior officer to put the applicant's medical report to one side or from his failure to perceive that the medical report revealed that the applicant mentioned a past medical problem which might affect the applicant's entitlements. Even if the situation were otherwise, a delay of three years appears too long, having regard to the purpose and policy of section 16 of the Act. The Tribunal is of the opinion that it is not a sufficient answer to say the Commissioner was understaffed.

18. The second question raised by the Federal Court is far more difficult and it is not made any easier by the subsequent decision of the Tribunal in Re Neal and Commissioner for Superannuation A85/90 (No. 2753, delivered 7 July 1986) a matter in which one of the Judges who sat on the Federal Court appeal in this matter was the presiding member. In Re Neal it was stated in relation to section 16(4) of the Act:-

"Notwithstanding Mr Crowe's argument and the persuasive

case put tentatively by Beaumont J. in Miller's case,

we do not think that a condition as to reasonable time

should be implied."

19. This puts this Tribunal in some difficulty. It is true of course that the remarks of Beaumont J. on appeal in this matter as to the law were strictly obiter dicta, but this Tribunal, which is merely an administrative body, is faced with the choice of following the decision in Re Neal or of deferring to admittedly tentative views expressed obiter dicta, by judges of the Federal Court, in a decision on appeal in this very matter. This Tribunal of course owes the greatest respect to judgments of the Federal Court, even if the remarks of the Judges are not strictly binding. In the face of this difficult conflict of opinion, the Tribunal feels it would be safer to adopt views expressed in the Federal Court rather than those expressed in Re Neal. However, as the views expressed in the Federal Court were obiter dicta and the matter under review has been remitted to us for determination we express our own view on the matter.

20. It appears to us that the duty imposed on the Commissioner under section 16(4) of the Act is mandatory and is a duty to be exercised not only for the benefit of the respondent but also for the benefit of the eligible employee. Nobody could say that the applicant lacked sufficient interest to seek an order from the Federal Court under section 7 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act, 1977 (Cth.) requiring the Commissioner to perform his duty under section 16(4) if he unreasonably delayed in so doing, or refused to so do. The section exists equally for the benefit of the employee and the respondent. If, after medical examination, an eligible employee were made aware that an adverse medical benefits classification was likely to be made by the Commissioner, the employee might very well consider it to be in his/her interests to resign from the public service and seek employment elsewhere and to enter into a superannuation scheme where there exists no barrier to obtaining full eligiblity in spite of pre-existing injuries, or alternatively to seek employment in an industry where no superannuation is deducted from the employee's salary. Alternatively if a benefit classification certificate does issue an employee might well immediately request revocation or variation of it under section 16(6) of the Act. If on the other hand a considerable period elapses after medical examination, is the employee entitled to assume that power given under section 16(10) will not be exercised - which is really to say is the Commissioner, even by virtue of section 16(10), still under a duty to act within a "reasonable time"?

21. The question arises whether or not section 16(4) gives the Commissioner a power or whether it is a power coupled with an obligation (see Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman [1985] HCA 41; (1985) 60 ALR 1, 44 per Brennan J).

22. The respondent provided the Tribunal with references to a number of decisions concerning the implication of time limits into statutes. They include Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Ganke (1975) 1 NSWLR 252, 258 per Nagle J; Re O'Reilly; Ex parte Australena Investments Pty. Limited (1984) 58 ALJR 36 per Murphy J.; Cessnock Municipal Council v Attorney-General (NSW) (1956) 1 L.G.R.A. 287, 294 per Herron J., as he then was and Donovan v City of Sale [1979] VicRp 47; (1979) VR 461 per Peter Murphy J. The Tribunal was reminded of the observation of Northrop and Pincus JJ "that there is a general disinclination to make implications in statutes, unless it is strictly necessary to do so" (Dallikavak v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1985) 61 ALR 471, 475). Cases concerning delay in criminal proceedings were mentioned, but we do not consider these to be in point. Our attention was also directed to the High Court's decision in Koon Wing Lau v Calwell [1949] HCA 65; (1949) 80 CLR 533, dealing with the power to declare a person a prohibited immigrant upon the expiration or cancellation of a certificate of exemption, especially the judgment of Dixon J. as he then was at pp. 573, 574 where he said:-

"But, in accordance with the ordinary rule, that must be

taken to mean within a reasonable time after the

expiration and cancellation of the certificate of

exemption".

23. It appears to the Tribunal that section 16(4) of the Act has an element requiring action by the Commissioner within a reasonable time after the furnishing of the report under section 16(3) of the Act. Sub-sections 16(2), (3) and (4) propose the setting in motion of a sequence of events within a temporal framework and it is not consonant with that framework in the Tribunal's opinion, for the Commissioner to be furnished with a medical report, to set it to one side without further consideration, upon the ground that the Senior Medical Officer had not recommended that the eligible employee failed to meet the General Medical Standard, and then to purport to more fully consider the report three years later after the employee had retired. A consideration of the Transitional Provisions in Part XII Division 4 of the Act dealing with existing contributors perhaps lends some weight to the view previously expressed. Section 184(3) of the Act is concerned with the position of employees who were contributors to the Provident Account established under the Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth.) ("the 1922 Act"). Instead of the mandatory nature of section 16(4) of the Act, section 184(3) of the Act is expressed in discretionary terms, which only become mandatory upon application in writing being made to the Commissioner by an eligible employee for the issue of a benefit classification certificate. The contrast between the imperative "shall" and the facultative "may", in similar statutory provisions, was noted by the High Court in Ward v Williams [1955] HCA 4; (1954-1955) 92 CLR 496 at 504.

24. The Tribunal has considered the Minister's Explanatory Memorandum (Exhibit 4) issued when a 1986 amendment to the Act was introduced, but this throws no light, in our opinion, upon the 1978 amendment.

25. Does section 16(10) give the Commissioner an independent power to still issue a certificate notwithstanding his failure to comply with section 16(4) of the Act?

26. The Tribunal considers that section 16(10) in both its original and its amended form was not inserted in the Act to take away the mandatory nature of the Commissioner's duty under section 16(4) or to relieve him of the duty to consider the medical report within a reasonable time, a duty which should be readily inferred.

27. It is our view that in reading section 16 as a whole it forms a code for the issue of benefit classification certificates. It is supplemented by section 184 solely as a transitional provision relevant to members of the superseded fund. The central provision governing the issue of such a certificate is to be found in section 16(4). There is a mechanism for revocation or variation of such a certificate to be found in sections 16(6) to (9) inclusive, and sections 16(10) and (11) provide quite distinct retrospective provisions to deal with certain limited situations of death or invalidity, retirement in the case of section 16(10) and failure to furnish information or the furnishing of false information in relation to a medical examination in the case of section 16(11). Section 16(12) provides for the service of certificates on the person affected.

28. The act being one to provide an occupational superannuation scheme for eligible employees any provisions imposing limitation on entitlement should be constructed so as to require the making of such a decision within a reasonable time and the clear communication of it to the affected person. It is our view that the scheme of section 16 not only readily permits of such a construction but produces anomalies if such a construction is not adopted. The review provisions of section 16(6) to (9) would have no practical application if no requirement as to time was imputed to the Commissioner's consideration of medical reports as outlined in section 16(4). If, as the respondent contends here, there is no time limit up to the 20 years of employment referred to in section 16(5) no employee would be clear that a benefit classification certificate would not issue for the whole time. In that time there would be no right to require the Commissioner to take account of updated reports on a medical condition which might have markedly improved as seems to be envisaged by sections 16(6) to (9).

29. It is our view that section 16(10) does give the Commissioner an independent power to issue a benefit classification certificate prior to the time of his decision being made under section 16(4). By its very terms its operation is limited solely to those situations where the overall process of the consideration of matters leading up to the decision whether or not to issue a benefits classification certificate under section 16(4) has not yet been concluded. The power given under section 16(10) is in the nature of a power to give a certificate only when the normal process under section 16(2) to (4) has been prematurely interrupted. Since we have no difficulty in finding that this latter process is required to be carried out within a reasonable time it follows that the independent power found in section 16(10) must itself be exercised within a reasonable time.

30. In this sense the relationship between the powers contained in sections 16(4) and 16(10) is quite different from that examined in Leon Fink Holdings Pty Ltd v Australian Film Commission (1979) 141 CLR 672. In that case the two powers - one a very general power to encourage activities and one a specific power to lend money for particular purposes were linked by the phrase "without limiting the generality of the foregoing". In such circumstances the High Court held that the specific power could not be held to limit the general power ". . . when it is followed by a clause whose purpose is to ensure that the presence of the particular powers does not erode the generality of the initial grant of power", per Mason J. at 680.

31. Yet in this case the more specific power contained in section 16(10) is by its words limited to the time frame for decision making set by the general provision of section 16(4).

32. Section 16(11) has a very different retrospective operation to section 16(10). It is not limited by the process of section 16(4) and it includes a specific time frame in section 16(11)(b), namely that the ex-employee's ". . . period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age." Since this section is aimed at allowing the Commissioner to deal with cases of fraud or non-disclosure it is not surprising that it has a very broad time frame. The time frame chosen repeats that found in section 16(5) as the limit of the operation of benefit classification certificates. The limit set in section 16(5) applies only when a benefit classification certificate is in force - but then this is not the case in a circumstance covered by either sections 16(10) or 16(11). The respondent contended that since there is no "reasonable time" limit on the operation of section 16(10) it is this time limit set by section 16(5) which should, by inference, be applied. Yet in that case why is the provision specified in section 16(11) and not in section 16(10). This structure further confirms our view that section 16(10), properly constructed, is meant to apply within the time frame set by section 16(4) and that is within a "reasonable time".

33. Accordingly, in light of our earlier expressed view that a delay of three years was not reasonable in the circumstances of this case, it is our view that the benefit classification certificate issued on 30 September 1980 was invalid as it was not issued within a reasonable time of the medical report on the applicant being made available to a delegate of the Commissioner. We thus agree with the decision of the earlier Tribunal given on 4 February 1985.

34. We also add that there could be real practical difficulties flowing from a different construction - a point conceded by the respondent. If the respondent's submission that time limits equivalent to those in section 16(5) were the only limits applying was accepted then all eligible employees accepted between 1976 and 1979 would be well advised to serve notice on the Commissioner requiring him to determine their entitlement under the Act. If this were not complied with they might well en masse then resort to action pursuant to section 7 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977. Such consequences can hardly have been intended by the 1978 amendments to section 16 of the Act.

35. The order of the Tribunal is that the benefit classification certificate dated 30 September 1980 is of no effect and that it be set aside as invalid.
