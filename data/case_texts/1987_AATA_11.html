Last Updated: 8 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Exempt income - whether pension paid pursuant to "Act Governing Benefits for the 1940-1945 Victims of Persecution" of the Netherlands has character of income.

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936)(Cth) S25

Atlas Tiles Ltd v Briers [1978] HCA 37; (1978) 144 CLR 202 at 205

FC of T v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540)

FC of T v Slaven (84 ATC 4077)

Artwinska v Secretary Dept of Social Services (1985 No N84/365)

Kolodziej v Secretary Dept of Social Services (1985 No S84/44)

Income Tax - Exempt income - whether pension paid pursuant to "Act Governing Benefits for the 1940-1945 Victims of Persecution" of the Netherlands exempt income pursuant to S23 AD(3)(c) Income Tax Assessment Act.

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) (Cth) 23AD (3)

Grain Elevators Board (Vic) v Dunmunkle Corp [1946] HCA 13; (1946) 73 CLR 70

re Samuel 1913 AC 514

Goodfellow v FCof T 77 ATC 4086

Income tax – exempt income – whether pension paid pursuant to "Act Governing Benefits for the 1940-1945 Victims of Persecution" of the Netherlands exempt income by reason of Racial Discrimination Act, (Cth) – whether S 23 AD (3) 1 TAA amended by implication.

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

NT81/13 NT 82/7-9 NT 82/18 NT 83/59

AAT Decision No 3160

Mr PM Roach (Senior Member)

19 January 1987

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No NT81/13, NT82/7-9, NT82/18, NT83/59

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

RE: NT81/13

Applicant

AND: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr PM Roach (Senior Member)

  1. In these reasons for decision I shall identify the Applicant who requested the reviews now before the Tribunal as "Visser", a false name by which he had been known for a period during the 1940's.
  2. Visser was born in Holland in 1922. He was a Dutch national and as a descendant of four Jewish grandparents, he was regarded by others as, and regarded himself as, a Jew. When the German armed forces invaded and occupied Holland in 1940 he became subject to the restrictions imposed on Jews. In due course he was arrested. At the time he was engaged to be married. His bride-to-be (as she then was) gave evidence that she believed that his arrest came in the course of his clandestine activities in circulating an underground newspaper. In September 1942 Visser entered his first concentration camp. The prisoners there were for the most part either Polish or French Jews. His written account of the years following, recounting his escapes and describing life in various prisons ranging from those conducted humanely to those characterized by extraordinary atrocities, - was placed in evidence. He survived the ordeal. After several years of service in the Dutch Navy, he migrated to Australia with his wife - the fiancee of the pre-war years. In Australia from the mid-1950's he supported his family by working as a kitchen-hand in a public hospital. Although he was qualified for higher duties his health was not good enough to enable him to secure advancement and hold it. From the late 1940's he had suffered from epilepsy which was accepted by his medical advisers as having been occasioned by head injuries suffered whilst in concentration camps. By September 1976, when aged only 54 years, he was unable to continue the employment of the past 18 years. He resigned on grounds of ill health and from shortly thereafter did not work again. The medical practitioner who had treated him over the years and others who assessed his condition at that time attested that he was mentally unfit to continue work and that they were satisfied that that condition was attributable to the atrocities he had observed and the injuries he had suffered in concentration camps.
  3. Thereafter the only revenue available to Visser came by way of a monthly pension paid to him pursuant to the "Act Governing Benefits for the 1940-1945 Victims of Persecution" of the Netherlands. That entitlement commenced 1 October 1976. (A certificate from the Consul-General for the Netherlands tendered in evidence refers to 1 October 1975. I suspect that that is a typographical error, but it is not material). In July 1977, at the time of lodging his income tax return to 30 June 1977, Visser told the Commissioner of the pension. Correspondence followed. The last letter in the series to be produced was a letter from Visser to the Commissioner in September 1978. On 3 November 1978 the Commissioner issued an original assessment against Visser in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1978 (sic) and in doing so he treated the pension as assessable income. That assessment was objected to and, so far as relates to matters before me, the objection was disallowed on 10 March 1981. On that date original assessments also issued in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1977, 1979 and 1980. They also treated the pension as assessable income. On 26 March 1981 Visser requested that his objection in relation to the 1978 year, disallowed earlier that month, be referred for review. The three original assessments last referred to were also objected to in due course and, after disallowance, were the subject of requests for review. At later dates original assessments also issued in relation to the 1981 and 1982 years and in time they also became the subject of requests for review. The requests so made, the first of which was made 26 March, 1981, were not referred on for review until 31 May 1985. By then the reference was too late to be of any assistance to Visser. He had died in January 1985.
  4. At the hearing Counsel for the Applicant sought, and was granted, leave to amend the grounds of objection and the case was argued in relation to those three points. They embraced all the issues which had been canvassed over the years. The additional grounds of objection incorporated into the objection of each year were as follows:
  5. "Financial benefits received by Mr (Visser) under the Netherlands Act Benefits for the 1940-45 Victims of Persecution were not income for the purposes of section 25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.
  6. Further, and in the alternative, financial benefits received by Mr (Visser) under the Netherlands Act Benefits for the 1940-45 Victims of Persecution were exempt from taxation because they were payments of the kind referred to in S23 AD(3)(c) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.
  7. Further, and in the alternative, financial beneftis received by Mr (Visser) under the Netherlands Act Benefits for the 1940-45 Victims of Persecution were exempt from taxation by reason of the operation of section 10 of the Racial Discrimination Act, 1975, upon section 23 (kc) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for section 23(kc) is a law of the Commonwealth by reason of which persons of a particular race, colour, or national or ethnic origin do not enjoy a right that is enjoyed by persons of another race, colour or national or ethnic origin".

As so cast in technically correct and sufficient terms appropriate to raise the legal issues to be determined, they fail to adequately reflect the sense of injustice suffered by Visser during his life-time. He perceived himself to be a person persecuted unjustly in Europe in the 1940's for being a Jew; and discriminated against in Australia in the latter part of his life for the same reason. It was obvious during the hearing that his widow shared that perception.

  1. The first contention advanced on behalf of the estate of the Deceased is that the monies received by Visser did not constitute "income" within the meaning of Section 25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). It was contended that the monies he received constituted compensation for the sufferings experienced during the war and that in consequence the monies received had the character of capital. He supported that contention by pointing in particular to the decision of Barwick, CJ in Atlas Tiles Ltd v Briers [1978] HCA 37; (1973) 144 CLR 202 at 205. Unfortunately I find that decision unhelpful. Compensation for capital loss may be made by the provision of monies either by way of capital or by way of income. Where compensation is so made by an assured and indefinitely continuing series of payments made regularly - as was the case for Visser - each payment in my view has the character of income (FC of T v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540). As the payments were part of a series of payments to which Visser was entitled during his lifetime by statute they cannot be identified with the receipt by an injured person of a lump sum by instalments, where the lump sum was paid "to adequately compensate (the victim) for the deprivation or impairment of earning capacity that she had received" as in FC of T v Slaven (84 ATC 4077). Reference was also made on behalf of the Deceased to decisions of this Tribunal (General Division) in Artwinska v Secretary Department of Social Services (1985 No N84/365) and Kolodziej v Secretary Department of Social Services (1985 No S84/44). As the Tribunal was not concerned in either of those decisions to apply the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act, I do not find them to be of any assistance in the determination of the issues before me. I am satisfied that the monies received by Visser had the character of "income" within the meaning of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

6. The second argument is based on S 23AD of the Act. Consideration of the legislative history relating to pensions as exempt income begins with Section 23 of the Income Tax Assessment Act as enacted in 1936. At that time the only form of pension classified as exempt income was that referred to in Section 23 (k) which exempted:-

"pensions paid under the Australian Soldiers' Repatriation Act 1920-34, and wounds and disability pensions of the kind specified in sub-section (2) of Section 16 of the Finance Act, 1919 of the United Kingdom".

7. In 1941 Section 23 (ka) was introduced to exempt child endowment allowances. It was amended in later years to deal with certain classes of social service pensions. In 1946, with the enactment of repatriation legislation following the Second World War, Section 23 (kb) was introduced to deal with allowances payable under that legislation. In 1954 the Parliament legislated to substitute Sections 23 (k),(kaa) and (kab), and (ka) for the original Section 23 (k). By a further amendment of 1973, Section 23AD was introduced in substitution for the last-mentioned sub-sections. The result was that by force of section 23AD (3) the following pensions came to be exempt from income tax:

(a) payments (other than excepted payments) of pensions and attendants allowances, and payments (other than excepted payments) of a like nature, made under the Repatriation Act 1920-1973, the Repatriation (Far East Strategic Reserve) Act 1956 - 1973, the Repatriation (Special Overseas Service) Act 1962-1973 or the Seamen's War Pensions and Allowances Act 1940-1973;

(b) payments of pensions and allowances, and other payments, made by the Government of Australia or the Government of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland, being payments that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, are of a similar nature to payments (other than excepted payments) referred to in paragraph (a);

(c) payments of wounds and disability pensions of the kinds specified in sub-section 365 (2) of the Imperial Act known as the Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970, other than payments that, in the opinion of the Commissioner are of a similar nature to excepted payments; and

(d) payments (other than excepted payments) by way of:

(i) pension, allowance, endowment or benefit under the Social Services Act 1947-1973;

(ii) allowance under the Tuberculosis Act 1948; and

(iii) domiciliary nursing care benefit under Division 5B of Part V of the National Health Act 1953-1973".

8. Section 365 of the Income and Corporation Taxes Act 1970 (United Kingdom) provided:

(1) Income from wounds and disability pensions to which this subsection applies shall be exempt from income tax and shall not be reckoned in computing income for any of the purposes of the Income Tax Acts.

(2) Subsection (1) above applies to -

(a) wounds pensions granted to members of the naval, military or air forces of the Crown.

(b) retired pay of disabled officers granted on account of medical unfitness attributable to or aggravated by naval, military or air-force service.

(c) disablement or disability pensions granted to members other than commissioned oficers of the naval, military or air forces of the Crown on account of medical unfitness attributable to or aggravated by naval, military or air-force service.

(d) disablement pensions granted to persons who have been employed in the nursing services of any of the naval, military or air forces of the Crown on account of medical unfitness attributable to or aggravated by naval, military or air-force service, and

(e) injury and disablement pensions payable under any scheme made under the Injuries in War (Compensation! Act 1914, the Injuries in War (Compensation) Act 1914 (Session 2), and the Injuries in war (Compensation) Act 1915, or under any War Risks Compensation Scheme for the Mercantile Marine:

Provided that, where the amount of any such retired pay or pension to which subsection (1) above applies is not solely attributable to disablement or disability, the relief conferred by the said subsection (1) shall extend only to such part as is certified by the Secretary of State for Social Services, after consultation with the appropriate Government Department, to be attributable to disablement or disability".

  1. Despite the rearrangement and the changes effected Section 23AD upon its introduction in 1973, the basic structure established from at least 1954 in regard to the foregoing pensions had been preserved.
  2. However, there had also been a further line of legislative development. In the years following World War II, migration from Europe on a large scale opened up Australia to new residents from many and diverse backgrounds, including many persons from countries which in the 1940's had been at war with Australia and its allies. In some cases the persons migrating had been members of the armed forces of those countries. Others had been victims in greater or lesser degree of armed conflict whether as citizens or members of the Defence Forces of allied countries which had been overrun and occupied by enemy forces or, as with Visser, as persons who by reason of race and/or religion had suffered persecution commonly in concentration camps. Against that background in 1959 the Parliament legislated to establish as exempt income,

"23 (kc). Pensions, annuities and allowances paid as or by way of compensation by a State of the Federal Republic of Germany under the laws of that Republic relating to compensation of victims of National Socialist persecution.

  1. Then in 19S4, whether it needed to do so or not, the Parliament further amended Section 23 to expressly constitute as sxempt income:

"(kca) Payments of pensions, annuities and a11owances -

(i) that are made under a law of the Federal Republic of Germany, being payments the entitlement to which depends in whole or in part upon the treatment as a period of contribution of a period during which the recipient or another person was a victim of, or a fugitive from, National Socialist persecution;

(ii) that are made under a law of the Kingdom of the Netherlands as or by way of compensation in respect of -

(A) the persecution, during the Second World War, of the recipient or another person by forces of an enemy of the Commonwealth, being forces occupying the Kingdom of the Netherlands or the former Dutch East Indies; or

(B) a disability arising out of the participation of the recipient or another person in the Dutch resistance movement in the Kingdom of the Netherlands during its occupation during the Second World War by forces of an enemy of the Commonwealth; or

(iii) that, not being payments to which sub-paragraph (i) or (ii) applies, are made under a law (including a law of the Federal Republic of Germany or of the Kingdom of the Netherlands) other than a law of the Commonwealth, a State or a Territory and, in the opinion of the Commissioner, are related to, or take into account-

(A) the persecution, during the Second World War, of the recipient or another person by forces of an enemy of the Commonwealth, or flight from such persecution; or

(B) a disability arising out of the participation of the recipient or another person in a resistance movement during the Second World War against forces of an enemy of the Commonwealth".
  1. It is common ground that from the date Section 23 (kca) took effect (3 March, 1982), the pension received by Visser constituted exempt income. Counsel for Visser contends that it always was exempt. On the other- hand Counsel for the Commissioner supports his contention as to liability by saying that regard must be had to the action of the Parliament in specifically extending exemption in 1934, thereby indicating the Parliament was of the view - as it appears from the Second Reading speech of the Honourable, the Treasurer, that he was that the enactment of the amendment was necessary to effect the exemption; otherwise the amending legislation would have been otiose. He supports that contention by pointing to the observation of Dixon, CJ in Grain Elevators Board (Vic) v. Dunmunkle Corporation, [1946] HCA 13; (1946) 73 CLR 70 where his Honour had regard to subsequent amending legislation saying,

"It would be a strange result if we were to interpret the prior legislation as giving a wider exemption than that conferred by the provision so that the express exemption it makes would prove unnecessary and the qualifications it places upon that exemption would be futile".

However, that observation does not negate the observation of Viscount Haldane, LC in re Samuel 1913 AC 514 at 526);

"It is not a conclusive argument as to the construction of an earlier Act to say that unless it be construed in a particular way a later enactment would be surplusage. The later Act may have been designed, ex abundante cautela, to remove possible doubts".

  1. From a consideration of the foregoing I discern that Parliament has made provision for tax exemption in relation to two distinct groups of persons; first those who receive pensions by reason of experiences in, or the consequences of, military service in the interests of Australia and some, at least, of its allies; and secondly, persons who have received compensation by reason of their disabilities and sufferings as victims of some regimes which engaged in armed conflict in opposition to Australia and its allies.
  2. I accept that para (kab) of Section 23 did not embrace only pensions payable under the Imperial Act (Jacobs J in Goodfeilow v FC of T 77 ATC 4086 at 4087) and that Section 23 (kaa) was "not concerned with identity but with similarity, so that there must necessarily be some difference, and for that reason instances may occur in which questions of degree arise as to which opinions may differ....so that cases may arise which are close in character to the pensions referred to under Section 23 (k) but there can be no doubt that they are of a similar nature and other instances may arise when differences are so great that it would not be possible to describe them as similar in nature. In between a wide range of possibilities may exist in some of which two views may be open". (Aickin, J -ante at 4093).
  3. On the facts of this case I am of opinion that the circumstances giving rise to Visser's claim are such that the pension received is not - pursuant to S23AD (3)(b) - "of a similar nature" to the pensions payable pursuant to the Commonwealth legislation specified in Section 23 AD (3)(a). Nor is it "of the kinds" specified in the Imperial Act - Section 23 AD (3)(c). Visser was the victim of a barbarous regime which Australia and its allies resisted, fought and in the end defeated. He is to be identified with the victims others risked their lives for; not with their rescuers acting under the discipline of military service. In my view the pension in question did not constitute exempt income by reason of the provisions of Section 23AD (3) of the Act.
  4. Nor do the payments by way of pension acquire the status of exempt income by reason only of Section 23 (kc) because although the pension "was paid as or by way of compensation... (and was paid to Visser as one of many) victims of National Socialist persecution" - as I so find - the amounts were not paid "by a State of the Federal Republic of Germany under the laws of that Republic relating to compensation of victims of National Socialist persecution". That was not disputed. However the final argument in support of the objections is that the enactment by the Commonwealth Parliament of the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 has had the effect of amending Section 23 (kc) of the Income Tax Assessment Act so as to make available to recipients of a pension under the Netherlands "Act Benefits for 1940-45 Victims of Persecution" the same exempt status as Section 23 (kc) of the Act conferred on recipients of pensions paid by way of compensation by a State of the Federal Republic of Germany under the laws of that Republic relating to compensation of victims of National Socialist persecution. In particular reliance is placed on Section 10 (1) of that Act which provides:

"If, by reason of, or of a provision of, a law of Australia or of a State or Territory, persons of a particular race, colour or national or ethnic origin do not enjoy a right that is enjoyed by persons of another race, colour or national or ethnic origin, or enjoy a right to a more limited extent than persons of another race, colour or national or ethnic origin, then, notwithstanding anything in that law, persons of the first mentioned race, colour or national or ethnic origin shall, by force of this section enjoy that right to the same extent as persons of that other race, colour or national or ethnic origin".

  1. In my view the argument fails at the threshold. I accept that Visser was persecuted because he was a Jew. But nothing in the assessment turns upon whether or not Visser was a Jew or on the recipient of any pension payable under German legislation being or not being a Jew. As I understand the evidence and the history of this century it is undoubted that the Jews were the principal target of Nazi persecution. They suffered in their millions, but they were not the only persons to so suffer and they are not the only persons who can qualify for pensions under the relevant German or Netherlands legislation. It was not suggested in evidence that the German legislation is directed to the benefit only of Jews or persons who suffered persecution because they were Jews. Similarly, the legislation of the Netherlands makes no distinction on the basis of race, colour or national or ethnic origin. It is true that the operation of the Income Tax Assessment Act distinguishes for tax purposes between compensation for persecution paid by States of the Federal Republic of Germany and compensation for persecution paid by other persons or authorities or under other legislation, but that is not to draw any distinction between recipients based upon race, colour or national or ethnic origin. In particular it does not base any distinction upon whether the recipient was or was not a Jew.
    1. I would affirm the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection.
