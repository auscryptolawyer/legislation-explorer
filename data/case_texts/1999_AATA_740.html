REASONS FOR DECISION

7 October 1999 J. Block, Senior Member

1. The objection decisions in this matter are the disallowance by the Respondent of objections dated 1 May 1994 against notices of amended assessment issued on 31 March 1994 in respect of the years ending 30 June 1989 and 30 June 1990 (referred to individually and respectively as the "1989 year" and the "1990 year" and collectively as the "relevant years").

2. The Applicant appeared on his own behalf, while Mr K Connor of Counsel, instructed by Ms P. Hannigan of the Australian Government Solicitor's office appeared for the Respondent. The Tribunal received a tender of the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, together with a large number of exhibits. The Tribunal does not consider it necessary to set out all of the exhibits because, and as the matter developed, many of them proved in the result to be of little or no relevance; references are confined to those exhibits which were relevant.

3. In order to make these Reasons easier to read, while at the same time preserving their confidential nature, the Tribunal intends to use pseudonyms in respect of certain persons who gave evidence before the Tribunal or whose statements were accepted, because the Respondent did not require them for cross-examination. All excerpts from the transcripts have, to the extent necessary, been edited for this purpose. In addition the following abbreviations or definitions are used:

) "other proceedings" means certain legal proceedings in which the Applicant was involved some six years ago.

The Tribunal notes in this context that nearly two years ago (and on 5 December 1997) an application of an interlocutory nature was argued before it by the parties. The Respondent had issued a summons calling for certain documents referable to those other proceedings to be produced by a government body and a box of documents was produced in reference to that summons. However, the Applicant objected to their being made available to the Respondent by reason of certain provisions of a relevant Commonwealth statutory enactment. The matter was argued at some length and eventually a direction was made in a manner agreed by the parties. The statutory provision in question prevents disclosure in certain circumstances of certain information concerning a person without his or her consent. Even though the Applicant is not named in these Reasons, and they are written so as to preserve confidentiality, the Tribunal considers it desirable to confine references to those other proceedings to a minimum; it is for this reason also that the relevant statutory provision is referred to in general and not specific terms. However, those other proceedings constituted a starting point in respect of this matter; the transcript of those other proceedings (and certain other relevant documents) form part of the T Documents and some references to those other proceedings are relevant.

(b) "Company" means a corporation incorporated under the Associations Incorporation Act 1984 (Nsw); the Applicant was the chairperson until November 1988 when he resigned in order to become a project officer of the Company. There is some dispute on the evidence as to when precisely that employment began; the Applicant contends that it began in January 1989 whereas the Respondent contends that this may have occurred rather earlier than that, and perhaps in November 1988.

(c) "Department" refers to a government department which provided certain funding to the Company. (This aspect is dealt with more fully later in these Reasons).

(d) "Applicant Company" means a company owned and controlled by the Applicant and which was incorporated in November 1989. The business of the Applicant Company was, prior to its incorporation, conducted by the Applicant himself under a business name similar to that adopted in respect of the Applicant Company. That business originally related to the provision of financial services and in particular debt collections; at some time after its commencement, the business became focused on the hiring out of computers. The Applicant said that he had originally thought that the Applicant Company had been incorporated at an earlier date; that gave rise to a number of difficulties and misconceptions so far as the Applicant was concerned. The term "business" means the business referred to in this paragraph (d) which was originally conducted by the Applicant but was, as from November 1989, conducted by the Applicant Company.

(e) "solicitors" means a firm of solicitors by whom the Applicant was employed as a law clerk during a part of the relevant years. The Applicant's evidence was that as a law clerk he received a percentage of the fees billed in respect of work generated by him but that he was obliged to bear his own expenses in relation to the performance of that work.

(f) "Sydney Living Skills courses" or "SLS courses" or "courses" refers to five courses which were conducted during the relevant years (the first two in the 1989 year and the other three in the 1990 year, but all prior to the incorporation of the Applicant Company) by the Applicant. The first four courses were differentiated from the fifth in that the fifth was designed for unemployed persons from a different region and throughout the hearings that distinction was maintained. However, nothing much turns on that distinction and they are referred to in these Reasons simply as courses, and distinguished by the number referable to them. The courses were conducted in the order of their numbering.

(g) "lecturers" in respect of each course or "staff" means the persons, including the Applicant, his sister and others, who lectured to persons who attended the courses. The Applicant lectured in banking and finance while other subjects were taught by other lecturers.

(h) "students" in relation to each course means the unemployed persons who attended the courses; in respect of each course there were approximately 30 students.

(i) "Father" means a priest who furnished the statement which is Exhibit A14.

(j) "Larkin" means a female employee of the solicitors, who furnished the statement which is Exhibit A15.

(k) "Morgan" means the barrister, now retired, who furnished the statement which is Exhibit A16.

(l) "Jones" means a senior female employee of a major bank who prepared certain accounts and which were tendered as Exhibits A1, A5, A6, A7 and A8. Jones gave oral evidence before the Tribunal.

(m) "sister" means the lecturer who is the Applicant's sister and who gave oral evidence before the Tribunal.

(n) "Smith" means the female lecturer who gave evidence before the Tribunal.

(o) "Brown" means an accountant who prepared accounts and who gave oral evidence before the Tribunal.

(p) "Jackson" means an executive of the Company who gave oral evidence before the Tribunal.

4. ) The hearings before the Tribunal extended over a period of eight sitting days. The first five took place in July 1998; on the fifth day, and although this matter had been listed to be heard on further days, the Applicant sought a postponement to enable him to prepare in respect of certain matters and more particularly because issues had arisen as to which (so he contended) he was unprepared. That postponement was granted; at the same time a direction was given requiring the Respondent to furnish an Amended Statement of Facts and Contentions by not later than six weeks after 13 July 1998 and directing the Applicant to file a Statement of Facts and Contentions and all outstanding witness statements by not later than six weeks after the Respondent had filed its Amended Statement of Facts and Contentions.

(b) The Amended Statement of Facts and Contentions was filed by the Respondent, in respect of the 1989 year on 21 September 1998, and in respect of the 1990 year on 15 October 1998. The Applicant did not file his reply within the period of six weeks specified. Accordingly, a directions hearing took place on 26 February 1999 at the request of the Respondent. The Applicant advised the Tribunal that it was difficult for him to comply with his direction obligations, and that he sought an extension of time within which to do so until 31 May 1999; in the result, the Tribunal granted him an extension until 30 June 1999 within which to comply with his obligations in this regard. The Applicant did file his Statement of Facts and Contentions (referred to in the hearings as "Document 2") on 30 June 1999. The matter was again listed to be heard for six days in September, three days in the week commencing 6 September 1999 and three days in the week commencing 13 September 1999.

(c) At the end of the first two days in September 1999 the Tribunal was told that the parties were having discussions, and the third day was vacated. The first two hearing days in the subsequent week were vacated for similar reasons and the hearings eventually concluded on 17 September 1999.

(d) When the hearings recommenced in September 1999, the Respondent advised the Tribunal that the issues between the parties had been curtailed and narrowed down to some considerable extent. Those issues were further narrowed down prior to the final hearing day on 17 September 1999.

(e) It is for these reasons that the hearings fall neatly into two different periods and being the 1998 and 1999 hearings respectively. After the issues were narrowed prior to and during the course of the 1999 hearings, at least some of the evidence before the Tribunal, and whether in the form of the T Documents or otherwise, became redundant.

11. As regards the evidence in the 1998 hearings and including evidence in exhibits and the T Documents:

) Jones accepted that the accounts first produced by her (Exhibit A1) were incorrect for a number of reasons. She went on to do her best to rectify them and her rectified accounts for courses 1 to 4 (inclusive) are Exhibits A5 to A8 (inclusive).

(b) In view of later developments in this matter it was unnecessary for me to give any further consideration to the accounts prepared by Brown. His accounts were, it may be noted, inconsistent with those prepared by Jones. One example only will suffice to illustrate the point. In respect of one course, work commenced before the Department finance was received. Accordingly, seed funding was provided by the Company which was later refunded to the Company when the Department funding was received. Brown, however, believed that the seed funding was provided by the Applicant and his accounts were prepared accordingly.

(c) Again in view of later developments it became unnecessary for me to consider yet another set of accounts prepared by a leading firm of accountants and commissioned by the Respondent, and which were included in the T Documents.

(d) The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant paid for all expenses of whatever nature incurred by the students and the lecturers in respect of the courses at all times and both prior to the stay in Sydney, and in Sydney itself. Expenses funded included not only living expenses such as board and food in Sydney but also the cost of numerous excursions (said to be of a bonding nature) and including canoeing, ten-pin bowling and the like. This was said to be calculated to give the students both tuition and also entertainment and all with a view to inculcating a sense of self-confidence and self-worth in them. The expenses funded often included items of a personal nature and including clothing, the cost of hair cuts or hair-dos, and other such grooming.

(e) The Tribunal accepts that however extraordinary in nature these courses may have been, they achieved some considerable degree of success in that an appreciable proportion of the students did in the result obtain employment.

(f) The expenses funded included expenses of both staff and lecturers. In fact, and as subsequent evidence revealed, some items in respect of the lecturers were incurred without cost; this is so in particular in relation to the hotels and boarding houses in Sydney in which the groups stayed. The rate paid included free accommodation for the Applicant and his staff.

(g) Put in summary form neither the lecturers, and including the Applicant, nor the students had to put their hands into their own pockets for anything at all; everything was funded by the Applicant out of funds provided by the Department.

(h) The contractual evidence furnished (and see for example Exhibit R4) indicated that for each course the Department provided the funding to the Company who was represented by the Applicant; indeed, the contract was signed by the Applicant in the following terms:

"On behalf of the Company I accept the funding from the Commonwealth Government through the Department on the same terms and conditions set out in this agreement.

Signed: [Applicant] Date: 10/2/89

Name: [Applicant] Position: Manager"

(i) The acquittals furnished to the Department were plainly fictional. They simply corresponded with the budgets which were themselves cast in very broad terms. A global item of say $5,000 would be budgeted for a category of expense and the acquittal would refer to the same category and the same amount, ie. $5,000.

(j) Although the Applicant was a senior employee (project officer) of the Company in respect of its work generally, he was in control for all practical purposes of all aspects of each course. He was either the sole signatory or one of two signatories but with power to sign on his own in respect of all accounts referable to all courses. For all intents and purposes, the Applicant ran, conducted and governed each course as if it were his own project.

(k) Each and every set of accounts, whether prepared by Jones, Brown or otherwise was, of necessity, a reconstruction and based to a large extent on information provided by the Applicant.

(l) The manner in which the Applicant dealt with money provided by the Department was simply bizarre. Moneys came in; those moneys were sometimes converted into cash in whole or in part. Payments were made to organisations (such as Kitchen Company) on behalf of the Applicant; other payments in turn were made to third parties on his behalf. Substantial amounts of cash were employed. It is no wonder then that to reconcile the various sets of accounts proved to be extraordinarily difficult. It is also understandable that in the circumstances the Applicant's barrister in the other proceedings used descriptive terms such as 'cavalier' and 'debris' to describe the Applicant's activities.

(m) The Tribunal pauses at this point in order to note that it is not (as was made clear to the Applicant at the hearings) concerned with the extent of his expenditure on the courses. If moneys were expended on entertainment, no matter how lavish, those moneys were for the Tribunal's purposes not moneys which the Applicant received and converted to his own use. There was evidence as to expenditure in respect of one course on travel expenses referable to a trip to Thailand. In the result it became unnecessary for the Tribunal, in the light of later developments, to be concerned with this and certain other related expenditure.

(n) As set out previously the acquittals were fictional. It is doubtful whether the Department maintained proper control over the funds it made available for the purpose of the courses. The role played by the Company in relation to the courses is also open to criticism. However, these aspects do not directly concern the Tribunal.

(o) It is clear that in relation to the courses the Company left the Applicant to deal with them at his discretion. The evidence of Jackson as to acquittals, fringe benefits tax and the like was not of assistance. It is relevant to note that he was not even aware of the fact that the course contract with the Department required (as one would expect) an acquittal and an accounting for the excess.

(p) The evidence of the lecturers and the student was acceptable so far as it went but it was, understandably enough after so long a time, often vague and frequently couched in terms which indicated that the witness simply did not remember precisely what had occurred. In any event it did no more than emphasise that the Applicant, who was in control, bore all expenses.

(q) The Applicant's outburst as to a conspiracy and including an allegation that a government department took the books and records and did not ever return them is simply not credible. There were no proper books and records as was indicated by the Applicant's own barrister in the other proceedings. At a later stage, on the final day of the 1999 hearings, the Applicant repeated his allegations as to a conspiracy and that the government department had taken and not returned his records.

(r) The Applicant was generous in relation to his students and staff and perhaps that generosity to them was, in some respects, advantageous to the students. He may perhaps have been generous also to himself having regard to the amounts taken by him or on his behalf although it would seem that the Applicant did expend considerable time and effort on the courses.

(s) The evidence as to living away from home allowances and allegations as to hearsay statements that the Tax Department had confirmed that they were exempt cannot be accepted, more particularly in the light of the fact that section 51A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("ITAA") was repealed in 1986 when fringe benefit tax was introduced.

(2) Section 82KZAA(1) of ITAA sets out the circumstances in which relief from the substantiation requirements may be available. Section 82KZAA(4) of ITAA makes it clear that the relief provisions can operate retroactively since the section applies to expenses incurred before, on or after 8 January 1991. However, the Respondent is required to have regard for the purposes of section 82KZAA of ITAA (and see 82KZAA(1)(b) of ITAA) to:

() The nature and quality of the evidence available to substantiate the taxpayer's claim; and

(B) Any special circumstances affecting the taxpayer - these include, but are not limited to, the extent to which the taxpayer attempted to comply with the substantiation requirements and whether the failure to do was inadvertent or deliberate.

(g) Section 82KZAA is a provision which is designed to neutralise to some extent the harsh results which may follow from a strict application of the substantiation provisions. The specific concern is that the substantiation provisions should not be allowed to operate so harshly as to deny a deduction in exceptional individual circumstances where it is found that, having regard to all available documentary and other evidence, it would in all the circumstances be unreasonable for the provisions to have that outcome. In determining whether to exercise the discretion, the extent to which the taxpayer has attempted to comply with the substantiation provisions will be a relevant consideration. See in this context the comments of Deputy President McMahon in Case 13/96, 96 ATC 210 (at 212 - 213):

"Relief may be obtained from those requirements in special circumstances through the provisions of s 82KZAA. This was considered in Case 2/95, 95 ATC 107 where the applicant persuaded the Tribunal to exercise the discretion in his favour. The most significant discretionary reason for this decision appears to have been that the applicant was misled by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office who is said to have advised the applicant that keeping a log book record of his trips would be a sufficient compliance. That circumstance is not present here. Furthermore the applicant has made no attempt to comply with the substantiation sections during the course of the year prior to filing his return of income. This is a circumstance that must be taken into account pursuant to s 82KZAA(1)(ii)(A). In my view, the applicant has not put forward any reason to show that it would be unreasonable for the substantiation sections to apply in relation to the expenses claimed by him."

The taxpayer in Case 2/95 (cited in Case 13/96 supra) who successfully invoked the discretionary provisions of section 82KZAA of ITAA maintained a detailed log book record of his trips.

See also the comments of Deputy President Forrest in Case 9/96, 96 ATC 186 as follows (at 192):

"I accept that the taxpayer has attempted to comply with the substantiation requirements by providing a diary with details of the dates, locations and cost of meals for which a deduction is claimed. These dates coincide with the log book entries which he has signed recording each journey. The failure to strictly comply with the substantiation provision was inadvertent and not deliberate and I accept that he had a reasonably held belief that he was generally acting in accordance with the substantiation provisions, having been subject to an audit previously and the respondent being, at that time, satisfied with a diary maintained in the same format.

...

Having regard to the foregoing matters and taking into account the degree of compliance with the substantiation provisions evidenced by the details supplied, it would in my opinion be unreasonable for the substantiation requirements to apply in relation to the meal expenses."

(h) The Respondent has stated in Taxation Ruling IT 2645 (which is of course not a binding ruling) that each case has to be considered on its merits and that a common-sense approach should be applied in determining whether to grant relief under section 82KZAA. For example, failure to complete all details in a motor vehicle log book where the omissions are limited and the relevant details are manifestly obvious from the taxpayer's activities (eg. a surgeon who travels from his or her practice to hospital every day), or failure to sign entries in a log book, may not result in the relevant deduction being disallowed. However, the ruling also notes that it is consistent with the terms of the substantiation requirements that no relief would be available in respect of a claim for which there is no supporting documentation or factual material evidencing the expense in question.

20. I turn next to deal with the claims made in respect of the living away from home allowance. In this regard:

) The allowances were claimed to have been exempt in the hands of the Applicant. The Applicant said that he had received advice to this effect from a Tax Office representative. As set out previously section 51A of ITAA was repealed in 1986.

(b) There was no evidence before me that the Company reflected the relevant amounts as fringe benefits and indeed the evidence of Jackson was that this aspect had not been considered. It must be remembered that in respect of each course all of the financial aspects were administered by the Applicant. The Company did not apparently play any part at all in the courses even though it was (however nominally) the contracting party in relation to the courses with the Department. (The Applicant said at one point that the Company was exempt but did not specify how or why this might be so).

(c) Section 30 of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 ("FBTAA") reads as follows:

"Section 30 Living-away-from-home allowance benefits

30 (1) [Provision of benefit] Where:

(a) at a particular time, in respect of the employment of an employee of an employer, the employer pays an allowance to the employee; and

(b) it would be concluded that the whole or a part of the allowance is in the nature of compensation to the employee for:

(i) additional expenses (not being deductible expenses) incurred by the employee during a period; or

(ii) additional expenses (not being deductible expenses) incurred by the employee, and other additional disadvantages to which the employee is subject, during a period;

by reason that the employee is required to live away from his or her usual place of residence in order to perform the duties of that employment;

the payment of the whole, or of the part, as the case may be, of the allowance constitutes a benefit provided by the employer to the employee at that time."

(d) In Case Y40 91 ATC 393 it was held that the requirements of section 30 were as follows (at 369 per Gerber DP):

" ... it is a condition precedent that the employee is required to live away from his/her usual place of residence in order to perform the duties of the employment and the allowance must be wholly or partly compensation for the additional (non-deductible) expenses and/or additional disadvantages incurred during the relevant period."

(e) In Roads and Traffic Authority of New South Wales v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 314; (1993) 116 ALR 482, Hill J considered whether an amount paid to employees for staying in a camp was a living away from home allowance. The employees were in camp for varying lengths of time (some stayed for lengthy periods while others stayed for short periods). The decision turned on the purpose of the payment. It was held that it was a living away from home allowance, as it was an amount to compensate for the disadvantageous conditions of living in a camp and to compensate employees for the additional costs of food beyond the cost of living in their own homes and perhaps for other expenses caused to them by camping.

(f) In Atwood Oceanics Australia Pty Ltd v Federal Commission of Taxation 89 ATC 4808, Lee J stated that it was a requirement that the circumstances of payment of the allowance be such that a reasonable person would conclude, applying an objective view, that the allowance bore the character described in section 30(1)(b). Furthermore, it is necessary to determine the underlying purpose of the allowance and to which end regard may be had to any award pursuant to which the allowance is paid, and the history of that award. His Honour also made the following relevant observations (at 4816 and 4818):

"In stipulating that the additional expenses and disadvantages be incurred or suffered by reason of the employee being required to live away from his or her usual place of residence to perform the duties of employment, the Act required demonstration of some causal connection between the likelihood of additional expenses and disadvantages and the requirement to live away from home for a period.

...

The proper conclusion must be that the legislature intended that an allowance paid by an employer to compensate an employee for intangible disadvantages of employment required to be performed away from the usual place of residence, and to make such employment more attractive to the employee would not be a fringe benefit assessable under the Act, unless such compensation was a composite payment which included compensation for additional expenditure likely to result from the requirement that the employee live away from home for any period."

(g) The distinction drawn between travel allowances and living away from home allowances may assist a decision as to the correct characterisation of the payments. Taxation Ruling MT 2030 provides some guidance in this respect. Clause 41 indicates that as a practical general rule where the period away does not exceed 21 days, the allowance will be treated as a travelling allowance rather than as a living away from home allowance. The evidence before me was that in respect of each course the period in Sydney totalled about 17 days although there was, at least for the lecturers (or some of them), a 'clearing up' period at some point in time thereafter. The period specified in MT 2030 is of course as indicated, a guide only, and cannot be regarded as being in any way determinative.

(h) It must be remembered that section 30 of FBTAA in its terms provides that a living away from home allowance is designed specifically and categorically to cater for expenses to be incurred by the employee concerned. The evidence was that all of the staff (and including the Applicant himself), paid no expenses of any kind and that every expense whether or not of a personal nature was borne out of course moneys. This evidence was given (on various occasions) by the Applicant himself, by one of his students and by certain lecturers. In respect of the hotels and boarding houses in Sydney, and as set out previously, the Applicant and his staff incurred no expenses.

(i) In any event a stay in a hotel or boarding house in Sydney cannot be regarded as a disadvantageous place at which to stay and is not comparable with the camp which was relevant in the Roads & Traffic Authority case (supra).

(j) I conclude then that the Applicant categorised payments to himself (and also members of his staff) in this fashion purely because he considered that they would then be exempt from tax (although there was no basis whatever for that conclusion) and because he expected thereby to derive a tax advantage and to which he (and the other lecturers) were not entitled. If it was properly categorised as an allowance of some kind (and this is doubtful), it must nevertheless be caught by section 26(e) of ITAA.

(b) In Commissioner of Taxation v Munro 97 ATC 5041 Lockhart J, at page 5049 said (and I acknowledge my indebtedness to Mr Connor's written submission in this regard):

"PRINCIPLES RELATING TO ONUS OF PROOF

The taxpayer has the burden of proving that the amended assessment is excessive (s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act). That section is expressed in substantially the same language as s 190(b) of the Assessment Act which was repealed by Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No 3) 1991 (Cth).

In McCormack v Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 411; [1979] HCA 18; (1978-1979) 143 CLR 284 Gibbs J said that s 190(b) of the Assessment Act placed the burden of proving that the assessment was excessive upon the taxpayer and that on ordinary principles the taxpayer therefore bore the burden of proving the facts necessary to make out his or her case (at ATC 4120; CLR 301). To discharge the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive the taxpayer in McCormack (a s 26(a) case) was required to prove affirmatively on the balance of probabilities that the property was not acquired for the purpose of profit making by sale. His Honour said that the burden may be discharged by drawing inferences from the evidence, but he rejected (at ATC 4121; CLR 303) the argument that a taxpayer can succeed simply because there is no evidence from which it can be concluded that the relevant purpose of profit making existed; since this would mean that the burden of proving the existence of that purpose lies on the Commission. That would, his Honour said, be to invert the onus of proof. His Honour also said at ATC 4121; CLR 303:

"... The taxpayer will succeed if the proper inference from the evidence is that the property was not acquired for the relevant purpose, but if there is no evidence as to the purpose for which the taxpayer acquired the property, the appeal must fail."

Gibbs J agreed with the statement of Mason J a few years earlier in his dissenting judgment in Gauci and ors v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4257 at 4261; [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 CLR 81 at 89-90 in these terms:

"The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessment should be sustained or supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with s190(b) for it is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.

...

The crux of the matter is that when in a s26(a) case an appellant seeks to overcome the onus created by s190(b) by adducing evidence as to his intentions with a view to establishing the purpose of the acquisition was not a s26(a) purpose and that evidence is not accepted, he has not discharged the onus which he bears. At best, from the appellant's viewpoint, the evidence stands in a situation in which it is equivocal, neither establishing a s26(a) purpose nor denying the existence of such a purpose. At worst, the judge may, in the circumstances, be able to infer the existence of a s26(a) purpose. In either event the appellant fails to discharge the onus and the appeal fails."

Stephen J agreed with what was said on this matter by Gibbs J and he too endorsed the statement of Mason J in Gauci (at ATC 4123; CLR 306).

In MacMine Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4133; (1979) 53 ALJR 362 Gibbs J confirmed the views expressed in McCormack by him about the onus of proof which lies upon a taxpayer by virtue of s190(b) of the Assessment Act (at ATC 4139; ALJR 366).

Stephen J confirmed his agreement in McCormack with what Gibbs J said on the subject of s190(b)(at ATC 4146; ALJR 371).

Murphy J said (at ATC 4156; ALJR 378) that s190(b) places on the taxpayer the burden of showing that the assessment is excessive and therefore that the circumstances giving rise to liability to such assessment did not exist. His Honour said that:

"... The plain intention of Parliament is to raise a presumption of the existence of the circumstances which attract liability to the tax assessed."

In McCauley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4605, Lockhart J at 4613 stated:

"... It is for the taxpayer to prove that the assessment is excessive and the onus lies upon the taxpayer to prove that funds to acquire the assets in question were not derived from undisclosed income. The source of these funds is peculiarly within the knowledge of the taxpayer."

In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco 90 ATC 4,088 the views expressed in McCormack's Case were followed by the Full Court of the High Court. Brennan J said at 4093 that ordinarily:

"... The Commissioner is entitled to rely upon any deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the amount assessed to uphold the assessment, ..."

(c) It was in fact the Respondent who attempted to reconcile the figures. Although that attempted reconciliation did not constitute an attempt on the part of the Respondent to make out a case, Galea v FC of T 90 ATC 5060 indicates that even where the Commissioner attempts to establish a case and fails to do so, the Commissioner is nonetheless entitled to rely on the fact that the onus of proof is on the taxpayer. See Hill J at 5067 as follows:

"To the extent that the applicant seeks to rely upon the description of what the Commissioner did here as being an attempt to mount a positive case, it is not clear to me at all why this has any relevance. As is clear from Dalco (supra) and as the Tribunal itself said, it was not necessary for the Commissioner to seek to establish affirmatively that the applicant's assessable income was at least a particular figure. The fact that the Commissioner sought so to do and failed has no bearing, at the end of the day, on the question whether the applicant has discharged the onus of showing, as he is required by sec. 190(b) of the Act to show, that the assessment is excessive. The Commissioner's failure to establish a positive case, if that is what he sought to do, leaves the Tribunal in no different position than it would have been in if the Commissioner had not sought at all to advance a positive case."

27. ) The Applicant appeared in person. He was treated as a self-represented Applicant, and granted (as the transcript indicates) numerous indulgences and this notwithstanding the fact that he does have some legal experience.

(b) The Applicant was advised on a number of occasions during the hearing as to the nature of the onus on him. His attention was drawn in particular to the decisions in Munro and Dalco (referred to in Munro). He was reminded on various occasions that he is obliged not only to establish that the amended assessments issued by the Respondent were incorrect, but also to establish what the correct assessments would be. The Tribunal recommended to him that he seek legal advice and indeed he said that he had obtained assistance from Mr Hamilton of Counsel. At the end of the hearings, the Applicant was allowed a period, until 30 September 1999, within which to furnish further submissions on the law by Mr Hamilton or any other qualified practitioner; further submissions were received on 30 September 1999; the further submissions, which are some five pages in length, cannot be categorised as submissions as to the law. They are moreover couched in terms which must lead me to infer that they were prepared by the Applicant himself. I note in this context that I afforded this concession to the Applicant (and as the Applicant well knew) not in order to give him a further or additional right of reply, but to give him the opportunity to obtain assistance from Mr Hamilton or, if the Applicant so chose, any other qualified practitioner. The further submissions in question do not, in my view and in any event, advance the Applicant's case in any way.

(c) It must be noted that Mr Connor and Ms Hannigan treated the Applicant throughout the hearings with singular consideration. The Respondent made a number of concessions and notwithstanding that the Applicant had produced no evidence whatever in support of certain of his claims. The concession in respect of the replacement path is just one example. There was no evidence before the Tribunal as to whether the path cost was in the nature of a repair or a renovation or in fact as to the amount involved. In the absence of evidence, that deduction claim would not have been allowed. Nevertheless the Respondent conceded it. On a number of occasions the Respondent agreed by way of assistance to the Applicant to follow a procedure which differed from the normal order.

(d) That the Applicant's courses produced some favourable results is not in dispute and is to the Applicant's credit. The Applicant does, in the view of the Tribunal, have ability. He taught banking and finance to his students in the courses. Quite what is meant by this is not clear to the Tribunal. His methods of accountancy in respect of moneys received for the courses were simply chaotic. The absence of any relevant books and records inevitably had the effect that the Applicant would find it difficult to discharge the onus on him. He must have known or ought to have known that this was so. But nevertheless these hearings went on for eight sitting days over a period of fifteen months and generated an enormous quantity of evidence.

(e) The Applicant's allegations as to a conspiracy against him were without foundation. Some at least of his evidence was untrue. He knew perfectly well that he had not kept proper (or probably any) accounts. He produced accounting evidence which he must have known was incorrect. He knew that the seed capital for course 1 was furnished by the Company and not by him. The original accounts by Jones were demonstrably incorrect; he must have known that he received substantial sums from the courses on which tax was not paid, and indeed towards the end of the hearings he admitted that this was so in respect of at least some of the moneys. I accept his evidence that he used his car at least in part in connection with his various occupations. But at the same time, he received car expenses from the Company and, in all likelihood, through the courses. He said that he left his tax affairs to his accountant; one wonders then why the accountant was not called before the Tribunal. Similarly, one wonders why there was no evidence by the Company as to amounts paid to him and whether in respect of project officer wages or in any other respects. Likewise, if the Company considered that certain payments were living away from home allowances and as such fringe benefits, one would have expected there to have been evidence by the Company to this effect. The conduct of the Applicant in relation to the courses was characterised by his own barrister in the other proceedings as a little "cavalier". I consider that that is too mild a term. The evidence indicated that for any given course a large amount was received. That amount might then be converted (or part of it might be converted) into cash. The Applicant then proceeded to spend those funds, and including the cash, in an extravagant manner. Moneys were spent on excursions for the students and in respect of their own individual and personal needs. Payments were made directly to the Applicant's creditors. And for all of this there are no records. The Applicant commenced by calling one witness after another who could say only that he (the Applicant) paid for everything. And at a later stage the evidence produced by him as to car expenses was cast in such broad terms that it was of little or no probative value. The Applicant is undoubtedly determined. He conducted this matter with tenacity, and which resulted in the actual hearings commencing in the middle of 1998 and concluding in September 1999 in relation to objection decisions which issued in 1994. The Applicant in summary could not and did not discharge the onus on him, and indeed his attempts to do so (such as they were) fell a very long way short of what was required.

28. ) It is to be noted that in respect of the 1989 year, the final 1989 statement indicates that the amended assessment was issued on the basis of taxable income of $65,354.46. The actual taxable income as calculated by the Respondent was $78,274.69. It follows that even if I had allowed all of the disputed deduction items for that year, the amended assessment would still not have been reduced.

(b) In respect of the 1990 year the final statement indicates that the amended assessment was excessive by $1,317.52. However the Respondent noted that that calculation was incorrect in that it did not take into account the computer hire payment of $13,000 made by the Company to the Applicant (and as to which see clause 21(k)) and so that the amount assessed was still significantly below the amount which could have been assessed. The assessment in respect of the 1990 year cannot therefore be disturbed.

(c) In respect of each relevant year the Respondent towards the end of the hearing on the final day made a concession, and notwithstanding that the Applicant did not make any submissions whatever as regards additional tax. In accordance with that concession the Respondent consented to a reduction of the additional tax for incorrect return (but not the additional tax for late return) in such manner that the rate at which it was calculated be reduced from 50% to 30%. Although I would not have been inclined, in the absence of that concession, to grant it, it was made and accordingly (although not without some hesitation) I propose to allow it.

29. Excepting only that the rate of additional tax for incorrect return is reduced in respect of each of the 1989 and 1990 year from 50% to 30%, the objection decisions under review are thus affirmed.
