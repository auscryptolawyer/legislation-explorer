Re John Richard Lennard and Commissioner of Superannuation [1988] AATA 252 (12 August 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: JOHN RICHARD LENNARD

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. T88/1

AAT No.4552

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.C. Jennnings QC (Deputy President)

Dr. A.P. Renouf (Senior Member)

Professor R.W. Webster (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - whether eligible employee unfit to perform duties - discretion to deem retirement on ground of physical or mental incapacity - moral entitlement - relevance of settled damages claim relating to abolition of statutory office.

Superannuation Act 1976 - s.7(2), 58, 59, 154

Australian Electoral Office Act 1973 (repealed)

Commonwealth Electoral Legislation Amendment Act 1983 - s.150

Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918 - s.7Z, 7P(1), s.31

HEARING

HOBART

12:8:1988

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and the matter remitted to the respondent with a direction -

(1) that the applicant should be deemed to

have been retired on the ground of

physical and mental incapacity; and

(2) that appropriate provision should be made

for benefits to the applicant under the

Superannuation Act 1976.

There will be liberty to apply.

DECISION

The applicant seeks to review a decision by a delegate of the respondent made on 14 December 1987 whereby he confirmed the decision of another delegate on 17 December 1986 refusing a request that termination of his employment with the Australian Electoral Office on 29 February 1984 be deemed pursuant to s.7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") to have been a retirement on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.

2. Jurisdiction to entertain the application is conferred upon this Tribunal by s.154 of the Act. The Tribunal was constituted in accordance with s.154(7). At the hearing the T documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were received without obligation together with other material which will be referred to in so far as it is relevant to this decision. The applicant, his wife and his general practitioner Dr. Pitt gave evidence as did four witnesses for the respondent.

3. The applicant who was born on 30 January 1926 became a state public servant in 1954. He became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act when he was appointed on 25 March 1974 as an Australian Electoral officer under the Australian Electoral Office Act 1973. His term of office expired after 7 years, but he was reappointed on 23 March 1981 as Australian Electoral Officer for Tasmania (AEOT) for a further 7 year period.

4. For reasons which were not revealed his period of contributory service for the purposes of the Act began sometime in 1958.

5. The Australian Electoral Office Act 1973 was repealed by s.150 of the Commonwealth Electoral Legislation Amendment Act 1983 the date of commencement of which was 21 February 1984. On that date the applicant therefore ceased to be AEOT, but he was appointed on that day pursuant to s.7Z of the Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918 (as amended by the 1983 Act) as Assistant Australian Electoral Officer for Tasmania (AAEOT).

6. The Australian Electoral Commission which came into being on 21 February 1984 thereupon engaged the applicant as a member of its senior executive staff, but the engagement was expressed to cease when an appointment was made pursuant to s.7P(1) of the 1918 Act (as amended) to the office of AEOT.

7. Section 7Z provided that an AAEO should act as AEO during any vacancy in the office whether or not an appointment had previously been made to the office. By reason of renumbering s.7Z now appears as s.31 of the Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918.

8. On 1 March 1984 another person was appointed as AEOT and accordingly the applicant ceased to be engaged as AAEOT on 29 February 1984.

9. The consequence of these events for the applicant was that he ceased to be an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act and was deemed to have retired involuntarily. He was as at 29 February 1984 the holder of a statutory office and the term of his appointment to it (one week) expired. He was not reappointed although he desired to be. Section 58(3)(c) was accordingly applicable to him.

10. He was therefore treated as entitled to early retirement benefits in accordance with the provisions of s.59 of the Act.

11. The applicant had sought selection as AEO for every State of Australia, but despite an interview about 16 February 1984 by a Committee appointed by the Special Minister of State, he was excluded from a short list of applicants for all positions.

12. The applicant's immediate reaction to the news that he was no longer employed as AEO for Tasmania was to maintain that he was merely on leave, that the appointments made by the Commission on 1 March were not legal and that he was entitled to a further 4 years service. He consulted his union and was advised to make an application to the Federal Court. The precise nature of those proceedings was not disclosed, although in ministerial correspondence in July 1984 reference was made to a claim for compensation in respect of the premature termination of his appointment.

13. On 3 July 1984 the Minister for Finance wrote to the Special Minister for State indicating that subject to withdrawal of legal proceedings against the Commonwealth an act of grace payment based on an amount equal to 12 months salary (based on the figure he was receiving in February 1984) would be paid to him.

14. A document entitled "Terms of Settlement" was filed in the Federal Court on 14 November 1984 recording that an agreement to this effect had been made "in consequence of the early termination of the office held by the applicant by the passing of the Commonwealth Electoral Legislation Amendment Act 1983 and by the consequential redundancy of the applicant". The action was accordingly dismissed by consent. The sum payable was stated to be $36,600 with no order as to costs.

15. On 20 June 1985 the applicant requested by letter to the respondent that his "retirement for superannuation purposes be reviewed with the purpose of being classified as being on the grounds of ill health". His attention was then drawn to the provisions of s.7(2) of the Act and he supplied material in support of his claim.

16. Section 7 of the Act provides as follows:-

"7.(1) A reference in this Act to the retirement of an

eligible employee on the ground of invalidity shall be

read as a reference to his having been retired, or his

services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground

of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.

(2) Where a person has, before attaining his maximum

retiring age, ceased to be an eligible employee

otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his

having been retired, or his services having been

otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or

mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the

Commissioner is satisfied, that, at the time he ceased

to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason of

physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his

duties, the Commissioner may direct that the person

shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have

been retired on the ground of that physical or mental

incapacity."

17. Both the request and the reconsideration of it addressed one question only, namely whether the respondent was satisfied on the material before him that he was on 29 February 1984 by reason of physical or mental incapacity unfit to perform his duties. Both delegates were not so satisfied.

18. The Tribunal is required to determine that question and if it finds that it is so satisfied it must decide whether or not in its discretion there should be a direction in accordance with the latter part of the subsection.

19. That the power of the respondent to make a direction is discretionary has been clear since the decision of a full Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (1986) 70 ALR 625.

20. For about 10 years the applicant was the principal Commonwealth officer in charge of federal elections in Tasmania. It would appear to be common ground that for about 6 years that whatever health problems he may have had they in no way affected his capacity to discharge his duties competently and efficiently.

21. However, there is a detailed history of heart pains, hiatus hernia, hypertension and anxiety since 1978 or 1979. It is not helpful to analyse in detail the nature and extent of his physical and mental incapacity from time to time during the ensuing five years because the opinions expressed in medical reports were obtained for different purposes.

22. Moreover the applicant is a war veteran who has a long history of claims for various disabilities arising from war service including pulmonary tuberculosis, hypertension, myocardial ischaemia, depressive neurosis, cerebral arteriosclerosis and hiatus hernia. Some have been allowed and others refused, but allowed on review. The issues which the Repatriation Board or Commission were required to determine from time to time (both before and since the relevant date) tend to cloud the issue which is now before this Tribunal.

23. Furthermore in August 1980 the applicant sought retirement from his employment on invalidity grounds and was examined by several doctors, but he was found by them to be fit for continued employment. It serves no purpose to analyse whether that was the right decision.

24. Our approach to the problem of deciding whether or not the applicant was unfit to discharge his duties in February 1984 by reason of a mental incapacity has been to consider first the evidence which is suggestive of unfitness at that time and then to determine probable causes for that unfitness.

Fitness for Duties

25. When the applicant was interviewed by the then first Assistant Secretary of the Department of the Special Minister for State and an Assistant Commissioner of the Public Service Board, they formed the opinion that they had strong reservations about his ability to satisfactorily perform the functions of AEO. This was despite their knowledge of his prior experience and appropriate academic qualifications including 10 years as AEO for Tasmania. They were also satisfied he had a "reasonably detailed understanding of recent electoral reforms".

26. The reasons for their reservations were clearly: (1) His unimpressive performance at interview; and (2) an opinion expressed by the Chief Australian Electoral Officer (his only superior) that his performance had seriously deteriorated during the preceding 3 years and that he had caused concern at times by irrational responses to stress situations.

27. One member of that committee gave evidence before the Tribunal and we were thus satisfied that the committee's reasons for rejecting the application for appointment were very seriously considered and soundly based.

28. After hearing the applicant as a witness at length and reading a detailed report by a psychiatrist in September 1980, we have no difficulty in appreciating the reservations felt by the interview committee in 1984.

29. Fitness to perform the duties of the Chief Electoral Officer for Tasmania, or any other State, obviously requires a number of qualities - quite apart from knowledge of the legislation and administrative ability.

30. The duties of the office of AAEO which the applicant held on 19 February 1984 involved an extension of the duties he had held under the repealed legislation.

31. One of the functions of the new Commission is:-

"S.7(1)(c) to promote public awareness of electoral and

Parliamentary matters by means of the conduct of

education and information programs and by other

means."

32. It is reasonable to anticipate that the staff of the Commission (including of course Electoral officers) would be required to implement the policy and decisions of the Commission in performance of this function.

33. An examination of other amendments effected by the 1983 legislation leaves no doubt that it was incumbent on the government which had responsibilities for implementing the reforms contained in that legislation to appoint only persons fit to perform the duties required of them. The duties and responsibilities of chief electoral officers were similar, but not identical to those which they had carried during the preceding 10 years. It is not unreasonable to infer that because they were new they would require performance by persons readily adaptable to change.

34. The question then arises as to whether the applicant's seriously doubted ability arose by reason of mental or physical incapacity.

35. In February 1983 the Fraser government called a "snap election". The announcement was on Thursday 4 February and the electoral rolls closed the next day. The election was a month later. The applicant said the pressure of that event made him realise that he was "over the hill", i.e someone whose former ability to cope well under stress no longer existed.

36. In August 1983 he took a week's sick leave with anginal pains. In October-November he was away for a fortnight with a medical certificate marked "depressive neurosis" (by this time a recognised war-caused disability) and on 5 December 1983 he was admitted to hospital for a week for investigation into anginal pains. He took recreation leave in January and returned in February to pursue his application for re-appointment under the new legislation.

37. We are quite satisfied that by 1984 the applicant was a very worried man due to constant ill-health which had been aggravated in more recent years by rectal bleeding. We also accept that he believed that he might have the same kind of cancer as a colleague at work had developed with terminal consequences.

38. Whether or not he was justified in being concerned about the extent of rectal bleeding is immaterial. In actual fact it was not cancer, but it was so serious that he was subsequently very ill when his true condition was discovered. The significance of the bleeding for present purposes is that it was yet another physical factor to explain the development of a mental incapacity for work.

39. We accept that the deterioration noted in his work performance over several years was due to an aggravation of physical complaints which produced a depressive neurosis and anxiety. He was thus rendered a person who was unable to meet the stressful challenges which face State Electoral officers especially whenever a federal election or referendum is called.

40. The respondent both at the time of the decisions under review and at the hearing before the Tribunal relied on the fact that the applicant sought reappointment and maintained his ability to return to duties for some time after his employment was terminated. That attitude was said to evidence his fitness.

41. On the contrary our own observation and findings as to his actual condition at the time and the evidence of Dr. Pitt satisfy us that he had quite a false impression of his own abilities. The manner in which he responded to the events of February 1984 was in itself irrational. The letters which he subsequently wrote are themselves testimony to those conclusions.

42. Accordingly we are satisfied that the applicant was at the relevant time unfit to perform his duties by reason of physical and mental incapacity.

43. The broad test for determining whether we should direct that he should be deemed to have retired on that ground has been described by the Federal Court as one of moral entitlement to be determined on the merits of the individual case. (see Hastings supra)

44. In Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation (unreported - 23 December 1987) the majority decision of Beaumont & Gummow JJ referred to matters which might constitute special circumstances justifying a refusal to exercise the discretion given by s.7(2). They said at page 19:-

"We accept that the existence of special circumstances

may justify a refusal to exercise the discretion given

by s.7(2). That is to say, we accept that, even if an

employee can demonstrate that he ceased employment on

invalidity grounds, a residual discretion remains in the

Commissioner to decline to give the statutory direction.

If, for instance, Mr. Scott had held an adequate

understanding of what was involved in dealing with his

several pension entitlements or if he had been guilty of

unreasonable delay in seeking invalidity benefits, it

may have been proper that no direction be given under

s.7(2)."

45. Applying those remarks to the present case there is no doubt that the applicant failed to realise that he could seek the application of s.7(2) of the Act to his case. If we thought that he deliberately pursued his claim for damages to a conclusion before turning his attention to s.7, we might be disposed to reject his claim for the exercise of discretion. There is no material before us to warrant the assumption that he was aware of his rights or that he postponed exercising them until he achieved "a settlement" of his claim which was made in the context of his quite erroneous belief that he should never have rejected.

46. The delay which occurred between November 1984 and June 1985 is also explicable and warrants no finding that it was unreasonable.

47. It becomes necessary in this context to refer briefly to the history of his claim for war pension.

48. In May 1980 a Repatriation Board found that certain conditions (myocardial ischaemia and cerebral arteriosclerosis) were not service-related. In April 1984 that decision was reversed with effect from 15 May 1983, being six months prior to the date of his appeal against the 1980 decision. The degree of incapacity from these conditions remained to be assessed. Another Board decided on 8 August 1984 that the Temporarily Totally Incapacitated Rate should now apply subject to review in January 1985. It decided that this rate should be effective from 29 February 1984, the day he ceased work.

49. On 17 May 1985, following the January review, the TTI Rate was further extended to January 1986.

50. It is reasonable to assume, although the applicant was himself unable to assign any reason for his failure to write to the respondent until June 1985, that he was preoccupied with the repatriation claim during the early part of 1985 and failed to advert to the realities of his situation until his pending claim had been dealt with in May.

51. Moreover, so far as delay is concerned, we are not faced with any statutory provision which requires applications to be made within a prescribed time unless special circumstances are shown. There is no time limit in the statute. This presumably is because there may well be cases where a person resigns without realising, until a formerly quiescent serious illness manifests itself, that his or her general malaise and indifference are in fact occasioned by a physical or mental condition.

52. Two arguments were advanced in support of the contention that the applicant has not established a moral entitlement to the exercise of discretion in his favour.

53. The first is that his state of health was not a significant factor in the decision not to recommend him for appointment to the new position. That is obviously answered by the fact that the committee was not concerned to examine the causes for his deterioration in performance. Moreover, they were not equipped or required to do so.

54. The second is that he was properly and adequately compensated for his loss of office. It was said that he received an adequate pension and lump sum together with a settlement of $36,600.

55. We have been concerned only with the effect of the ex gratia payment. No argument was advanced that his entitlement to a pension by reason of war-caused disabilities was a factor to be taken into account when considering the merits or the morality of his claim. His right to that pension is based on his war-service and in the absence of any specific legislation charging us to take it into account we would decline to do so.

56. We cannot however ignore the so called settlement figure. It matters not whether it is called an ex gratia payment or the settlement of an action. It was money received because he claimed that he was unjustly dealt with when he was not reappointed.

57. In November 1984 the Commonwealth and the Chief Electoral Officer secured a release from "all claims, rights, remedies and entitlements which the applicant might have had against each of them arising out of or in consequence of or related to the termination of office held by him after the (1983 electoral legislation)". The proceedings were not against the Commissioner for Superannuation and he was not therefore mentioned in the release. It was at no time suggested, despite the fact that benefits are paid out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund, that the release precluded the applicant securing the invalidity benefits now sought.

58. If the sum paid had been substantially more than the actual "settlement" it may have been appropriate to measure it against the amount which the applicant would receive in the event of the exercise of the discretion in his favour under s.7(2).

59. This is not however appropriate in this case. The applicant is entitled to say that having been deemed to have been retired on the ground of invalidity it is necessary to consider what total benefits may accrue in his favour or that of his spouse during the years to come.

60. It is not easy to find and more confusing than helpful to set out in full the provisions of the law which determine the precise benefits to which the applicant is or may be entitled. They are to be found in Part V of the Act and modifications of the Act which have been effected by the Superannuation (Former Contributors for Units of Pension) Regulations ("the Regulations") and relate only to persons to whom s.183 of the Act apply (of whom the applicant is one).

61. The applicant's entitlement to pension for early retirement is to be found by applying the provisions of s.55(1), s.56(1) and (4) (as modified by the Regulations) and s.60.

62. After applying each of these provisions the result is 41.4% of his full salary which as at the time of retirement resulted in a pension of $15,169.37 per annum. If he had chosen not to elect for a lump sum pursuant to s.61 and s.64 his rate of pension would have been increased by $2,752.88 per annum.

63. The maximum annual pension which he could have secured at 1984 rates of salary was $17,922.25 if assessed as early retirement.

64. If he were treated pursuant to s.7(2) as being entitled to invalidity pension the provisions which would apply for the purpose of determining the appropriate sum would be s.67(2) and 68(2) both as modified by the Regulations.

65. Thus if he did not make an election his total pension would be 50 per cent of his full salary ($18,320.50 per annum) plus a further 20 per cent ($7,328.20). The result is $25,648.07 or a sum which was $7,726.45 greater than early retirement benefits in 1984. Increases in salary to July 1988 have further increased the present difference to about $8,780 per annum.

66. To argue that the applicant has no moral entitlement to additional pension benefits amounting to about $8,000 per annum because at the end of 1984 he received a sum capable of producing about half that sum if prudently invested ignores several factors.

67. Once it is conceded that the applicant is entitled to invalidity benefits as provided by the Act, there is no justification in taking those benefits away from him merely because he has other sources of income.

68. The fact that he may never have received the settlement in November 1984, if attention had been directed to the possibility of him establishing a claim under s.7 is as irrelevant as the fact that he became entitled through other legislation to other benefits by reason of his war service.

69. On one view the settlement received was no more than compensation for being unable to earn full salary for a further 4 years.

70. Provision could have been made in the settlement for the return of the monies paid to him if he became entitled to greater superannuation benefits than he was then receiving. It was not.

71. When regard is being had to moral entitlement it should also be remembered that the difference between benefits payable is increasing each year and may continue for many years to come.

72. We are not persuaded having regard to the amount of the settlement and the circumstances leading to it that it is a factor which should cause us to refuse an exercise of discretion in favour of the applicant.

73. Accordingly the decision under review is set aside and the respondent is requested to make an appropriate direction under s.7(2) of the Act.
