Sunrise Auto Limited v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and Anthony Steven Radisich [1994] FCA 1296; (1994) 94 Atc 4536 (1994) 124 ALR 425 (30 August 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

RADISICH

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - administration of income tax legislation - service of notice of assessment - whether Commissioner has to rely on regulations to establish service - whether requirements for service pursuant to Act and Regulations met - whether correspondence may be left at a 'mailing' address - meaning of "record in the custody of the Commission".

Income Tax - objections and appeals - whether appeal direct to Federal Court on an issue arising in respect of a notice of assessment lies despite s. 177(1) of Income Tax Assessment Act - whether third party has standing in s. 218 proceedings to raise question arising out of assessment of another taxpayer.

DECISION

SPENDER J At the request of the parties, two issues were argued as preliminary issues. They arise out of the proceedings initiated in the Supreme Court of Queensland on 24 September 1993 and transferred to this Court by Mr Justice Derrington of the Supreme Court of Queensland on 20 October 1993.

2. In the Supreme Court, the applicant, Sunrise Auto Limited ('Sunrise') sought an injunction restraining the Commonwealth Bank of Australia ('the bank') from paying $385,246.91 from an account of the second respondent to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation ('the Deputy Commissioner') pursuant to notice under s. 218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ('the Act') until agreement or court order, as well as a mandatory injunction requiring the bank to release that sum with any accretions to the applicant, and a declaration that the monies held by the bank in the name of Mr Radisich is the property of Sunrise.

3. The second respondent was and is a New Zealand citizen, resident and taxpayer. On 9 August 1988 the second respondent deposited with the Toowong Branch of the bank cash funds in the order of $712,000.00. The funds were held by the bank in an account in the name of the Mr Radisich. Interest was earned on those funds in each of the 1989, 1990, 1991 and 1992 income years, such interest being credited either to an account in the name of the second respondent or added to, and reinvested with, the capital sum.

4. In early 1993, Mr Herman Mergler, an employee of the Australian Taxation Office, commenced an investigation into the affairs of the second respondent and, in particular, the funds held by the bank. On 16 April 1993, Mr Mergler attended at and sought information from, the accountant at the Toowong Branch of the bank for the purposes of ascertaining information from the records of the bank relevant to the second respondent's affairs, pursuant to a s. 264 notice of the Act. Included in the information given to Mr Mergler was an address for the second respondent, such address being 830 Great South Road, Penrose, Auckland N.Z., which address Mr Mergler wrote in an office memorandum. Also in the information given to Mr Mergler was a mailing address for the 2nd respondent. Mr Mergler recorded that address as:

" Geoff Klooger and Assoc.

288 Edward St

Jetset building

22nd Floor."

He obtained details of the income earned on the funds held by the bank in the name of the second respondent. These details were used as a basis for making an assessment of the taxable income of the second respondent for the relevant years.

5. On 4 May 1993, the Deputy Commissioner made assessment pursuant to s. 167 of the Act of the taxable income of the second respondent, and the tax payable thereon, for each of the relevant years. The effect of the notices of assessment was to require the second respondent to pay $385,246.91.

6. On the same day as the Deputy Commissioner made assessments pursuant to s. 167, the Deputy Commissioner also issued a notice pursuant to s. 218 of the Act requiring the bank to pay to the Commissioner the sum of $385,246.91 from the moneys held in the name of the second respondent.

7. At no time prior to 4 May 1993 had the second respondent lodged an income tax return with the Deputy Commissioner and no address for service of the second respondent had been given to the Australian Taxation Office. At about 4 p.m. on 4 May 1993, notices of assessment were personally served on a female person in the employ of Mr Klooger at the offices of Geoff Klooger and Associates, 22nd Floor, 288 Edward Street, Brisbane, by an employee of the Commissioner. At about 4.30 p.m. on 4 May 1993 the section 218 notice was served by Mr Mergler upon an employee of the bank at its Toowong Branch.

8. On 5 July 1993, under a cover letter from Clayton Utz, solicitors, the second respondent objected to each of the assessments. The objection proceedings are continuing but the Commissioner has not as yet made an objection decision in relation to those objections.

9. Sunrise is a company registered in New Zealand. Mr Radisich in an amended statement of claim asserts that until 13 July 1993 he was a director of Sunrise and holds 99,999 shares in Sunrise, being all but one of the issued shares in Sunrise.

10. In these proceedings, Sunrise contests the s. 218 notice on the grounds that it claims that all the funds of the second respondent held by the bank and any interest accruing thereon were held solely for and on behalf of Sunrise by the second respondent. Sunrise claims that such funds were and have remained the property at law and/or in equity of Sunrise. In addition, Sunrise states in paragraph 10 and 11 of its amended statement of claim:

10. The Notice of Assessment referred to in paragraph 8:-

(a) was not served upon Radisich in accordance with the

provisions of regulations 38 and 170 of the Income Tax

Regulations;

(b) if it was served upon Radisich in accordance with the

provisions of regulations 38 and 170 of the Income Tax

Regulations (which is denied) it was not served upon

Radisich prior to service of the Notice issued

pursuant to s. 218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act as

referred to in paragraph 9 upon the Commonwealth Bank

of Australia.

11. In the premises the Notice issued pursuant to s. 218 as

referred to in paragraph 9 was and is of no force and effect.

11. The two preliminary questions are:

1. Whether hand delivery of the four notices of assessment to a female person in the employ of Mr Klooger at the offices of Geoff Klooger and Associates constituted valid and effective service on the second respondent pursuant to the provisions of the Act and Regulations, and whether by reason of any defect in service of them, the s. 218 is of no force and effect.

2. Whether it is open to Sunrise to have determined in these proceedings the question of whether or not Mr Radisich is a taxpayer to whom s. 128B(3)(h)(ii) of the Act applies.

13. Sunrise contends that Mr Radisich is not a taxpayer as defined by the provisions of the Act, in that the provisions of s. 128B(3)(h)(ii) of the Act did not apply to him. It is said the s. 218 notice was of no force and effect for this further reason. The Deputy Commissioner has pleaded by paras. 10 and 11 of the amended defence that Sunrise's contention in relation to the second respondent's status as a taxpayer are matters that may only be raised in proceedings under Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 ('the Administration Act') and that those matters may only be raised by Mr Radisich and not by Sunrise.

The Service Issue

14. Section 218 relevantly provides:

" 218 (1) The Commissioner may at any time, or from time to

time, by notice in writing (a copy of which shall be

forwarded to the taxpayer at his last place of address known

to the Commissioner), require -

...

(b) any person who holds or may subsequently hold money

for or on account of a taxpayer;

...

to pay to the Commissioner, either forthwith upon the money

becoming due or being held, or at or within a time specified

in the notice (not being a time before the money becomes due

or is held) -

(e) so much of the money as is sufficient to pay the

amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax or, if

the amount of the money is equal to or less than the

amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax, the

amount of the money;

... "

15. Section 218 imposes an obligation in respect of "the amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax". The section requires that an amount is due by the taxpayer.

16. In Clyne v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1, the High Court considered when money becomes due by the taxpayer to the Commissioner for the purposes of s. 218. Although s. 218 has since been amended, observations in that case are still apposite. Mason J, with whom Aickin and Wilson JJ agreed, stated (at 16):

" ...the correct view, in my opinion, is that income tax is

due when it is assessed and notice is served of that

assessment and that the tax does not become payable before

the date fixed by s. 204.

...

Section 206, which enables the Commissioner to grant an

extension of time for payment or to permit payment to be

made by instalments and provides that the tax shall be due

and payable accordingly, is more easily accommodated to the

view that tax is due no later than the time when the notice

of assessment is served on the taxpayer than to the

appellant's interpretation which makes tax due when it

becomes payable.

For all these reasons I consider that 'due' in par. (i) of

s. 218 means 'due' in its primary sense, not 'due and

payable'. " (my emphasis)

17. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Naidoo 81 ATC 4537, was a case involving s. 205 of the Act, which concerns the Commissioner's powers in circumstances where there are reasonable grounds to suspect the taxpayer may leave the country to avoid tax, Everett J observed (at 4,540):

" The remaining argument against the application of the Deputy

Commissioner for liberty to sign final judgment is that the

notices of assessment were not served on the defendant in

accordance with law.

It is beyond question that no legal obligation in respect of

the payment of income tax was imposed on the defendant until

notice of assessment was served on him (my emphasis) in a

manner required or authorised by law. In Batagol v. FC of

T [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243, Kitto J, in considering the

definition of 'assessment' for the purposes of the Income

Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1936-56

(Cth.), said at pp. 251-2:

' The word 'assessment' is defined in sec. 6 to mean,

unless the contrary intention appears, the ascertainment

of the amount of taxable income and of the tax payable

thereon. There is nothing in sec. 170 to show the

contrary intention. But the definition is not sufficient

by itself to answer the question before us, because

'ascertainment' is a word the force of which depends upon

the context. It is here used in an Act under which the

service of a notice of assessment is the levying of the

tax. Assessment in the sense of mere calculation

produces no legal effect. No step that the Commissioner

may take, even to the point of satisfying himself of the

amount of the taxable income and of the tax thereon, has

under the Act any legal significance. But if the

Commissioner, having gone through the process of

calculation, serves on the taxpayer a notice that he has

assessed the taxable income and the tax at specified

amounts, the tax becomes by force of the Act due and

payable on the date specified in the notice or (if no

date is specified) on the thirtieth day after the service

of the notice: sec. 204. Thus, and thus only, there is

brought about an 'ascertainment' of the taxable income

and of the tax, in the sense that thereafter it is

possible to say what could not have been said before:

that amounts have been fixed so that they are to be taken

for all purposes (except those of appeal: see sec. 177)

to be the result flowing from the application of the Act

in the particular case. The respective amounts of the

taxable income and the tax have been rendered certain.

The word 'ascertainment' being understood in this sense,

the definition of 'assessment' means, in my opinion, the

completion of the process by which the provisions of the

Act relating to liability to tax are given concrete

application in a particular case with the consequence

that a specified amount of money will become due and

payable as the proper tax in that case. The idea

coincides with that which Isaacs J expressed in FC of T

v. Hoffnung and Co Ltd (1928) 42 CLR 39 in relation to

war-time profits tax when he said: 'If an assessment

definitive in character is made, it assumes that, so far

as can there be seen, a fixed and certain sum is

definitely due, neither more nor less. In short, it

ascertains a precise indebtedness of the taxpayer to the

Crown' (1928) 42 CLR., at p. 55. On this construction of

the Act nothing done in the Commissioner's office can

amount to more than steps which will form part of an

assessment if, but only if, they lead to and are followed

by the service of a notice of assessment.'

It is clear from the judgment of Owen J in the same case

that the process of 'assessment' (defined in the Act as

meaning 'the ascertainment of the amount of taxable income

and of the tax payable thereon') is not complete until

notice of it has been effectively given (and cf. the

decisions of the High Court of Australia in F. J. Bloemen

Pty Ltd v. FC of T 81 ATC 4280 and Clyne v. DFC of T 81 ATC

4429. "

18. These authorities establish that tax is due when an assessment is made, and notice of that assessment is served.

19. Sunrise's submissions are based on the statutory requirements of service of the notice of assessment contained in the Act, in particular, s. 174(1) of the Act and regs. 38 and 39 of the Income Tax Regulations. Section 174(1) provides:

" As soon as conveniently may be after any assessment is made,

the Commissioner shall serve notice thereof in writing by

post or otherwise upon the person liable to pay the tax. "

Regulations 38 and 39 provide:

" 38. The address for service last given to the Commissioner

by any person shall, for all purposes under the Act and

Regulations, be his address for service, but where no

address for service has been given to the Commissioner or

where the departmental records disclose that such person has

subsequently changed his address and he has not notified the

Commissioner, either in the return or by separate written

advice, of such change, then the address of the person, as

described in any record in the custody of the Commissioner,

shall be his address for service.

39. The address for service as prescribed in regulation 38

shall, for all purposes under the Act and Regulations, be

deemed to be the last known place of business or abode in

Australia of any person. "

20. Regulation 170(1) provides:

" Any notice or other communication by or on behalf of the

Commissioner may be served upon any person -

(a) by causing it to be personally served on him; or

(b) by leaving it at his address for service; or

(c) by posting it by pre-paid letter post, addressed

to him at his address for service;

and in any case to which paragraph (c) applies, unless the

contrary is proved, service thereof shall be deemed to have

been effected at the time when it would, in the ordinary

course of post, have arrived at the place to which it was

addressed. "

21. The Commissioner submits that, as the notices were actually received by the second respondent, the requirements of s. 174(1) are met. The second respondent has not only objected to the assessments, but has also given his address as the address used by the Commissioner when serving the notices of assessment. The Commissioner suggests that where s. 174(1) has been complied with, issues of compliance with particular regulations are irrelevant: Briggs v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation 88 FLR 235.

22. In Briggs, the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Western Australia was concerned with the question of whether notices of assessment had been served in accordance with s. 174(1) in circumstances where the service of the notice was not in compliance with the relevant regulations but, despite that, the notices had been received by the taxpayer. Kennedy J, with whom Burt CJ and Brinsden J agreed, said (at 240):

" As Lord Cranworth LC expressed it in Hope v. Hope (1854) 4

De GM and G 328 at 342; 43 ER 534 at 539-540:

' The object of all service is of course only to give

notice to the party on whom it is made, so that he may

be made aware of and may be able to resist that which is

sought against him; and when that has been substantially

done, so that the Court may feel perfectly confident

that service has reached him, everything has been done

that is required. '

And see also Kistler v. Tettmar (1905) 1 KB 39.

Section 174(1) permits service by post. Each of the notices

in question was posted....to the appellant at his

residential address, and each was received by him at that

address on or before 26 July 1983. In my opinion, in terms

of s. 174(1), the respondent has therefore served notice of

the assessments and amended assessments in writing, by post,

upon the appellant. There is no foundation for the

contention that the service was ineffective because the

address at which the appellant received the notices was not

his address for service even assuming for the purposes of

this argument, that to be the fact. "

23. As Briggs indicates, the object of service is receipt. Where the question is whether a person has received a notice of assessment, the fact of receipt is sufficient and the regulations do not matter. There is no need to have recourse to them. Here, however, the question is when notice was received, and to establish the time of service, as opposed to the fact of service, the Deputy Commissioner has to rely on the regulations.

24. Regulation 170(1)(b) permits the Commissioner to serve any notice by leaving it at the taxpayer's address for service. Regulation 38, set out above, has two limbs; one applies where the Commissioner already has an address given to the Commissioner by the taxpayer, the other applies when no address has been given by the taxpayer to the Commissioner.

25. In this case, the Deputy Commissioner has to rely on the second limb, which relevantly provides that "where no address for service has been given to the Commissioner...then the address of the person, as described in any record in the custody of the Commissioner, shall be his address for service".

26. The question in this case is whether the office of Geoff Klooger and Associates, 22nd Floor, Jetset Building, 288 Edward Street, Brisbane, is Mr Radisich's address for service. If it is, service of the notices of assessment was effected when the notices were left at that address, and income tax was then "due" by Mr Radisich, and the s. 218 notice which was served subsequent to service of the assessments, was a valid notice.

27. Sunrise submits that the personal address of the applicant, held by the Deputy Commissioner, was an address in New Zealand. The applicant submits that it was wrong for the Commissioner to use the address of an agent in Australia in circumstances where the Commissioner had available to him a personal address. The applicant says the Commissioner ought either to have served on the New Zealand address (if the regulations permit use of an address outside of Australia) or not at all (if reg. 38 is limited to Australian addresses). It says that if the Parliament had intended that an address of an agent of the person could be used to fulfil the requirements of reg. 38, it could easily have said so.

28. It is unnecessary to decide whether the regulations dealing with an "address for service" require an Australian address. Regulation 39 would tend to suggest that it does. In my view, "the address of a person" means the place at which communications to that person might be received. There is no implication that address be the place where a person resides.

29. It was submitted on behalf of the applicant that the address at which the notices were served was recorded in bank records as a mailing address, but that the notices were hand delivered. Regulation 170(1) provides that service can be effected either by leaving the relevant communication at, or posting to, the address for service. However, it was submitted that the address relied on was an address solely for mail, not for hand deliveries, and that, if the Deputy Commissioner wishes to effect service in reliance on the regulations, then the Deputy Commissioner was required to post the notices to that address, not hand deliver them. It was pointed out that if the notices of assessment had been posted on 4 May 1993 instead of being hand delivered, the notices of assessment would not have been served before the service of the s. 218 notice on the bank.

30. The applicant relies on Naidoo both for the general submission that strict compliance is required in making use of the statutory service provisions, and in the specific provision that serving by hand on a postal address does not sufficiently comply. In Naidoo, Everett J in the Supreme Court of Tasmania said at 4,544:

" It is obviously an advantage to the Australian Taxation

Office in some cases to be able to service notices and other

communications by following the method prescribed by either

para. (b) or para. (c) of regulation 59 (now para. (b) and

(c) of regulation 170) but I consider that, if it does so,

it is bound to adhere strictly to the letter of the

regulation. Any departure from a plain and natural

interpretation of the words of regulation 59 could bring

doubt and uncertainty into an area which is clear and

definite. Regulation 59 is a protection for the Deputy

Commissioner against taxpayers who may seek to evade their

tax obligations by avoiding service of documents. The

Taxation Office merely has to follow an appropriate option

given by regulation 59 to be able to pursue its

administrative duties. It will only encounter legal

difficulties if it disregards strict compliance with reg.

59, even though departure may appear completely technical

and really of no significance. "

His Honour found that a number of notices of assessment which had been manually delivered at the office of the taxpayer's accountant did not amount to "leaving" them at the taxpayer's address for service, which was c/o a post office box number. No doubt strict compliance is required when the Commissioner relies on the regulations to effect service. Everett J found that it was not effective service to hand deliver documents to an office address when a post office box address is the address given. The address at which the documents were left was not the address for service of the taxpayer. The facts in Naidoo were different from the present.

31. If the address at which the notices were left was, for the purposes of reg. 38, the address of Mr Radisich, it is in my opinion immaterial whether the notices were posted to it or left there. Both avenues of effecting service were open to the Deputy Commissioner, and the consequence of using one rather than the other is simply that the time at which service was effected would be different.

32. The applicant further submitted that the requirement in reg. 38 is that the Commissioner use the address of the person as described. The applicant says, and it is an agreed fact, that the address as described was:

" Geoff Klooger and Assoc

288 Edward Street

Jetset Building

22nd Floor "

when in fact the address on the notice was:

" C/Geoff Klooger and Assoc

22nd Floor

Jetset Building

288 Edward Street

Brisbane 4000 "

33. In my opinion, the address as described is the same address as the address on the notice, which is the office of Geoff Klooger and Associates found on the 22nd floor of the Jetset building at 288 Edward Street, Brisbane.

34. The applicant's next submission focuses on the expression "records in the custody of the Commissioner". According to the statement of agreed facts, on 16 April 1993, Mr Mergler attended upon Mr Spalding, the accountant at Toowong Branch of the bank, for the purposes of ascertaining certain information from the records of the bank pursuant to a notice issued pursuant to s. 264 of the Act. The information given by Mr Spalding to Mr Mergler was recorded by Mr Mergler in the form of an internal office memorandum, which was placed by Mr Mergler on a file kept in the office of the Deputy Commissioner in the name of the second respondent. On that memorandum, Mr Mergler recorded as Mr Radisich's address for mail:

" Geoff Klooger and Assoc.

288 Edward St

Jetset Building

22nd Floor. "

35. The applicant says that the Deputy Commissioner fails to fulfil the requirements of reg. 38 because the above events did not result in there being created "a record in the custody of the Commissioner". First, the applicant says that the memorandum upon which Mr Mergler wrote the address was not a "record", and second, the applicant says that, even if it was a record, it was not a record in the custody of the Commissioner.

36. First, as regards the meaning of "record", the applicant submits that the internal memorandum created by Mr Mergler's notation of the address which he acquired from the bank was not a "record". The applicant submits that the memorandum was simply a document recording secondary information. It was submitted that "record" and "document" are not co-extensive terms. A "record" is a document which is an "original or primary source" and not a "digest or analysis" of (other) records: H. v Schering Chemicals Ltd (1983) 1 All ER 849 at 852. The applicant submits a record is a document which either gives effect to a transaction itself or which contains a contemporaneous register of information supplied by those with direct knowledge of the facts: Schering at 852. The applicant accepts that the address may have contained a record of the bank but that the mere notation of a record of the bank by the Deputy Commissioner's office does not make that notation itself into a record. It is but a secondary reproduction of the record.

37. In my opinion, there is no doubt that if Mr Mergler had taken the document in which the bank had recorded the address at which Mr Radisich could have been contacted, and placed that on the file, the document would fall within the description in reg. 38.

38. Counsel for the Deputy Commissioner does not disagree with the principle in H. v Schering Chemicals Ltd. For the Deputy Commissioner, it was pointed out that information was given by Mr Spalding to Mr Mergler and that Mr Mergler recorded that information in an internal memorandum. This, the Deputy Commissioner says, amounted to a contemporaneous registering of information supplied by those with direct knowledge of the facts, as required by Schering. Section 264(1) relevantly provides:

" The Commissioner may by notice in writing require any

person, whether a taxpayer or not, including any officer

employed in or in connection with any department of a

Government or by any public authority -

(a) to furnish him with such information as he may

require; and

(b) to attend and give evidence before him or before any

officer authorised by him in that behalf concerning

his or any other person's income or assessment, and

may require him to produce all books, documents and

other papers whatever in his custody or under his

control relating thereto. "

39. For the Deputy Commissioner, it was submitted that Mr Mergler was acting pursuant to the powers contained in s. 264(1)(a) rather than s. 264(1)(b) when he acquired the information regarding Mr Radisich's addresses, i.e. Mr Spalding was furnishing him with such information as Mr Mergler required. I agree.

40. The document in which the information required to be furnished by the bank is recorded, in my opinion, is a "record" of that information.

41. The s. 264 notice itself (Exhibit 2 in these proceedings) is headed "Request to Provide Information s. 264(1)(a) Income Tax Assessment Act 1936". However, in the body of the notice, under the sub-heading "Records required", appears "Full particulars on accounts conducted including commercial bills".

42. Further, in my view, the word "record' in reg. 38 should be given a wide ambit. In my opinion, the word "record" is used to refer to that by which information is recorded, and covers not only documents but computer information and suchlike.

43. Counsel for the applicant submitted that if the mere writing down of an address by an officer of the Deputy Commissioner was sufficient to make such an address a record for the purposes of reg. 38, it would result in a situation open to abuse. It was also submitted that the use in the first limb of reg. 38 of "departmental records", indicated that of "records in the custody of the Commissioner" in the second limb did not encompass departmental records. The argument was that the failure to use the expression "departmental records" indicated the intention by the parliament that "record" ought have a different meaning. Counsel for the applicant posited the situation where an officer of the Deputy Commissioner simply wrote down from the telephone book an address for a taxpayer, or walked up to anyone on the street and asked them what an address for a certain taxpayer may be, and wrote down the answer. This result, it was suggested, demonstrated that the contention on behalf of the Commissioner was untenable. I do not agree.

44. It is unnecessary in the present case to answer speculation as to the ambit of the word "record", but it seems to me at least to require a bona fide recording of information. The memorandum of Mr Mergler, in my view, is a "record" within the meaning of that word in reg. 38.

45. Counsel for Sunrise next submitted that the notion of custody used in reg. 38 carries with it the idea of guardianship without legal ownership. The applicant says "record in the custody of the Commissioner" means records which are owned by, and are in the usual custody of, somebody else, but which the Commissioner has taken into his custody, e.g. pursuant to a notice under s. 10 of the Crimes Act (Cth) which authorises seizure by the Commonwealth of documents/records from somebody else. The applicant seeks to support this submission by reference to FC of T v. Australia and New Zealand Banking Group Ltd (1977-79) 143 CLR 499 (Smorgon's Case). That case was concerned with the meaning of the words "custody and control" used in the context of s. 264 of the Act. Gibbs ACJ said ( at 519):

" ...s. 264 does not speak of 'possession'; it uses the wider,

and vaguer, words 'custody' and 'control'. The two words

are sometimes used as synonyms. In Pollock and Wright:

Possession in the Common Law (1888), at p. 26, a distinction

is drawn between 'physical control, detention, or de facto

possession', which is said to be 'an actual relation between

a person and a thing...matter of fact', and 'legal

possession', which is 'a definite legal relation of the

person to the thing possessed'. The learned author goes on

to state that in this connection physical control is

generally called 'custody'. In Stephen's Digest of Criminal

Law, 5th ed. (1894), p 243, in a passage cited in Moors v.

Burke (1919) 26 CLR at p. 270, it is said: The word

'custody' means such a relation towards the thing as would

constitute possession if the person having custody had it on

his own account.' The meaning which the words are intended

to bear in s. 264 depends, amongst other things, on the

context in which they appear. The object of the section is

to give the Commissioner power to require the production of

documents which relate to the income or assessment of any

person, and 'assessment' in this provision has the wide

meaning given to it in s. 6 of the Act - 'the ascertainment

of the amount of taxable income and of the tax payable

thereon': see Smorgon v. Australia and New Zealand Banking

Group Ltd [1976] HCA 53; (1976) 134 CLR 475, at p 480. The aim is the

practical one of having documents produced so that an

officer of the Taxation Department can obtain from them

information concerning the income or assessment of some

person. The section is not concerned with the legal

relationship of the person to whom the notice is given to

the documents which he is required to produce: it is

concerned with the ability of the person to whom the notice

is addressed to produce the documents when required to do

so. Therefore, in my opinion, a notice can be given under

the section to any person who has physical control of the

documents in question, whether he has or has not the legal

possession. "

46. The applicant also referred to the observations of Mason J at 532-533, where his Honour said;

" The primary definition of 'custody' in the Shorter Oxford

English Dictionary is 'safekeeping, protection; charge,

care, guardianship'. Without more, the degree of protection

for the security of the contents of the boxes which the bank

provides justifies the conclusion that the documents were in

the custody of the bank...

The content of control is somewhat different from that of

custody; however, both are 'wide enough to include many

types of possession which are not commensurate with full

ownership'...It is difficult to ascribe a precise meaning to

control in s. 264 as the content of the word is normally

dictated by its context and can vary from sole, absolute

dominion over the object 'controlled' to something weaker

than 'restraint', something equivalent to regulation. "

47. In Smorgon's Case, "custody" and "control" were construed in the context of s. 264 of the Act. It is also true to say that they are terms which, in the words of Gibbs ACJ "depend, amongst other things, on the context in which they appear". In the above passage, Gibbs ACJ considered the object of s. 264, and considered that that object suggested that the word "custody" should not be given a narrow meaning. In my view, it is appropriate to consider the meaning of "custody" in reg. 38 in a similar fashion. Regulation 38 is part of the scheme providing for service by the Commissioner on persons so as to allow the Commissioner to proceed with his obligations under the Act. It seeks to facilitate the service by providing that certain addresses may be used by the Commissioner. In that context, in my view, "custody" is not restricted to those things which are in the control of the Commissioner, but are the subject of legal ownership or right to possession is in others. It extends to those records which are in the control or guardianship of the Commissioner, regardless of who is the owner of those records.

48. For these reasons, the first preliminary question should be answered "yes": the hand delivery referred to in the agreed statement of facts constituted valid and effective service on Mr Radisich and the s. 218 notice is valid.

Jurisdiction

49. The Agreed Statement of Facts outlines:

. In these proceedings Sunrise contends by paragraphs 12 and 13 of

its Amended Statement of Claim that Radisich was not a taxpayer as

defined by the provisions of the Act in that the provisions of

Section 128B(3)(h)(ii) of the Act did not apply to him, this being

the section which founded the basis of the assessment. Thus, it

is said, the Section 218 notice was of no force and effect.

. In answer to that contention the DCT has pleaded, by paragraphs 10

and 11 of his Amended Defence, that;-

(a) those matters may only be raised in proceedings under Part

IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953;

(b) those matters may only be raised by Radisich and not by

Sunrise;

(c) it otherwise does not admit the matters alleged in

paragraphs 12 and 13 of the Amended Statement of Claim.

. The question which the Court is asked to decide is whether it is

open to Sunrise to have determined in these proceedings the

question of whether or not Radisich is a taxpayer to whom Section

128(B)(3)(h)(ii) of the Act applies.

50. The statutory provisions upon which the Commissioner relies, which are referred to in paras. 10 and 11 of the amended defence, are as follows:

51. Section 177 of the Act provides:

" The production of a notice of assessment, or of a document

under the hand of the Commissioner, a Second Commissioner,

or a Deputy Commissioner, purporting to be a copy of a

notice of assessment, shall be conclusive evidence of the

due making of the assessment and, except in proceedings

under Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 on a

review or appeal relating to the assessment, that the amount

and all the particulars of the assessment are correct. "

Part IVC of the Administration Act is headed "Taxation Objections, Reviews and Appeals". Section 14ZL provides:

" (1) This Part applies if a provision of an Act (including

the provision as applied by another Act) provides that a

person who is dissatisfied with an assessment,

determination, notice or decision may object against it in

the manner set out in this Part.

(2) Such an objection is in this Part called a 'taxation

objection'. "

Section 14ZL picks up the reference to the Administration Act contained in s. 177 of the Act. Section 14ZU provides:

" A person making a taxation objection must:

(a) make it in writing; and

(b) lodge it with the Commissioner within the period set

out in section 14ZW; and

(c) state in it, fully and in detail, the grounds that the

person relies on. "

52. Counsel for the Commissioner contends that the effect of F. J. Bloemen Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 27; (1980-81) 147 CLR 360, is to preclude the applicant from making in these proceedings a submission that the second respondent was not a taxpayer.

53. In Bloemen, the taxpayer instituted proceedings seeking statutory appeal under the equivalent of the appeal contained in Part IVC of the Administration Act, and also commenced originating proceedings in the general jurisdiction of the Supreme Court for declaratory relief. The taxpayer sought to have notices of assessment set aside because the notices were not the result of a bona fide exercise of the powers vested in the Commissioner, but rather were issued in an attempt to place the Commissioner in a position to support the issue of s. 218 notices. The taxpayer was met with the same argument from the Commissioner as the taxpayer is met with here, that is, that proceedings on notices of assessment can only be taken pursuant to the statutory appeal regime set out in Part IVC of the Administration Act, and that the notices of assessment cannot, because of the effect of s. 177 of the Act, be subject to collateral attack through other proceedings outside the statutory framework.

54. Mason and Wilson JJ, with whom Stephen and Aickin JJ agreed, said at p. 375:

" An explicit and, in our view, correct statement of the

effect of s. 177(1) made by Taylor J in McAndrew v. FC of T

(1956) 98 CLR at 281-282. For the reasons there expressed,

his Honour concluded that 's. 177(1) was intended to make it

impossible for a taxpayer, in proceedings other than appeal

against it, to challenge an assessment on any ground'. He

conceded that the word 'excessive' in s. 190(b) was

inappropriate. However, he conceded that an assessment

'made in purported but not justifiable exercise of a

statutory power' could properly be described as excessive

(at 282).

This interpretation gives expression to the policy which

underlies, and is manifest in, the statutory provision. The

effect of this policy is that, once the Commissioner takes

advantage of s. 177(1) by producing an appropriate document,

the taxpayer is precluded from contesting that the

Commissioner has made an assessment or that in making the

assessment he has complied with the statutory formalities.

The taxpayer is entitled to dispute his substantive

liability to tax in proceedings under Part V (now Part IVC

of the Administration Act). "

55. In these proceedings certified copies of the notices of assessment have been produced. In reliance on Bloemen's Case, the Commissioner submits that the consequences set out in s. 177(1) of the Act and discussed in Bloemen's Case must follow, i.e., this court is bound to conclude that the assessments have been duly made and that the amounts and all the particulars in the assessments are correct. It follows that it is not open to the second respondent to assert here that he was not a taxpayer in these proceedings and, a fortiori, it is not open to the applicant to make that assertion.

56. The applicant's response to this submission is to say that F J Bloemen is no longer an authority binding on me in relation to this question. The applicant relies on David Jones Finance and Investments Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation 1991 28 FCR 484. In that case, the taxpayers were companies which had received dividends on shares held by nominee companies on the taxpayers' behalf. Departing from a longstanding practice, the Commissioner denied the taxpayers s. 46 corporate dividend rebates and issued notices of assessment accordingly. The taxpayer sought orders from the Federal Court, in accordance with the court's jurisdiction to review administrative action conferred by s. 39B of the Judiciary Act, preventing the Commissioner from taking proceedings to collect or recover the tax. The taxpayer also sought declarations that the notices of assessment which were issued were invalid and other similar declarations. O'Loughlin J at first instance declined to grant the orders sought on the basis that, by virtue of the conclusive evidence provisions of s. 177, the appeal provisions of Part V of the Assessment Act (now Part IVC of the Administration Act) were a code governing the means by which taxpayers might challenge an assessment. Were it not for Part V, the taxpayer's pleadings disclosed grounds that might justify the court intervening.

57. Section 39B(1) of the Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth), which in its original form was inserted in 1983, provided that the original jurisdiction of the Federal Court of Australia "includes jurisdiction with respect to any matter in which a writ of mandamus or prohibition or an injunction is sought against an officer of the Commonwealth'.

58. Section 75 of the Constitution provides, in part, as follows:

" In all matters -

...

(v) in which a writ of Mandamus or prohibition or an

injunction is sought against an officer of the

Commonwealth:

the High Court shall have original jurisdiction. "

59. The Federal Court, by a majority, Morling and French JJ, with Pincus J dissenting, held that the intention of parliament passing s. 39B by way of amendments to the Judiciary Act was to confer on the Federal Court, subject to the express limitations in that section, the full amplitude of the like jurisdiction conferred on the High Court by the s. 75(v) of the Commonwealth Constitution. The jurisdiction so conferred on the Federal Court will not be displaced, qualified or limited by privative provisions in statutes preceding the amendment; for statutes following the amendment the presumption, absent clear contrary words, is that no such displacement, qualification or limitation is intended. The majority concluded that s. 177 of the Act cannot displace jurisdiction conferred on the Federal Court by s. 39B of the Judiciary Act, and in the proper exercise of that jurisdiction in the Federal Court, the "due making" of an assessment, its amount and all particulars of it, are open to inquiry.

60. Morling and French JJ considered the relevant cases, including F J Bloemen, which construe s. 177(1) as making it impossible for a taxpayer in proceedings other than an appeal against an assessment pursuant to the statutory regime to challenge an assessment. Their Honours also considered the circumstances in which the courts had allowed a taxpayer to challenge an assessment other than by the statutory regime. In particular, they considered the case of Briggs v. DFC of T; Ex parte Briggs 12 FCR 301. Such cases, however, proceed on the basis that the purported notice of assessment was not in fact an "assessment" for purposes of the Act and therefore s. 177 could not be said to apply. As Mason and Wilson JJ in Bloemen said at 371:

" (Section 177) does not, and cannot, create a valid

assessment where no assessment has been made at all. "

61. Morling and French JJ said (at 497):

" ..it is apparent from the language of s. 39B its identity

with that of s. 75(v) (of the Commonwealth Constitution) and

the Second Reading Speech, that the intention of the

legislature was to confer on the Federal Court the full

amplitude of the original jurisdiction of the High Court

under s. 75(v). Consistently with that intention, and the

case law, the jurisdiction so conferred will not be

displaced, qualified or limited by privative provisions in

statutes predating the amendment. And for statutes which

postdate it, there will be a powerful presumption, in the

absence of clear words to the contrary, that no such

displacement qualification or limitation is intended.

Statutory erosion of the jurisdiction will effectively

return it, contrary to the legislative intention, to the

exclusive province of the High Court. The interaction of s.

39B with s. 177 of the Income Tax Assessment Act must

therefore be considered on the same footing as the

interaction of paragraph 75(v) of the Constitution with that

provision. "

Their Honours then proceeded to consider that interaction and came to the conclusion, at 502-3:

" On that characterisation, its (s. 177) effect is purely

jurisdictional and cannot displace the jurisdiction

conferred on this court by s. 39B. In this court and in the

proper exercise of that jurisdiction the 'due making' of the

assessment and the amount and all particulars thereof is

open to inquiry.

62. On this basis, the applicant contends that it is able to attack the notice of assessment in these proceedings. The applicant adds that the applicant has standing to raise the question whether Radisich is a taxpayer, so as to impeach the purported issue by the Commissioner of the s. 218 notice on the bank, because the applicant claims that the moneys upon which the s. 218 notice purportedly became a charge, are its moneys and that the applicant is therefore a person who has an interest in the question whether the charge is valid or not.

63. Pincus J dissented. He observed, at 509-10:

" Whatever may have been the effect of s. 39B on other

privative provisions, it seems to me difficult to accept

that s. 39B of the Judiciary Act was intended to exclude or

limit the application of s. 177(1) of the ITA Act in

proceedings in this Court; the substantial weakening of such

a critical part of the tax collection system in this country

could hardly have been intended to be effected by a mere

implication. "

And later:

" ...as a matter of interpretation, it appears unlikely that

Parliament intended s. 39B of the Judiciary Act to alter the

law laid down in Bloemen's case, with respect to proceedings

challenging tax assessments under that section. "

I respectfully share those doubts.

64. I am, however, of the opinion that David Jones cannot be distinguished by confining its application only to circumstances where the conduct of the assessment has been alleged to have been in bad faith.

65. I feel that I am constrained by the ratio of the majority judgment, namely, that:

" In this Court, and in the proper exercise of the

jurisdiction (conferred on the Federal Court by s. 39B) the

'due making' of the assessment and the amount and all

particulars thereof is open to inquiry. "

66. In Seymour v. DFC of T 92 ATC 4774, Northrop J considered the decision in David Jones. He said, at 4778:

" The decision in David Jones is binding on me. Counsel for

the Commissioner conceded that but contended that David

Jones was wrongly decided, thus laying the basis for an

appeal. "

67. In DFC of T v. Worn 93 ATC 4185 at 4189, the David Jones principle was considered by Hayne J sitting in the Supreme Court of Victoria. In that case, Hayne J considered that he was exercising jurisdiction under s. 39(2) of the Judiciary Act and that the David Jones principle, whatever may be its ambit, did not apply in such circumstances. In Worn's Case, Hayne J referred to DFC of T v. Stjepovic 91 ATC 4715, in which J.D. Phillips J considered David Jones but found it to be inapplicable because his Honour, sitting in the Supreme Court of Victoria, said (at 4,723) that the reasoning in the joint judgment in David Jones:

" depended wholly on s. 39B of the Judiciary Act"

and:

" (n)o such considerations apply in this Court, where s. 39B

is irrelevant. "

68. The respondents also raised the fact that these proceedings were not based on s. 39B of the Judiciary Act. The applicant submitted that it was not necessary to include reference to s. 39B because of the effect of s. 11(3) of the Jurisdiction of Courts (Cross-Vesting) Act 1987 (Cth). When the correctness of this statement was disputed, the applicant sought leave to amend, if necessary, the prayer for relief in these proceedings. The Commissioner indicated that such application would not be opposed by the Commissioner and that the Commissioner would only wish to be heard on the question of costs. In those circumstances, I give leave to amend the prayer for relief to include relief under s. 39B of the Judiciary Act.

69. The applicant also made submissions directed to the possibility that it would fail on the David Jones point. In those submissions, the applicant submitted that the issue of whether Mr Radisich is in fact a taxpayer is outside the framework of issues excluded by s. 177(1) on the Bloemen test. In the light of my conclusion concerning David Jones, it is unnecessary to consider those submissions further.

70. Finally, in relation to the question of whether the applicant can, in the general sense, leaving aside s. 177, raise issues going to the correctness of affairs as between Mr Radisich and the Deputy Commissioner, it is the submission of the Deputy Commissioner that matters as to Mr Radisich's status as a taxpayer are not matters which the applicant has standing to challenge. However, I think that in circumstances where the applicant seeks to challenge the validity of a s. 218 notice, which operates as a charge on moneys which the applicant claims are its moneys and not the moneys of the party assessed to pay tax, and where the applicant further contends that the s. 218 notice permits a charge only on moneys due or accruing to "the taxpayer", and the applicant contends that for the purpose of s. 218, "taxpayer" is defined to exclude a party liable to withholding tax: s. 218(6B), the applicant has standing.

71. For the above reasons, the first issue is determined as the Commissioner submitted, the second as the applicant contended.

72. I will hear the parties as to the form of orders I should make, and as to costs.
