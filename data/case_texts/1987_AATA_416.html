INCOME TAX – legal expenses - whether incurred in course of earning income - whether of capital nature.

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 November 1987 Deputy President C J Bannon QC

These applications for review of objection decisions relate to taxation assessments for the years ending 30th June 1978 and 30th June 1979 upon the first and second applicants ("the First Taxpayer") and ("the Second Taxpayer") respectively. By consent they were heard together.

The questions in dispute relate to the deductibility from assessable income of legal expenses incurred by the taxpayers. The First Taxpayer and the Second Taxpayer are related companies, the Second Taxpayer being a holding company of which the First Taxpayer is a wholly owned subsidiary. The First Taxpayer is the operating company, carrying on the business of meat processing and wholesale meat supply. The First Taxpayer (and presumably the holding company) was largely a family business. Evidence was given that at relevant times access to abattoirs was in short supply. The Second Taxpayer owned a shareholding amounting to approximately 40% of the issued capital of a country abattoir. Apparently the shareholding was a factor of importance in securing access to the abattoir for killing and processing.

There was a falling out in the family controlling the First and Second Taxpayers. An uncle, a cousin and two unrelated persons set up a new business, but traded under the family name which is the principal feature of the titles of both taxpayer companies. The new group set up business at the wholesale meat market at Homebush in adjoining space to where the First Taxpayer's name was prominently displayed.

The First and Second Taxpayers brought proceedings in the Equity Division of the Supreme Court of New South Wales against the uncle and his supporters for passing off, to restrain the use of alleged confidential information, to restrain any attempted inducement of employees to terminate employment with the two taxpayer companies and certain other companies and for other related and ancillary relief. Those proceedings were dismissed by consent upon payment of a certain sum of money by the uncle to the Second Taxpayer and presumably the activities of the uncle and his supporters ceased.

It was submitted on behalf of the respondent that the proceedings were brought to preserve the position of both taxpayers and that the legal expenses incurred were really of a capital nature. It is true that the fruits of the settlement resulted in a payment to the Second Taxpayer rather than the First Taxpayer, but there is no dispute that the First Taxpayer incurred the expenses claimed which amount to $8,569. That the action of the uncle and his associates was likely to lead to deception is evidenced by the alleged attempt to use the family name and the adjoining trading position at the markets in competition with the First Taxpayer. I accept the submission made by Mr Sweeney of Counsel that the expenditure was not essentially directed to preserving or enlarging an existing asset but in order to carry on the existing business and to exploit the First Taxpayer's rights to the fullest extent in the course of its business. True passing off is one element of the matters in dispute, but the affidavits filed in support of the summons in the Supreme Court, if correct, show that substantial disruption of the First Taxpayer's business and feared loss of its employees to the rival group were of primary concern. This appears to me to fall on the revenue side, and within the observations of Taylor J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Duro Travel Goods Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 32; (1953) 87 CLR 524, at 527/8. While I feel some doubt about the First Taxpayer's arguments, I consider I should give it the benefit of the doubt and follow the decision of Taylor J. The First Taxpayer's objection to the failure of the respondent to allow the deduction of the legal expenses of the First Taxpayer in proceedings No NT86/8001 is therefore upheld.

The second proceedings involve the Second Taxpayer and the country abattoir. The Second Taxpayer instituted proceedings in the Equity Division of the Supreme Court of New South Wales seeking a declaration that allotments to two different companies of 150,000 and 75,000 ordinary shares in the abattoir operating company were void and should he set aside, rectification of the abattoir operating company's share register, a declaration that a special resolution increasing the share capital of the abattoir operating company was invalid and ancillary relief. In those proceedings the uncle and his supporters and also two companies which were the proposed allottees of shares were named as defendants. Those proceedings were also settled, after the administration of extensive interrogatories. The legal expenses of the Second Taxpayer amounting to $67,718 were disallowed as a tax deduction by the respondent.

I was informed by Mr Sweeney and it was confirmed in evidence by a director of the Second Taxpayer, that the settlement of the proceedings was precipitated by the purchase by the Second Taxpayer of the total shareholding of the company to which the allotment of 150,000 ordinary shares in the abattoir operating company had been made. Mr Sweeney submitted that these proceedings should be decided in the same way as those relating to the First Taxpayer, because the proceedings and the subsequent purchase of the shares by the Second Taxpayer were for the purpose of protecting continued access to the abattoir by the subsidiary of the Second Taxpayer. However Mr Burns of Counsel for the respondent submitted that the expenditure was essentially of a capital nature and cited the judgment of Dixon J., as he then was, in Hallstroms Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634, referred to in Broken Hill Theatres Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 85 CLR 423 at 434. Counsel also referred me to Sun Newspapers Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337, to John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at 34, 35 per Dixon CJ and to BP Australia Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1966] AC 224, especially at p 265 ((1965) [1965] UKPCHCA 2; 112 CLR 386, 397) and to Federal Commissioner of Taxation v South Australian Battery Makers Pty Ltd 78 ATC 4412 at 4420 per Stephen and Aickin JJ. On the question of the corporation's purpose in incurring the expenditure, Mr Burns referred me to Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4542 at 4551 per Brennan J., and 4558 per Deane and Fisher JJ. Lastly I was referred to PBL Marketing Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 85 ATC 4416, especially to the observations of Yeldham. J bridging pp.4422 and 4423.

In my opinion Mr Burns' submissions are correct. In John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd, Fullagar J observed at p43:

"The lawsuit was an incident of the competition for control, and the costs of defending it were an incident of one step in that competititon - the acquisition of the unissued shares."

In the same case Menzies J at p48 said:

"To make a payment to acquire or to defend the acquisition of a favourable position from which to earn income or to enter into arrangements that will yield income is not in general an outlay incurred either in gaining or in carrying on business for the purpose of gaining assessable income; such a payment in the case of a trading company, occurs at a stage too remote from the receipt of income to be so regarded. To be deductible an outlay must be part of the cost of trading operations to produce income, i.e., it must have the character of a working expense."

In the present matter two points should be noted. The Second Taxpayer is a holding company, it does not carry on the trading operation. Secondly, taxpayers who choose to organise their activities by separating different activities into different corporations and exercise overall control through a holding company, no doubt acquire some tax and business advantages by so doing. The advantage of using artificial personalities carries with it the disadvantage from the taxpayers' viewpoint, that the activities of the separate personalities are the subject of separate accounting and it is not reasonable to predicate a battle to control an abattoir company as part of a business operation conducted through a subsidiary company of meat processing and wholesale meat supply.

In the second proceedings, No NT86/7999, the Second Taxpayer's objections to the assessment are disallowed and the decision under review is affirmed.
