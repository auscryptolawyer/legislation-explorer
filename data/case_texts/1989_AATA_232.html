ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



TAXATION - DEDUCTIONS - CHARACTERIZATION OF EXPENDITURES - CAPITAL AND REVENUE ACCOUNTS - ONCE FOR ALL PAYMENTS - NATURE OF THE ASSET. OR ADVANTAGE - STRUCTURE YIELDING PROFIT - DEFENCE OF TOTAL STRUCTURE - TOTAL RECONSTRUCTION NOT REPAIRS - building destroyed by fire - fire brigade attended - at neighbour's instance - brigade charged for service - whether the charge an allowable deduction.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

No. VT87/659

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

3 November 1989 . Mr B J McMahon (Deputy President)

1. The applicant was the owner of a number of properties

from which he derived rental income. In the year ended 30 June 1983, one of the properties was completely destroyed by fire. It was of insufficient value to warrant insurance, in the opinion of

the applicant and was consequently uninsured. Had it been insured, a sum would have been added to the premium payable to the insurance company to represent a fire brigade levy imposed pursuant to the (Victorian) Metropolitan Fire Brigades Act 1958. In that event, the cost of attendance of the Fire Brigade at the

property would not have been debited to the applicant. The applicant was not aware of the fire at the time of its occurrence. A neighbour called the brigade. Notwithstanding the endeavours of the firemen, the property was completely destroyed.



Notwithstanding the fact that the applicant neither knew about the fire nor called the brigade, an account was sent to him pursuant to s 66 of the said Act.

2. That section is in the following terms -

"s 66(1) The owner of any uninsured property situate within the metropolitan district which is damaged or destroyed by fire shall be liable to pay to the Board the reasonable costs and expenses incurred by the Board in providing fire fighting services for him in relation to that property.

(2) The amount payable under sub-section (1) shall be determined by the Board.

(3) Notice of the amount so determined shall be sent by post to the owner concerned.

(4) The amount specified in the notice shall be payable to the Board by the owner within 28 days after receipt of the notice.

(5) In default of of payment within the time limited by sub-section (4) the amount in question may be recovered in the name of the Board in any court of competent jurisdiction as a civil debt recoverable summarily.

(6) The Board may upon the application of any person who is liable in respect of an amount payable under this section and who in the opinion of the Board is in necessitous circumstances -

(a) remit of excuse the payment of such amount or any part thereof; or

(b) defer the payment of such amount or any part thereof for such time as the Board thinks fit.

(7) Any person who is aggrieved by any determination of the Board under this section may appeal to the Minis'er whose decision shall be final.

(8) The provisions of this section do not apply to the Crown.

(9) In this section "uninsured" means not insured against fire -

(a) with an insurance company registered or

deemed to be registered under section 96 of the Stamps Act 1958; or

(b) under a contract of insurance in respect of which contributions have been paid to the Board under section 44A."

  1. The amount charged was $1,105. In his return of income the applicant claimed a deduction for this amount. The deduction was refused on the ground that the levy or fee was capital in nature. This application is brought to review that objection decision.
  2. The applicant claims to be entitled to the deduction pursuant either to s 51 or s 53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act.
  3. Section 51(1) is in the following terms -

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpcse of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private, or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. Thus, it will be seen that whether the expenditure is claimed under the first or the second limb of the sub-section, the claim will not be allowed if it represents a loss or outgoing of capital or of a capital nature. The applicant sought to derive some comfort from the tact that he owned a number of rental properties and might be said to be carrying on the business of a rentier. However, if the expenditure is to be viewed as capital, or of a capital nature, this argument is

irrelevant.

  1. Section 53(1) is in the following terms -

"Expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income for repairs, not being expenditure of a capital nature, to any premises, or part of premises, plant, machinery, implements, utensils, rolling stock, or articles held, occupied or used by him for the purpose of producing assessable income, or in carrying on a business for that purpose, shall be an allowable deduction."

  1. Once again expenditure of a capital nature is excluded from deductibility under the terms of this sub-section.
  2. The applicant argued that the fee was similar in nature

to insurance. True it was that it was paid after the event,

whereas insurance is usually paid prior to the occurrence of the catastrophe insured against. Nonetheless the applicant considered that the fee paid was analogous to the normal expense of insurance and should, on that account, be deductible.

  1. Alternatively, the argument was put under s 53 that the payment was made in an endeavour to maintain and protect the property from deteriorating further. It was submitted that whether or not the fire brigade was successful was not relevant. The fee for their services (it was submitted) was paid in connection with an attempt to maintain the property in a serviceable condition, and to prevent it from deteriorating further.

11. In my view the arguments of the applicant cannot succeed under either section. It is necessary to characterise the payment of the fee so as to come to a decision as to whether either section has application. One of the ways of characterising it would be to have regard to the purpose of the

applicant in paying the fee. However this aid is not available in the present circumstances. The applicant was not aware of the fire, was not aware that the brigade was called and indeed was not aware that he was obliged to pay the brigade if it was called

in these circumstances if the property was uninsured. In the

circumstances it could not be said that he had any purpose that

was relevant for the present proceedings. It is therefore necessary to characterise the expenditure having regard to its own inherent qualities. The terms of the section authorising the charge make it clear that the money is recoverable only as a

civil debt. The reservations expressed by Brennan J in Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4542 that moneys paid by way of penalties could

not deducted would have no applicatio in the present case. The section does help to characterise the payment, however, by describing the charge as the costs and expenses of providing services "in relation to that property'.

12. One useful criterion that helps one to recognise a

capital payment is the characteristic of a "once for all"

payment. In Sun Newspapers Limited v Federal Commissioner of

Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 361 Dixon J said -

"In the attempt, by no means successful, to find some test or standard by the application of which expenditure or outgoings may be referred to capital account or to revenue account the Courts have relied to some extent upon the difference between an outlay which is recurrent, repeated or continual and that which is final or made "once for all" and

to a still greater extent upon a distinction to be discovered in the nature of the asset or advantage obtained by the outlay. If what is commonly understood as a fixed capital asset is acquired the question answers itself."

The reference to the nature of the "asset or advantage" derives from the speech of Viscount Cave LC in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Limited v Atherton [ 1926] AC 205.

13. It is clear in the present circumstances that the

payment to the fire brigade was a once for all payment. This

alone would distinguish it from the typical insurance premium referred to in the applicant's submission. The payment to the brigade must be characterised by reference to the services which

it provided and for which it is entitled to charge. These services were rendered in an attempt to preserve an asset or advantage of a permanent nature. It is not necessary to give the wide interpretation of "asset or advantage" discussed in Foley Brothers Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1965) 13 ATD 562. A house is tangible property capable of appearing in a balance sheet. It is more than a notional "profit yielding structure". It is literally a structure that yields profit to the owner. In this case the expenditure relates to the defence of the total structure from which profits are earned and the expenditure therefore takes upon itself the character of that

inherent purpose. The purpose was to protect the entire building. In accordance with the principles discussed in Lunnev v Commissioner of Taxation; Harley v Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 CLR 478 at 498 and 499, this characterised the expense.



  1. Having concluded therefore, that the payment was capital in nature, this excludes the operation of either s 51 or s 53. In my view s 53 would also have no operation because the payment could not be characterised as expenditure for repairs. The word "repair" connotes the making good of defects, including the renewal of parts. The definition of the word in Shorter Oxford English Dictionary is as follows -

"Restoration cf some material thing or structure, by the renewal of decayed or worn out parts, by refixing what has become loose or detached: the result of this."



  1. A total reconstruction is not a repair. It was argued that the application cf water and other preservative techniques by the brigade was analogous to preparatory work that is destructive before ordinary repairs are undertaken. It was pointed out that before paint can be applied, the old paint must be scraped off or otherwise removed. This is true where part of the profit yielding structure is restored, renewed or refixed. In the present case, however, the physical techniques that were applied were applied to the whole of the building - "an entirety". There is no evidence that the application of water was confined to any particular area. Indeed if the evidence is that the whole structure was destroyed, it is a fair inference that water and other preservative techniques were applied to the whole of the fabric of the building. What constitutes an entirety when portions of a building are repaired has been the subject of a number of decisions. I cite by way of example W Thomas & Co Ptv Limited v Federal Commissioner of taxation [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 14 ATD 78. In the present case, the facts do not admit of any argument that what was sought to be preserved was anything other than the entire structure.

16. For the above reasons I am of the view that the objection decision under review should be affirmed.
