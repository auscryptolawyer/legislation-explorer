Masterton Corporation Holding Company Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2026] ARTA 160 (9 February 2026)

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2024/11252

Tribunal: Deputy President G Lazanas

Place: Sydney

Date: 9 February 2026

Decision: The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution decides that the objection decision is allowed in full.

Catchwords

TAXATION – income tax – deductibility of temporary display model homes – where display homes are mock ups and intended to be demolished - whether expenses on revenue or capital account – whether deductible under s 8-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) – whether a capital expense of a plant nature pursuant to Division 40 – whether a capital expense of a capital works nature pursuant to Division 43 – held that expenses incurred in constructing temporary display model homes are a marketing expense and not of a capital nature – expenses deductible under s 8-1 – reviewable objection decision set aside and objection allowed

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The applicant, Masterton Corporation Holding Company Pty Ltd (Masterton) is a custom home building company that has been operating in New South Wales for over 60 years. Masterton is in dispute with the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation (Commissioner), as to the income tax treatment of its temporary display model homes. Masterton is the head entity in a tax consolidated group and Masterton Homes Pty Ltd (Masterton Homes) is a subsidiary and member entity of the tax consolidated group and, relevantly, the contract residential builder.
  2. Masterton has applied to the Tribunal seeking a review of the Commissioner’s objection decision dated 14 October 2024 (Objection Decision) disallowing Masterton’s objection against the notice of assessment issued to it for the year ended 30 June 2022 (2022 income year).
  3. The central issue in this proceeding is whether the costs of constructing temporary display model homes are a revenue or operating expense pursuant to s 8-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 1997). There are subsidiary issues which only have to be determined if the conclusion reached is that the expenses were on capital account, including if the construction costs are a capital expense of a “plant” nature pursuant to Division 40 of the ITAA 1997 or a capital expense of a capital works nature pursuant to Division 43 of the ITAA 1997.
  4. The Commissioner’s position was that Masterton’s expenditure on the construction of display model homes is on capital account and that paragraph 8-1(2)(a) of the ITAA 1997 applies to disallow a deduction on the construction costs. Further, according to the Commissioner, the display model homes are “buildings” that are not “plant” and, therefore, Division 43 of the ITAA 1997 applies to the expenditure on the construction of the display model homes, instead of Division 40 and the temporary fully expensing measures in Subdivision 40BB of the Income Tax (Transition Provisions) Act 1997 (Cth) (ITTP Act). If it were “plant”, the Commissioner conceded that Subdivision 40BB of the ITTP Act applied to allow an instant write off.
  5. For the reasons explained below, I have decided that the expenditure incurred by Masterton in the construction of the temporary display model homes is of a revenue nature and deductible under s 8-1(1) of the ITAA 1997 on the basis that it is a marketing expense which is not of a capital nature. The parties agreed before me that if I decided the expenditure is on revenue account, then the subsidiary questions do not need to be decided.

THE EVIDENCE

  1. References to documents throughout this decision are to the documents which were contained in an agreed Tribunal Book (TB) before the Tribunal. They included the respective Statements of Facts, Issues and Contentions of the parties,[1] the T-Documents filed by the Commissioner[2] and, in addition, the written evidence comprised of the following statements of witnesses for the applicant:
    (a) Statements of James Crestani dated 8 May 2025[3] and 25 September 2025;[4]

    (b) Statement of Paul Bannister dated 8 May 2025;[5]

    (c) Statements of Amir Awad dated 9 May 2025[6] and 26 November 2025.[7]

  2. All the evidence was accepted as being uncontroversial and none of the witnesses were required for cross-examination. It is appropriate at this juncture to set out that evidence and my findings.

Mr Crestani

  1. Mr Crestani is a chartered accountant and tax agent in the firm Crestani Partners. Masterton has been a client of Mr Crestani’s since 1985.
  2. Based on Masterton’s instructions to him, from the 1990s to date, the nature of the display model homes had not changed, although the product lifecycles of the products varied.[8] Mr Crestani stated that the display model homes which he referred to as “mock ups” were temporary representations of designs of houses offered for sale by Masterton. They could not be lived in and were always intended to be destroyed and replaced with updated designs once the previous design was no longer marketable.[9] Mr Crestani further stated that Masterton not only builds temporary mock ups, but it also builds fully functional traditional display homes.[10] Only the temporary mock ups or display model homes (as referred to in this decision) are in issue in this proceeding.
  3. Mr Crestani stated that before the 2022 income year, the Commissioner had consistently treated the mock ups as being “plant” under Division 40 of the ITAA 1997 with an effective average life of 7 years. This view was not challenged by the Commissioner in comprehensive tax risk reviews conducted by him in or about 2006 to 2008, and again in or about 2012 to 2016.[11] The Commissioner changed his view in or about 2022. He decided to treat the mock ups as capital works under Division 43 of the ITAA 1997. That income tax treatment was how Masterton had for many years expensed the other kind of display homes, namely, the fully functional traditional display homes.[12]
  4. Mr Crestani explained that in Masterton’s audited accounts, it accounted for the fair value of the land at Warwick Farm the subject of a lease from a related entity in the Masterton group to Masterton Homes on the basis of independent valuations that had been obtained. He referred to four of these valuations, copies of which were attached to his statement.[13] In summary, the valuations proceeded on the assumptions that the mock ups would need to be destroyed, or that the mock ups were unlettable and vacant possession of the land was to be provided on termination of the lease, as well as noting that the land was zoned for general industrial use. These assumptions were not challenged.
  5. Mr Crestani stated that the reason for the land value not including the mock ups was because they were not considered to be improvements to the land. Mr Crestani reasoned and I accept that the mock ups had no value to anyone other than Masterton as they were Masterton’s marketing products and, moreover, could not be used as residential accommodation as persons were not able to reside in them due to the fact they did not comply with applicable regulations.[14]
  6. In his second witness statement filed and served on or about 25 September 2025, Mr Crestani noted that Masterton’s original tax return for the 2022 income year was filed on the basis the mock ups were “plant” per Division 40 of the ITAA 1997.[15] Following a request by Masterton for the Commissioner’s opinion on whether an instant tax write off was available in the 2022 income year under the temporary fully expensing measures in Subdivision 40BB of the ITTP Act, the Commissioner stated the mock ups should be treated as “capital works” per Division 43 of the ITAA 1997 in the 2022 income year and in future tax returns. Consequently, Masterton lodged an amended tax return in accordance with the Commissioner’s view and then lodged an objection to the notice of amended assessment issued by the Commissioner dated 20 October 2023 as it did not agree with the Commissioner.[16]
  7. Mr Crestani set out in detail in his second witness statement the differing consequential tax amendments that would be required based on the possible income tax treatment of the display model homes. Relevantly, Mr Crestani stated that if the s 8-1 treatment is correct, and the construction costs are allowable deductions, the assessable income in the 2022 income year would be $0 but the carry forward losses on the amended assessment would have to be reduced by $111,745. It followed that, on that premise, while the amended assessment issued by the Commissioner would therefore not be excessive, it would nevertheless be incorrect.[17] The Commissioner did not dispute the numbers.

Mr Bannister

  1. Mr Bannister is the General Manager of Masterton Homes and has worked there for the past 8.5 years with 14 years’ experience in residential construction. Broadly, Mr Bannister explained that there are two types of so called “display homes” (or display model homes as referred to in this decision), the first being the temporary kind which were constructed at Warwick Farm, and the subject of this tax dispute. The second kind were permanent display homes built on greenfield sites for ultimate sale to purchasers.[18] The latter are not contested in these proceedings.
  2. Mr Bannister explained it was inaccurate to call a temporary display a display “home” as it is incapable of being lived in.[19] It offers no amenities such as running water and it is illegal to live in a temporary display home.[20]
  3. Mr Bannister’s evidence was that the temporary display homes are used as a marketing advertisement of the stock of homes Masterton offered for sale and that a temporary display is only designed to exist as long as the design it represents is available for purchase.[21]
  4. Mr Bannister further stated, and I find, as follows:
    (a) “At Warwick Farm, there are only temporary displays and no houses of a permanent nature.”[22]

    (b) “The temporary displays in Warwick Farms are means by which we use to show and market our design offerings. They show our build quality, feature inclusions (flooring and tiling, for example) and the general feel of the actual homes. However, the display homes are... not capable of being bought as residential housing and cannot be lived in.”[23]

    (c) “Once a temporary display is no longer marketable, it is destroyed. ...all temporary displays are built with the intention that they will be destroyed.”[24]

    (d) “Some designs last only 4 years (such as the Carolyne design), or 6 years (such as the Inglewood design) or 7 years (such as the Symphony design), though others last longer. It is all determined by market preferences for the product... I would say on average, in the 2000s, 10 years for those we have knocked down to date.”[25]

    (e) “... the temporary displays are fine for the purposes of a temporary structure, but we would not sell them as a home to anyone because they have not undergone the geotechnical certification to last for at least 30 years...”[26]

    (f) “One way of demonstrating how these homes are built not just superficially differently (for example, the lights are not connected to accessible physical light switches) but fundamentally structurally differently, is that the temporary displays do not have piers. They are built on merely compacted ground with a concrete slab on top.”[27]

    (g) “The display homes are not habitable, because

    (i) No mains water is connected ...

    (ii) No sewage is connected (in fact the toilets are filled with concrete to prevent their use) ...

    (iii) Wet areas with the temporary displays are not waterproofed ...

    (iv) There are few functioning power points ...

    (v) Ovens, stoves and dishwashers are not connected to electricity (or water) and cannot function for those reasons ...

    (vi) The lighting in the house is all centrally controlled by a sensor and timer ... This is obviously an improper configuration for a normal home, but is sensible for a showroom environment. ...

    (vii) The glass and insulation as well as other construction choices do not meet the seven-star requirement that is required for all new homes. ...

    (viii) Each temporary display is not electrically metered ...

    (ix) There is no certification process undertaken, meaning that there is no possibility of Certificates of Occupancy being issued for the display homes...”[28]

  5. As the display model homes do not have proper foundations nor plumbing, Mr Bannister stated it was cheaper and easier to engage in the regular cycle of demolishing and rebuilding display models, as required by market demand, than to build real houses. An attachment to Mr Bannister’s witness statement marked ‘Tab B’ described the various display model homes constructed between 2000 and 2022 and the dates of demolition, where applicable, as well as the costs of construction. The costs ranged between about $105,000 to $810,000.[29]

Mr Awad

  1. Mr Awad is the Group Chief Executive Officer of Masterton. Mr Awad stated that Masterton is responsible for the design of the houses and that customers enter contracts with Masterton to build a home. Masterton purchases all building materials and organises the building of the customer’s home. Masterton then provides the statutory warranties and organises the certification.[30]
  2. Mr Awad stated that the Warwick Farm temporary display homes are a unique marketing tool in that a potential customer can come and experience the layout of each of the designs and thereby help them visualise the designs on offer.[31] Mr Awad stated they were temporary displays and only ever intended to last for the length of the product lifecycle of the design, and their lifecycle was determined by sales. Where sales were strong, Masterton continued to market the product and the temporary display home was part of that marketing strategy and where the sales were low, the display home was destroyed and replaced with another temporary display home.[32]
  3. Mr Awad referred to the average display home as lasting roughly 10 years noting again that no temporary display home is built to last, rather it was “a marketing tool for a limited product lifecycle.”[33] He provided the following examples: the ‘Carolyne’ design lasted for 4 years; the ‘Inglewood’ design lasted for 6 years; and the ‘Haven 2’ design lasted for 7 years.[34] Between 2014 and 2022, 11 temporary display homes had been destroyed and replaced, averaging 1.4 temporary display homes per year such that “the cost of building new temporary homes is a recurring marketing or promotional expense.”[35]
  4. Mr Awad stated the expenses were not a once and for all expense.[36] Mr Awad also stated that the expense was not one that concerns the expansion of the productive capacity of Masterton’s business as the temporary display homes were not permanent and were replaced as needed. He stated the expense was about the core business of building residential homes.[37] Each display model does not create some new business but supports sales in an existing and long-standing business of contract building houses.[38]
  5. Mr Awad explained that approximately 60% of sales in New South Wales are from people who visited the temporary display homes at Warwick Farm.[39] The display homes are considered an essential and very successful part of Masterton’s marketing for sales.[40] In Mr Awad’s view, they served the same promotional purpose as television, print or virtual reality advertisements.[41]
  6. Mr Awad attached to his first statement the lease of the Warwick Farm site where the temporary display homes are built and pointed out that if Masterton were to leave the site it would be required to destroy all the temporary display homes.[42]
  7. In his second witness statement which was provided after receipt of the Commissioner’s written outline of submissions, Mr Awad explained that the ‘Macarthur Manor’ - constructed in 2000 and closed in June 2020 - was not demolished at that time due to cost and cash flow issues. It was scheduled for demolition at the end of November 2025. Mr Awad also clarified that Warwick Farm had 16 models on display at the time of his second statement but noted the number varied based on how many products are offered to the public. The process of building and demolishing models within the Warwick Farm display village had been recurrent and repeated for decades.[43]

THE PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

  1. As stated at [10] above, the issue in dispute in this proceeding arose from the Commissioner undertaking his regular comprehensive risk reviews of Masterton and then changing his mind about the income tax treatment of display model homes in or about 2023 with respect to the 2022 income year. As also stated at [13] above, Masterton lodged an amended tax return in accordance with the Commissioner’s revised view and then lodged an objection to the notice of amended assessment dated 20 October 2023 as it did not agree with the Commissioner.
  2. Relevantly, in its notice of objection dated 15 December 2023, Masterton’s grounds of objection only referred to the Commissioner having erred in denying the application of the temporary full expensing provisions of the ITTP Act and instead applying Division 43 of the ITAA 1997. That is, Masterton did not initially rely on the ground that the expenses related to the display model homes were deductible under s 8-1(1) of the ITAA 1997. However, Masterton’s contentions in the proceedings evolved to include this argument which the Commissioner quite properly, did not oppose. Accordingly, I made an order pursuant to s 14ZZK(a) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA) permitting Masterton to rely on the additional ground that the cost of the display model homes were a revenue or operating expense pursuant to s 8-1 of the ITAA 1997.

IS THE EXPENDITURE INCURRED ON THE CONSTRUCTION OF THE DISPLAY MODEL HOMES DEDUCTIBLE UNDER SECTION 8-1 OF THE ITAA 1997?

  1. The starting point is s 8-1 of the ITAA which provides:
    (1) You can deduct from your assessable income any loss or outgoing to the extent that:

    (a) it is incurred in gaining or producing your assessable income; or

    (b) it is necessarily incurred in carrying on a * business for the purpose of gaining or producing your assessable income.

    Note: Division 35 prevents losses from non - commercial business activities that may contribute to a tax loss being offset against other assessable income.

    (2) However, you cannot deduct a loss or outgoing under this section to the extent that:

    (a) it is a loss or outgoing of capital, or of a capital nature; or

    (b) it is a loss or outgoing of a private or domestic nature; or

    (c) it is incurred in relation to gaining or producing your * exempt income or your * non - assessable non - exempt income; or

    (d) a provision of this Act prevents you from deducting it.

    For a summary list of provisions about deductions, see section 12 - 5.

    (3) A loss or outgoing that you can deduct under this section is called a general deduction .

    For the effect of the GST in working out deductions, see Division 27.

    Note If you receive an amount as insurance, indemnity or other recoupment of a loss or outgoing that you can deduct under this section, the amount may be included in your assessable income: see Subdivision 20 - A.

  2. The Commissioner accepted that expenditure incurred by Masterton in the construction of the display model homes was both incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, satisfying paragraph 8-1(1)(a) of the ITAA 1997 and necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing your assessable income, satisfying paragraph 8-1(1)(b). The Commissioner also accepted that the expenditure is not a loss or outgoing of a private or domestic nature (paragraph 8-1(2)(b) of the ITAA 1997) nor was it incurred in relation to gaining or producing exempt income (paragraph 8-1(2)(c)). Additionally, paragraph 8-1(2)(d) did not apply.
  3. As stated above, the primary issue between the parties was whether the display model construction expenses were of a revenue or capital nature per paragraph 8-1(2)(a) of the ITAA 1997. The Commissioner argued that the paragraph applied, and that Masterton was not entitled to a deduction because the expenditure was of capital or of a capital nature.
  4. The Commissioner submitted that the question as to whether expenditure falls under revenue or capital account requires a weighing of factors including the form, purpose and effect of the expenditure, the benefit derived from it and its relationship to the structure, as distinct from the conduct, of a business: Ausnet Transmission Group Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [2015] HCA 25; (2015) 255 CLR 439 (Ausnet) at [15] per French CJ, Kiefel and Bell JJ. In Ausnet, their Honours pointed out that the evaluative judgment required to distinguish between expenditure on capital or revenue account is made under the guidance of approaches developed in cases by the court over many years.[44] Further, some of the abovementioned factors may point in one direction and some in another and that there are no definitive and specific criteria.[45]
  5. The Commissioner principally relied on Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 73; (1938) 61 CLR 337 (Sun Newspapers) at 359, where Dixon J stated:

The distinction between expenditure and outgoings on revenue account and on capital account corresponds with the distinction between the business entity, structure or organization set up or established for the earning of profit and the process by which such an organization operates to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the difference between the outlay and returns representing profit or loss.

  1. The Commissioner also referred to the following three matters as identified by Dixon J in Sun Newspapers at 363, to be considered in determining whether expenses are incurred on revenue or capital account:
(a) the character of the advantage sought, and in this its lasting qualities may play a part,

(b) the manner in which it is to be used, relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and under the former head recurrence may play its part,

(c) the means adopted to obtain it; that is, by providing a periodical reward or outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payment or by making a final provision or payment so as to secure future use or enjoyment.
  1. In Sun Newspapers, the issue was whether a payment made to a competitor newspaper to prevent it from introducing a new and more competitive newspaper which was structured as a lease of the competitor’s printing and related equipment for a period of three years combined with a promise not to publish a newspaper was a revenue expense. The majority of the High Court held that the expense was on capital account because the taxpayer achieved a lasting benefit by effectively buying out the competitor. Dixon J reached the same decision but, as extracted at [33] above, he formulated a test based on whether the expense was for the business entity, structure or organisation or part of the income earning process. The structure versus process ‘test’ and the three factors which Dixon J set out in Sun Newspapers (see [34] above) are the leading guidance for the capital versus revenue analysis of expenditure. Both the Commissioner and taxpayers have come to rely on the same guidance in tax disputes.
  2. The Commissioner also relied on the following dicta of Dixon J in Hallstroms Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 (Hallstroms) at 648:

What is an outgoing of capital and what is an outgoing on account of revenue depends on what the expenditure is calculated to effect from a practical and business point of view, rather than upon the juristic classification of the legal rights, if any, secured, employed or exhausted in the process.

  1. Hallstroms concerned the deductibility of legal expenses incurred in preventing the extension of a patent of a competitor in the manufacture of refrigerators. This was in circumstances where Hallstroms had positioned itself to commence manufacture in anticipation of the competitor’s patent expiring. The majority of the High Court (Latham CJ, Starke and Williams JJ) held that the legal expenses were of a revenue nature as Hallstroms did not acquire an asset or add to its profit-yielding capital structure of the business, nor did it gain an enduring advantage; rather, it merely succeeded in maintaining its existing position. In a dissenting judgment, Dixon J found the expense to be of a capital nature based on its connection with the protection of the income-earning structure of the taxpayer’s business applying the principles he had earlier set out in Sun Newspapers.
  2. Against the background of the above cases, the Commissioner argued that the expenditure by Masterton for each display home is final or made once and for all and further that each display home built by Masterton should be viewed as a single outlay. Specifically, the Commissioner contended that:
    (a) each display home is part of the capital expenditure for establishing the new product line that the display home is a model for;

    (b) the display homes and the display village are an integral part of Masterton’s marketing for generating sales;

    (c) each display home is intended to last for the life of the product that it is selling; and

    (d) each display home is exploited by Masterton for inviting potential customers into the display home to view the building options and selections available to customers and this method of exploitation does not meaningfully contribute to consumption of the useful life of the display home during the product’s intended lifecycle.

  3. Therefore, the Commissioner submitted that the display model homes built by Masterton were part of its profit yielding structure as they provided an enduring advantage to Masterton.
  4. The Commissioner acknowledged that although the display model homes built by Masterton were not permanent, they were nevertheless intended to endure for a considerable time. In particular, the Commissioner argued the character of the advantage sought by the construction of the display model homes was the bringing into existence a tangible marketing aid designed to endure for many years. The Commissioner pointed to the example of the ‘Villina’ display model constructed in 2008 which remained on display as at the start of December 2025. While the Commissioner accepted that the longevity of a display model home depended mainly on consumer demand, the Commissioner observed that display model homes are clearly not so temporary as to require replacement during the lifecycle of the product it is marketing.
  5. Separately, the Commissioner acknowledged that expenditure on the construction of display model homes has the same functional purpose as other forms of marketing such as television advertisements but submitted that display model homes were an enduring marketing aid for the single product that was being promoted. He explained that this was because the construction of a display model home is a once and for all expenditure for the life of each product design. That is, the Commissioner emphasised that there was only ever one display home per product design constructed at Warwick Farm. Each display model home, once constructed, is repeatedly used and is only demolished at the end of the display model home’s usefulness once Masterton has decided to discontinue the product line being marketed.
  6. I disagree with the Commissioner’s characterisation of the expenditure as being of a capital nature and have concluded the expenditure was a marketing expense of a revenue nature. The case which in my view was most analogous to the present factual matrix and which provided the most useful guidance, including as to the application of the ‘test’ and three factors set out in Sun Newspapers is National Australia Bank Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1997] FCA 1394; (1997) 80 FCR 352 (National Australia Bank). In that case, the Full Court of the Federal Court (Northrop, French and Sundberg JJ) considered the deductibility of a single large payment made by a bank to market certain types of home loans to a segment of a community.
  7. In National Australia Bank, the taxpayer was a major, long-standing bank with a well-established existing framework that carried on a retail banking business, including making home loans. The bank paid $42 million to the Commonwealth for the exclusive right, for a period of 15 years, to participate as the lender under a scheme offering housing loan assistance to members of the defence forces with respect to home loans. In this way, the bank gained exclusive access to the members of the defence forces which was seen as completely consistent with its marketing strategy which included targeting segments of the community that were likely to produce valuable long term relationships for the bank.
  8. The evidence was that the bank was employing the same philosophy which it pursued in all its marketing, advertising and sponsorship activities in making the payment of $42 million for the right to be the exclusive lender for the Commonwealth and its scheme for members of the defence forces, namely, growing its income flows with targeted segments of the community. It was common ground that the question before the primary judge, and before the Full Court, was what was the payment calculated to effect from a practical or business point of view. Furthermore, it was common ground that the character of the advantage sought by the bank was the key factor in determining the character of the payment and whether it was on revenue or capital account.[46]
  9. The Commissioner argued in National Australia Bank that the payment secured for the bank a monopoly over a specific, valuable class of business and that was part of the business structure set up by the bank for the earning of profit. The Commissioner argued it was an asset or advantage for the enduring benefit of the bank’s business in reliance on the decision of Dixon J in Sun Newspapers at 358-361. It was also argued by the Commissioner that the payment was a one-off payment to secure the future use or enjoyment of a right, and the period covered by the payment was substantial: Sun Newspapers at 363 per Dixon J.
  10. The Full Court held that from a practical and business point of view what the bank sought to achieve was the expansion of its home loan customer base.[47] With respect to the argument that the $42 million was a once and for all payment and that the absence of recurrence suggested it was an outgoing is of a capital nature, the Full Court held that was not conclusive: Sun Newspapers at 362-363 per Dixon J. Significantly, with reference to the matter of whether the payment altered the framework within which income-producing activities are carried out, the Full Court held that the payment to the Commonwealth did not enlarge the framework within which the bank carried on its ordinary activities of borrowing and lending money. Rather, the payment was incurred as part of the process by which the bank operated to obtain regular returns and increased the flows of the bank’s ordinary lending activities.
  11. The Full Court noted that the primary judge was not attracted by the marketing expense argument. However, the Full Court reversed this on appeal and held at 365-366, as follows:
    In our view the payment in question is in the nature of a marketing expense. ... The payment secured access to a new body of customers ... Its nature as a commission or marketing expense points to the payment having a revenue rather than a capital aspect. It is true, as the primary judge pointed out, that even advertising expenses can be of a capital nature, as where the expenses are non-recurrent and directed towards the establishment of a market for a new business or new product: Sun Newspapers at 360-361. But the Bank’s business is of long standing, and its existing framework well established. Any expense which it incurs which is of an advertising, promotional or marketing nature will prima face be on revenue account.
  12. Significantly, the Full Court also rejected the proposition that the bank’s expense changed the profit yielding structure or framework of its business and held at 365:
    In Commissioner of Taxes v Nchanga Consolidated Copper Mines Ltd [1964] AC 948 at 960 Viscount Radcliﬀe spoke of “the importance of observing a demarcation between the cost of creating, acquiring or enlarging the permanent ... structure of which the income is to be the produce or fruit and the cost of earning that income itself or performing the income-earning operations”. And in Foley Brothers Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) (1965) 13 ATD 562 at 563 Kitto, Taylor and Menzies JJ said the “true contrast is between altering the framework within which income-producing activities are for the future to be carried on and the taking of a step as part of those activities within the framework”.

    The payment to the Commonwealth did not enlarge the framework within which the Bank carried on its ordinary activities of borrowing and lending money. It was incurred as part of the process by which the Bank operates to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the diﬀerence between the outlay and the returns representing profit or loss.

  13. Applying that analysis to Masterton’s factual matrix, first, the purpose or object of Masterton’s expenditure in constructing the temporary display model homes is to advertise and market Masterton’s long-standing business of building and selling residential homes. Masterton was incurring the expenses related to the construction of the display model homes at the display village to show the homes it built for sale, and to increase its sales. Masterton has conducted the same business for decades in the same way that the bank in National Australia Bank had been in the retail banking business for a long time and simply added to its income flows, home loans targeted to a new segment of the community. Similarly, Masterton also added updated designs to support its sales. Accordingly, the marketing expense, even if large and even if one off, is prima facie on revenue account.
  14. Secondly, Masterton incurred regular expenses of building and demolishing temporary display model homes, such that approximately 1.4 models per annum were demolished and rebuilt to promote updated designs for customers to choose from in the eight years between 2014 and 2022. The products were not entirely different products such as the construction of other structures, for example, bridges. Masterton’s long-standing core business is as a contract builder of residential homes.
  15. Thirdly, each display model home is constructed as part of its existing framework of marketing and promoting sales of homes at the Warwick Farm display village which has been in existence for a considerable period, and which has successfully accounted for approximately 60% of all sales. Each display model home merely markets the latest design to customers until it is replaced by a new design. People come, inspect and experience the various models at the display village to find a design that suits them. The expenditure on construction of the display model homes does not enlarge the business structure of Masterton to build and sell homes. This is because Masterton does not alter or add to its structure or capacity such as by acquiring assets or entering a new market. The display model home expenses were incurred as part of the continuous process by which Masterton operates to increase its ordinary sales and obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the diﬀerence between which represents profit or loss.
  16. Fourthly, in the period before the 2022 income year, Masterton had replaced more than one old model with a newer model in each year, and its outlays have been recurrent and continuous over a significant time as part of an established business (see [22] above). The marketing outlays in terms of construction costs are, in other words, a gradual expense, over time, that has proved to be very successful as part of Masterton’s overall advertising and marketing strategy. The evidence established that not one display model home is made once and for all, but they are updated to meet a continuous demand. The outlay on display model homes is recurrent, repeated or continual over many decades. The marketing expense would be on revenue account in accordance with the judgment of Dixon J in Sun Newspapers at 360-362, where he explained how marketing expenses are treated in the context of the capital and revenue question:
    In the beginning the goodwill may have been established by a great initial outlay upon a widespread advertising campaign carried out upon a scale which it was not intended to maintain or repeat. The outlay might properly be considered to be of a capital nature. On the other hand the goodwill may have been gradually established by continual advertisement over a period of years growing in extent as it proved successful. In that case the expenditure upon advertising might be regarded as an ordinary business outgoing on account of revenue... courts have relied to some extent upon the diﬀerence between an outlay which is recurrent, repeated or continual and that which is final or made “once for all”... But the idea of recurrence and the idea of endurance or continuance over a duration of time both depend on degree and comparison. As to the first it has been said it is not a question of recurring every year or every accounting period; but “the real test is between expenditure which is made to meet a continuous demand, as opposed to an expenditure which is made once for all”...
  1. Fifthly, in Case J39 (1977) 77 ATC 363 (Case J39), a Board of Review decision, and the only decision able to be identified by the parties with respect to the tax treatment of functional display homes (not display model homes), it was held by the majority that the sales of display homes did not represent the realisation of capital assets but were acts done in the carrying on of a business. The Chairman, who was in the majority, relevantly observed:
    The marketing technique adopted by the company involved the presentation to the public of fully furnished display homes built to diﬀerent designs and exhibited in a small group called a village or display centre (at [3])

    ... an exhibition village such as that built by the company would have a definite life – it becomes obsolete because of changes in design and so on (at [6])

  2. Before arriving at the conclusion that the profit in issue was on revenue account, the Chairman stated in Case J39 at [14]:
    Having regard to the nature of the company’s business, namely, the building of project homes, the sale of project homes built for display purposes on land owned by the company represent in my view not the realization of capital assets but, to use the words of the Lord Justice Clark in the oft quoted Californian Copper Syndicate case 5 T.C. 159 at p. 166, acts done in what is truly the carrying on or carrying out of a business. The sales of display homes are to be seen as incidents of its business viewed as a whole. The homes at no stage despite the use to which they were put, became part of the company’s fixed capital.
  3. Masterton submitted, and I agree, that the argument was even stronger for the outlay on display model homes to be treated as being on revenue account. This is because the display homes in issue in Case J39 were permanent and capable of being sold as residential houses whereas the display model homes at the Warwick Farm display village can only ever be marketing expenses. Each display model home is constructed in a disposable fashion in that they have temporary foundations, and no permanent utilities are connected so each can be easily destroyed. They are not able to be sold as residential houses (see [16], [18] and [19] above).
  4. Finally, while the Commissioner acknowledged the display model homes in issue have the same function and purpose as television advertisements and are solely used for the purpose of marketing activities, he nevertheless maintained that the practical and business effect of the display model homes was to market each of Masterton’s product lines. In this way, he sought to narrow his focus on the individual product lines and ignore the entirety of Masterton’s business activities. The totality of the evidence established there were up to 18 display model homes at the Warwick Farm display village and more than one display model was replaced on average each year in the eight year period before the 2022 income year, as part of the recurrent, repeated or continuing marketing expense of advertising the sales of residential homes generally. As recounted by the Full Court in National Australia Bank at 363:
    [i]n order to determine whether an amount is of a revenue or a capital nature one has to ‘make both a wide survey and an exact scrutiny of the taxpayer’s activities’[48]
  5. Plainly, the commercial context was Masterton’s business as a contract builder, building and selling residential homes. From a practical and business point of view, the models are rebuilt to meet changing consumer demands and support sales. The Masterton business treats and always has treated the display model homes as temporary. The expenditure on the display model homes was recurrent to meet a continuous demand and to increase the income flows from the sales of residential houses.

CONCLUSION

  1. For the reasons set out above, the expenses incurred by Masterton with respect to the display model homes were not a loss or outgoing of capital, or of a capital nature such that paragraph 8-1(2)(a) applied. The expenses were marketing expenses on revenue account and therefore deductible under s 8-1 of the ITAA 1997.
  2. As stated at [14] above, it was not in dispute that that if the correct treatment of the display model homes is a revenue expense under s 8-1, then the assessable income for Masterton although $0 will have been increased by $111,875 in the 2022 income year but the carry forward losses reduced by $111,745. Therefore, the amended assessment issued by the Commissioner to Masterton was otherwise incorrect for the purposes of s 14ZZK(b(i) of the TAA.

DECISION

  1. Masterton discharged its burden of proving that the assessment issued to it was incorrect and what it should be under s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA. It follows that the objection decision, being the decision under review, must be set aside and substituted with the decision that the objection be allowed in full.
