ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS DIVISION )



Re ANDREW CHARLES PICKFORD

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



Decision 13281



DECISION



Tribunal Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)

Date 14 September 1998

Place Adelaide

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



(Signed)

D.J. TROWSE

(Member)



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - whether amount paid at commencement of employment is assessable income in terms of sub-s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - income within normal concepts as distinct from a capital gain - loss of personal or proprietary rights

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.25(1), Division 13A, Part IIIA, 160M(3)(b), 160M(7), 160ZA

Jarrold (Inspector of Taxes) v Boustead (1964) 3 ALL ER 76

Pritchard (Inspector of Taxes) v Arundale (1971) 3 ALL ER 1011

Glantre Engineering Ltd v Goodhand (1983) 1 ALL ER 542

Hochstrasser (Inspector of Taxes) v Mayes 38 Tax Cases 673

Edwards (Inspector of Taxes) v Bairstow [1955] UKHL 3; (1955) 3 ALL ER 48

REASONS FOR DECISION

14 September 1998 D. J. Trowse (Member)



  1. This application relates to a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation (the Commissioner) to disallow an objection dated 29 January 1997 against a private ruling dated 3 January 1997 in respect of the financial year ended 30 June 1996. In issue is the manner of assessing tax on a lump sum payment of $20,000 received by the applicant from his new employer and which was described in a letter setting out the principal terms of employment as “compensation for the potential capital gain that may have been available to you in respect of shares to which you would have been entitled under the [E Australia Ltd] executive option scheme if you had remained an employee of [E Ltd]”.
  2. The core of the problem is the difference of opinion as to which sections of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act) have application in the bringing to account of the amount of $20,000 as assessable income. The applicant contends that the receipt is not income according to ordinary concepts but, instead, is a capital gain that is caught by the provisions of Part IIIA of the Act and more particularly sub-ss.160M(3)(b) and 160M(7). It seems that a favourable decision on this issue would permit the applicant to offset this gain against capital losses that are available to him for income tax purposes. The opposing view taken by the Commissioner is that the amount received is income according to ordinary concepts and thus assessable in terms of sub-s.25(1). Furthermore, the Commissioner relies upon s.160ZA - which states that the capital gains tax provisions do not apply to the extent that the gain is assessable under another provision of the Act - for the opinion that the capital gains tax provisions have no application.
  3. The parties had agreed that no formal hearing be scheduled and that the Tribunal make a determination on the papers lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, together with an agreed statement of facts and statements of facts and contentions lodged by both the applicant and the Commissioner.
  4. The Tribunal accepts that the facts pertaining to this reference are as follows:
    • In August 1993 the applicant was an employee of E Ltd.
    • In May 1995, the applicant was granted the option for no consideration to purchase 90,000 ordinary shares in the employer’s parent company, pursuant to an employee share scheme.
    • The original exercise price was $1.45 but was reduced to $1.33 for reasons which are not relevant to this dispute.
    • The applicant’s right to exercise the option was restricted - the applicant could exercise the options over a period of five years with 30,000 options being exercisable at the end of the first, second and third years; however, if not exercised in any of those years, any or all of the options could be exercised in the fourth or fifth years and, finally, the options would immediately lapse if the applicant was no longer an employee of E Ltd.
    • In early June 1995 the applicant received an unsolicited approach from a third party acting on behalf of W Ltd concerning the prospect of joining that company.
    • Further discussions and meetings between the applicant, the third party and senior personnel of W Ltd took place during June, July and August 1995.
    • The applicant had advised the third party on more than one occasion of his participation in the employee share scheme and of the consequences which would flow if he terminated his employment with E Ltd and that the loss to him resulting from the lapsing of the options, being the difference between exercise price and the market value of the shares, would be approximately $30,000.
    • In late August 1995, the third party advised the applicant that W Ltd was prepared to offer a salary package in excess of that paid by E Ltd and that, also, it would pay to the applicant $20,000 as a one off payment to compensate him for the loss of capital gain related to the options.
    • In a written offer of employment dated 1 September 1995 W Ltd, after reciting details of the proposed “cash remuneration package” consisting of salary, superannuation and company vehicle, undertook the following additional commitment:
“In addition to the amount disclosed above, a payment of $20,000 is to be made as compensation for the potential capital gain that may have been available to you in respect of shares to which you would have been entitled under the (E (Australia) Ltd) executive option scheme if you had remained an employee of (E Ltd).”


  • The applicant resigned his employment with E Ltd on 1 September 1995.
  • The applicant commenced employment with W Ltd on 3 October 1995.
  • A lump sum payment of $20,000 less tax instalment deductions made by W Ltd was paid to applicant on 19 October 1995.
  1. The parties have differing views as to the reasons for the payment of the $20,000. The applicant maintains that “there is a direct relationship between the payment made and the loss of the options and that, in the circumstances, the $20,000 was a reasonable valuation because the value of the option rarely equals the variation between the market value of the share and the exercise price of the option”. Statements in the documents before the Tribunal appear to indicate that the discount of $10,000 was in the recognition of the fact that, at that time, the applicant was not able to convert any of the 90,000 options. The contrasting opinion of the Commissioner is that the payment by W Ltd was made as an inducement to secure the employment services of the applicant and that the existence of the potential capital gain merely provided the means to quantify the incentive payment forming part of the overall employment package.
  2. On the matter of “reasonable valuation”, the Tribunal finds that the potential for gain upon exercising the option was subject to several unknown factors, such as, the financial ability of the applicant to convert the options into shares and, importantly, the market value of the shares at the various conversion dates. More significantly, it seems that any surplus resulting from such a plan would have constituted assessable income in terms of Division 13A of the Act and that, ordinarily, a liability of this kind would have caused an additional abatement in value. The assessability of gains under Division 13A was not referred to in the papers nor was it postulated that any substitute of an amount that would have attracted the operation of that division should of itself be treated as assessable income.
  3. A paucity of Australian case law on the treatment of amounts received at point of employment tends to a consideration of principles which have emerged from the English cases of Jarrold (Inspector of Taxes) v Boustead (1964) 3 ALL ER 76, Pritchard (Inspector of Taxes) v Arundale (1971) 3 ALL ER 1011 and Glantre Engineering Ltd v Goodhand (1983) 1 ALL ER 542.
  4. In the first of those cases, Mr Boustead, an amateur rugby union player, signed a contract to provide his services to a rugby league team in exchange for match payments. At the same time, it was agreed, in accordance with the by-laws of the Rugby League, that he relinquish his amateur status and that he be paid a signing-on fee of £3,000. The matter to be determined was whether the signing-on fee was assessable to income tax under Schedule E of the Income Tax Act 1952. The Court of Appeal held that the fee was a capital sum paid as consideration for the relinquishing of amateur status for life, viz, paid in compensation for the loss of a permanent asset, and thus not taxable as an emolument under the Schedule.
  5. The second case related to the assessability of the value of certain shares transferred to Mr Arundale under a tripartite service agreement entered into between himself, the employing company and Mr Lowe, who was a director of and controlling shareholder in the company. Besides reciting the more normal clauses usually found in such a document, it continued on to provide that, in consideration of Mr Arundale undertaking to serve the company, Mr Lowe was to transfer to him 4,000 shares in the company. It seems that the offer of shares was needed to attract Mr Arundale away from the benefits he was enjoying as a senior partner in a successful firm of chartered accountants.
  6. In the process of deciding that the Crown had failed to show that the transfer of shares constituted an emolument from an office or employment or was in the nature of a reward for or in reference to service, Megarry J of the Chancery Division referred to the following comments of Upjohn J in Hochstrasser (Inspector of Taxes) v Mayes (38 Tax Cases 673) which are useful in understanding the kind of test to be applied in cases such as the present:
“In my judgment the authorities show this, that it is a question to be answered in the light of the particular facts of every case whether or not a particular payment is or is not a profit arising from the employment. Disregarding entirely contracts for full consideration in money or money’s worth and personal presents, in my judgment not every payment made to an employee is necessarily made to him as a profit arising from his employment. Indeed, in my judgment, the authorities show that to be a profit arising from the employment the payment must be made in reference to the services the employee renders by virtue of his office, and it must be something in the nature of a reward for services past, present or future.”


  1. The case of Glantre Engineering Ltd involved an offer of employment to a Mr Wells, a chartered accountant, who worked for an international firm of accountants. The offer included a lump sum payment of £10,000 as an inducement to him to leave the firm and, had as its origin, Mr Wells’ concern that in accepting the new position he would be sacrificing, his professional status as a Chartered Accountant in a leading international accountancy firm, the possibility of partnership status at some future stage and, also, the certainty of security in the future. While the factual situation resembles that of Arundale, Warner J cited the following distinguishing features:
    • Arundale was a case not of a payment by the employer but of a transfer of shares by a third party.
    • Mr Arundale was not merely changing from one employment to another; he was exchanging the status of a partner for that of a joint managing director of, and shareholder in, a company.
    • Mr Arundale was entitled to the shares forthwith but was not to enter into the service of the company until some months later.
  2. In the course of his decision, the learned Judge expressed that the ratio decidendi of Boustead and Arundale was that the quality of a payment made to an employee at the inception of his employment, that is to say whether or not it constitutes an emolument from his employment, is essentially a question of fact to be decided by the appellate commissioners on a consideration of all the evidence before them, and that, on the familiar principles laid down in Edwards (Inspector of Taxes) v Bairstow [1955] UKHL 3; (1955) 3 ALL ER 48, their determination is not to be set aside by the court unless it is inconsistent with the only reasonable conclusion to be drawn from the evidence. He then proceeded to decide that he could not hold that the only reasonable conclusion to be drawn from the evidence was, as contended by counsel for the company, that the payment of £10,000 to Mr Wells was severable from the other benefits to which he became entitled under the agreement between himself and the company, and was other than an added inducement to him to change his job and enter the full-time employment of the company.
  3. In his reasons for decision on the applicant’s objection, the Commissioner refers to the loss of “personal” as opposed to “proprietary” right and how, in his submission, it is only the loss of a substantial personal right which may rebut the natural presumption that the prospective employment is the real source of any payment made by an employer at the commencement of employment. Mistakenly, that view was based on a statement reputedly made by Warner J in Glantre Engineering Ltd when summarising the principle extracted by him from the case of Boustead. In reality, that statement appears in the decision of the Special Commissioners which was appealed and decided by Warner J without any reference to the said theory. In any event, the Tribunal considers that a discourse on the subject of losses emanating from personal as opposed to proprietary rights would be of limited value. A focus on the real nature of the transaction and the true construction of the arrangements between the applicant and W Ltd is, in the Tribunal’s view, the key to the solution.
  4. The facts of this matter, when viewed objectively, leave the Tribunal in no doubt that the agreement to pay the $20,000 was an integral part of the offer made by W Ltd and accepted by the applicant. To exclude the $20,000 is to omit one of the main attractions from the overall salary package which, in the Tribunal’s opinion, should be regarded as a single bundle of entitlements attaching to the offer of employment with W Ltd.
  5. A consideration of all the material including the letter containing the offer (T3) leads the Tribunal to the conclusion that, irrespective of the description furnished to the amount in contention, it represented a straightforward inducement for the applicant to enter the employment of W Ltd. Furthermore, the Tribunal is satisfied that the source of the payment is to be found in the service to be rendered by the applicant to W Ltd and that it was in the nature of a benefit for future service.
  6. The Tribunal concludes that the $20,000 was an incident of the applicant’s income earning activities as an employee and that, as such, it constitutes income according to normal concepts and thus it is assessable in terms of sub-s.25(1) of the Act.
  7. For the above reasons the Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
