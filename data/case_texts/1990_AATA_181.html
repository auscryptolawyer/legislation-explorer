WT86/3080 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 181; (1990) 21 ATR 3624; 90 ATC 562 (10 August 1990)

Last Updated: 2 June 2009

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



INCOME TAX - Investments in unit trusts - computer based commodity futures trading system - receiver appointed - units purchased with a view to making a quick profit - units capital in nature - units not sold - activity not sufficient to be classed as a profit making undertaking or scheme in terms of S25A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Loss of a capital nature.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, S25(1), 25A, 26(a), 51(1) and 52

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, S37



Case X38 90 ATC 335

Coberg Investment Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1960] HCA 90; (1960) 12 ATD 242

XCO Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4152

McClelland v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4115

Steinberg and ORS v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4221

REASONS FOR DECISION



10 August 1990 Mr R D Fayle, Member



The facts in this case are similar, but not the same as those in Case WT86/3070 (Case X38, 90 ATC 335), inasmuch as the applicant is claiming a deduction of $20,000 for the tax year ended 30 June 1984 in connection with losses suffered as a result of subscribing to two unit trusts which purported to then undertake computer based trading on the commodity futures markets. In addition the applicant is claiming $345 for losses sustained in dealings involving gold commodity futures. These losses, totalling $20,345, are claimed pursuant to either S51(1) or S52 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").



Evidence was given by the applicant and by his accountant, Mr Church. In addition, documents filed in accordance with S37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were admitted into evidence as were certain other copies of documents including a, (but not the), relevant trust document, promotion material issued by Central Commodities Services Pty Ltd ("Central"), income tax returns for the applicant for the years ended 30 June 1983 and 1984, relevant correspondence, invoices and an agreed statement of facts.



Like the applicant in Case WT86/3070, this applicant also attended a seminar conducted by Mr Segler, a Director of Central, at which a computer based commodities trading system known as the "Cybercast Action Trading System" ("the Cybercast system") was promoted. The applicant subsequently subscribed to two parcels of units in separate unit trusts, each parcel costing $10,000. These subscriptions were made partly in June and July 1983. These unit trusts were known as the Cypart Unit Trust No 7 and No 8 respectively. None of these facts are in dispute. For convenience the Tribunal will firstly deal with these two subscriptions, which resulted in the claim for a deduction in the year ended 30 June 1984 of $20,000. As the respondent's representative, Mr Munks, has conceded the deductibility of the $345 claim that matter can be separately dealt with later and with alacrity as it has no bearing on the substantive issue of the $20,000.



This case can be distinguished from Case WT86/3070 quite simply on the facts relating to the purpose of the respective applicants at the time of subscribing for the units. For all intents and purposes the same facts prevail - the promoters of the Cybercast system and the unit trusts had disappeared by September 1983 when, following an investigation by the New South Wales Corporate Affairs Commission, a receiver was appointed to Central and soon after it became evident that the applicant's original subscribed amount of $20,000 would not be recovered in full if at all. As recently as January 1990 Mr Munks has been advised by a Mr Christiansen from the office of the liquidator of Central, Mr John Walker, that only a small dividend is expected to be paid to the creditors, presumably one of whom is the applicant.



In Case WT86/3070 the applicant's principal purpose in subscribing for the units was to reap "quality dividends" as he intended to retain the units indefinitely as long as they earned income. Those units were not purchased for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The loss sustained by the applicant in that case was of a capital nature and was not deductible since the requirements of S52 of the Act could not be satisfied.



In this case the applicant gave evidence that he intended to realise his investment to make a profit in the short term. Whilst the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant was confused as to exactly what was the nature of the property rights, if any, he had acquired upon subscription of the $20,000, it accepts the evidence that the applicant believed that he could realise the investment within a few months and make a profit. As to the actual method of realising the investment, the applicant was not definite but the Tribunal accepts that he believed he would have been able to sell them through a stock broker or if he so chose, to have the

trustee redeem them for their then market value. The applicant admitted with hind-sight that his then perception of what would be possible was naive. His background was not in finance but in marketing. He is an educated man and not new to the world of business affairs. Nevertheless, it seems that driven by a desire to make money to defray a large mortgage on his recently acquired home and make improvements to it by speculating an inheritance, he accepted the Cybercast system as something that would provide quick profits by dealing with the units in the unit trust. The applicant is accepted as a credible witness caught up in what turned out to be a scam.



The applicant was not carrying on a business of dealing in securities or in commodity futures. He merely contributed money to a trustee of a unit trust, Kratock Pty Limited, which pursuant to the deed of trust, was to use the offices of a manager, Mr Segler, to carry on the business of commodities futures contracts trading using the Cybercast system. The applicant had only one other experience of dealing in securities and that related to an option over gold futures which gave rise to the loss of $345, (the other matter under review) and in relation to which the respondent has conceded that it is an allowable deduction under S52 of the Act.



If the applicant is to succeed in the claim for a deduction of $20,000 then it must be in terms of S52 since he is precluded from a claim under S51(1) as the loss was one of a capital nature and was not, in any event, a loss incurred in the course of carrying on a business. Sub-section 52(1), so far as it is relevant for the present case states:



"Any loss incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income upon the sale of any property or from the carrying on or carrying out of any undertaking or scheme, the profit (if any) from which sale, undertaking or scheme would have been included in his assessable income, shall be an allowable deduction: ..."



The sub-section requires that the applicant should have either disposed of the units in the unit trusts, being the relevant property to which the sub-section alludes, at a loss or, incurred a loss as a result of having carried on or carried out a profit-making undertaking or scheme. As no sale of the property (the units) has occurred then the deduction would only be available if it resulted from the applicant having carried on or carried out a profit-making undertaking or scheme.



The proviso to that sub-section requires a taxpayer to notify the respondent within a prescribed time limit, that the relevant property was acquired for the purpose of either resale at a profit or to enter into a profit-making undertaking or scheme. Such a notice was not given in the return submitted in relation to the year ended 30 June 1983 as was required by that proviso. However that is not necessarily fatal to a claim under S52 since the respondent is given a discretion to direct that a realised loss be allowed where the respondent is satisfied that the requisite purpose existed at the time the property was acquired by the applicant. The respondent has not so directed and therefore it falls to this Tribunal to consider whether it is satisfied that it would be reasonable to direct that the loss be allowed.



Notwithstanding that the liquidation of the trustee company had not been finalised as at the end of the year of income, the evidence as set out in the statement of agreed facts clearly indicates that a substantial loss was inevitable but the exact quantum would not be known for some time and indeed, was still not ascertained at the date of the hearing. In the circumstances it is reasonable to conclude that a loss of $20,000 or thereabouts had been sustained as at 30 June 1984, when claimed.



The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant did acquire the units in the two Cypart Unit Trusts for the purpose of resale at a profit. But as there was no sale nor even a disposal of property then no deduction can be allowed on the basis; c.f. Coburg Investment Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1960] HCA 90; (1960) 12 ATD 242 where Windeyer J at pp.248-9 expressed the view that for S26(a) purposes a sale occurs when the property is actually realised.



For a deduction to be allowed under S52, the applicant is therefore left to rely on what is known as the second limb of S25A(1), which provision replaced S26(a) effective from 25 June 1984. The second limb of sub-section 25A(1) provides:



"The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising .... from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme."



Can it be concluded that what the applicant did in relation to his venture into commodity futures trading amounted to carrying on or out either a profit-making undertaking or a profit-making scheme? If the answer to either is in the affirmative then the Tribunal has to decide whether, on balance, it would be reasonable for the Commissioner to have reached such a conclusion given the advantage of the additional evidence adduced at the hearing and therefore, to have exercised his discretion in terms of S52 to allow the deduction even though no requisite notification was given by the applicant.



To make sense of S25A(1) there has to be a distinction between the mere act of acquiring and selling property, which is clearly covered by the first limb of the sub-section, and the carrying on or carrying out either a profit-making undertaking or scheme. The applicant intended only to either sell the units on the market or if this was not possible then to redeem them in accordance with the provisions of the trust deed. These acts, had they been accomplished and not thwarted by the scam, would fall within the first limb and therefore by inference not within the second limb of S25A(1). There was no suggestion, nor has the Tribunal been directed to any case law precedent which suggests, that the first limb is otiose and only the second has any relevant operation.



On behalf of the applicant Mr Church argued that a taxpayer whose actions clearly fall within the scope of the first limb is not necessarily excluded from assessment under the second limb of S25A(1). The decision of Gibbs J, as he then was, in XCO Pty Ltd v Federal Commission of Taxation 71 ATC 4152, supports the view that if the first limb is not available then one can turn to the second. His Honour states, at p4155,



"It was not, and could not be suggested that the first limb of ... [S26(a)] has any application to the present case because the debts acquired by XCO were not intended to be resold. It is the second limb whose application is in question."



In the present case the applicant's purpose would dictate that the first limb ought to apply but since the first is precluded then it seems appropriate to consider the operation of the second.



Mr Church pointed to the decision of the Privy Council in McClelland v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4115, in support of the contention that a profit-making undertaking or scheme was carried out by the applicant. The facts in that case were quite complex. Lords MacDermott and Pearson (dissenting) were of the view that what the taxpayer did which led to the contested assessment under S26(a) was an undertaking or scheme inasmuch as, proceeding to the preconceived plan she acquired her brother's undivided half interest in land which they had both inherited, sub-divided off portion from the whole and sold it, using the proceeds in part to repay the debt incurred in obtaining funds to acquire her brother's interest. The majority, Lord Donovan, Viscount Dilhorne and Lord Wilberforce considered the plan amounted to an undertaking or scheme but found that the resultant gain was not a profit assessable under the section. Those facts in McClelland are a far cry from the present and no corollary could reasonably be drawn. An undertaking or scheme for the purposes of S25A(1) would ordinarily amount to more than a mere purchase and sale. Because the first limb is not otiose the second would seem to imply a plan or series of steps to bring about a desired outcome as distinct from a simple purchase and sale of property. Something would necessarily be done to the property to transform it from its original state - in McClelland the undertaking resulted in the land being sub-divided and a truncated part being sold whilst the remainder, being in two different titles, was retained. This view is supported by what Barwick CJ said in Steinberg & Ors v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4221 at p4228:



"For there to be a scheme there must be a plan: it must be the taxpayer's plan and it must exist, in my opinion, at the time of acquisition of the property: indeed that acquisition, in my opinion, must be itself a part of the scheme and the property acquired the intended vehicle for carrying the scheme into execution. Whilst it need not be fully conceived in all its details at the time of acquisition it must exist as a scheme which in principle embraces all the details yet to be worked out."



Mr Church argued that there is a difference between the concept of an undertaking and that of a scheme for the purpose of S25A(1) on the basis that if these were interchangeable terms then one would be unnecessary. No authority was provided to support the contention that the two terms have separate and distinct meanings. The Tribunal was not led to an instance where a court had suggested a distinction, all references cited by both parties being references where "undertaking" was not used independently of "scheme". Nevertheless, Mr Church contended that what the applicant did was tantamount to an undertaking, relying on a dictionary definition in support - "a task, venture or enterprise; an agreement to do something; ...". He then cited the dictionary definition of "venture" as "an undertaking that is risky or of uncertain outcome; the commercial undertaking characterised by the risk of loss as well as the opportunity for profit." However, even if it is not misleading to describe a planned transaction consisting of nothing more than the purchase and sale of property as a "venture", such a profit-making venture of the kind contemplated by the second limb of S25A did not occur in this instance since no such undertaking (or scheme) actually eventuated. No undertaking or scheme was actually carried on or out as the section requires, it was not carried into execution. It seems that what was done, to invest and do nothing further due to circumstance, would not amount to the carrying out of an undertaking. Kitto J in Clowes & Another v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1954] HCA 10; (1954) 10 ATD 316 said, at p323,



"If it be said ...that this investment was itself a profit-making scheme, the answer must be given that paying a sum of money in consideration of a promise of a possibly larger payment upon the happening of a future event cannot be described as carrying out a scheme, according to any ordinary use of language."



This implies that the mere purchase and disposal of units in a unit trust is not the carrying out of a scheme or undertaking. On the authority of Kratzmann v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4043, unless the undertaking or scheme contemplated at the outset, albeit not being one where every single detailed step had been pre-planned (re Steinberg's case supra), did come to fruition then any profit arising as a result of an exception or abandonment would not be assessable income under S25A(1). So for the same reason, that is, that no sale or realisation of property eventuated, the applicant fails to satisfy the Tribunal that either the first or second limb of S25A(1) could have applied had a profit resulted. (In passing it is observed that it is inconceivable in these circumstances to presume that a gain could ensue as a result of the liquidation). The applicant fails to support the contention that (i) he carried on or out a profit-making undertaking or scheme; and (ii) an undertaking or scheme was executed because a purchase alone of units in a unit trust is not tantamount to the carrying out of an undertaking or scheme. If, on the other hand, it were to be conceded that the applicant, upon subscribing for the units, had entered into a profit-making undertaking or scheme then none was carried out or eventuated becuase a vital part of that contemplated undertaking or scheme, the sale, did not occur. Therefore, since in the circumstances the loss of $20,000 did not result from the carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme the applicant fails to satisfy the preconditions of S52 and no deduction is allowable.



What the trustee did on behalf of the unit holder beneficiaries, even if that were to be seen as a scheme in terms of S25A(1), could not be seen as a scheme carried on by the unit holders.



"The scheme must, however, be one which is carried out by the taxpayer himself or on his behalf; it is not enough that the taxpayer derives a receipt from somebody else who obtained it by carrying out a scheme of profit-making."



(Clowes v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1954] HCA 10; 1953-54 91 CLR 209 at pp217-8); and see Official Receiver v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (Fox's case) [1956] HCA 63; 1956 96 CLR 370 at p387 per Gibbs J in XCO at p4155.



Finally, the respondent concedes that the applicant did enter into the commodity futures transaction to acquire property or rights with a view to selling at a profit and indeed sold at a loss. The loss realised of $345 is therefore an allowable deduction pursuant to S52. No dispute arises in this instance as the concession of the respondent in this regard is supported on the facts and the evidence tendered. In the result this Tribunal directs that the respondent amend the applicant's assessment accordingly by allowing a further deduction of $345. In all other respects the assessment decision is affirmed.
