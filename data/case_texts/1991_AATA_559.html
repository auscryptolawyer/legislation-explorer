QT87/4216-4219 and Commissioner of Taxation [1991] AATA 559; (1991) 22 ATR 3237; 91 ATC 306 (12 June 1991)

Last Updated: 27 May 2009



Income tax - Assessable Income - Gross income derived directly or indirectly - business of sub-contract carpentry - establishment of family trust - whether income derived by applicant or trust - whether trust in fact a "sham"



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : Ss. 25(1), 95(1)



Snook v London and West Riding Investment Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786



AAT Decision no 7023



DECISION AND REASONS



Re:



APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT



No: QT87/4216-4219



Date: 12 June 1991



Place: Brisbane



Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



DECISION



The Tribunal decides that the objection decisions under review be affirmed.



(Sgd) K L Beddoe

(SENIOR MEMBER)



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

) NO. QT87/4216-4219

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



AAT Decision No 7023



Re:

APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT



REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



Date: 12 June 1991



Place: Brisbane



The applicant seeks review of objection decisions disallowing objections against assessments in which income was assessed as income of the applicant and not as income of a family trust. The question to be decided is whether the income was derived by the applicant or the trust. The years of income in dispute are the years ended 30 June 1982 through to 30 June 1985.



2. Subsection 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") relevantly provides that the assessable income of a resident taxpayer shall include the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia. Net income of a trust estate is defined in subsection 95(1) to include the total assessable income of the trust estate calculated under the Act as if the trustee were a taxpayer in respect of the income and were a resident.



3. Exhibit D is a stamped deed of settlement dated 29 January 1982 whereby William Matthew Schoch settled an amount of five dollars on the applicant as trustee for the benefit of the beneficiaries described in the deed of settlement. The deed is a standard form deed with a schedule attached which identifies the applicant as the trustee, the applicant and his wife as the primary beneficiaries and members of their families as the secondary beneficiaries. There is also provision for the nomination of tertiary beneficiaries. Distributions or accumulations of the income of the Trust are at the express discretion of the trustee. In the event of the trustee not exercising the discretion then by force of the deed the income is distributed to the primary beneficiaries then living or if there be no primary beneficiaries then to the secondary beneficiaries etc. The deed authorises the trustee to carry on any business at the discretion of the trustee and the trustee is given wide powers in relation thereto. Clauses 6 and 13 of the deed effectively deny the trustee any beneficial interest in the trust.



4. The business said to have been undertaken by the trust is that of sub-contract carpentry and sub-contract building. In each of the years of income under consideration descriptions to that effect were used to describe the business of the trust. In each year of income the income disclosed by the trust's income tax returns was from "sub-contract carpentry" or "building sub-contracting". Offset against that income were various outgoings and depreciation including an amount described in the applicant's income tax returns as "trustee's remuneration" but in the trust return as "wages and salaries". The amounts returned by the applicant as trustee's remuneration and which are included in a larger amount claimed by the trust as a deduction for wages and salaries are:-

$

Year Ended 30 June 1982 2,012

1983 5,429

1984 6,686

1985 7,176



5. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, his wife, two sons, the applicant's accountant and two witnesses from building companies doing business with the applicant.



6. The applicant apparently approached his accountant for advice in relation to his business which I will describe as that of a sub-contract carpenter. While I cannot be certain as to exactly what transpired it appears that the meeting was motivated by the applicant's concern to obtain superannuation cover. The accountant canvassed various issues including well known and popular business structures with the applicant and his wife. In that review consideration was apparently given to a number of structures on the basis of establishing separate legal identity and the results which flow when considering potential debts, law suits and bankruptcy, and legal liability generally. That advice seems to have been based ultimately on taxation considerations but it would be wrong to say that such considerations were the only issues considered. The evidence on the applicant's account made it clear, however, that consideration of the taxation consequences of the various structures loomed large in the advice given to the applicant. Exhibit A is a statement by the accountant which lists the factors leading to the creation of the trust. It is dated the day of the hearing and is in the nature of a self serving statement to which I have attached little weight. I have formed the view that it is manufactured evidence. This is made clear by paragraph 17(7)(f) of the statement which relies on the exclusion of trusts from the substantion provisions of the Act. Given that those provisions were not introduced into the Act until some three years after the creation of the trust it is unlikely that the substantion rules played any part in the decision to create the trust.



7. In the result the applicant wound up the partnership previously conducted with his wife and was appointed trustee of a trust which I will describe as the family trust. The trust deed is dated as having been signed on 29 January 1982 but this cannot be correct. Exhibit C is a letter sent to the applicant and his wife by the accountant dated 15 February 1982. It reads as follows:-



"Enclosed is the original and two copies of the Family Trust Deed settled on the 29 January 1982.



As previously instructed the trustee of the trust is (applicant), the beneficiaries yourselves and children and family in the wider sence (sic) together with provision for other beneficiaries to be included if required.



As from the 29 January 1982 any work undertaken is undertaken by (applicant) in his name as trustee for the . . . Family Trust. Accordingly your sub-contract with Jennings should reflect this. It is important to establish that the income derived by the trust is derived in your name for the trust as opposed to in joint names for the previously existing partnership.



Please review the deeds and sign as indicated on Page 18 and 19 and return to me as soon as possible for lodgment with the Commissioner for Stamp Duties. A stamped copy will be returned to you for your records after stamping. Should you have any queries in relation to the above please advise."



8. The deed was stamped by the Stamps Office on 26 March 1982. It is reasonable to infer that it was executed on a date after 15 February 1982 and before 26 March 1982. It follows that the deed was not executed on 29 January 1982.



9. Exhibit R is copies of income tax returns lodged with the respondent in the name of the applicant and his wife as partners for the years of income ended 30 June 1980, 30 June 1981 and 30 June 1982. The business of the partnership is shown variously as building contractors, finishing contractor and sub-contract carpentry with the applicant and his wife sharing the net income of the partnership equally.



10. The partnership commenced trading on 14 September 1979 with contracts being made in the name of the applicant and his wife. The Depreciation Schedule attached to the income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1980 disclosed acquisition of the following items of plant:-



Cost

$

Drill 26

Jig Saw 26

Electric Sander 40

Electric Planer 35

Electric Saw 323

Electric Drill 107

H J Holden 2000

Trailer 210



Those are items of plant are consistent with the occupation of a carpenter. Not all of those items were acquired at the same time.



11. Prior to 14 September 1979 when the partnership commenced business the applicant was employed by a company and described himself in his income tax returns as a carpenter. In the years preceding 14 September 1979 the applicant was employed by a number of organisations but from 27 March 1979 he was continuously employed by a company which I infer, on the basis of its name, to be a builder of houses.



12. The applicant's taxable income for the years ended 30 June 1978, 30 June 1979 and 30 June 1980 was returned as follows:-



1978 $ 9513

1979 $10025

1980 $ 6388



If the wife's share of the net income of the partnership in the 1980 year is added to the applicant's taxable income the amount would be $9,784. For the years ended 30 June 1981 and 30 June 1982 when the partnership was trading the applicant's taxable income was returned as follows:-



1981 $7800

1982 $7214



The figure for 1982 includes $2012 as trustees remuneration and $186 distribution from the trust allegedly for the period 29.1.82 to 30.6.82. It will be apparent that the partnership structure, which is not in dispute, had the effect of reducing the applicant's taxable income.



13. I return now to the period after the termination of the partnership when the business was conducted in the applicant's name alone. Exhibits L, O, Y, 9 and 10 make it clear enough that the applicant was trading in his own name but without reference to the existence of a trust. Exhibit N which is three copies of prescribed payment advices issued by Jennings are made out to the applicant as trustee of the family trust as is another such advice forming part of Exhibit 10.



14. The essence of the activities of the business of applicant is that he was at all relevant times a sub-contractor engaged by a major building company to do finishing off work on houses. He supplied his own equipment and was paid as a sub-contractor subject to deductions made under the prescribed payments scheme. From time to time he had assistance from members of his family as the need arose. I formed the impression that such assistance was not remunerated and was spasmodic. The equipment used by the applicant in his work is set out in Exhibit U. That listing must refer to the equipment held at the time of hearing because it is not reconcilable with the depreciation schedule included in the trust's return of income for the year ended 30 June 1988. I have therefore refrained from giving any weight to Exhibit U.



15. While I do not doubt that the applicant was a witness of truth, the difficulty with his evidence is that the evidence in chief was lead from him by a series of leading questions with the result that the Tribunal has had to give careful consideration to the weight to be given to the applicant's evidence. The applicant is knowledgeable in relation to the business of a building contractor but relies upon his accountant for advice in relation to business structures. I have come to the conclusion that the applicant was content to adopt the advice of his accountant in deciding to carry on business using the trust structure and that he did so because of his concern for his legal liability and because of tax consequences. The examination in chief of the applicant made it clear that he was concerned to use the trust to minimise tax payable by his family but not to benefit his family as this quite remarkable exchange during examination in chief makes clear at page 54 of the transcript.



"Q. But you were aware that there were distributions to a number of people other than (your wife) and your sons?

A. Yes.



Q. And generally, you were aware that the nature or the basis upon which that was made was that it was to take advantage of basically the zero rates of tax applicable for those other beneficiaries other than your sons. In other words, the allocation of income across the basis of it was that - so that the other beneficiaries did not have to pay any extra tax by way of that extra allocation?

A. They would not have, no.



Q. So far as the income - sorry, were you aware exactly what that meant as far as the allocation of income to those beneficiaries - so far as creating perhaps a debt from them to the trust, were you aware of the significance of that?

A. No.



Q. Let me put it another way: the fact that these beneficiaries, namely your mother and (your wife's) mother, and the other relation goes, you were aware that besides allocating income to them, that you, in fact, owed them the money - the trust owed them the money?

A. Not directly.



Q. On the books - in the books there, there is a debt basically owing by the trust to them for the amount allocated?

A. It would have been paid in other ways but not directly to them.



Q. Paid in other ways: you mean - these people - you would make payments on their behalf?

A. Yes, on that line."



No separate bank account was kept for the partnership - the partners using a joint bank account for business and private purposes (Exhibit J and p.80 transcript). A new bank account was opened on 18 February 1982 in the applicant's name and without reference to a trust.



16. There was also no reference to a trust on the annual contract signed by the applicant with Jennings. Exhibits 10 and Y are the contracts dated 1-7-84 and 1-7-85 respectively. They are standard form contracts signed by the applicant and witnessed by a Jennings supervisor and are aptly described as Labour and Equipment Contractor Acknowledgment. There is nothing in those documents to suggest the existence of a trust or that Jennings understood that it was contracting with the trustee of a trust. Advice was given to Jennings that payments were to be made to the trust and, as already noted, this was acknowledged on prescribed payments system advices. Knowledge of the existence of the trust did not extend to the applicant's sons who gave evidence. They did not know of the existence of the trust although they were beneficiaries to whom distributions of the net income were said to have been made. While that may be irrelevant in determining the present entitlement of the sons to distributions made in their favour it is relevant to the question as to whether the business was being conducted for the benefit of the beneficiaries of the trust and therefore as to whether the income was derived by the applicant in his capacity as trustee.



17. There is a further difficulty in relation to the applicant's sons. It is the applicant's evidence that his son Robert was not a beneficiary of the trust. That evidence at pages 85-6 of the transcript was quite dogmatic in its denial of Robert's position as a beneficiary. Yet the terms of the trust deed are quite clear - Robert was a beneficiary at all relevant times and one who worked on a casual basis in the business of the applicant. I must infer that the applicant did not regard Robert as a beneficiary because he had a taxable income in his own right and would therefore be taxable on any distributions made to him.



18. There is a sound basis for this view to be found in the evidence relating to distributions made to a niece, the applicant's mother and his wife's mother. Although distributions were made to those persons they were not paid and were never intended to be paid those distributions. The reality is that the net income of the trust only found its way into the hands of the applicant and his wife. Formal distributions were made to the applicant and his wife albeit that clause 13 of the trust deed specifically excluded the applicant as trustee, from participating as a beneficiary in the trust.



19. The amounts of distributions by the trustee were in fact formulated in the accountant's office at the end of each fiscal year without regard to the directions of the trustee or the trust deed but with regard to the fiscal consequences to the beneficiaries. What was clearly sought to be done was to "cream off" so much of the net income as would not be taxable in the hands of members of the family and then distribute the balance of the net income to the applicant and his wife. All of this occurred in the accountant's office. I have come to the conclusion that the trust never really escaped from the accountant's office. It was kept there and dusted off each year for the purpose of preparing the income tax returns. For the rest of the year it remained there unnoticed and of no concern to the applicant. There was no intention to benefit the family relatives. The basis for benefiting the nephew's daughter but not their own son is not explained by affection for the girl. A distribution was made to her because she did not have any other income. It was never intended that she may one day call for payment of her interest in the trust estate.



20. The answer is straightforward. The applicant derived the income from his personal exertion as a carpenter. He was the person who contracted with the builders and that contract was not intended to be for the benefit of anyone other than himself and perhaps his wife. The trust was created for the purpose of convincing the Commissioner that the factual situation was different to the reality. The reality was that the income was derived by the applicant for the benefit of himself and his wife.



21. The partnership between the applicant and his wife was terminated as part of the arrangement for the creation of the trust. However, the partnership was also terminated because the wife was concerned as to her liability at law under the partnership agreement - she did not want her assets to be at risk in the building industry. It must be accepted therefore that whatever view is taken in relation to the trust the partnership was terminated at the express wish of at least one of the partners. The basis for calculation of tax benefits set out in Exhibit 2 is therefore unsound because it assumes the partnership continuing beyond February 1982 for each of the relevant years of income. The evidence does not support the assumption.



22. I do not need to consider whether there were tax benefits as defined in this arrangement because the answer is to be found on a more fundamental level.



23. In Snook v London and West Riding Investment Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786 at 802, Diplock J defined a sham in these words:



". . . for acts or documents to be a `sham', with whatever legal consequences follow from this, all the parties thereto must have a common intention that the acts or documents are not to create legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating."



24. In this case the business was not conducted for the benefit of the beneficiaries of the trust but for the benefit of the applicant and his wife. The trust was created to form the illusion that the applicant derived his income as a trustee and to deal with the problem of the termination of the partnership. The trust was ignored except in the preparation of income tax returns and other taxation papers. There was no intention to benefit the beneficiaries and they were not, in fact, benefited. The trust was a sham.



25. It follows that I am not satisfied that the assessments are excessive.



26. The objection decisions under review will be affirmed.



I hereby certify that the preceding nine (9) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matters of QT87/4216-4219



Signed: (Secretary)



Date: 12 June 1991
