Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The Applicant is an entrepreneur. Between the financial years ended 30 June 2010 and 2015 he was associated with various business ventures selling gambling related software products and property development opportunities, working both as a contractor and an employee.
  2. In or around 2016, the Applicant was charged with dishonest inducement to deliver money, and possession of tainted property from his role in these business ventures. Prior to that warrants were executed by the relevant State Police Service and associated law enforcement agencies (the Police) and documents associated with the business ventures were seized from the Applicant and others.
  3. The documents taken from the Applicant included records relating to his personal affairs as well as business records and equipment.[1] The Police prepared spreadsheets recording alleged payments of monies to the Applicant and these were obtained by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent). He undertook a covert audit of the Applicant for tax purposes and determined he had failed to report all his income and net amounts of goods and services tax (GST). He raised assessments and imposed penalties and interest on the Applicant. The Applicant objected to those assessments.
  4. In 2017, the Applicant sought independent review of the Respondent’s decision by this Tribunal.[2] The Tribunal applications were held in abeyance pending his prosecution but were reinstated after the criminal charges against him were permanently stayed. Thereafter, the applications have followed a contested and somewhat bumpy path to final hearing, submissions and decision.[3]
  5. This decision records the Tribunal’s reasons for decision on the applications for each of the years in dispute.[4]

Summary of the net GST assessed to the Applicant following objection

Findings on penalty at objection

  1. The Respondent maintained penalty on the Applicant’s shortfall tax (income) for the financial years 2010 to 2013 on the basis that he had made a materially false or misleading statement resulting in the shortfall and arising from intentional disregard of a taxation law (intentional disregard).[34] He also maintained penalty for the 2014 year and the GST tax periods 30 September 2011 to 31 December 2015, on the basis that the Applicant had failed to provide a document.[35]
  2. The Respondent increased the base penalty shortfall tax (income) by 20% for the financial years 2010 to 2013.[36] He also increased the base penalty amounts for the GST tax periods other than the tax period ending 30 September 2011.[37]
  3. The Respondent did not reduce the base penalty amounts imposed.[38]
  4. For the financial year 2014 and the relevant GST tax periods, the Applicant did not lodge an income tax return or a GST activity statement by the required dates and so the Respondent applied administrative penalty at the rate of 75% for failure to give or return a document which is necessary to determine tax liability and where the Respondent had been required to determine liability without the document.[39]
  5. The Respondent considered but declined to remit penalty for the making of a false or misleading statement on the basis that he did not consider there were exceptional circumstances warranting remission and the resulting increases of income for each applicable year resulting from the statement/s.

In the course of these proceedings, the Respondent disclosed that the Police were his ‘third party source’ for the documents obtained during the covert audit.[41] On direction from the Tribunal at the final hearing, the Respondent produced the source documents he referred to and relied upon in his original audit decision. These were exhibited to Mr Ramsden’s affidavit.[42] Mr Ramsden told the Tribunal the source documents obtained by the Respondent from the Police were spreadsheets sourcing the transactions he had compared to the Westpac bank account transactions and attached to his audit report.[43]

The parties lodged their submissions on 17 April and 24 April 2026.

THE ISSUES IN THESE PROCEEDINGS

  1. The following interlocutory issues arise in these proceedings and were the subject of oral reasons given to the parties during the hearing:

Interlocutory issues

(a) the Tribunal’s refusal of the Applicant’s oral application made on the first day of the final hearing to reissue a summons to the Police and adjourn the final hearing pending the production of documents; and

(b) the Tribunal’s decision to exclude the statement of Andrew Harris dated 27 August 2025.

  1. The Tribunal undertook to set out those reasons in its final decision. They are recorded in Attachments A and B.

Substantive issues

  1. The following substantive issues arise for determination in the proceeding:
    (a) Whether the Applicant has discharged his statutory onus in the proceedings under s 14ZZK TAA 53 in respect of the income tax and GST issues to show both that the Respondent’s assessments are excessive and what his correct taxable income was, for each of the years in dispute;

    (b) whether any penalty imposed and shortfall interest should be remitted in whole or in part.[44]

Matters which this Tribunal does not have authority to decide

  1. The Applicant also raised the following arguments about the assessments which issued from the Respondent to him.
    (a) whether the primary tax and/or penalty assessments which issued to the Applicant were valid at law;

    (b) whether the Respondent had the power to issue further amended assessments pursuant to ss 167 and 170 of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (ITAA 36); and

    (c) whether the Respondent should have remitted the remaining interest.

  2. An assessment once issued by the Respondent, is final and is presumed to be valid.[46] Neither the Tribunal nor the Federal Court is empowered by Part IVC TAA 53 to vary assessments and undertake review of objection decisions subject to the statutory test in Part IVC. [47]
  3. It follows that the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to consider and rule upon the validity of an assessment which is a matter which should properly be the subject of an application for judicial review to the Federal Court of Australia under s 39B Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) (the Judiciary Act).
  4. The Applicant’s submission that the Respondent was not lawfully empowered to further amend an already amended assessment or to raise default assessments on multiple occasions also challenges the validity of the amended assessments. These are also matters which the Tribunal does not have authority to decide.
  5. Likewise, the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to review the Respondent’s decision to impose and not remit the full amount of interest on the income shortfall amount. That is a matter which should also properly be the subject of an application for judicial review under s 39B of the Judiciary Act.

THE APPLICANT’S STATUTORY ONUS UNDER S 14ZZK TAA 53

  1. Where the Respondent is dissatisfied with the income reported in a taxpayer’s income tax return/s he is entitled to make an assessment of the amount/s upon which in his view, tax should be imposed. In forming a view as to a taxpayer’s taxable income, the Respondent is required to take into account the taxpayer’s relevant circumstances and to have a reasonable basis for his view.
  2. Default assessments are, by their nature unlikely to be precise and of necessity may be “inaccurate in fact” and “involve guesswork”.[48] The Respondent is a stranger to a taxpayer’s affairs and it is accepted that a default assessment may not in truth be a correct representation of the taxpayer’s income for a particular year”.[49] Nevertheless, he must demonstrate a method by which he has arrived at the default assessment[50] and is not permitted to base a default assessment on a “guess” or by “plucking figures from the air with random”.
  3. On review of an objection decision, a taxpayer must satisfy the Tribunal that the assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect, as well as identifying what his correct taxable income was for each of the tax periods in dispute.[51]
  4. Before the Tribunal, the Respondent is not required to defend his objection decision.[52] Instead, it is the Applicant who bears the onus of establishing that that the amount assessed exceeds their taxable income for the year/s assessed.[53] To do this, he must demonstrate each the matters set out in s 14ZZK TAA 53 to the civil standard - on the balance of probabilities.[54]
  5. In seeking to meet that standard in this case, the Applicant:
    • - cannot simply point to errors in the Respondent’s calculations in the default assessment (including calculation errors which the Applicant says were occasioned by application of the Respondent’s methodology). The onus to prove that the assessment/s are excessive remains with the Applicant who must satisfy the Tribunal that the amount assessed exceeds his actual taxable income;[56]
    • - cannot simply raise or infer matters without supporting evidence or ask the Tribunal to draw inferences about a matter or to effectively investigate to calculate their correct taxable income. He needs to do more than simply point to or raise ‘possibilities’;[57] and
    • - is required to satisfy the Tribunal on the balance of probabilities of each of the facts on which he relies which in turn support his ultimate proposition/s as to his correct taxable position.[58]
  6. A taxpayer’s uncorroborated evidence of disputed transactions must be carefully assessed given their natural self-interest in the outcome of the review by the Tribunal, but it is not always necessary for a taxpayer’s evidence to be corroborated by contemporaneous records, to be believed.[59] Nevertheless, contemporaneous records which corroborates a taxpayer’s recollections, can provide strong evidence on which the Tribunal can rely. In this regard, the Respondent drew the Tribunal’s attention to the observations of Hill J in Imperial Bottleshops Pty Ltd & Egerton v Federal Commissioner of Taxation.[60]
  7. In saying a taxpayer is self-interested, the Tribunal does not suggest that a taxpayer will always be presumed to be ‘dishonest or unreliable unless they can demonstrate otherwise’.[61] Each case will necessarily be decided by the Tribunal’s evaluation of the evidence presented by the Applicant. Their task was described by Burchett J in Ma v Commissioner of Taxation in the following terms:[62]
    But if a taxpayer denies any undisclosed source of income, provides acceptable evidence of how he spends his time, and demonstrates a reasonable explanation for any appearance of the possession of assets, he will generally discharge his burden of proof unless some positive reasons is shown why he is to be disbelieved. Any other view would introduce a degree of arbitrariness into liability for tax.

    [emphasis added]

  8. The Respondent’s obligation to give notice of his intention to make adverse submissions about the Applicant’s evidence can be satisfied in a number of ways, including by giving notice in his Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions and submissions (written and oral) of his intentions in respect of a particular matter as well as by cross-examination of a witness.[63]

The “all or nothing approach”

  1. In Condon v Commissioner of Taxation [2023] FCA 561, Derrington J noted that the term “all or nothing approach’ has been used to describe the test under s 14ZZK TAA 53 on the basis that “either the taxpayer is able to establish what their assessable income is or else the appeal fails”.[64]
  2. The Applicant asserted but the Tribunal does not agree that the all or nothing approach has been ‘abandoned’ by the Respondent in this proceeding.[65] In any event, the Applicant contended that the Tribunal is not required to adopt an ‘all or nothing approach’ in his case to the test in s 14ZZK TAA 53 and that the Tribunal can if necessary, make positive findings for the Applicant in respect of some transactions in issue.[66] He contended that,

there are no increases in the taxpayer’s assets that might have their provenance from unexplained income, and so this is largely a matter of the Applicant giving his account for deposits paid into his accounts that are not income according to ordinary concepts.[69]

  1. The Tribunal does not accept that such an approach accords with the Applicant’s statutory obligation under s 14ZZK TAA 53 as described by Derrington J in Condon and subsequent authorities.[70] The question whether the Applicant has failed to adequately explain all the disputed deposits into his account is a live issue in this case.[71]

Allowable deductions

  1. Section 8-1 Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 97) relevantly provides for the claiming of deductions for expenses.
  2. In order to claim a deduction, a taxpayer is required to establish the necessary nexus (in s 8-1(1)(a) or (b) between the loss or outgoing incurred and their gaining or producing their assessable income. That is to say, a taxpayer must demonstrate that they have incurred a loss or outgoing for the purpose of gaining or producing their assessable income or in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing their assessable income.[72] In the case of a business, while any profit gained from a single transaction is capable of being characterised as income, it must have the character of a business operation or commercial transaction.[73]
  3. The Applicant contends that certain reimbursements comprise allowable deductions. He told the Tribunal he worked as a contractor to various companies. He submitted he incurred deductible expenses when paying for the expenses of the companies he worked for. He contends that the items purchased benefited those companies and he effectively provided short-term finance to them by purchasing the items as a sub-contractor.
  4. The Tribunal does not accept this is so because it contradicts the Applicant’s own evidence, that these transactions were not expenses he incurred for the purposes of s 8-1. He told the Tribunal they were expenses of the various companies to which he was contracted and that he agreed to pay for them because he had heard he could do so and obtain reimbursement in order to earn frequent flyer points on his credit card.[74]

The GST provisions

  1. A person must be registered for GST purposes if they are carrying on an “enterprise” and their GST turnover meets the “registration turnover threshold”.[75] The term “enterprise” is broadly defined in s 9-20 of the GST Act.
  2. The term ‘taxable supply” for GST purposes is explained in s 9-5 GST Act. Were the Applicant to make any taxable supplies, he was legally obliged to pay GST in the order of 10% of the value of that taxable supply.[76] The net amount of any GST owed by the Applicant is calculated by deducting any allowable input tax credits[77] from the GST owing as a result taxable supplies – resulting in net GST owed to the Respondent.
  3. A taxpayer who is dissatisfied with an assessment[78] issued by the Respondent is entitled to lodge a notice setting out the grounds on which they object[79] and the Respondent must consider and decide whether to allow it in whole or part or to disallow it.[80]
  4. At objection,[81] the Applicant conceded that he was required to be registered for GST for the years in dispute but then sought to resile from that concession and contended before the Tribunal that his assessable income for the period under review was such that he did not meet the threshold requiring registration for GST until the 2015 year.[84]
  5. Ultimately, the Applicant appears to accept that his liability for GST will rest with the Tribunal’s findings as to the characterisation of the disputed amounts.[85] He did not submit the disputed deposits were not taxable supplies for GST purposes. Nor did he contend that he was entitled to input tax credits. Nor did he address the GST issue or GST penalty in his closing submissions or reply submissions.
  6. In the circumstances, the Tribunal has proceeded on the assumption that the Applicant accepts the GST issue will flow from the Tribunal’s finding about the tax shortfall.

Penalty

  1. The Respondent has a discretion to impose penalty (the base amount) on a tax shortfall in certain circumstances. These relevantly include where a taxpayer has made a materially false or misleading statement which led to a tax shortfall amount.[86]
  2. Liability under s 284-75 Schedule 1 TAA 53 arises automatically if any of the prescribed circumstances are satisfied, subject to the exceptions in ss 284-75 (5)-(9). Relevantly s 284-75(5) provides that a taxpayer will not be liable for penalty in circumstances where they took “reasonable care” in the making of the relevant statement.[87]
  3. The base penalty amount to be applied is provided for in s 284-90 TAA 53, including where (as in this case) the Respondent forms the view that a tax shortfall resulted from an intentional disregard of a taxation law.[88] The circumstances in s 284-90 TAA 53 have been described as imposing “fixed criteria” for the application of penalty at various rates.[89]
  4. Penalty uplift to a base penalty amount may also apply by force of Schedule 1 TAA 53 s 284-220(1) in particular circumstances.[90]

  1. In considering penalty, the Tribunal must have regard to the statutory test in s14ZZK of TAA. It is the taxpayer who bears the onus of satisfying the Tribunal on the evidence presented that the penalty should not be imposed at all or imposed at a lesser rate.[91]
  2. As already noted, the question of whether the penalties imposed were excessive appears to no longer be in issue in this case but for the avoidance of doubt, the Tribunal is not satisfied in the circumstances that he has demonstrated the penalty imposed was excessive.

Remission of penalty

  1. The Respondent has a broad discretion to remit the part or whole of penalty imposed[92] and in considering whether to do so must take into account the taxpayer’s particular circumstances.[93] However, in considering whether there is a basis to remit part or the whole of the penalty imposed, the Tribunal is required have regard to the “subject matter, scope and purpose” of the statute which establishes the power to impose penalty, including the purpose for which the penalty regime exists which is to “deter infringements”.[94]
  2. The Applicant contends that he made a “voluntary disclosure” to the Respondent which he contends would support a decrease to base penalty amount or remission.[95] He has also raised a number of grounds in support of remission of penalty which are addressed later in these reasons.
  3. In Ross, Derrington J considered in some detail the question of the scope of the discretion to remit penalty with reference to the observations of Griffith J in Sanctuary Lakes and concluded:

From the foregoing and, in particular, the majority’s reasons in Sanctuary Lakes, it can be concluded that the considerations which might be relevant to the exercise of the discretion to remit in s 298-20 are those which concern circumstances that could be regarded as mitigating the taxpayer’s behaviour in some way or, in other words, those which go to reducing the taxpayer’s culpability for the circumstances which brought about the imposition of the penalty. Other matters are necessarily irrelevant.

Interest and remission of interest

  1. Statutory interest charge (SIC) will be imposed for any additional income tax payable from an amended assessment.[96] The Respondent has a broad discretion to remit part or all of any SIC payable[97] but a taxpayer’s right to object is limited to the circumstance where the amount of the unremitted SIC is greater than 20% of the taxpayer’s tax shortfall.
  2. The Applicant relied on the same grounds he advanced in support of remission of penalty for remission of SIC.

K&S Freighters

  1. The Tribunal recently afforded both parties an opportunity to lodge written submissions on the recent Federal Court judgment in K&S Freighters, for its procedural decision to exclude Mr Harris’ statement.
  2. The Tribunal has received and had regard to the parties’ submissions about K&S Freighters.[101] It accepts K&S Freighters was a case which largely turned on facts which are not relevant to the facts in these proceedings and concerned particular issues relating to legal professional privilege and confidentiality.
  3. Nevertheless, the Tribunal accepts that the judgment in K&S Freighters has potential application beyond those facts. In particular, Feutrill J makes relevant observations as to how the Tribunal should evaluate the late tender of evidence by a party and reminds the Tribunal that it should not inflexibly apply its practice directions. Two deposits of $100

  1. These two deposits were narrated in the relevant bank statement as “subcontractor fees”.[167]
  2. The Applicant does not appear to have advanced any further evidence about these transactions in the first affidavit but did tell the Tribunal that he was not responsible for maintaining the records of the Business Group and even if he did, it was not reasonable to expect him to keep extensive records of the sort demanded by the Respondent in this case.
  3. In closing submissions, the Applicant submitted that it was procedurally unfair of the Respondent to refuse to disclose the name of the source of these payments.[168]
  4. In light of the Applicant’s evidence that he was engaged as a “sub-contractor” for the Business Group and the Property Group,[169] and the narrations on the deposits, one reasonable explanation is that these were fees paid to the Applicant for services he provided as a sub-contractor and were income.
  5. On the face of the evidence, the Tribunal is not satisfied these amounts were other than income paid to the Applicant.

The Applicant’s explanation for large deposits of funds into his account

  1. The Tribunal’s lack of confidence in the Applicant’s knowledge of his accounts and transactions was heightened by the following evidence. When taken to his bank accounts under cross-examination, the Applicant had difficulty recalling the source of some large deposits to his account - $100,000 and $80,700 (both made on 29 April 2014). There was a subsequent withdrawal of $201,000, the same day, and the Applicant thought it might be a loan to his parents for property purchase but was not certain. When asked, he said he recalled the loan to his parents had been repaid over time, but still could not recall/explain the two deposits into his account.[170]

The Respondent submitted that it is difficult to accept that the Applicant would not “recall’ the source of such large sums of money into his account. However, this absence of clarity was consistent with the many other facts, the Applicant could not independently recall when giving evidence.

The Applicant’s attempt to explain his correct taxable income

  1. The Applicant’s second statement was dated 26 August 2025 and it was tendered on the third day of the final hearing.[171] In this statement, the Applicant sought to address the second limb of the statutory test in s 14ZZK(b)(i) TAA 53 by purporting to present a calculation in the form of a table of his correct taxable income for each of the financial years in dispute.[172]
  2. The Respondent objected to the tender of the second statement. He argued that it was opinion evidence that KBHN was not entitled to advance, [173] that the basis upon which it had been prepared and the conclusions drawn in the table attached to the statement were unclear and that it had no probative value. Ultimately, the Tribunal admitted the statement into evidence with redactions after hearing from the parties and making rulings about contents. The Tribunal made clear to the parties that the admission of the statement was subject to the Tribunal considering what weight to apply to it after hearing from the Applicant.[174]
  3. In evaluating the table, the Tribunal has had regard to the parties submissions about the second statement, but has approached cautiously the Applicant’s written closing submissions which attempt to explain how the totals in the table relating to betting, loans, reimbursements, motor vehicles and sales of chattels were calculated. Instead, the Tribunal has relied on the Applicant’s evidence in the second statement to evaluate the table.[175]
  4. Having considered the statement, the table within and having heard the Applicant’s evidence, the Tribunal has determined that the table lacks probative value and that it would be unsafe to rely upon it. The reasons for this are explained below. The Tribunal has reproduced the table at Attachment C to these reasons to assist the reader.
  5. The Applicant’s states that the calculations in the table were made with reference to the Respondent’s documents lodged in the proceeding. The Applicant provides very broad descriptions in his statement of the basis for the totals arrived at in the table and then states they were based on the s 23 Tribunal Act documents lodged by the Respondent.[176] The Applicant submits but the Tribunal does not accept looking at the statement and the table that the calculations are a “rational extension” of the Applicant’s evidence and a “simple mathematical exercise of cross-referencing the Applicant’s evidence against his calculation of his taxable income.”
  6. The general descriptions the Applicant gives in the second statement do not assist the Tribunal to understand how each of the figures in the table were arrived at for each year in dispute.[177]
  7. The final figures depicted in that table appear to result from subtraction of the totals for each year for categories named “reimbursements”, “betting” and “unexplained” to reach a total figure for each year in dispute. It is not possible for the Tribunal to reverse engineer these amounts in these columns to understand which transactions were included to calculate the various column totals. That is a material issue and a concern for the Tribunal because the Applicant’s own evidence addressed both irrelevant transactions and failed to address others which appeared to be in contention.
  8. The following concerns also arise from the amounts presented in the table:
    • - the totals described as “reimbursements” do not appear to match the Applicant’s evidence about reimbursements (in his first affidavit);
    • - the totals described as “Total Deposits/Other of Applicant – ATO(1)” are also unexplained and the Respondent submitted they do not correlate with the Respondent’s calculations in the s 23 documents;
    • - the totals in the column described as “betting” does not appear to correlate with either the Applicant’s evidence or the Respondent’s calculations in the s 23 documents;
    • - the transactions which were included in the column described as “Other” are not identified;
    • - the Respondent submits and the Tribunal accepts as correct that for both the 2012 and 2013 financial years, the amounts reported in the table as the Applicant’s correct taxable income, appear to be less than the amounts he originally reported in his income tax returns but no evidence was led to explain this difference;[178]
    • - the table presents alternate options for the Applicant’s correct taxable income. The second option is presented as a 50% discount which is described as “appropriate” given “documents destroyed or not returned by the [Police]”.[179] The Applicant refers to further bases to support the “generosity” of the figures he is proposing in the statement,[180] but it is not clear to the Tribunal how that alternate option was arrived at. [181]
  9. The Tribunal is not persuaded by the Applicant’s submissions that the Tribunal should treat the table as ‘unexpunged evidence’ or that procedural fairness demands that it be accepted and relied upon by the Tribunal.[182] The Applicant was on notice before giving evidence that the Respondent objected to the second statement as well as the bases for his objections. He was also on notice from the Tribunal that the evidence was admitted subject to the Tribunal’s consideration of whether to give any weight to it. Further, the Applicant was also on notice that the Respondent, having given notice of his objections, declined to cross examine him about the table but reserved the right to make adverse submissions about this evidence.
  10. Next, the Tribunal is not persuaded by the Applicant’s submission that the second statement is simply a recitation of facts. Having examined the table, the Tribunal is persuaded it includes a series of calculations which comprise opinion evidence. They involve the assessment and characterisation of transactions for tax purposes and based on those characterisations, calculate taxable income for each year in dispute. The Applicant is not (as far as the Tribunal is aware) a qualified accountant who is qualified to provide opinion about such matters and even if he were so qualified, he has a personal interest in these proceedings which precludes him from providing independent opinion about the correct taxable income for each of the years in dispute.[183] There is also no explanation of the basis upon which the Applicant is qualified to opine about the “alternate option”.
  11. For all these reasons, the Tribunal was not confident it can understand much less rely upon the figures presented in the table. In such circumstances, the Tribunal is not persuaded that it should give weight to the second statement as providing a comprehensible and satisfactory explanation of the Applicant’s correct or nearly correct taxable income for each year in dispute for the purposes of discharging his onus under s 14ZZK TAA.
  12. The Tribunal does not accept the Applicant’s submission that these proceedings cannot fairly proceed to determination in light of an adverse evaluation of the Applicant’s second statement and the table. The Tribunal afforded procedural fairness to the parties in respect of this issue and ruled on the Respondent’s objection to the tender of the second statement after hearing from them. It made clear to both parties that the tender was accepted subject to submissions on the weight which should be afforded to that evidence. It afforded the Applicant an opportunity to lead further evidence at the hearing before forming a concluded view.

Did the Respondent’s investigation lack reasonable methodology?

  1. The Applicant contended that Respondent had proceeded on an “unintelligible basis” at audit by obtaining spreadsheets of transactions from the Police and comparing them to the Applicant’s bank and credit card statements and by making erroneous calculations. He also contended the Respondent appeared to have “simply plucked a figure ‘out of the air’ albeit one based on bank deposit records only”.[184]
  2. The Applicant also submitted in general terms that the Respondent had failed to take into account relevant information about the Applicant’s personal and commercial history in reaching his decision such that he may have breached his statutory duty under s 166 ITAA 36.[185] The Tribunal understands that the particular information he contends should have been taken into account was that a university graduate could not specifically recall and substantiate with contemporaneous documentation, six years of financial transactions.[186]
  3. The Tribunal is not persuaded by either of these arguments. The Respondent’s audit methodology was explained in his audit reasons.[187] That record discloses that the Respondent examined the spreadsheets he obtained from the Police and then cross-checked the relevant transactions against the Applicant’s bank account statements, with particular attention being paid to a series of deposits narrated as wages, cash deposits, credits and other transfers.[188] The Applicant’s own submissions suggest that he well understood but disagreed with that process, as well as the Respondent’s conclusions. He is entitled to disagree and in these proceedings, he has been afforded the opportunity to challenge the correctness of both the final assessment amounts and the process the Respondent used to reach those assessments.

PENALTY AND INTEREST

  1. The consequence of the Tribunal’s findings on the primary tax and GST issues mean a tax shortfall arises. In the circumstances, the Tribunal is required to consider the penalty assessments imposed.
  2. The Applicant confined his submissions on a collective basis (income - base penalty and uplift and GST) to whether the penalties and interest imposed should be remitted. He sought full remission of both penalty and interest for the following reasons:[189]
    • - the exceptional circumstances of the case which he contended impeded the Applicant’s ability to comply with the audit;
    • - the prolonged prosecution which left him impecunious;
    • - the financial penalty imposed has caused the Applicant “undue hardship”;
    • - the ‘seizure’ of the documents in May 2015 by the Police which he contended adversely impacted his ability to meet lodgement or reporting obligations;
    • - his engagement of a tax agent to prepare his income tax returns;
    • - a “voluntary disclosure” made via the tax agent which reduced his liability and the associated penalty amounts and resulted in a “large number of documents” being produced to the Respondent;
    • - the need for proportionality and fairness in the Respondent’s approach and the resulting hardship caused by the significant penalty imposed.

Remission of penalty

  1. The relevant question for the Tribunal, is not whether the penalties imposed would cause hardship to the Applicant or would presently cause him financial difficulty in general terms but whether he has demonstrated circumstances which mitigate against his behaviour or reduce his culpability resulting in the imposition of the penalty.[190]
  2. The Tribunal, while having a broad discretion to remit penalty, is required to consider the purpose of the penalty regime and the conduct of the Applicant in engaging with the Respondent during the audit and objection process.
  3. With those points in mind and noting the Applicant’s submissions, the Tribunal:
    • - accepts the original audit was conducted covertly which meant the Applicant had no relevant opportunity to make a voluntary disclosure for the purposes of s 284-225 Schedule 1 TAA 53 prior to or after notification of audit;[191]
    • - acknowledges the Respondent’s second audit report closely followed the first audit report and was completed without prior notice, leaving the Applicant with a relatively short period of time in which to provide information to the Respondent about any relevant transactions;
    • - accepts the evidence discloses that the Applicant provided material and responses to the Respondent at objection (both voluntarily and in response to requests for information) and consequently enjoyed some success on objection;[192]
    • - is not satisfied on the available evidence that the Applicant made voluntary disclosures against interest to Respondent during the objection process and also notes that the Applicant has maintained throughout the objection process and before the Tribunal that the assessments were “invalid”;
    • - is satisfied that the taxable income assessed by the Respondent differs significantly from the amounts originally reported by Applicant in his income tax returns;
    • - notes the Applicant’s evidence which is corroborated the affidavit from his criminal lawyer[193] that a significant number of documents relating to the Applicant’s financial affairs (including receipts) were seized by the Police in May 2015 and that there was significant overlap between the financial issues arising from the former charges brought against him and his tax dispute;[194]
    • - does not accept as proven, the Applicant’s contention that the Respondent relied on copies of the source documents from the Police for the purpose of auditing the Applicant which he did not share with the Applicant; but in any event
    • - accepts that a practical consequence of the seizure of the primary records by the Police in 2015 was that during the course of the objection, the Applicant may have lacked physical access to some receipts and other primary records relevant to his dispute with the Respondent;
    • - acknowledges the Applicant did provide receipts to the Respondent at objection, when requested;[195]
    • - is not satisfied on the evidence that any ‘missing receipts’ would have wholly or even materially addressed the Applicant’s tax shortfall for the years in dispute; but nonetheless
    • - accepts as plausible, his contention that the absence of complete copies of primary records made his (or his lawyers’) attempts to engage with the Respondent during the objection process more challenging.
  4. In such circumstances, and on the evidence presented, the Tribunal is satisfied and finds that the Applicant has demonstrated a basis for a modest remission of 10% administrative penalty imposed on the income tax shortfall for the relevant years in dispute. This reflects the practical circumstances which may have temporarily impeded his ability to engage with the Respondent during the objection process or made it more complicated because of the overlap between the Police action, the criminal prosecution and the objection process.
  5. In saying this, the Tribunal remains satisfied that before this Tribunal, the Applicant has been afforded multiple opportunities to summons any missing receipts, but has not done so.
  6. The Tribunal is not otherwise satisfied that the Applicant has demonstrated a basis for remission of part or all of the remaining penalties applied, including any penalty uplift.

Remission of Interest

The Tribunal, having considered and evaluated the reasons advanced by the Applicant in opening submissions, is not satisfied there is a proper basis in all the circumstances for any further remission and particularly notes (in addition to the above reasons) the following:
    • - the Tribunal is not satisfied there are additional reasons to justify the Commonwealth bearing part or all of the cost of delayed payments; and
    • - the question of any possible material disadvantage arising for the Applicant in producing receipts during the objection process has been addressed by the Tribunal’s decision to partially remit penalty.

APPLICATION FOR CONFIDENTIALITY ORDERS

  1. Prior to the final hearing, the Applicant requested that his application be heard in private. He had a statutory right to make that request and his election has been honoured.[196] Nevertheless, he contended that confidentiality orders should also be made under ss 69 and 70 Tribunal Act over both the hearing and the Tribunal’s reasons for decision (the request).[197] Specifically, the Applicant told the Tribunal he was concerned for both his physical safety and the safety of his property arising from his prior business dealings with various parties.[198]
  2. The Tribunal has considered whether there is any gap in protection already offered by ss 14ZZE and 14ZZJ TAA 53 which could be met by orders being made under the Tribunal Act. It is not persuaded there is such a gap for the following reasons.
  3. First, while the Applicant delayed making the election under s14ZZE TAA 53, his identity has now been protected for almost 12 months by use of a pseudonym and all hearings and evidence presented are presently protected from disclosure.
  4. Second, in light of that election s 14ZZJ TAA 53 requires the Tribunal to ensure as far as practicable that its reasons for decision are published in a form which does not enable identification of the Applicant’s identity. To date, the Tribunal has published two practice and procedure decisions. The Applicant was afforded the opportunity to review both decisions before they were published. It has not been suggested that there have been adverse consequences arising from publication of these reasons.
  5. Third, the Tribunal, in considering whether to make additional orders for confidentiality must comply with the Tribunal’s (Common Procedure) Practice Direction 2024 and must also have regard to the relevant factors in s 71 Tribunal Act. After considering the Applicant’s submissions, the Tribunal has identified and weighed the following material factors:
    (a) the Applicant has given sworn evidence, which the Tribunal accepts gives cause for him to be concerned about his personal safety. While it is not clear whether and to what extent identification of the Applicant by former business associates as the Applicant in these reasons would put him at risk, the Tribunal nonetheless accepts he has reason to seek to ensure his identity is protected;

    (b) the Applicant is concerned that evidence arising in these proceedings might somehow be used against him by law enforcement. Here, the Applicant contends that confidentiality orders are required to ‘restrain’ the use by the Respondent and/or a third party of evidence produced to the Tribunal in these proceedings; and

    (c) there is a public interest in the Tribunal being able to publish its final reasons for decision (bearing in mind its obligations under s 14ZZJ TAA 53).

  6. Weighing these factors, the Tribunal is satisfied that the Applicant’s safety concerns are adequately addressed by ss 14ZZE and 14ZZJ TAA 53 without requiring confidentiality orders to be made by the Tribunal. As it has done previously, the Tribunal will consult both parties before its reasons are published.
  7. As to factor (b), the Tribunal is satisfied that given ss 14ZZE TAA 53, the evidence in the proceedings will (absent any approval of the Tribunal) be subject to the implied undertaking and cannot be used for any other purpose without leave of the Tribunal.[199]
  8. The Tribunal cannot identify any utility in making orders under the Tribunal Act in the circumstances and the Tribunal is not persuaded in the circumstances that its reasons should not be published.

ATTACHMENT A - RECORD OF THE TRIBUNAL’S ORAL REASONS FOR REFUSING THE APPLICATION TO REISSUE A SUMMONS TO THE POLICE[200]

  1. The Tribunal’s task was a difficult one in the circumstances where the application to adjourn was made on the first day of the final hearing which had been listed for 5 days.
  2. In the circumstances, the Tribunal’s job was to weigh the issues to decide what is in the best interests of the just resolution of the matter. The Tribunal referred the parties to Aon Risk Services Australia Ltd v Australian National University.[201]
  3. The Applicant’s counsel had contended the key issue in Aon Risk Services was whether a party should be permitted to replead its case while the question on this application is about the admissibility of evidence. However, the Tribunal observed that the majority judgment of the Court in Aon Risk Services at [114][202] provides reasoning which goes beyond the facts of that case and recognises the purpose of case management by the courts. The majority recognised that delay and costs are undesirable and that delay has deleterious effects not only upon the party to the proceedings in question but to other litigants:

Rule 21 of the Court Procedures Rules recognises the purposes of case management by the courts. It recognises that delay and costs are undesirable and that delay has deleterious effects, not only upon the party to the proceedings in question, but to other litigants. The Rule's objectives, as to the timely disposal of cases and the limitation of cost, were to be applied in considering ANU's application for amendment. It was significant that the effect of its delay in applying would be that a trial was lost and litigation substantially recommenced. It would impact upon other litigants seeking a resolution of their cases. What was a "just resolution" of ANU's claim required serious consideration of these matters, and not merely whether it had an arguable claim to put forward. A just resolution of its claim necessarily had to have regard to the position of Aon in defending it. An assumption that costs will always be a sufficient compensation for the prejudice caused by amendment is not reflected in r 21. Critically, the matters relevant to a just resolution of ANU's claim required ANU to provide some explanation for its delay in seeking the amendment if the discretion under r 502(1) was to be exercised in its favour and to the disadvantage of Aon. None was provided.

  1. The Tribunal is not a costs jurisdiction. Its objective is set out under s 9 Tribunal Act and requires it to provide an independent mechanism of review that is fair and just, that ensures that applications are resolved as quickly and with as little formality and expense as a proper consideration of the matters before the Tribunal permits and is accessible and responsive to the diverse needs of parties and improves the transparency and quality of government decision making and finally and not least importantly, that promotes public trust and confidence in the Tribunal.
  2. A number of those factors were relevant in weighing up the best course of action to take.
  3. The Applicant’s counsel told the Tribunal that the outcome, on the summons ‘could be good for Mr [KBHN], or it could be bad’.[203] The point there was that if the Tribunal agreed to adjourn this hearing it could not know when (this process) was going to end. The Tribunal was told that it was simply a matter of allowing a further summons to issue to the Police but what was said in oral submissions suggested that it will not be the end of the matter – in circumstances where this matter has now been reactivated for 13 months, where it was 8.5 months since the Applicant was first told that he had an opportunity to request any summonses that should issue and where there had been three attempts by the Tribunal to provide an opportunity to the Applicant to request that a summons issue. In respect of these, it was noted there was no response by the Applicant to the first and second sets of directions made and on the third, the summons was not served and there was no affidavit before the Tribunal to explain why that did not happen.
  4. Instead, what had to happen (on the first day of the hearing) was that a whole day was given over to try to discern what occurred.
  5. Ultimately, a material issue for the Tribunal in determining a just resolution was there had been a prior consistent pattern of non-compliance by the Applicant with directions issued by the Tribunal in the proceeding such as the need for a reinstatement hearing as well as the multiple opportunities afforded to the Applicant to comply with those directions.
  6. If the Tribunal were to allow the application for adjournment, the necessary consequence would be prejudice to the Respondent, because even on the shortest time frame possible and (the Tribunal could not be certain that this matter could be relisted within the time frame the Applicant proposed), the Respondent would have to re-prepare his case.
  7. There was also prejudice to the Tribunal given the 5-day listing for hearing as well as the numerous prior interlocutory hearings and directions hearings in these proceedings to bring the matter to final hearing.
  8. Finally and looking at the Aon Risk Services factors and taking into account the Tribunal’s reasons for reinstating the proceeding,[204] there was also critical prejudice to other applicants to consider. The Tribunal is currently experiencing unprecedented demand which it is managing. Other litigants for a variety of reasons, all of them compelling, are desirous of a hearing and the Tribunal’s s 9 objectives require it to demonstrate that it is dealing with processes quickly and efficiently and justly.
  9. But most importantly in that respect, and looking at the Aon Risk Services factors and the timing with which the application for adjournment was made, the Tribunal has an obligation to promote public trust and confidence in its processes.
  10. In this case, the Tribunal, had afforded the Applicant multiple opportunities to summons the records from the Police. There comes a point where to continue to do so in circumstances where the Tribunal does not have confidence that it can predict when the proceeding will come to an end, where it has not been given an adequate explanation for what has previously happened, and where it cannot be confident it has a proper appreciation of what the Applicant is asking for in his case must inevitably mean that the public would lose trust in the Tribunal and its ability to manage its processes.
  11. For reasons explained below, the Tribunal reached the difficult decision to decline to reissue the summons to the Police and the inevitable consequence of declining the request for adjournment:
    • - the Applicant was on notice from the 18th of June 2025 that compliance with the hearing time was relevant to the Tribunal’s consideration in exercising its discretion to reinstate his applications;
    • - despite that, the timetable of correspondence and the tenor of the correspondence since 5 August 2025, gives rise to significant concern. One possible interpretation of the correspondence when read as a whole, is that the Applicant was not focussed on bringing this matter to hearing or even trying to preserve the hearing dates;
    • - rather it was open to infer from the correspondence and the way it was drafted that the Applicant was mounting a case to cause the hearing not to continue;
    • - adding to that, regrettably, it was left to the Police to correspond with the Tribunal to explain to the Tribunal that the summons had not been issued until it had gone stale. This was despite a request from the Tribunal that the Applicant inform the Tribunal when the summons had been served.
  12. The Tribunal acknowledged that the Applicant did not have a solicitor instructing counsel but considered that those representing the Applicant have an obligation to ensure that the matter is capable of being progressed without prejudice. If there had been a concern that the hearing could not progress, that is something that should be the subject of urgent correspondence to the Tribunal.
  13. Turning to the request for other summons to issue to police and tax officers to attend and give evidence, the Tribunal considered that request to be inextricably linked with and contingent on the outcome of the Police summons arising from the Applicant’s own correspondence with the Tribunal. Reading that correspondence, the Tribunal was reasonably satisfied that the intention was that request should only be acted upon if necessary and if there was not a state of satisfaction with what was returned from the Police.
  14. Turning to the Applicant’s affidavit, the Tribunal admitted it into evidence but continued to have concerns about the relevance of many of the matters in the affidavit. It foreshadowed that if it were proposed to be relied upon for the substantive proceedings, there may need to be rulings made about some paragraphs within it but for present purposes, even at its highest, the Tribunal could not discern its relevance. The request for witnesses to be summonsed, was always contingent upon the outcome of the summons to the Police. Further, it was only shortly prior to the hearing that it was suggested that it needed to be acted upon. These issues were not raised at the 29 July 2025 hearing (when the proposed summons were considered and agreed between the parties and the Tribunal).
  15. For the record, the Tribunal accepted the terms of the proposed additional summons had been drafted in good faith but also accepted that if the summons were proposed to issue there might be questions as to the imprecision in the drafting. However those were not the material factors in its decision to decline to issue them.
  16. The Tribunal also observed that submissions had been made about missing records of transactions and the third party source. It was not the task of the Tribunal in these proceedings to identify the third party source for the Applicant. Its task is to characterise the transactions and determine the matters set out in s 14ZZK TAA 53.
  17. Turning to the s 26 request (for additional documents to be lodged by the Respondent), to the extent there is any belief that there are transactions that have not been identified or documents or records that have not been identified by the Respondent that relate to the objection decision, the Tribunal foreshadowed that it proposed to direct that the Respondent investigate afresh whether there are any additional documents and to provide those to the Tribunal on short order.
  18. The Tribunal then heard from the parties about that matter and it was resolved that the Respondent should lodge an affidavit exhibiting all documents produced to him from the third party source.

ATTACHMENT B - RECORD OF THE TRIBUNAL’S ORAL REASONS FOR REFUSING TO ADMIT THE STATEMENT OF ANDREW HARRIS INTO EVIDENCE[205]

  1. First, while it is correct to say the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence, that does not mean it should not act in accordance with them. There are good reasons why it should and the Act does not say it should not follow them.
  2. As was noted by the former President in Pochi and Minister for Immigration and Ethic Affairs [1979] AATA 64; (1979) 26 ALR 247 the Tribunal must critically consider whether evidence serves any probative purpose.[206] It is not always sufficient to simply admit evidence subject to weight. The Tribunal referred the parties to its observations in FLLH v Commissioner of Taxation[207] and the decision to exclude purported expert evidence which lacked probative value as to material facts.
  3. The Tribunal has published a Practice Direction for expert witness evidence in the Tribunal which was not followed in this case. An independent expert witness enjoys a unique position. They have an overriding duty to provide impartial assistance to this tribunal in matters relevant to their area of knowledge or experience. They are not to be an advocate for a party to the proceeding.
  4. The Tribunal’s practice direction has been thoughtfully prepared to reinforce and support their role. That practice direction is expressed to apply to all proceedings (clause 1.3) but more importantly in Part 2 it explains the expert’s duty to the Tribunal (clauses 2.1-2.3) and their obligation to disclose any pre-existing relationship with the party who engaged them or any remuneration arrangements as well as any conflict of interest, real or perceived (clauses 2.4-2.6).
  5. Any expert engaged by a party to Tribunal proceedings must be given a copy of the practice direction at the time they’re engaged. They are bound to comply with it.
  6. These are not insignificant matters. These are fundamentally important. The reason they’re important is that the Tribunal then takes in that evidence, relying on the independence and expertise of the expert. And it has the benefit of weighing that evidence, knowing the evidence is opinion expressed by an expert with specialised training and qualifications. And so, the Tribunal can rely on the fact that it’s being expressed for the tribunal’s benefit.
  7. None of these requirements have been met with this piece of evidence. But the matter goes further because Mr Harris is not independent. He is incapable of meeting these requirements because he is engaged by the Applicant.
  8. Looking at the case law on which this practice direction is based, and in particular, Dasreef Pty Ltd v Hawchar (2011) 243 CLR 588; 85 ALJR 694; [2011] HCA 21, where a court, allows a witness to give opinion which is normally not admissible, it’s an exception to the rules of evidence, and because the expert enjoys that exception, the expert needs to comply and demonstrate clearly the basis upon which the expert has been engaged, any questions that have been put to them, the assumptions that underpin the opinion they give, the facts that form the basis of the opinion and how that opinion connects to those facts. The Tribunal was not satisfied that those requirements have been complied with in this case.
  9. The Tribunal was not satisfied it could discern how the opinions Mr Harris expressed in the statement had been formed, what the assumptions were, and as to the tables of calculations the Tribunal could not calculate or understand the basis for the calculations in them.
  10. Further, the Tribunal observed that either Mr Harris was purporting to be an expert witness or he was not. There were opinions expressed in the statement which did not relate to Mr Harris’ professional training and obligations.[208]
  11. At any point in this proceeding, it would have been possible for the Applicant’s legal representatives to engage an independent expert to provide independent expert opinion, but that has not been done. The first time the Tribunal was alerted to such attempts is this document lodged part way through the final hearing.
  12. Mr Harris has argued that he does not think his position is untenable by virtue of his role as a ‘consultant’. For the reasons the Tribunal has given, it cannot accept that as a correct statement. In the circumstances, it is impossible for Mr Harris to be independent in these matters.[209]
  13. the Applicant’s counsel submitted that this is just a mechanical process. If what Mr Harris did were just a mechanical process, then there would be something around the tables of calculations that is discernible.
  14. The Tribunal also expressed concern that there was material in the statement which purportedly relates to settlement discussions, which is not appropriate.
  15. There is also a reference to Mr Harris having engaged with the Applicant around his calculations but there is no transparency about what that involved, what the basis of that discussion was, what the instructions were that flowed from it. The Applicant’s counsel referred in oral submissions to a ‘process’, but the Tribunal could not discern from the statement a methodology or steps that then leads the Tribunal to understand the conclusions that are expressed, assuming the table is the ultimate conclusionary opinion.
  16. The statement also suggested the methodology was derived from the Tribunal documents, but it is not clear which documents were referred to, how they were referred to, what relevance they have, whether there was any qualification of those documents, why some documents were referred to and not others if, in fact, that happened.
  17. Finally, in so far as the statement attempts to apply a percentage reduction to the Applicant’s assessable income for particular financial years, the Tribunal cannot understand how that results from any professional opinion or basis. The concern overall is whether parts of this statement are purporting to, in fact, do the job of the Applicant, which is to say what his taxable income is, and that’s not permissible. The Tribunal queried whether the statement was effectively purporting to do the job of the Applicant which was to demonstrate what his correct income was but observed that if the Applicant wanted to refer to those tables, or anything in those tables by way of submissions to the Tribunal, that was a matter for him but was not admitted into evidence.
