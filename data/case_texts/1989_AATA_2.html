Re Barry Martin Heritage and Commissioner of Housing [1989] AATA 2 (10 January 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: BARRY MARTIN HERITAGE

And: COMMISSIONER FOR HOUSING

No. A88/134

AAT No. 4863

Housing

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Housing - whether applicant should be granted priority transfer under housing assistance program

Public Rental Housing Assistance Program 1987, s.4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 19

Housing Assistance Ordinance 1987, s.12

Interpretation Ordinance 1967 (ACT), s.27

Acts Interpretation Act 1901, s.33(3)

Collector of Customs (N.S.W.) v Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty. Ltd. [1979] FCA 21; (1979) 2 ALD 1

HEARING

CANBERRA

10:1:1989

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

In this application for review the applicant seeks review of a decision of the Commissioner for Housing for the Australian Capital Territory ("the A.C.T.") determining that the applicant should not be granted priority transfer under the Public Rental Housing Assistance Program ("the Program").

2. The primary facts are set out in the statement lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act"), for convenience of reading renumbered from the original:

"Public Rental Housing Assistance Program

(1) On 19 August 1987 the Housing Assistance Ordinance 1987

commenced. Section 12 allows the Commissioner for

Housing for the Australian Capital Territory ("the

Commissioner") to prepare housing assistance programs.

The section further provides that a program shall not be

implemented except with the approval of the Minister.

(2) On 16 December 1987 William John Harris, as delegate of

the Minister of State for the Arts, Sport, the

Environment, Tourism and Territories, approved a program

entitled 'Public Rental Housing Assistance Program' which

was notified in the Commonwealth of Australia Gazette of

23 December 1987.

(3) Clauses 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 19 of the program provide as

follows:-

Eligibility for Assistance

4.(1)Subject to this Program, where an Applicant makes

an application for assistance, that Applicant shall be

eligible for assistance if:

(a) the Applicant is a person whose lawful presence in

Australia is not subject to any limitations as to

time imposed by law;

(b) the Applicant is resident or employed in the ACT;

(c) the Applicant, or each of joint Applicants, has

attained the age of sixteen years;

(d) the applicant does not possess any interest in

residential real property located anywhere in

Australia;

(e) the total value of the assets of the Applicant, or

the combined total value of the assets of the joint

Applicants as the case may be, does not exceed

$20,000;

(f) where the household consists of more than two

independent persons - each independent person, other

than an Applicant, is an immediate family relation

of an Applicant;

(g) where the household consists of one person alone -

the weekly income of the Applicant does not exceed

60% of ACT Average Weekly Earnings;

(h) where the household consists of two persons - the

weekly income of the Applicant, or the combined

weekly income of the joint Applicants as the case

may be, does not exceed 100% of ACT Average Weekly

Earnings; and

(i) where the household consists of more than two

persons - the weekly income of the Applicant, or the

combined weekly income of the joint Applicants as

the case may be, plus 10% of the total of the weekly

incomes of all independent persons in the household

other than the Applicant, or the joint Applicants as

the case may be, does not exceed 100% of ACT Average

Weekly Earnings plus 10% of ACT Average Weekly

Earnings for each person in the household, whether

independent or not, in excess of two persons.

(2) Where the household consists of more than one

independent person:-

(a) at least two and no more than two persons in the

household shall apply as joint Applicants; and

(b) the Commissioner may require that the person with

the highest weekly income in the household apply as

one of the joint Applicants.

Exceptional Circumstances

5. Where an Applicant is not eligible for assistance

but would experience severe hardship if assistance

was not granted, the Commissioner may nevertheless

approve an application made under this Program and

allocate assistance, provided that the provision of

assistance would be in accordance with the object of

the Program.

Temporary Assistance

6.(1) Where an Applicant is not eligible for assistance,

the Commissioner may nevertheless register the

application and allocate temporary assistance for a

specified period, and such further period as the

Commissioner may subsequently approve, if that

Applicant:-

(a) has recently lost their accommodation due to

circumstances beyond their control and is unable to

obtain adequate alternative accommodation;

(b) has acquired a lease from the Commonwealth over

vacant residential land in the ACT, would otherwise

be eligible for assistance and proposes to

construct, but has not completed construction of, a

residence on that land; or

(c) possesses an interest in residential real property

somewhere in Australia, would otherwise be eligible

for assistance and undertakes to dispose of that

property within a period of 6 months, or within such

further period as the Commissioner may approve.

(2) Where a tenant is in receipt of assistance by virtue

of paragraph (1)(c) and becomes eligible for

assistance, the Commissioner shall allocate

assistance, subject to this Program, for an

indefinite period.

Allocation of Assistance

8.(1) Assistance shall not be allocated to an Applicant

unless the application of that Applicant is

registered at the time of allocation.

(2) Where an Applicant is not eligible for assistance at

the time of allocation of assistance, registration

of the application shall be cancelled.

(3) Where an Applicant has not been eligible for

assistance at some time while their application has

been registered, registration of the application may

be cancelled.

(4) Where, in relation to residential accommodation in

the ACT, an Applicant has an outstanding debt in

respect of rent owed to the Commissioner or to the

Commonwealth, or has previously breached a condition

or term of a tenancy agreement to which the

Commissioner or the Commonwealth was a party, the

Commissioner may refuse to allocate assistance.

(5) Subject to this program, the Commissioner shall

allocate assistance, as it becomes available, in

order that applications have been registered.

Priority Assistance

10.(1) An Applicant, who is eligible for assistance or

whose application has been approved under clause 5

or 6, may apply for the priority allocation of

assistance, in advance of their position on the

waiting list, where the Applicant has a special need

for the early allocation of assistance due to:

(a) ill health or disability;

(b) the inadequate nature of their current

accommodation;

(c) severe financial difficulties;

(d) domestic violence;

(e) imminent eviction from their accommodation; or

(f) other similar problems.

(2) The Commissioner shall not grant priority allocation

of assistance unless:-

(a) the Applicant has been resident or employed in the

ACT for the six month(s) period immediately prior to

the time at which the application for priority

allocation is considered by the Commissioner; or

(b) in any other case, the Commissioner considers that

the circumstances are exceptional.

Transfers

19.(1) The Commissioner may:

(a) upon application by a tenant who is eligible for

assistance; or

(b) where it is reasonably required for the purposes of

good administration;

transfer a tenant to other accommodation under the

Commissioner's control, provided that notice of the

transfer has been served on the tenant not less than

14 days prior to the date of the transfer.

(2) In determining whether to approve an application for

transfer the Commissioner shall have regard to,

amongst the other circumstances of the particular

case:

(a) the availability of suitable accommodation; and

(b) the reasons stated in the application.

(4) For the purposes of transferring tenants under Clause 19,

conditions of eligibility, registration and priority

assistance are the same as those for new Applicants

applying for housing assistance.

(5) The Applicant applied on 2 June 1988 for priority housing

to accommodate himself and his family;

(The applicant, his wife and three children aged 11, 10

and 6 were here named)

(6) The application was approved on 7 June 1988 for a 3

bedroom house in the Inner South District. Priority

waiting time for such a unit at that time was

approximately 7-8 weeks.

(7) Mr Heritage had previously been a tenant of the

Commonwealth in the ACT, but had an unsatisfactory

tenancy history. When he vacated his previous property

there were arrears well in excess of $2,000 outstanding

on his rental account.

(8) Mr Heritage had negotiated an agreement, to which he has

adhered, to clear the arrears by instalments, and given

this undertaking, it was agreed in accordance with Clause

8(4) of the program to grant tenancy of a further

property.

(9) On 29 June 1988 the Applicant advised that he had lost

the temporary housing he had obtained, and required

accommodation immediately.

(10) The only suitably sized unit immediately available was a

3 bedroom flat at Jerilderie Court, Reid.

(11) The Applicant was offered the flat and accepted it the

following day, 30 June 1988. The offer was made and

accepted on the understanding that it would be in full

satisfaction of the Applicant's registration on the

waiting list for priority approval. If a house was

desired at a later date the Applicant would need to

re-register on the list and wait the full waiting time.

(12) On 15 September 1988 the Applicant registered on the

waiting list for transfer to a house, and lodged an

application for priority. The reason given was the

unsatisfactory environment at the Flats which was

resulting in stressful living conditions for the family.

(13) The priority application was considered by the Priority

Housing Committee. The Committee recommended that the

application for priority assistance be rejected. The

grounds for rejection were that the Applicant's needs

were not considered to be greater than others on the

waiting list and that there was therefore no basis for

placing them ahead of other people on the waiting list.

This recommendation was considered and accepted by Mr

Richard Neville McDonald, a person authorised to act on

behalf of the Commissioner under Clause 10(2) of the

Program. Mr McDonald accepted the recommendation of the

Committee.

(14) By letter dated 19 September 1988 the Applicant appealed

to the Commissioner for Housing against the decision to

refuse priority."

3. At the hearing evidence was given by the applicant; by two tenants of the Jerilderie Court flats; by a rehabilitation consultant; by the District Manager of the City Housing Office; and by the Assistant Manager in the Rentals Operations Area in the Housing Trust who is also the chairman of the Priority Housing Committee. The evidence so given supported the findings of fact set out above, but established also some further points of detail which should be set out.

4. The applicant had previously lived and worked in Canberra, where he had a government house, but had moved to Newcastle where he worked until he suffered an injury to his shoulder which has prevented him from working. He has been receiving worker's compensation, and has obviously been living in straitened circumstances. He decided to return to Canberra and for two weeks the whole family lived, he said, in a car. They then moved into a caravan, but in June 1988 the owners of the caravan wanted to take the caravan away on holiday. The applicant applied to the Housing Trust for a three bedroom house on 29 June 1988, and on 30 June he was offered the Jerilderie Court flat which was the only accommodation available at the time, save for Jerrabombera House, at which only single rooms were available. He was told, and accepted, that if he accepted the flat he would have to apply for a transfer to a house and to take his turn. The procedure for this is that the applicant is put on a "Wait Turn" list and could be expected to wait some eighteen months for a house.

5. The applicant's complaints about the flat allocated to him are not related to its physical features but rather to environmental factors. His children have picked up a syringe and some kind of smoking apparatus related to drug use in the garden. He says that he does not get on with the area manager, and that there have been altercations with tenants over his children and over the family cat. There are some sixty flats in the complex and the applicant says that in fact he gets on with all but about five tenants. He complains of the environment, referring not only to the apparent propinquity of drug users but to incidents involving noise and alcoholic behaviour in the street. One side of the flats has a frontage to Ainslie Avenue, which is en route to Canberra's Civic area from a housing complex which, for reasons that need not be stated here, can give rise to problems.

6. From the evidence of Mr. P.D. Kiley, the rehabilitation consultant, and from my own assessment of the applicant it is clear that he has become fixated on what he sees as a housing problem which he claims is having an adverse effect on his family and in particular on his children. He worried about it so much that at one stage he went to The Canberra Times about what he perceived as the injustice of the situation. As a result the matter received publicity, and it appears to me that this has exacerbated his problems in fitting in with other tenants at the flats. He has had some very unpleasant and unnecessary notes, which were tendered in evidence, put in his letter box. On a rational and general level however the evidence of the two tenants, who appeared for the respondent, makes it clear that at least some tenants resented the adverse publicity given to Jerilderie Court, which they regarded as unfortunate and inaccurate. Why they did so is explained by reference to the character and quality of Jerilderie Court.

7. Anyone reading these Reasons so far might be pardoned for thinking that the flats are some kind of high density/low grade/low status housing. I suppose that they should be called high density, but low grade or low status they are not. The applicant himself affirmed that they are of a high standard in physical terms. They are two-storied, or rather built on two levels, arranged around small central courtyards and having pleasantly treed and grassed surrounds. From the evidence of the tenants it appeared that people are proud and happy to live there. The evidence called by the respondent indicated that this is reflected by the fact that the flats are regarded as popular with tenants and that very few problems come from them. It is one of the very few flat complexes that applicants single out as a preference, and if they want a flat there they have to wait for a longer time than average: "... most people once they become residents ... are usually very hesitant about leaving it and will often put up with their own self-imposed overcrowded situation just to remain in the complex" (Tr.45). The witness who made this statement, the district manager, was not referring to the flats as a whole being overcrowded, but rather to the fact that families needing a larger flat nevertheless preferred to remain at Jerilderie Court notwithstanding.

8. The applicant is a man who, in my opinion, is not really able to relate to some features of living in this kind of flat complex. Unfortunately it had to be his children who found the objectionable items that someone had thrown on to the ground, in all likelihood while passing by, and he has come to brood on what he sees as threats to the well-being of his family, so much so that he is apparently generally unwilling to leave the flat during the day. I think that the truth is that he and his family are simply incompatible with the concept of high density living.

9. For the applicant, Mr. Anforth of the Welfare Rights Centre submitted that the applicant satisfied clause 19 of the Program and that the only question was whether clause 10(1) should be seen to have been satisfied. In particular he submitted that -

(i) 10(1)(a) was satisfied because of evidence of

stress-related symptoms suffered by the

applicant's children;

(ii) 10(1)(b) was satisfied because of inadequacy of

playing areas for children;

(iii) 10(1)(f) was satisfied because of the poor

relations existing between the applicant and the

Area Manager and other tenants.

There is some force in these points, though not much, in my opinion, in point (ii). But clause 10(2)(b) must in any event be satisfied. No point was taken by the respondent that clause 10(2)(a) was not, but in any event the provisions of clause 10(2)(b) require that the circumstances be exceptional.

10. I do not consider that the circumstances are exceptional, or that on general discretionary grounds a decision should be made in favour of the applicant. The fact is that we are not here concerned with optimum satisfaction of individual desires for housing. We are dealing with a scheme of government housing and, in particular, with the priority sector of that scheme. To put the applicant into the priority list for a house would disadvantage others who are waiting for such, some of whom have been waiting for as long as eighteen months. I cannot think that this is a decision that could properly be made. The applicant and his family were given, effectively on one day's notice, accommodation that large numbers of people would fall over themselves to have, and it would not be right to advance the applicant to the head of the queue for a house. The decision under review should in my opinion be affirmed.

11. Mr. Anforth, with the care that has characterised his work, and that of his colleagues and predecessors at the Centre, also contended that once a priority application had been granted the respondent had no power to revoke it. He referred to s.27 of the Interpretation Ordinance 1967 (A.C.T.) (corresponding to s.33(3) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 (Cwlth)) and to Collector of Customs (N.S.W.) v Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty. Ltd. [1979] FCA 21; (1979) 2 ALD 1. On this footing it was submitted that what was put as the "voluntary" alteration by the application of his application to one for a three bedroom flat was either not valid or constituted a new application which would not necessarily have the effect of rescinding the first application.

12. The flaw in this submission in my opinion is that the first application was not for a house or for a flat but was an application for priority housing, and priority approval was recommended for a three bedroom house (T11/28). When at the end of June the applicant's need became urgent, that priority approval was satisfied, with his consent, by the allocation to him of the Jerilderie Court flat. The original approval was thereby, in my opinion, both satisfied and exhausted.

13. It is nevertheless appropriate in this case to make one recommendation. The fact is that there is demonstrated incompatibility between the applicant and a small number of other tenants, in which number I do not include the two tenants who gave evidence, and perhaps between the applicant and the area manager, although I only heard the applicant's side of this. A new charity on the part of the applicant and his family on the one side, and on the part of the small number of tenants with whom he has had some degree of confrontation on the other might lead to a fresh start. For this to happen however could require a degree of counselling and support that may not be available, and to resolve the situation it would in my opinion be proper to provide accommodation for the applicant in a house if it were available on an exchange basis. It may admittedly be unlikely that the situation will arise, but if there were a tenant of a three bedroom house who wished to move to Jerilderie Court, an exchange could properly be arranged. There was in fact evidence that there are tenants of two bedroom houses who wish to transfer to Jerilderie Court. I am conscious of the evidence that was given that any move involves a substantial administrative cost, but it may be that any continued friction at Jerilderie Court would have its own cost. It could be better, if an opportunity such as that which I have envisaged did arise, to resolve in this way what has become an awkward situation. Normally, I was told, such an exchange would require that both parties not be in default, but I consider that in all the circumstances of this particular case that requirement could properly be waived. This recommendation however is no more than that, and forms no part of the expression of the Tribunal's decision.
