ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - accountant - member of accountancy partnerships proceeds from commissions derived from promotion of tax minimisation schemes - proceeds paid into trusts associated with the taxpayer - proceeds assessed against taxpayer - additional tax imposed - whether income properly assessable in hands of taxpayer

REASONS FOR DECISION

10 June 1988 Deputy President C.J.Bannon Q.C.

Mr C.J. Stevens (Member)

Mr G.R. Taylor (Member)

These proceedings, heard together by consent, relate to objections to Income Tax Assessments, or Amended Assessments, upon the taxpayer in respect to financial years each ending 30 June, being the years 1978 to 1983 inclusive. In each case, the particulars of objection are lengthy. In each case, the amended assessment, or the assessment, includes sums assessed as penalties with some remissions.

The taxpayer, at all relevant times, was a registered public accountant and a graduate in Commerce of an Australian university. Having practised as an accountant, in partnership with others, in a country town, for some time, during the year ended 30 June 1976 he became aware of the activities of a company which approached his firm regarding a proposal that its higher earning clients participate in partnership share trading arrangements (Tr.pp.14 and 23). The proposal was that by doing so the accountancy firm's clients would minimise their taxation liabilities. The company would charge the accountancy firm's clients a fee for its services and would arrange for preparation by Victorian accountants of tax returns for each partnership showing a substantial and deductible loss (Tr.pp.14 15). The accountancy firm would then prepare its clients' income tax returns, including a claimed share of the loss on share trading as set out in the partnership tax returns prepared by the Victorian accountants (Tr.p.15). The scheme was said to be backed by "the usual plethora of counsels' opinions". The scheme succeeded and was upheld by the High Court of Australia in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Westraders Pty.Ltd. [1980] HCA 24; (1980) 144 CLR 55.

The taxpayer said that in December 1976, he and another accountant partner in the accountancy firm (for convenience he will be called "Morgan") acquired an existing proprietary company (to be dubbed "Fabrications") to promote tax minimisation schemes of the type known as "Son of Curran" share trading partnerships (Tr.pp.16,17). Fabrications had its office at the taxpayer's home (Tr.p.18). The taxpayer and Morgan approached various people, including clients of other firms (Tr.p.18) to participate in the tax scheme. The clients of Fabrications included clients of the taxpayer's accountancy firm and clients of other firms (Tr.pp.18,19). In similar vein to the firstmentioned company, Fabrications charged a fee in the vicinity of 15 per cent of the tax loss achieved by the clients. In the financial year ending 30 June 1977, its income was net $18,500 (Tr.p.19). In May 1978, a company with a more euphonious name was incorporated (to be dubbed "Guardians") and it became the trustee of a trust which we will dub the "Guardian Trust" (Tr.pp.20 and 25).

The partners in the accountancy firm, other than the taxpayer and Morgan, were not happy with the expansion and risk taking proposed by the taxpayer and Morgan. On 7 March 1978 the partnership was dissolved, the taxpayer and Morgan opened new offices and set up business under a new trade name which may be dubbed "Silver and Morgan". Silver and Morgan took with them the staff of the old accountancy firm and "a sufficient client following from the previous firm so it was able to carry on the normal functions of an accounting practice" (Tr.p.27).

During the financial year ended 30 June 1979, the firm of Silver and Morgan operated. It was agreed that the taxpayer (Silver) would devote himself to the administration of Guardians while Morgan administered the accountancy firm for the partnership. According to the taxpayer, by this time a number of persons who were not clients of Silver and Morgan, participated in the partnership tax schemes administered by Guardians (Tr.p.28).

New tax minimisation schemes were being adumbrated amongst the cognoscenti, including one relating to partnerships designed to gain advantage under S.59AA of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 (Cth.) ("the Act") and one relating to sales tax. Some lawyers assured the taxpayer "that everything was quite within the law" (Tr.p.28) as regards the sales tax scheme. Guardians set up an office and managed the sales tax scheme, provided documents and ongoing service to the retail organisations participating, charging a fee of 25 per cent of the sales tax "saved" (Tr.p.29). Legal matters were referred to a firm of solicitors or to the retailers' own lawyers (Tr.p.29). The taxpayer alleged (Tr.p.71) that this scheme was the subject of the stated case in Brayson Motors Pty .Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1985] HCA 20; (1985) 156 CLR 651.

In evidence, the taxpayer (Tr.p.31) said Silver and Morgan charged Guardians fees for services in preparing income tax returns for "the tax loss partnerships" relating to S.59AA.

Guardians is now an insolvent company (Tr.p.36) but in 1978/79 it caused a three storey building to be erected in the country town where Silver and Morgan practised. Silver and Morgan occupied the top floor, while Guardians occupied an office on the middle floor and later moved to a part of the top floor (Tr.p.44). The taxpayer asserted the two businesses were quite separate (Tr.p.44) and also claimed that Guardians promoted tax minimisation partnership schemes (Tr.pp.37,38). But one is caused to wonder if anyone would have heard of Guardians, if it were not for the presence of the taxpayer and Morgan.

In March 1980, a significant change of pace occurred.

According to the taxpayer:

"(Morgan) had moral misgivings about his involvement in the tax minimization ... And he opted in around March of that year not to take any further personal involvement in [Guardians] and after discussing it with himself it was agreed that fees paid to [Guardians] after that date, whichever the date was, would in fact be paid to the Ctaxpayer's3 Family Trust by means of a distribution" (Tr.p.39).

The taxpayer's Family Trust was created on 7 March 1978 (Tr.p.40) the settler being Morgan and the trustee being Fabrications. On 19 July 1978 a new trustee was appointed, being a company of which the taxpayer and his wife were the directors (Tr.p.40). Large sums of money were received by the taxpayer's family trust from Guardians and in the year ended 30 June 1980 the trust had a net income of $200,071.21. Evidence was given as to tax assessments issued to the trustee of the family trust, to Guardians and the taxpayer (Tr.p.41). He said that tax had been paid, but it is not clear to which assessment he was referring.

Another venture in which the taxpayer was a moving party, was a reafforestation scheme on land owned by a family company or directly by the taxpayer (Tr.p.38). The scheme was managed by Guardians and subscribers claimed tax rebates for money spent in growing trees (Tr.p.38). This scheme appears to have involved partnerships (Tr.pp.37/38).

The taxpayer was at pains to allege (Tr.p.46) that Guardians clientele came from clients which included some clients of Silver and Morgan, but that the majority were drawn from other sources. During the year ended 30 June 1981, Guardians disposed of its business to another company which we will call Old Guard (Tr.p.46), as trustee for the Old Guard Trust created on 5 August 1980 with Guardians as settlor, and beneficiaries including the Silver and Morgan Family Trusts.

As at 1 July 1980 the partnership of Silver and Morgan merged with another firm of whom the principal will be referred to as "Hands". Mr Hands also had a family trust which was a beneficiary in the Old Guard Trust. The new accountancy partnership of Silver Morgan and Hands was dissolved on 30 June 1981. The taxpayer then went into partnership with two new persons in what will be called "the New Partnership". At Transcript p.44 the taxpayer said that Guardians, as trustee for the Guardian Trust, "again promoted the minimisation scheme partnerships involving partnerships acquiring the rights to fell timber".

At Transcript pp.46.47 the taxpayer gave evidence of the Old Guard Trust becoming a beneficiary of two other trusts. These two trusts made distributions to Old Guard so that its income consisted of management fees, trust distribution and interest (Tr.p.47). Old Guard was assessed for all of its income with the exception of income derived from trust distributions (Tr.p.47).

In the early months of the tax year ending 30 June 1982, Old Guard was replaced as trustee of the Old Guard Trust by another company which we will call "Hispaniola". The directors of Hispaniola were Silver, Morgan and Hands (Tr.p.51). According to the taxpayer, the income of Hispaniola as trustee of the Old Guard Trust for the year ended 30 June 1982 was $1,829,891.02 consisting of $1,680,451.21 for management fees, $149,439.75 profit on sale of land, less expenditure of $51,707.47 leaving a net income of $1,778,183.55 (Tr.p.51). During the year ended 30 June 1982 the taxpayer's family trust received income, being commissions, from the promotion of sales tax minimisation schemes (Tr.p.51), presumably from Hispaniola, Old Guard and/or Guardians.

At the end of February 1982, Morgan and Hands determined to end their association with Hispaniola (Tr.p.54). A new company purchased the business of Old Guard Trust. The new company will be referred to as Pandora (Tr.p.54). Pandora, according to the taxpayer, "became involved in the same type of trust stripping arrangement as had (Old Guard) to reduce its tax liability" (Tr.p.54). Pandora's directors were the taxpayer and the company which was the trustee of his family trust. Pandora was trustee of another trust which actually distributed $1077 each to two reputable charities (Tr.p.54).

Proceedings were instituted in the Supreme Court of New South Wales by the respondent to recover from the taxpayer the various amounts assessed against him for the years ending 30 June 1978 to 1983 inclusive, totalling $3,800,316.24. The Commissioner also sought interlocutory relief by way of Mareva type injunction against the assets of the taxpayer. In support of that application, two affidavits, by the same deponent, were sworn and filed. The deponent was a qualified auditor and a high ranking officer in the respondent's office. At the taxpayer's request, the affidavits were admitted into evidence in these proceedings as Exhibits B and C. In Exhibit C, paragraph 3 of the affidavit sets out the basis of the Commissioner's assessments. Leaving out the actual names of the trusts and the various accountancy partnerships of which the taxpayer was a member, part of the paragraph reads as follows:

"The assessments relate to amounts returned as income by four trusts associated with the first defendant, being... and... and described on the respective returns as "commissions received". The notices of assessment were issued on the basis that the "commissions received" by those four trusts was not income earned by the trusts but was income of the partnerships, of which the defendant was a member, trading at different times as... and... The share of the "commissions received" which has been assessed to the first defendant, was in 1979 one half; in 1980 one half; in 1981 one third; and in 1982 one third ..."

The affidavit annexed copies of various trust deeds and documentation referring to the source of the funding of the trusts which appears to come from the corporations already mentioned under the pseudonyms of Guardians, Old Guard and one other. The affidavit Exhibit B gives further details of some of the trusts. Paragraph 6 of the affidavit reads in part:

"The distribution described above as made by the four named participant trusts were ultimately stripped by means of several different strip plans. Whichever strip plan was used the end result was to leave the income deriving trust in a position where its income had been ostensibly distributed but a sum representing the bulk of that income came back into its hands and was treated by the trust as a capital item. The source of the funds comprising the capital item was the original trust income."

There seems to be no reason to dissent from the bulk of the two quoted statements by the taxation officer, which appear to be supported by the accompanying documents and it appears to us that the factual statements in those affidavits are correct. Whether the funds in the hands of the family trusts were derived by them immediately or mediately through distributions made by corporations such as Guardians and Old Guard, they appear nevertheless to be income derived from the tax minimisation schemes offered by the various partnerships of which the taxpayer was a member. The distributions of funds made by the trusts do not affect the question whether or not the income of each trust is to be treated as income of the accountancy partnerships for taxation purposes.

The only real question which concerns us is whether or not the income generated was income generated by the accountancy partnerships or by the corporations such as Guardians and Old Guard, and the various trusts.

We are conscious of the taxpayer's evidence that clientele of one or more of the companies was drawn from sources which included partnership clients but were not exclusively so. It is obvious that the income of a corporate entity cannot be attributed, for tax purposes, to its corporators or to trusts which it manages, simply because of association of interests. If the taxpayer had been able to satisfy us that his accountancy partnership was not responsible for diverting fees for tax minimisation schemes from the partnership businesses to the companies, or to the family trusts, then the situation would be quite different from that discussed in Tupicoff v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4851. But as was pointed out in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55, the choice principle does not apply to an arrangement which does not attract tax benefits provided by specific provisions of the Act, but simply diverts income from one taxpayer to another. Such arrangements made prior to 27 May 1981 are caught by s.260 of the Act and from thence by Part IVA ibid. Further, the taxpayer has not established, to our satisfaction, how many clients came to the various companies directly and what quantum of fees were derived from them as alleged. One matter raised on behalf of the respondent was a submission that one of the alleged trusts had never been formally established. We see no reason to doubt the taxpayer's assertion to the contrary although the trust deed was not produced.

We are left with a very strong belief that the companies and trusts were nothing more than vehicles for carrying on the tax minimisation schemes marketed by the taxpayer and his partners. It is a somewhat melancholy, but fairly well known fact that tax minimisation and advice thereon in these times, begins with advice given by an accountant rather than a lawyer. In this case, the corporations and trusts appear to have been dealing with schemes within the scope of the taxpayer's accountancy practices.

Bearing in mind the onus cast upon the taxpayer by s.190(b) of the Act, we consider the onus is not discharged in the case of any assessment in dispute. The objections to the assessments are disallowed and the objection decisions confirmed.
