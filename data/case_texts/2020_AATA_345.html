REASONS FOR DECISION

5 February 2020

INTRODUCTION

  1. This matter concerns the characterisation, for taxation purposes, of the profits made when three parcels of land were sold. The Applicant contends that the profits from each sale are capital gains while the Respondent contends that the profits are income. If they had been capital gains they would have attracted concessional taxation treatment.
  2. For reasons that follow, I am of the view that the reviewable decisions should be affirmed. As will be demonstrated in these Reasons, the Applicant has failed to discharge the onus upon him to prove that:
    • (a) the net proceeds of sale of the three land parcels were capital gains and not income;
    • (b) the shortfall interest charges should not have been imposed or should have been further remitted; and
    • (c) the administrative penalty from the 2008 financial year should not have been imposed or should have been further remitted.

SUMMARY OF THE ISSUES

  1. Section 6.5 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA) stipulates that assessable income includes income according to ordinary concepts, which is called “ordinary income”.
  2. The net proceeds will be income if they were obtained in the ordinary course of business, or as part of a transaction entered into for the purpose of making a profit or financial gain.
  3. Between 1993 and 2001, the Applicant engaged in property development with respect to residential property. He purchased land, developed it and sold it in residential lots.[1] He did this through various entities controlled by him that were part of what is collectively known as the Doyle Group. From 2004, the Doyle Group purchased, developed and sold industrial land. The land parcels relevant to this application were situated at Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale, in Queensland.
  4. At all relevant times, the Applicant was:
    • (a) the controlling mind and director of the entities comprising the Doyle Group;
    • (b) the sole director and shareholder of South East Queensland National Rail Centre Bromelton Pty Ltd (Bromelton Trustee) as trustee for the South East Queensland National Rail Centre Bromelton Trust (Bromelton Trust);
    • (c) the sole director and shareholder of Mackay Industrial Land Pty Ltd (Mackay Trustee) as trustee for the Mackay Industrial Trust (Mackay Trust); and
    • (d) the sole director and shareholder of Brendale Industrial Pty Ltd (Brendale Trustee) as trustee for the Brendale Industrial Trust (Brendale Trust).[2]
  5. The Craig Doyle Property Trust was the sole unit holder in the Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale trusts. The Applicant had an entitlement to distributions from the Craig Doyle Property Trust.
  6. The character (income or capital) of the profits from the sale of the parcels of land at Bromelton by the Bromelton Trust, Mackay by the Mackay Trust, and Brendale by the Brendale Trust impacted the Applicant’s assessable income, including whether deductions were available.
  7. The Applicant treated the profits from the sales of the Mackay and Brendale properties as capital gains in the 2008 financial year. He treated the profit from the sale of the Bromelton property as capital gain in the 2009 financial year. However, on 17 March 2015, the Commissioner issued amended assessments with respect to the Applicant, treating the profits as income on the basis that the each of the sales had been made either:
    • (a) in the ordinary course of the business of the Doyle Group or of the trust that owned the property; or
    • (b) as part of an isolated transaction that was entered into with a purpose that included developing the property to sell for profit.
  8. The amended assessments increased the Applicant’s assessable income and resulted in the imposition of administrative penalties and shortfall interest charges.
  9. On 15 May 2015 the Applicant lodged an objection to the assessments.[3]
  10. On 22 June 2016, the Commissioner of Taxation (the Commissioner):
    • (a) confirmed the Applicant’s taxable income for the 2008 financial year should be increased from $141,484 to $12,357,752 (an increase of $12,216,268);[4]
    • (b) confirmed the Applicant’s taxable income for the 2009 financial year should be increased from $120,645 to $1,122,993 (an increase of $1,102,348);[5]
    • (c) reduced the shortfall interest charge for the 2008 financial year from $1,683,447.39 to $1,087,260.86;[6]
    • (d) reduced the shortfall interest charge for the 2009 financial year from $136,298.30 to $80,963.53;[7] and
    • (e) Reduced the administrative penalty charge from 1,420,034.70 to $284,005.95 (being 5%).[8]
  11. On 19 August 2016 the Applicant applied for review of the Objection Decision pursuant to s.14ZZ(1)(a)(i) of the TAA.[9] He contends that, at the time he contracted to purchase each property, he intended to develop and lease it long-term, thereby building an asset base for the Doyle Group.
  12. The Applicant asks the Tribunal to decide that:
    • (a) the profits from the sale of the Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale properties were capital gains and should have been assessed as such, rather than as income, by the Respondent;
    • (b) the shortfall interest charges should be remitted in full; and
    • (c) the administrative penalty for the 2008 financial year should be remitted in full.
  13. Section 14ZZ(1)(a)(i) and 14ZQ of the TAA provide that a person who is dissatisfied with the Commissioner's objection decision may apply to the Tribunal for review of the decision if the decision is not an ineligible income tax remission decision.[10] I am satisfied that none of the objection decisions that the Applicant wishes the Tribunal to review are ineligible income tax remission decisions.
  14. Section 280-170 of schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a person may object, in the manner set out in Part IVC, against a decision of the Commissioner not to remit an amount of shortfall interest charge if the amount of the charge that was not remitted is more than 20% of the additional amount. The shortfall interest charges have already been partly remitted. Neither charge is more than 20% of the additional amount. Accordingly, the Applicant has no objection right in respect of the charges.

DETERMINING WHETHER PROCEEDS OF SALE ARE CAPITAL IN NATURE

  1. A financial gain will be income if it arises in the ordinary course of the taxpayer’s business. It may also be income if it arises from an isolated business operation or commercial transaction entered into otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on the taxpayer’s business but with the intention or purpose of making a profit or gain. Whether it does depends on the circumstances of the case, and generally if the circumstances give rise to an inference that the taxpayer’s purpose in entering into the transaction was to make a profit or gain, the profit or gain will be income.[11]
  2. If the decision to sell an asset is taken after its acquisition, there having been no such intention at the time of acquisition, the profit will be capital because it proceeds from a mere realisation (unless the asset is a revenue asset for other reasons). However:

[I]t is quite another thing if the decision to sell is taken by way of an implementation of an intention or purpose, existing at the time of acquisition, or profit-making by sale, at least in the context of carrying on a business or carrying out a business operation or commercial transaction.[12]

  1. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium (Myer)[13] concerned a fixed term loan that was made with the intention of selling the right to the interest under the loan to a third party in exchange for a lump sum payment. The lump sum payment was held to be income because the intention in making the loan was to sell the right to interest for a lump sum (and because converting the right to future interest income to a lump sum did not strip it of its character as income). With respect to the nature of the transaction the High Court, in a unanimous judgment, observed:

Because the business is carried on with a view to profit, a gain made in the ordinary course of carrying on the business is invested with the profit-making purpose, thereby stamping the profit with the character of income. But a gain made otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on a business which nevertheless arises from a transaction entered into by the taxpayer with the intention or purpose of making a profit or gain may well constitute income. Whether it does depends very much on the circumstances of the case. Generally speaking, however, it may be said that if the circumstances are such as to give rise to the inference that the taxpayer’s intention or purpose in entering into the transaction was to make a profit or gain, the profit or gain will be income, notwithstanding that the transaction was extraordinary judged by reference to the ordinary course of the taxpayer’s business. Nor does the fact that a profit or gain is made as the result of an isolated venture or a ‘one-off’ transaction preclude it from being properly characterised as income.[14]

And later:

The transactions in question here were entered into by Myer in the course of its business. The transactions, more particularly the assignment, were novel in the sense that it was the first time that Myer had entered into such an arrangement. But this fact does not take them out of the course of the carrying on of Myer’s profit-making business.[15]

  1. The Applicant controlled each of the Trustee companies and the Doyle Group as a whole. He describes himself as the “controlling mind of the Doyle Group”.[16] As will become apparent, he took a hands-on approach to managing these entities, personally dealing with the Doyle Group’s legal advisers, finance provider, investors (and potential investors) and other entities that conducted business with the Doyle Group. Accordingly, the Applicant’s intention with respect to each property is the intention of the Trustee company, and his intention may be inferred from the actions of the Doyle Group and his own words and actions.
  2. The Applicant contends, and the Respondent does not dispute, that for the purposes of determining the Applicant’s intention at the time of acquisition, the time a property is acquired is when the contract to purchase that property was entered into. I respectfully agree. The Applicant’s intention at the relevant time may be gleaned not only from evidence of representations and conduct leading up to, and contemporaneous to, the acquisition of each property. It could potentially be gleaned from the representations and conduct of the Applicant and the Doyle Group after acquisition of each property.
  3. The Applicant submits that an intention to sell the property must be the sole or dominant purpose for the net proceeds to take the character of income, relying on McCurry v Federal Commissioner of Taxation[17] in support of this proposition. That case involved two brothers who purchased land, constructed three units on the land, advertised the units for sale, lived in the units with relatives, and ultimately sold the units for a profit. Davies J held that the character of the proceeds was income on the basis that “it is the main or dominant purpose of the scheme which is important”.[18] However, this statement was based on the case of Admin Exploration Pty Ltd (in Liq) v FCT,[19] a single Judge decision of the High Court that pre-dated the decision in Myer and was concerned with the interpretation of the first limb of the old s26(a) of the ITAA which provides:

The assessable income of the taxpayer shall include - (a) profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit –making by sale.

  1. In Admin Exploration Mason J stated:

[I]t is accepted that the first part of sec. 26(a) is not satisfied if the taxpayer shows that the acquisition of the property was not actuated by the sole or dominant purpose of profit-making by sale.[20]

  1. The second limb of the old 26(a) read “or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit–making undertaking or scheme” and in relation to this limb His Honour said:

[T]here is no reference to ‘purpose' in the second part of sec. 26(a), but it is firmly established that it has no application to what is the mere realisation of a capital asset...[21]

  1. It is noteworthy that His Honour considered the lack of a reference to “purpose” in the second limb to be significant, and that he decided the second limb did not apply where the character of the asset in question had already been determined to be capital in nature. What is clear is that His Honour did not consider the second limb to pose the same test as the first limb.
  2. In Moana Sands Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation[22] the taxpayer had acquired beachfront land with the twofold purpose of working and/or selling the surplus sand and holding the land until it became appropriate to sell it at a profit. The land was ultimately resumed by the government which paid compensation for it. In a joint judgment, the Full Federal Court held that an intention to sell need only be a purpose and need not be the sole or dominant purpose. In that regard their Honours observed:

In the course of the argument, there was discussion whether, notwithstanding the language of the second limb of s26 (a) there was the need for the taxpayer to have the dominant purpose of profit-making by sale before the second limb would apply.[23]

  1. Their Honours agreed with the analysis of Gibbs J (as he was then) in FCT v Bidencope[24] in the following passage (which I have abridged):

Schemes are various, and it is not necessarily possible to say that one purpose of a scheme is predominant; many schemes are designed to secure a number of purposes. If a scheme is designed to secure a profit, it is a profit-making scheme, although it has other purposes as well...The present is of course not a case in which property was acquired for resale, but once it is established that profit arose from the carrying on of a scheme, it is enough that the making of a profit of that kind was one of the purposes of the scheme... [Referring to a previous case] The facts of that case distinguish it from the present, but the decision did not depend on the acceptance of the view that the purpose of profit-making must be the dominant purpose of the scheme if it is to fall within the second limb of s26(a) In the present case, one of the purposes of the scheme was to make a profit, and it can rightly be described as a profit-making scheme.

  1. In reaching their decision on that issue, their Honours also took into account the (then) recent High Court decision in Myer.
  2. To my mind, the requirement that the acquisition of a property be actuated by the sole or dominant purpose of making a profit by sale for the proceeds to be characterised as income is an unduly narrow test, having its origins in only one of the limbs of the old s26(a) of the ITAA. I am satisfied that correct test is the one applied in Moana Sands which is whether the property was acquired as part of a profit-making scheme that included, as one of its purposes, profit making by sale.
  3. The Respondent submits that it is not necessary to show that the Applicant had decided in advance which exact means he would use to realise a profit; and it is sufficient that the trusts acquired the properties with a purpose of making a profit by whichever means would prove most suitable and a profit was later obtained by a means that implemented the initial profit-making purpose. An illustration is found in Steinberg v FCT[25] where Gibbs J (as he then was) said:[26]

I am in agreement with the view expressed by Mason J that ‘it is not an essential element of a profit-making scheme in s26(a) that every step which culminates in the making of a profit should be planned or foreseen before the scheme is put into operation’. Schemes may be precise or vague; every detail may be arranged in advance, or the working out of the plan may be left for decision in the light of circumstances as they may arise. It is no objection to a plan that allows room for manoeuvre. When property is bought with the purpose of making a profit in the easiest and most advantageous way that may present itself, and the taxpayer adopts ‘one of the many alternatives’ that his plan leaves open, thereby returning himself a profit, he will rightly be said to be carrying out a profit-making scheme.

  1. I respectfully accept the Respondent’s submission, and therefore it is incumbent on the Applicant to prove that profit by way of sale was not one of the ways he had in mind that he could realise a financial gain from each property at the time the relevant trusts acquired each property.
  2. This is to be distinguished from a scenario whereby a profit is realised by a means that was not intended such as in the matter of Kratzmann v FCT[27] in which the profit from the sale of a property was held not to have arisen from a profit-making scheme but after the taxpayer’s profit-making scheme had been abandoned, and therefore it was not held to be income. The facts (described in Moana Sands) were that the taxpayer had acquired land to carry out a profit-making scheme that involved borrowing money to erect a building, selling units in the building to repay loans and the cost of the project, and keeping the surplus. For financial reasons he gave up the idea and sold the land at a profit.[28]

THE ONUS AND STANDARD OF PROOF

  1. Under s14ZZK(b) of the TAA the Applicant bears the onus of proving that an objection decision is incorrect.
  2. The relevant standard of proof is the civil standard, specifically, the balance of probabilities. In essence, this requires the Applicant to establish it is more likely than not that the Respondent’s assessments for the two years of income were incorrect. The Full Court of the Federal Court has instructively differentiated between actual “probabilities” compared to “mere possibilities”: “There is... a distinction of substance to be drawn between the probabilities on the one hand and mere possibilities, even if they are real as distinct from fanciful, on the other...”.[29]
  3. As noted by the High Court in Gauci v Federal Commissioner of Taxation[30] there is no “...onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be sustained or supported by evidence. ...unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it [the assessment] will prevail.”

COMMON ELEMENTS GLEANED FROM THE OBJECTIVE EVIDENCE

  1. There are common elements in the Doyle Group’s dealings in relation to the three parcels of land. They are:
    • (a) land was acquired by a unit trust that had been specially created for that purpose. The trust had no assets other than the purchased property, and required short-term, high-interest finance to fund the purchase and development of the property;
    • (b) CFAL lent the money on the basis that it would be repaid by way of re-financing or sale of the subject property;
    • (c) development applications were made to reconfigure and/or re-zone the land;
    • (d) each project was publicised and marketed as an industrial development that would offer lots for lease or sale;
    • (e) there was never any designation of certain lots to be held long-term or ratio of lots that were to be kept for long-term lease;
    • (f) long-term re-financing was not secured, or it appears even sought, in the period between acquisition and sale;
    • (g) no investors were secured;
    • (h) no tenants were secured;
    • (i) there was never any physical development or construction of the land; and
    • (j) the land parcel was sold, in its entirety, for profit.

DISCUSSION

  1. The Applicant has sought to explain away much of the contemporaneous documentary evidence that tends to show that selling the properties for profit was a serious option from the time each property was acquired. This calls for an analysis of his evidence in the context of the objective facts.

The Motorway and Parkinson Projects

  1. The Applicant’s evidence is that the Doyle Group was established in 1993 when it undertook its first sub-division of residential land. The Group bought land, developed it and sub-divided it into residential lots, then sold those lots.[141]
  2. Around 2001, the Applicant wanted the Doyle Group to build an asset base in long-term industrial land holdings by developing industrial lots, and acquiring high value “blue chip” tenants who would provide an income stream, thereby increasing the value of that industrial land so that members of the Doyle Group could leverage borrowing against that land and seek cash injections from commercial third parties and thus be able to use the additional borrowing capacity to become involved in the acquisition of agricultural land over the longer term.[142]
  3. The Doyle Group became involved in a joint venture with Australand to develop industrial land at Larapinta, Queensland. Motorway Business Park Pty Ltd (Motorway), part of the Doyle Group, purchased the land and Australand provided funding to develop it. The Applicant intended to hold and lease the land on a long-term basis.

  1. Mr John Thomas, who was a senior manager at Australand between 2003 and 2008, provided a statement in this matter. His evidence was unchallenged and I accept it. He says that the Doyle Group needed Australand’s financial assistance for the development. Specifically he says:

Mr Doyle told me at the negotiation stage that he required a balance sheet the size that Australand could provide, and the associated funding available through a joint venture with Australand...

Mr Doyle had told me at this stage that it was his intention to hold the land long-term and develop his own portfolio of industrial land he could find other ventures in a similar way that Australand was doing.

  1. In or around mid-2004 Woolworths offered to purchase the land at above market value, Australand wanted to accept the offer, and as the smaller partner in the joint venture, the Applicant agreed.[143] Mr Thomas corroborates this. The profits from the sale were accounted for as revenue.[144]
  2. The Applicant claims that Australand had told him that when they had an intention to hold land long-term they used a “wholesale trust”. Describing the sale of Motorway, the Applicant said “So Motorway Business Park got completely sold and was taxed on revenue account because I set up, and it was set up incorrectly”.[145]
  3. In 2004 to 2005 another Doyle Group entity, Treton Pty Ltd (Treton), purchased land at Parkinson for industrial development. The Applicant says that in negotiations in relation to a joint venture with Australand, it was agreed that some parts of the project were to be held as long-term assets and some parts were to be developed for the purposes of sale.[146] This is consistent with a file note of a meeting between the Applicant, his lawyers and an Australand representative, dated 6 April 2005, that refers to “trading stock” being kept in Treton for “JV purposes” with respect to the Parkinson land. [147]
  4. Ultimately this did not transpire. Coles Myer Limited (Coles Myer) was interested in leasing part of the Parkinson land for a cold storage facility, however it was reluctant to do business with the Doyle Group given the private nature of the group and the heavy cross-collateralisation of the group’s assets to its finance provider. In 2005, under pressure from Australand who did not want the Coles Myer deal jeopardised, the Applicant agreed to relinquish the title to the Parkinson land.[148]
  5. Again, the Doyle Group had needed financial assistance from Australand for this project. Mr Thomas’s evidence is:

... Doyle had effectively taken the risk associated with acquisition that Australand would not otherwise be willing to take, and in return Australand would:

(a) provide the additional capital funding uplift to allow for the development of the land;

(b) attract “blue-chip”, long-term tenants to the project.[149]

  1. The Applicant’s evidence about the Doyle Group’s involvement with Motorway and the Parkinson project is corroborated by Mr Thomas’s evidence, and I therefore accept it.

Advice on Structuring Affairs

  1. The Applicant claims that, wanting to use a model similar to Australand, he sought legal advice about structuring his affairs to allow for, among other things:
    • long-term acquisition and cash flow;
    • the potential to allow external parties to inject funds into the Doyle Group without unnecessary complexity or adverse commercial consequences;
    • favourable taxation treatment (within the law);
    • the ability to diversify the Doyle Group’s income through trust structures to the extent that it was commercially beneficial to do so; and
    • the ability to borrow against industrial assets held by the Doyle Group to branch into agricultural acquisition.
  2. He was advised that long-term acquisitions should be held in a “unit trust” structure and that this would allow him, in the event of an unexpected sale, to “utilise capital gains tax relief that would not otherwise be available in a non-trust corporate structure”.[150]
  3. Mr Christopher Davis, Principal at McInnes Wilson Lawyers, provided a statement in this matter. His evidence corroborates the Applicant’s evidence regarding the advice he sought and was given.[151] Mr Davis’s evidence is unchallenged and I accept it.
  4. Mr Ross Vile of Vincents Chartered Accountants provided a statement in this matter. His evidence is unchallenged and I accept it. He states that around June 2005, he was approached by the Doyle Group’s legal advisers for advice on, among other things, strategies to be directed to long-term asset acquisition and cash flow. He says that from numerous discussions he was involved in with the Applicant, he understood the Doyle Group’s modus operandi was that where real property was purchased with the intention of holding it long-term, it was purchased in a trust in order to take advantage of capital gains tax benefits.
  5. The Applicant has produced a memorandum from his legal advisers, dated 8 April 2005, advising that:
    • (a) with respect to the development and sale of lots held by Treton, transferring the land to another structure would give rise to significant capital gains tax and stamp duty consequences;
    • (b) the land that was intended to be held long-term could be transferred to a unit trust on the basis of its unimproved value (to minimise capital gains tax and stamp duty); and
    • (c) in relation to the land to be held long-term, “holding the land in a unit trust provides obvious benefits for Australand and Craig Doyle in terms of taxation”.[152]
  6. The applicant claims that he was told by his legal advisers that if property was not to be held long-term, the trust structure gave no practical benefit. However, he did not point to any detriment that would flow from using the structure for property that was not to be held long-term, and neither has Mr Davis nor Mr Vile. Nor can the Applicant recall receiving any advice to that effect.[153]
  7. The Applicant’s evidence about the advice that he sought and received is consistent with the Mr Davis’s evidence and the contemporaneous documentary evidence, and I accept it.

Use of Trusts

  1. The Applicant claims that he set up trusts for the Bromelton, the Mackay and the Brendale properties following his lawyer’s advice, and that where he intended to purchase land and sell it for profit he did not use a trust. The conclusion the Applicant wishes the Tribunal to draw was that his use of trusts to hold those properties proves that he acquired them with the singular intention of holding them long-term. I am not prepared to draw such a long bow given the evidence that holding property long-term was not the only goal the Applicant expressed to his lawyers when seeking advice on the appropriate structure (he also wanted a structure that would allow cash injections from third parties and the diversification of the Doyle Group income without commercial detriment) and the fact that his lawyers did not advise that any detriment would flow from using a trust to hold property only in the short-term. Further, the Applicant knew from his recent experience with Motorway that where land is intended to be leased long-term, sometimes a very good offer to purchase can arise.
  2. I accept that the use of the trust structure indicates the Applicant acquired the properties with the intention of having the option to hold the property long-term, but I do not accept that it necessarily indicates that holding the property long-term was the Applicant’s only intention, particularly in light of other evidence such as the email on 2 November 2005 from the Doyle Group to its legal advisers referring to an option in a feasibility study that was a “combination of land sales, D&C and group leases” about which the Applicant said, when giving evidence “That’s right, and it keeps going back to the same thing, it’s good commercial sense, you’ve got to spread your risk.”[154]
  3. Indeed the Applicant’s own evidence does not support his contention that his use of trusts proves he solely intended to hold the properties long-term. In the hearing the Applicant said:

The trusts - you know, we made it very clear that the trusts were worded, of course they could be sold. It’s no secret. There is no - the lawyers allowed for all options to whatever action had to be taken when it had to be taken. Now, the pressure referred to resulted in I had to sell, and that’s what happened. If the structures weren’t done correctly that would have stopped that process and I would have been in a pretty bad situation. I took my adviser’s advice, they documented accordingly and that’s where we are.[155]

  1. I further note that, with respect to the land at Parkinson, the reasons given by his legal advisers for not transferring trading stock (land that was intended to be sold) from Treton to a trust was that it would have given rise to adverse tax consequences, not that a trust was otherwise inappropriate for land that was to be held short-term.
  2. In relation to land that was to be held and leased long-term, the applicant’s legal advisers proposed that a separate management company be put in place to “undertake all the property services required in negotiating with tenants, negotiating with banks finance etc”.[156] However, the Applicant did not produce any evidence that this had been done.
  3. I do not accept that the use of the respective trusts to hold the properties proves a singular intention to hold the properties and lease them long-term.

The Financial Viability of Holding the Properties for Long-term Lease

  1. Each of the three properties was acquired by a trust that otherwise had no assets, that relied on short-term, high-interest finance from CFAL, and that did not have an income stream capable of servicing its CFAL loan (discussed in more detail below).
  2. Mr Gregory Ramsay was a senior manager at CFAL during the relevant period. He was approached for finance by the Applicant in 2001 and he managed the Doyle Group’s credit facilities from that time onwards, assessing applications and making recommendations to his superiors.[157] He described the Applicant’s financial situation in one such recommendation, dated 16 July 2007 (to increase the loan facility for the Mackay property) thus:

Doyle is asset rich and in the past has not had a reliable substantial cash flow, however his allegiance in recent times with Australand to undertake the Motorway Business Park and Parkinson projects has assisted in this regard.[158]

  1. The Doyle Group had needed financial assistance from Australand for the Motorway and Parkinson projects. The Applicant envisaged investment from Australand and QR for the Bromelton development.[159] However, I am not satisfied on the evidence that the involvement of either of these entities was ever certain. Nor am I satisfied that the Doyle Group was negotiating with any other potential investors with respect to the Bromelton property or the smaller Mackay and Brendale properties.
  2. Further, in a meeting between the Doyle Group and QR on 8 February 2006, it was noted that the Applicant had made unsecured loans to the Bromelton Trust for the purpose of progressing a development application, which indicates the Bromelton Trust’s debt level was higher than just what it owed to CFAL. How then, did the Applicant plan to manage the CFAL debt until the properties were sufficiently developed to attract significant income from blue chip tenants?
  3. The Bromelton property had an income stream from the agistment of cattle,[160] and the Brendale property derived around $100,000pa in rental income from pre-existing tenants. However, given the loans with respect to each property were in the millions of dollars, it seems unlikely that those income streams were capable of covering the interest. While other Doyle Group entities and assets were used to guarantee the loans for the Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale properties, it is not clear whether other Doyle Group assets or income streams were intended to be used to service those loans. No specific claim to that effect was put forward.
  4. In his statement, the Applicant contended that as CFAL was a short-term, high-interest lender, his intention when each of the properties were purchased was to develop and sell a small portion of each property if needed,[161] and following the completion of the construction stage, re-finance with a “first-tier” bank at a lower interest rate.[162] He thought once sufficient interest in the D&C and leaseback project was achieved, it would be easier to secure finance for the development with the purpose of producing rental income.[163] Mr Ramsay’s evidence supports this - he said the Applicant always told him he intended the properties for long-term lease and that he told the Applicant that CFAL could fund the acquisition, design and construction stage, but could not offer competitive pricing for “long-term hold of leased property”.[164] Further, the memorandum dated 10 November 2005 from the Doyle Group’s legal advisers to QR, with respect to the Bromelton property, states that “once new lots are created, some lots will be sold to retire as much of the CFAL debt as possible”.
  5. However, when giving evidence at the hearing, the Applicant claimed he did not intend to sell individual lots in the Bromelton property. Specifically, he said the references in the memorandum to QR to selling lots to retire debt were there because QR did not want the joint venture to owe money to CFAL, a high-interest lender.[165] When Senior Counsel for the Respondent asked if he was suggesting that the reference to selling lots to retire debt did not reflect the truth he said that it did but it was put there because of QR’s attitude to CFAL and that it was an exit strategy, describing it thus:

“But I will emphasise again the purpose of why it is there because Queensland Rail’s complete negativity towards CFAL. If we were ever put under pressure I needed an exit strategy and that’s always - the only exit strategy we can exercise is a sale of some land, a small portion to retire the debt.[166]

  1. However, later in his evidence, when he was asked about the press release that indicated that the Bromelton property would include lots for sale and lease, he said:

“The reference is all about Queensland Rail’s dissatisfaction with having CFAL involved. My solution was if we had to because I knew - in the back of my mind I knew their investment in the trust would pay CFAL out, they didn’t want CFAL at all.”[167]

  1. The Applicant’s claimed belief that the QR investment would repay the CFAL is supported by file notes of a meeting that took place between the Doyle Group and QR three months after the date of the abovementioned memorandum.[168] Those notes say “QR will then assume liability for principle and interest to CFAL”.[169]
  2. It appears, on the basis of the contemporaneous documentation, that both options – repaying debt by selling lots or by QR’s investment - were considered. This is unsurprising when one considers that QR’s investment was not certain, and that developing and selling lots relies on development approval which is an uncertain process (in fact, the Bromelton and Brendale Trusts applied for, but never obtained, development approvals). I prefer the evidence to be gleaned from the contemporaneous documentation to that of the Applicant in this regard.
  3. Mr Ian Wilson, who was general manager in respect of intermodal rail terminal development for QR between 2002 and 2007, provided a statement in this matter. His evidence is unchallenged and I accept it. He states that QR’s preference was to acquire a parcel of land at the Bromelton site but the Applicant’s preference was to grant a long-term lease to QR. This tends to support the Applicant intending the property for long-term lease. However, the abovementioned file notes of a meeting between QR and the Doyle Group suggest an intention to sell lots, and to do so for reasons other than repaying debt. Specifically, they state that the Applicant’s unsecured debts are to be given priority “as to payments following sales”, and:

NB. Very important. It was agreed very late in the meeting that because decisions of the joint venture must be unanimous Queensland Rail must not provide any objection or refuse any potential deal nor must it vote against any prospective deal that would enable a competitor to take a position as either lessee of a lot, a purchaser of a lot or as a rail user.[170] [My underlining]

  1. When this passage was put to the Applicant, he said it concerned Pacific National which was used by BlueScope Steel:

Q: So it’s very important and what’s very important is there needs to be flexibility in whatever the arrangements are that there be the scope for allowing Pacific National to enter as either purchaser or as a lessee?

A: That’s exactly right. And that’s good.

Q: That’s good?

A: Normal common sense business.[171]

  1. I consider it significant that the options of securing an investor or developing and selling lots to reduce the CFAL debt were not certain and were contingent on factors beyond the Applicant’s control. While Mr Ramsay told the Applicant that CFAL could fund the acquisition, development and construction stages, I am not satisfied that the Applicant had a reliable plan in place to manage the CFAL debt during those stages. I am therefore not satisfied that he had a reliable plan that would enable him to hold each property for long-term lease.

Efforts to Lease Properties

  1. There is no evidence before the Tribunal of any serious negotiations with prospective tenants (apart from QR), despite some companies expressing interest in leasing lots. Nor is there any evidence of targeted efforts to secure blue-chip tenants, although the Applicant asserts that he sought to generate interest in leasing by advertising for sale and lease (which I consider in the next section). Without a satisfactory explanation, the absence of such evidence tends to undermine the Applicant’s contention that his plan was solely to lease the properties long-term.
  2. The Applicant sought to explain the apparent dearth of evidence of efforts made by the Doyle Group to secure tenants thus:

...we had two offices in Dayboro, one went underwater in the floods.... Now, I went to great pains, and I found these documents that sit before us here today, and there were other documents that I don’t have that would absolutely cement my case, all the proposals for lease of offers that we presented. Like I said, that’s all Chris Matheson did was present offers for lease.[172]

  1. I do not accept that all Mr Matheson did was present offers for lease. On the Applicant’s own evidence, it was Mr Matheson who arranged for press releases and media articles, and who brought him the offer to purchase the Brendale property in December 2006.[173] Further, in an age where documents are often stored on servers, remote devices and in email accounts, the Applicant did not explain how “all the proposals for lease of offers that we presented” were lost in a flood and I do not accept this explanation. I am not satisfied that the evidence existed.

The Feasibility Studies and Other Reports

  1. There were multiple feasibility studies done, and data acquired, with respect to each property. Some were on a sales only basis while others also contemplated D&C and lease-back.
  2. The Applicant contends that the feasibility studies that were prepared on the basis of sales only, were prepared for the purpose of obtaining finance. This was because lenders wanted comfort that they can sell the property to recover outstanding amounts. Whereas the feasibility studies that were done on the basis of D&C and lease-back were prepared to asses internally the worth and ongoing viability of the long-term hold of the properties.[174]
  3. Mr Ramsay gave evidence that in order to obtain approval from his superiors to extend credit, he needed to be able to assure them that a loan could be repaid within its term, and with short-term finance that could only happen by way of sale or re-finance.[175] I accept this evidence, and I find that it support’s the Applicant’s evidence that the Doyle Group prepared feasibility studies on the basis of sales only for the purpose of obtaining finance. However, I do not accept that they were all prepared only for that purpose because this is not borne out by the evidence.
  4. For example, the Doyle Group’s March – May 2007 “Commercial & Industrial Quarterly Project Report”, an internal document prepared for governance purposes,[176] referred to two feasibility studies of the Bromelton property – one in May 2006 that was based only on sales and the other in June 2006 that was based on sales and leases. Further, the report referred to a feasibility study for the Brendale property in January 2007 that was based on land sales only.
  5. Further, within one week of a feasibility study of the Mackay property that was based on sales only, the Mackay Trust contracted to purchase two adjoining lots that were required to access the Mackay property. The evidence does not suggest that the feasibility study was prepared to obtain finance. Rather, it appears to be related to the decision to purchase those adjoining lots. In drawing that inference, I am cognisant of the Applicant’s evidence that he had been considering the Mackay property for years before purchasing it, however the timing of the feasibility study strongly suggests a connection with the subsequent purchase of the adjoining lots.
  6. Under cross-examination, the Applicant, while maintaining his intention to hold the land parcels for long-term lease, accepted that feasibility studies had been prepared on the basis of "good commercial sense" because "you've got to look at options and alternatives", and "you can't put all your eggs in one basket because a financier or joint venture partner would laugh if you said, and said, “We’re going to do this, but what if, but what if."[177]
  7. With respect to other types of reports, the Applicant gave evidence that the Core Economics market assessment that was obtained before the Mackay Trust contracted to buy the Mackay property, and that included data on sales, was obtained on his authority and instructions[178] “to assist in making sure that it was a viable project and that we weren’t going to be sunk by it”[179] and “...we wanted to determine if there was a market. If we’re going to put a building there we wanted to determine somebody would go there. That was the purpose”.[180] Although he said that the sales information was simply an indication of overall demand in the area, “They don’t look at leasing opportunities. Demand reflects into leasing opportunities”.[181]
  8. The only market data obtained by the Doyle Group during the due diligence period for the Mackay property was sales data for industrial land in Mackay and a feasibility study projecting revenue based on sales. This suggests that the information was obtained to assess whether to proceed with the purchase rather than to obtain finance.
  9. With respect to that sales data, the following exchange occurred when the Applicant gave evidence:

Q: So this was part of your due diligence, just looking at the sales prices for industrial land in Mackay?

A: Yes, that’s right. And I’ll go back to what I said before, everything must be looked at to get a good overview[182]

...

Q: I can’t go to Core Economics and explain I’m setting up a wholesale trust, I need you to tell me the leasing, this, that and the other. Because those factors all come in after the fact. There are only value that we can add. We have to look at the whole of Mackay and is the easiest way, just when a valuer values the property, the only way you can do it is it’s got to be black and white, it’s sales related and that’s the only way you can apportion a value or a take-up rate.[183]

  1. The Applicant was asked about his contention that sales data is indicative of rental viability:

Q: You say that in relation to obtaining an indication for the tenancies that it was sufficient if you like to simply look at sales data. Is that your position?

A: Because it’s the - the sales data are a representation of take-up rate, and it is the only data available to look at.

Q: It’s the only data available?

A: So that’s what you have to - the big picture, yes.

Q: All right. But you would accept that there is a difference between buying and leasing from the purchasers?

A: Absolutely

Q: Yes. So buying would require very significant finance from the purchaser?

A: Yes

Q: Whereas leasing it will be an ongoing commitment?

A: Yes

Q: But for a long-term?

A: Yes

Q: And of course from the vendor’s point of view if you’re selling it as you say you’re in and out, you’re in, you might develop it, you might not, and you’re out?

A: That’s right

Q: But if you’re going to do long-term leasing there’s a whole range of other responsibilities you have got to look at?

A: Yes

Q: Particularly if you have the ambition, as you did?

A: Yes

Q: - to have blue-chip tenants?

A: Yes[184]

  1. The Respondent’s point is well made: it is common sense that there are differences for prospective occupiers of industrial land between buying it and leasing it, and there are differences in preparing land for lease or for sale. Further, I am not satisfied that sales data was the only data available and I find it significant that during the due diligence period for the Mackay property, the Doyle Group obtained information relating only to sales. The Applicant has not explained, to my satisfaction, the Doyle Group’s almost myopic reliance on sales data as an indication of the financial viability of leasing the Mackay property long-term, and I do not accept his explanation. Rather, I find that the Doyle Group obtained sales data to inform itself of the financial viability of acquiring, developing and selling the Mackay property.
  2. With respect to the Brendale property, the Applicant accepted that the acquisition proposal that was prepared shortly after the Brendale Trust contracted to purchase it reflected the intentions of the Doyle Group at the time that it was prepared.[185] This document provided for, among other things, revenue derived in lump sums from D&C, and calculated an overall “return on sales”.
  3. On 5 December 2006, a feasibility study based on sales only was prepared shortly after Evans Harch offered to buy the Brendale property for $10,500,000, and it was followed by Evans Harch increasing its offer to $11,500,000 (and that offer was accepted). The evidence does not support the feasibility study having been prepared to obtain finance. Under cross examination, the Applicant denied that the purpose of the feasibility study was to increase the offer from Evans Harch. He claimed he was not involved in the negotiations, and he said Mr Matheson did some things behind his back. However, he admitted to having been aware of the first offer when it was reduced to writing and of the second offer.[186] Given all of the evidence, I consider it more likely that the feasibility study was prepared for the purpose of negotiating with Evans Harch than for the purpose of obtaining finance.
  4. The feasibility studies based on sales predicted revenue at each stage, with each stage including a number of lots being sold, up to the final stage where all lots were sold, indicating a flexible approach that could have included selling some or all lots. I am satisfied that in relation to each property, the Doyle Group prepared or obtained reports that included sales data for its own internal purposes so that it could, among other things, make informed decisions about the financial viability of acquiring the properties and selling some or all lots.

The Marketing of the Properties

  1. With respect to the press-releases that stated the properties would be offered for sale or D&C lease-back, the Applicant sought to distance himself from them, stating they were prepared by Mr Matheson. Further, he claimed that the reference to lots being available for sale or lease was intended to generate interest in leasing the properties. When asked about this explanation, the following exchange occurred:

Q: That was true and correct at the time.

A: No, not necessarily.

....

Q: So you were prepared in the development of this land to make

misrepresentations to the public as to what this project was about.

A: At the –

Q: Yes or no?

A: No, I wasn’t prepared to make misrepresentations.

...

A: Because it’s not black and white.

...

A: A lot of our leasing enquiries is [sic] generated through opportunities – it’s from land sales and I mean that’s how things are done... It’s not misleading, its advertising is more the point.[187]

  1. The Applicant was asked about the Doyle Group’s appointment of Knight Frank as selling or leasing agents for the Brendale property, to which he said:

... to appoint an agent on a commercial basis, to appoint an agent to just go out and find a tenant and generate interest it just doesn’t happen. They’re not interested[188]...It’s Australand that generated the majority of their leases for the wholesale trust out of sale enquiry.[189]

  1. And in relation to the Knight Frank advertising material:

Q: And you approved of this advertising?

A: Yes.

Q: And it identifies, as you say, that the land was offered for sale or lease?

A: That’s right because that’s how it’s done.[190]

  1. While the Applicant referred to Australand generating interest in rentals from sales enquiries, he did not produce any evidence of that, or any independent evidence that marketing for sales is an effective way to generate interest in rentals, or is more effective than advertising for rentals. Given that, and my earlier observation that there are differences between buying industrial property and leasing it, I am not satisfied on the evidence that marketing for sale or lease in order to attract tenants is indeed “how it’s done”.

The Sale of the Properties

  1. The Applicant claims that with respect to the Mackay property, the reduced density from the planned 60 lots to 39 lots made it less attractive for leasing long-term. However this is not substantiated by the documents. The feasibility study that was prepared on the basis of 39 lots was prepared on the basis of sales only, therefore it was not capable of accurately demonstrating the financial viability of leasing 39 lots. Even if one applies the Applicant’s reasoning that information about sales is relevant to leases, that does not help his case because the most recent feasibility study that was based on 60 lots (January 2007) projected a cumulative net profit of $18,000,080 from land sales, and the latest feasibility study prepared on the basis of 39 lots (June 2007) projected a cumulative net profit of $18,843,000. I give the Applicant’s evidence in this regard very little weight.
  2. However, the key reason the Applicant gave for selling the properties is financial pressure. The Applicant submitted that he was forced to sell the three properties because of the financial circumstances of CFAL, given the impending Global Financial Crisis (GFC), and rising debt levels within the Doyle Group.
  3. In his second witness statement he said:

By August 2007, as previously noted, the debt level within the group was approaching unserviceable levels which threatened the ongoing financial viability.

...Commencing in or around late 2007, the Doyle Group began to face significant financial issues. These resulted in part from significant loans requiring repayment and also from commitments made by me on behalf of the Doyle Group entitles that were becoming difficult to meet.

...and in the later half of 2008 there was a significant global financial crisis which is well known. At that time there were a number of outstanding facilities and obligations that the Doyle Group had and I required funds in the short term.

...I was compelled to consider the sale of the industrial land that I had earmarked previously for long - term hold.

...my original intention for the industrial lots could not be realised, and I was forced to sell to Mirvac...[191]

  1. The timing - late 2007 - coincides with the Doyle Group entering into Put and Call options with ICPS (Mirvac) regarding the sale of all three properties. However, much earlier, in December 2006, the Doyle Group had accepted the Evans Harch offer to purchase the Brendale property (which did not proceed) and in March 2007 it had appointed McGees Property to sell that property with a reserve price of $12,000,000.
  2. In evidence before the Tribunal the Applicant brought forward the timing of the onset of the financial pressure thus:

Greg Ramsay... told me that at towards the end of 2006... ‘Craig, word is we’re in for some tough times’. Capital Finance...was owned by the Bank of Scotland overseas and they were actually one of the worst hit banks by the GFC. They had a lot of intel coming from overseas of what was about to happen...Greg said ‘Craig, you’re very exposed, we’re concerned’...And it was a polite tap on the shoulder, he said ‘You need to start getting your house in order’...I thought, well, what are my options?...Could I sell some land to get rid of debt? Could I - I didn’t know what else I could do. We had an approval. I had no presales because the whole project was deemed for long-term hold...I got a phone call from my manager Chris Matheson who knew everybody that was working as a subsidiary of Mirvac. They said they want to look at our properties. Now, we’d been approached before and I said ‘No, it’s not for sale’. A few weeks later they were very persistent. I mentioned it to Mr Ramsay and he said ‘Mate, you need to think seriously about this. And my advice is you engage with them’. I would like to point out...that a portfolio of that size, if it was ever put to the market, if I had any intention whatsoever of selling that property, it would be a major, major advertising campaign nationally. I never did that because I had no intention whatsoever of selling it and that’s hence why I set up my industrial trust and my portfolio.[192]

  1. I have difficulty with some aspects of the Applicant’s evidence. First, Mr Ramsay evidence did not corroborate his claim that he warned the Applicant about the GFC. Further, when asked when the GFC started Mr Ramsay said January 2008.[193]
  2. Second, the Applicant points to the absence of pre-sales despite having development approval as evidence that the properties were “deemed for long-term hold”. However, the Bromelton and Brendale properties did not have development approval prior to their sale to Mirvac. The Mackay property obtained development approval and completed the purchase of the two key land parcels in late July 2007, and there is a document dated 13 August 2007 that indicates that the Doyle Group had been negotiating with Mirvac to sell that property. There does not appear to have been an opportunity to pre-sell lots in any of the developments before the Applicant entered into negotiations with Mirvac to sell the properties, so the point the Applicant seeks to make is an illusory one.
  3. Senior Counsel for the Respondent questioned the Applicant with respect to his evidence that he was told to get his house in order from late 2006, in the context of the documentary evidence that the Doyle Group sought (and was granted) an additional $8,000,000 in credit for the Brendale property in December 2006:

Q: I put it to you, Mr Doyle, that history proves that at this time that you say you were under stress you’re applying for an extension of the facility of $8 million?

A: That’s right

Q: You were extending your credit, not reducing it?

A: And I had an offer on the table of 11.5, I was very comfortable with the proposition.

Q: You had accepted an offer on the table of 11.5?

A: That didn’t proceed

Q: But you told the bank that it was likely to proceed and settle?

A: Could be, would be is completely different to facts and contracts.

Q: And you had a structure that allows you the flexibility to either proceed with that sale or to go down a different path, you could do one or the other?

A: And that’s not a crime.

Q: No?

A: That’s common sense.

Q: It is, it’s commercial common sense?

A: Thank you.[194]

  1. The Applicant was later asked “Okay, so it’s not until August 2007 that we have a problem with your debt levels approaching unserviceable levels?” to which he replied “Approaching, but I’d been approaching for quite a while....”[195]
  2. The Applicant’s evidence about exactly when he began to feel pressured to sell the properties is not consistent. He contended in his witness statement that the fact that the December 2006 contract to sell the Brendale property did not proceed shows that he did not intend to sell the property.[196] It is difficult to reconcile the Applicant at once: thinking he had to reduce the CFAL debt; contracting to sell a property that he had no intention of selling; then using that contract to persuade CFAL to increase his credit facility by $8,000,000.
  3. It is also difficult to accept that the Applicant felt compelled to reduce the Doyle Group’s debt around August 2007, given he increased the loan facility for the Bromelton property in September 2007 by $14,000,000 to fund the purchase of Lot 2. When asked about extending his credit by such an amount when allegedly under financial pressure, he said “That’s just commercial commons sense. What am I going to do? Let it fall into default”,[197] without giving further explanation.
  4. Mr Ramsay gave evidence that around the time when the Bromelton credit facility was extended, he had been having conversations with the Applicant about managing the Doyle Group’s debt.[198] He explained his approach as follows:

Well, my job was to manage the connection but we didn’t get to that point, and - and, you know, if you get to that point it’s really too late. I mean, it really is to sort of manage it and tap clients on the shoulder to get them to go and do things so that you stay within limits that we can deal with. If we get to the situation where, you know, I’ve got credit departments that have got concerns about facilities, I’ve sort of lost control and it, you know, does become a tough situation, so it’s just planning to stay within limits and we were certainly up heading towards our limit and I could see it and I would have been saying we need to divest ourselves of some things to remain within those limits.[199]

...I’ve got to keep the whole thing together. At the same time saying, ‘Mate, I need you to start repaying some of these things; we just cannot keep increasing these facilities.’ Like, you know, there’s a limit to where somebody’s going to say to me, ‘Hey, hey, what are you doing?’ And at 117 million on this sort of stuff, I think that, from memory, I was pretty much approaching that limit...my job is to see into the future and – and make sure problems don’t happen. So, I think you mentioned, you know, a letter of demand earlier. A letter of demand – it’s all over. Like, you know what I mean? Like, I’m trying to manage this well before that. And it’s telling the borrower, if you’re any good as a lender, it’s telling a borrower where the boundaries are and where you can go to and where you can’t go to. And it’s no good telling - after you’ve got through the boundary, because it’s too late.

Q: Is it fair to say this, at this time at least you’re recommending that Mr Doyle be lent more money?

A: That’s so I don’t have defaults and I don’t have to issue a letter of demand, because that’s what happens. If he hasn’t got the money in the bank to pay the interest, I’ve got to keep - and this is highly leveraged lending, and we’re getting paid fees and so forth for doing it...You can only capitalise it to a point, because otherwise we’re going to start to breach barriers, be it a 7 per cent LVR or a 75 per cent LVR. On this sort of stuff that’s up there lending. And my job is to keep us within the boundaries and not breach them, because that’s - you know, the minute I get a ‘no’ from credit on something, we’ve got a problem, because you won’t be able to service the interest, and then the snowball effect all starts, and we will get to your letter of demand. And my job was to make sure we didn’t get there.[200]

  1. When asked if CFAL forced the Applicant to sell the properties to Mirvac, Mr Ramsay said:

No, I wouldn’t say forced, but I would say again we can’t keep going in this direction. You’re going to have to sell something, otherwise we’re going to have a problem in six or 12 months, because I’m not going to be able to keep getting the approvals.[201]

  1. I accept Mr Ramsay’s evidence. I am satisfied that he did verbally warn the Applicant that the Doyle Group’s debt levels were concerning and some of the debt needed to be repaid. However, his evidence was somewhat vague and did not take things further than that.
  2. An investment by QR in the Bromelton development would obviously have assisted to reduce the debt in relation to that property, and thereby the over-all debt of the Doyle Group, while allowing the property to be retained for long-term lease. Mr Wilson’s evidence, which I accept, is:

Negotiations between Queensland Rail and Mr Doyle’s entity regarding the Swan Creek Industrial Estate were never finalised, nor any terms agreed to, as in around mid-2007 Mr Doyle’s entity entered into a contract with Mirvac Limited to sell the Bromelton land, without prior consultation or notice to Queensland Rail.[202]

  1. The Applicant did not explain why the negotiations with QR were abandoned in favour of selling the group of properties to Mirvac. For example, he did not give evidence that the QR investment would not have reduced the debt levels by enough.
  2. Nor did the Applicant adequately explain the “portfolio” aspect of Mirvac’s acquisition of the properties. It is not apparent whether Mirvac was only prepared to buy all five properties[203] as a package deal or whether there was some flexibility. However, the fact that Mirvac purchased the Mackay and Brendale properties long before there was a contract to purchase the Bromelton property suggests that Mirvac did not have an all-or-nothing approach. The absence of evidence that the sale of the other properties was contingent on the sale of the Bromelton property calls into question why the Applicant abandoned negotiations with QR in favour of selling that property.
  3. What is more, in the first half of 2008, a Doyle Group entity (Wacol Industrial Pty Ltd) purchased land for $7,651,000[204] and Craig Doyle Rural Holdings Pty Ltd purchased land on the following dates in the following amounts:
    • 14 January 2008 - $1,800,000;[205]
    • 21 January 2008 - $1,125,000;[206]
    • 22 January 2008 - $12,200,000;[207]
    • 2 April 2008 - $3,350,000;[208] and
    • 8 July 2008 - $11,825,000.[209]
  4. These purchases do not support the Applicant’s claim that he was trying to reduce the Doyle Group’s overall debt.
  5. With respect to the sale of the Bromelton property, the Applicant claims that CFAL was under pressure from the GFC and, in February 2009, told him to repay all of the Doyle Group’s loans. He says CFAL did not force him to sell immediately because it knew of the impending sale of the Bromelton property to Mirvac. He further claims to have been committed to exercising a Call option and a Put and Call option, respectively, to purchase two properties at around that time.[210]
  6. It is difficult to reconcile what the Applicant says about the financial pressure to reduce overall debt between late 2006 and July 2009 (when the Bromelton property was sold) with the evidence of what the Doyle Group did during that period, which includes abandoning negotiations with a potential investor, spending significant sums of money to purchase more properties and entering into financial commitments to purchase more properties. The Applicant did not produce sufficiently detailed evidence of the Doyle Group’s financial position and commitments during the relevant period to explain these apparent anomalies and substantiate his claim that he had to sell the Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale properties because of a need to reduce the debt levels of the Doyle Group.
  7. In any event, even a forced sale is not an abandonment of a profit making scheme if the option of selling for a profit was contemplated at the time of acquisition of the property. At various times when giving evidence the Applicant emphasised that it is sensible to consider all options, for example:

...

Q: It is good commercial sense?

A: You’ve got to look at - you’ve got to look at options and alternatives. You can’t put all your eggs in one basket because a financier or a joint venture partner would laugh if you said, and said, “We’re going to do this, but what if, but what if.”[211]

...

Q: Okay. Okay. It’s leaving your options open?

A: Yes. Yes, that’s correct, we do - you can’t lock yourself into a room.[212]

  1. In his evidence before the Tribunal, he described the option of selling as a mere exit strategy.[213] I accept that there is a relevant difference between an intended purpose and a contemplated exit strategy in the event that the intended purpose does not eventuate. However, the contemporaneous documentary evidence does not disclose any instance of the Applicant, or Doyle Group, describing or representing the option of selling as an exit strategy or the like. The contention is further undermined by the heavy focus on sales data in the documents used internally by the Doyle Group and the marketing of the properties for sale or lease before the time when the Applicant claims to have felt pressure to reduce the Doyle Group’s debt.
  2. The Doyle Group was asset rich and its industrial property development business was heavily reliant on short-term, high-interest finance. I am not satisfied that the Applicant had a reliable plan to manage the CFAL debt until rental income could be secured. In other words, I am not satisfied that the Applicant had a reliable strategy that did not involve selling property to ensure debt levels did not reach an unsustainable level. Further, the Applicant has not produced independent evidence that it was the GFC rather than the Doyle Group’s rising debt that prompted CFAL to warn him about his debt levels, or that the rising debt was the result of anything other than the way the Doyle Group conducted its property development business.

CONCLUSIONS

The Ordinary Course of Business

  1. The Respondent contends that the ordinary business of the Doyle Group, and the trusts that owned each parcel of land, included the acquisition of industrial land for development for sale or lease. Inherent in this is that each trust carried on business in a manner whereby flexibility was retained to permit the realisation of profit by whichever of those methods was advantageous in the circumstances.
  2. The Applicant submits that the business of the Doyle Group did not include the acquisition, development and sale of industrial land, but rather the acquisition of industrial land for long-term lease. However, he has failed to prove this on the balance of probabilities and thus failed to discharge the onus of proof incumbent upon him.
  3. The first, and most obvious, problem for the Applicant is that from 2004 the Doyle Group acquired four parcels of land (in Parkinson, Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale), commenced the process of redeveloping them to create industrial lots, and sold each parcel in its entirety for profit. This is indicative – although not conclusively so – that the business of the Doyle Group included doing exactly that.
  4. Second, there is a clear sequence of events that explains how the Doyle Group came to add this facet of property development to its property development business. The Doyle Group started in residential property developments that were largely intended for sale. In 2001, it entered industrial property development. The Motorway Business Park was intended for long-term lease, but was ultimately sold. The Parkinson project followed. The Doyle Group agreed with Australand that some of the Parkinson property would be held for long-term lease and some would be developed for sale. This is recorded in a file note dated 6 April 2005, which pre-dates the acquisition, by the relevant trusts, of the Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale properties. The Doyle Group ultimately sold the entire Parkinson property. The sale of the Motorway and Parkinson properties were not what the Doyle Group initially intended, however both outcomes were profitable.
  5. Third, while the Doyle Group went on to acquire the Bromelton, Mackay and Brendale properties with the stated intention of leasing them long-term, at the time of acquiring each property it obtained data and prepared reports relating to the financial viability of selling them. I have rejected the Applicant’s claims that this was done to give comfort to CFAL and that the option of selling was considered a mere exit strategy.
  6. Finally, in June 2006 the Doyle Group represented itself to a potential investor to be in the business of industrial and commercial property development for purchasers and tenants. It prepared a document for Colonial First State entitled:

DOYLE GROUP Development Portfolio Overview June 2006

Prepared for: Colonial First State

June 2006[214]

  1. Under the heading “Who We Are” appears:

The People – Commercial & Industrial

Craig Doyle, Managing Director

...

Mr Doyle has established developments in Commercial and Industrial, Residential, Rural and more recently the Design & Construct of purpose built facilities.[215]

  1. The Development Portfolio Overview contains descriptions of several commercial and industrial projects and includes the following:
    • (a) with respect to the Motorway Business Park, “The estate features five (5) stages, of which 98% has been pre-sold to both owner occupiers and investors”;[216]
    • (b) with respect to the Parkinson Business Park, “The estate has been designed to target the owner-occupier/investor market, comprising eight (8) stages...”;[217]
    • (c) with respect to the Archerfield Business Centre, “The estate is targeted to the owner-occupier market...The entire estate has been pre--sold and has achieved record sale rates...”;[218] and
    • (d) with respect to the Mackay Business Park, “The Paget Industrial Estate will offer land for sale and also arrange design-construct projects on a leaseback basis for clients”.[219]

(Note: the Development Portfolio Overview did not describe the Bromelton project as being for sale or lease but talked about demand in general. The Brendale project was not included in the document – this could be because its purchase, on 20 June 2006, post-dated the preparation of the document)

  1. The Applicant gave evidence that he wanted to attract superannuation funds as investors in his industrial projects and he named AMP and Colonial Mutual.[220] This is significant because it is reasonable to infer that a document provided to a potential investor would be accurate. The Applicant confirmed this when giving evidence as follows:

Q: No, it’s simply telling Colonial First State this is what we do?

A: That’s right

Q: This is the nature of our business?

A: This is what we - yes, this is what we’ve done in the past and what we’re doing

in the future.

Q: Sure. And so that’s why you state here, fourth bullet point:

‘The Paget industrial estate will offer land for sale and also arrange design construct projects on a leaseback basis for clients’

Q: So that’s true and correct?

A: Yes, that’s consistent with everything we discussed yesterday.[221]

  1. Considering all of this, I find that from 6 April 2005 at the latest,[222] the ordinary business of the Doyle Group included the acquisition and development of land for industrial use with a view to deriving profit by selling and/or leasing it, whichever proved more advantageous in the circumstances. As each trust was the vehicle by which this facet of the Doyle Group’s business was carried out with respect to the property it acquired, it follows that its ordinary business was the same.

Profit-making Scheme

  1. The objective evidence is that the Doyle Group purchased the three properties, set about obtaining development approvals to create industrial lots, represented to the public and specific parties that the lots would be offered for sale or lease, and ultimately sold each property in its entirety at a profit. The Applicant has sought to explain away much of the evidence that tends to prove an intention to realise a profit by way of sale. I have not accepted his explanations for the reasons I have given.
  2. In rejecting the Applicant’s explanations, I am mindful that he made representations to some individuals that he intended the properties for long-term lease and that there are references in some contemporaneous documents to land only being intended for long-term lease, for example, one of the Knight Frank marketing leaflets for the Brendale property. I accept that the Applicant aspired to build an impressive portfolio of industrial property. However, the weight of the documentary evidence (excluding the feasibility studies that were only used to obtain finance) and the media/marketing activity indicates a flexible approach whereby profit would be realised by way of sale and/or lease.
  3. Considering the totality of the evidence I find that, at the time each property was acquired by its respective trust, the Applicant’s purpose was to develop the property and put it to a commercially advantageous use, which could have been selling, leasing or a combination of both, depending on factors such as whether investors could be secured. Accordingly, each property was acquired as part of a profit-making scheme that included, as one of its purposes, profit making by sale.

FINDINGS IN RELATION TO THE CHARACTER OF THE NET PROCEEDS

  1. My findings in relation to each sale are as follows:
    • (a) each sale arose in the ordinary course of the business of the Doyle Group and of the trust that owned the property; and
    • (b) while it is unnecessary to make the following determination given my first finding, each sale arose as part of a transaction that was entered into with a purpose that included developing the land to sell as industrial lots for profit.
  2. Therefore, the net proceeds of each sale were income, not capital, in nature. The quantification of the net proceeds for income tax purposes is not in issue.

THE ADMINISTRATIVE PENATLY

  1. Section 284–75(2), schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a taxpayer who adopts a position in respect of income tax law that is not reasonably arguable is liable to an administrative penalty.
  2. A position is reasonably arguable:

“[I]f it would be concluded in the circumstances, having regard to relevant authorities, that what is argued for is about as likely to be correct as incorrect, or is more likely to be correct than incorrect”[223]

  1. The test whether a position is “reasonably arguable” is an objective one that asks whether the taxpayer’s position, although incorrect, is “about as likely as not correct” when regard is had to those authorities. The taxpayer’s position need not be more likely to be right than wrong. Rather, the question is whether the taxpayer’s argument can objectively be said to be one that (while wrong) could be argued on rational grounds to be right. The taxpayer’s position will not be reasonably arguable if it is founded upon an unreasonable view of, or disregard for the facts.[224] Further, while the absence of reasonable care will mean that a position is not reasonably arguable, the mere presence of reasonable care is not sufficient. A comparison between the taxpayer’s argument and the correct position must be one that is finely balanced where reasonable minds could differ.[225]
  2. Each property was acquired for a combined purpose and the net proceeds resulted from the carrying out of one of those purposes. The authorities are clear that, on those facts, the net proceeds are income. I do not therefore accept that this is a finely balanced matter where reasonable minds may differ. I am not satisfied that the Applicant’s position was reasonably arguable, therefore the Applicant is liable to an administrative penalty.
  3. While reliance on professional advice does not make a position reasonably arguable it can justify a reduction in penalty. The evidence is that the Applicant relied on legal advice with respect to structuring his business but I am not satisfied that he received, or relied on, legal advice that the net proceeds of each sale were capital. In any event, the penalty has already been reduced to 5%. I am not satisfied that any further reduction is justified.
