Re Joan Dorothy Roberts and Valentine Roy Smith v Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 643; 22 Atr 845/91 Atc 5036; (1991) 33 FCR 253 (18 December 1991)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: JOAN DOROTHY ROBERTS and VALENTINE ROY SMITH

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. G8 and 9 of 1991

FED No. 820

Income Tax

[1991] FCA 643; 22 ATR 845/91 ATC 5036

(1991) 33 FCR 253

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

TASMANIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Northrop J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 51(1) - partnership - whether interest paid on borrowed monies is an allowable deduction

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 51(1), s. 90, s. 91 and s. 92

HEARING

MELBOURNE

18:12:1991

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Abbott

Solicitors for the Applicant: Dobson, Mitchell and Allport

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr Pagone

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The appeal be allowed with costs.

The decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal made 19 June 1991 and in lieu thereof order that the decision of the Commissioner of Taxation be set aside.

The objection of the applicant be allowed.

The matter be referred to the Commissioner of Taxation to be dealt with in accordance with these reasons.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

These two applications, brought under the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, are related. They were heard together. Each application involves the application of s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") in assessing the net income of a partnership. At all material times the applicant Smith was a partner in a firm of solicitors in Hobart. The applicant Roberts became a partner in the firm at a later date. In each case, the issue is whether interest paid by the partnership on money borrowed by the partnership is an allowable deduction under s. 51(1) of the Act. The answers to the questions raised by these two applications will affect the assessments of tax of all the partners of the firm over a number of tax years.

2. The essential facts are not in dispute but some aspects of some of the facts and the inferences to be drawn from the facts are in dispute. Division 5 of Part III of the Act relates to the liability to taxation of partnerships. Under s. 91, a partnership is required to furnish a return of the income of the partnership but is not liable to pay tax thereon. Under s. 92(1)(a), the assessable income of a partner in a partnership includes "so much of the individual interest of the partner in the net income of the partnership of the year of income as is attributable to a period when the partner was a resident" (emphasis added). In that sub-section "net income" means "the assessable income of the partnership, calculated as if the partnership were a taxpayer who was a resident, less all allowable deductions ...", see s. 90. In June 1984 the partnership borrowed $125,000 from a Bank for use, so it was claimed, as capital by the partnership in carrying on its business. Thereafter the partnership paid interest to the Bank on the money so borrowed. The interest was claimed, in the books of account of the partnership, as a deduction under s. 51(1) of the Act with the result that the net income of the partnership was reduced by the amount of interest so paid. As a result, commencing in the 1985 year of income, the assessable income of each partner in the firm was reduced proportionately.

3. The applicant, Roberts became a partner in the firm in May 1987. By a notice of amended assessment issued on 14 November 1989 in respect of the applicant Roberts' assessment for the 1988 year of income, her taxable income was increased by $3,089 being her proportionate share of the interest paid by the firm in that tax year on the loan of $125,000. The applicant Roberts objected to the amended assessment. The Commissioner disallowed the objection. The applicant Roberts sought a review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The Tribunal affirmed the decision of the Commissioner. The applicant Roberts appealed, on a question of law, to this Court from the decision of the Tribunal.

4. The applicant Smith was a member of the partnership at the time the loan was obtained in June 1984. He has remained a partner. By a notice of amended assessment issued on 8 January 1990 in respect of the applicant Smith's assessment for the 1985 year of income, his taxable income was increased by $1,745 being his proportionate share of the interest paid by the partnership on the loan of $125,000. The applicant Smith objected to the amended assessment. The Commissioner disallowed the objection. On a review, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal affirmed the decision of the Commissioner. The applicant Smith appealed, on a question of law, to this Court from the decision of the Tribunal.

5. The two applications to this Court relate to 2 different tax years. In each application, the taxable income of a partner is in issue, but the essential question in each application relates to what is the net income of the partnership in the relevant year. The question is whether interest paid by the partnership on the money borrowed is deductable under s. 51(1) of the Act.

6. Before turning to the facts in some more detail, it is important to understand how the provisions of Division 5 of Part III of the Act operate. A partnership is not a corporate body separate and distinct from the members of the partnership but under Division 5 a partnership is required to furnish a return of the income of the partnership. The return must be in a form that discloses the assessable income of the partnership and "all allowable deductions" except specified deductions not relevant for present purposes. This is necessary to ascertain the net income of the partnership which then forms the basis of ascertaining the assessable income of a partner.

7. In Fletcher v Commissioner of Taxation, 14 November 1991, not yet reported, the High Court expressed opinions on questions similar to those before this Court. The facts were very different but the questions arose in connection with a partnership and deductions under s. 51(1) of the Act. In a joint judgment, the Court, Mason C.J., Brennan, Deane, Dawson, Toohey, Gaudron and McHugh JJ., under the heading "Partnership outgoings" said:-

"Under Div.5 of Pt III of the Act, a partnership must

furnish a return of the income of the partnership in respect

of a tax year but is not liable to pay tax thereon (s.91).

The net income or loss of a partnership for the tax year is

calculated as if the partnership were a resident taxpayer,

that is to say, by the subtraction of allowable deductions

from assessable income (s.90). A resident individual

partner's share of the "net income" or "partnership loss" so

calculated constitutes assessable income or an allowable

deduction for the purpose of calculating the taxable income

derived or the loss (for tax purposes) incurred by that

partner in the tax year (s.91). It follows that the

question whether the adjusted amounts of interest payable

under the two loan agreements in the present case were

wholly or partly deductible under s.51(1) arises in the

context of the calculation of the net income or loss of the

partnership for tax purposes and falls to be answered on the

basis that the partnership itself was a resident taxpayer.

The interest of each of the taxpayers in the availability of

that deduction is indirect in that it will be reflected in

any "net income" or "loss" of the partnership to be shared

among them - as assessable income or as an allowable

deduction - for their tax purposes."

8. A warning is then given, namely that errors can arise if attention is directed too much to the issue of whether a partner is entitled to claim interest payments made by the partnership as a direct deduction. To some extent, the reasons of the Tribunal in the present case, as well as the submissions made to this Court on behalf of the Commissioner, were affected by concentrating too much on the position of the partner taxpayer and not enough on the partnership as a deemed taxpayer.

9. In the same case, the High Court said:-

"In the present case, the outgoings of interest in the tax

years were incurred in the borrowing of money. The funds

borrowed did not constitute assessable income. To the

extent that the outgoings of interest incurred in the

borrowing can properly be characterized as of a kind

referred to in the first limb of s. 51(1), they must draw

their character from the use of the borrowed funds: see

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153,

at p 197; Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1981) 50

FLR 219 at 223 and 232."

10. Sub-section 51(1) is a well known and often debated provision of the Act. It provides as follows:-

"51.(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which

they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable

income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or producing such

income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent

to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a

capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in

relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

11. In the present case, it is to be remembered that it is the assessable income of the partnership that is relevant, not the assessable income of the partner taxpayer.

12. The facts relevant to the applicant Smith can be adapted from the findings made by the Tribunal. In June 1984 the applicant, who is now the senior partner in the partnership, was a partner. The partnership carried on the profession of solicitors at Hobart. The partnership is well known, reputable and has a long history. In June 1984 there were 5 partners. Until then it was the practice of the partnership to admit new partners upon the payment of a sum determined by reference to the capital of the partnership as appearing in the accounts of the partnership. To assist incoming partners, it was the practice to "enable in-coming partners to buy into the partnership on a "drip-feed" basis whereby the existing partners lent the incoming partner the amount necessary to buy into the partnership and that amount was repaid over a period of time". No finding was made on whether the money was lent to the incoming partner from the capital of the partnership or whether interest was paid by the incoming partner on the money lent either to the partnership or to a partner. In any event, it is fair to comment that any interest paid by the incoming partner would be an allowable deduction, while interest received, if received by the partnership would be assessable income of the partnership, but if paid to the individual partners would be assessable income in the hands of the individual partners. In June 1984 a new partner was about to be admitted into the partnership. The then existing partners decided that commencing with that incoming partner, "all future partners would have to provide their own funds in order to buy into the partnership". According to the accounts of the partnership, the incoming partner was required to pay $54,000. He did not have that amount and if required to pay, he would have had to borrow the whole amount of $54,000. If he had done that, it is fair to say that any interest he paid on the money so borrowed would have been an allowable deduction as far as ascertaining his assessable income.

13. Given the financial plight of the incoming partner "the then existing partners decided to reduce their capital input by $25,000 each, thereby allowing the new partner (and future partners) to buy into the partnership at a reduced cost. To give effect to that intention, the partnership borrowed "... $125,000 from the Bank". The partnership then "drew 5 cheques each for $25,000 and handed one to each of the five partners. By this device it was sought to reduce the cost price payable by solicitors who were to be introduced into the partnership after June 1984 by an amount which bore to the principal of the Bank loan the same proportion as the proportion of the equity of the partnership purchased by the new partner". The money so received by each partner was for the private use of that partner.

14. Beginning with the first incoming partner admitted into the partnership under this new arrangement, "it was and has remained a condition and consequence of entry into membership of the partnership that any partner, upon admission as such becomes and remains personally liable to an unlimited extent for the debts, liabilities and obligations of the partnership, including repayment of the principal and payment of the interest upon the Bank loan, subject to the right of such partner to contribution from each other partner to the extent that the debts, liabilities and obligations are paid or satisfied equally between the partners inter se. The incoming partner became a partner on 1 August 1984. Due to the existence of the Bank loan, the amount he was required to pay (and therefore borrow) in order to buy into the partnership was $34,434 rather than $54,000. Since then several of the 'original' partners have retired and several new partners have been admitted."

15. Although no specific finding was made by the Tribunal, it is not disputed that interest on the Bank loan was paid by the partnership. That interest appears as an outgoing in the accounts of the partnership and has the effect of reducing the net income of the partnership by the amount of interest so paid. "As at the end of the 1988 year of income, none of the Bank loan principal had been paid". The partnership was continuing to pay the interest on that loan.

16. The applicant Roberts was admitted as a partner on 1 May 1987. She was required to pay $35,072 to buy into the partnership. She had to borrow that amount. It is fair to say that interest paid by her on the loan to enable her to make the payment, in all probability, is an allowable deduction from her assessable income.

17. Although no findings were made by the Tribunal, it is clear that the proportion of the net income of the partnership included in the assessable income of the applicant Smith for the 1985 tax year included as a deduction interest paid by the partnership in that year on the Bank loan. Likewise the net income of the partnership included in the assessable income of the applicant Roberts for the 1988 tax year included as a deduction interest paid by the partnership in that year on the Bank loan.

18. In Smith, the Tribunal said "In discharge of the personal liability of the applicant, the interest on the Bank loan was paid during the subject year of income from partnership funds." In Roberts, the Tribunal said "In discharge of the personal liability of the applicant, the interest on the Bank loan was paid during the subject year of income from partnership funds." Each of these findings discloses seeds of error in failing to distinguish between the net income of the partnership and the assessable income of each individual partner. They are findings which do not take account of the warning given in Fletcher.

19. In discussing the law, the Tribunal correctly referred to Division 5 of Part III of the Act and sub-section 51(1). The Tribunal referred to what was said by Brennan J. in Ure, above, at p 223:

"Section 51 requires that a deductible expenditure be

incurred "in" gaining assessable income, that is to say, it

must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that

income (Ronpibon Tin N.L. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

[1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47, at p 56). An outgoing of interest may be

incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income

where the borrowed money is laid out for the purpose of

gaining that income (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v

Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153, at pp 170-171, 197; Texas Co.

(Australasia) Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1940)

[1940] HCA 9; 63 CLR 382, at p 468.) The laying out of the borrowed

money for the purpose of gaining assessable income furnishes

the required connexion between the interest paid upon it by

the taxpayer and the income derived by him from its use."

20. The Tribunal referred to what was said by Deane and Sheppard JJ. in Ure at p 232:-

"In a case such as the present where the outgoing claimed as

a deduction is interest paid on borrowed money, one cannot

ordinarily look to the direct object or advantage which the

outgoing was intended to achieve for the reason that that

will ordinarily be the receipt of the borrowed money which

is likely to be neutral in character. One must, of

necessity, look more to the objects or advantages which the

application and use of the borrowed money were intended to

gain (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro supra at p

197). Where there is a difference between intended and

actual use of the borrowed money or a change in use,

difficulties may arise in determining what are the relevant

objects or advantages which the payment of interest was

calculated to procure."

21. The Tribunal then quoted the essence of the case put on behalf of the applicants:

"when the loan is taken out, the advantage gained for the

partnership is to reduce the capital utilised within the

business and to replace that capital by the principal sum of

the loan. It is that which gives the interest payable upon

that loan the character of expenditure incurred in the

course of a business for the derivation of assessable income

or incurred in gaining assessable income itself."

22. The Tribunal rejected that submission as follows:-

"With respect, this assumes that the proceeds of the loan

became part of the business of the partnership. True it is

that the loan of $125,000 was credited to the partnership

bank account, nevertheless I am satisfied that that was

merely a shorthand way by which the then five partners each

obtained $25,000. However, that shortcut is fiscally no

different from, say, drawing five $25,000 cheques and

handing one to each of the partners. Had the latter

procedure been adopted, it could hardly be maintained that

the money thus withdrawn was used for purposes of the

partnership business. In slow motion, what occurred here

was a borrowing of money by the partnership from the Bank.

So far so good. The next question to be asked is: what

were the borrowed funds used for? Here, the answer is

clear: it was handed to the five existing partners to do

with as they pleased; indeed, it was conceded by the

applicant in cross-examination that each partner regarded

his use of his share of the $125,000 as a private matter."

23. In my opinion, the Tribunal was in error in rejecting that submission. There are great difficulties involved in characterising an outgoing; see, for example, what was said by Deane and Sheppard JJ. in Ure at p 233:-

"One of the most difficult aspects of the problem of

characterising an outgoing is the assessment of what, if

any, weight is to be given to indirect objects which a

taxpayer had in mind in incurring the outgoing. Such

objects form part of the relevant circumstances by reference

to which the problem of characterization must be resolved.

There is however no rigid principle which can be applied in

determining what, if any, weight should be given to them.

In the ordinary case, such as, for example, where the

immediate object achieved by the outgoing is the production

of assessable income which is commensurate with the amount

of the outgoing or where it is clear that the outgoing was

for the purchase of stock-in-trade or the acquisition of

services or hire of equipment used in earning assessable

income, indirect objects or motives of a personal or

domestic character will plainly not prevent the

characterization of the outgoing as having been incurred in

earning assessable income (see, for example, Cecil Bros.

Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1964) 111 CLR

430; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Phillips (1978) 36

FLR 399). In other cases, the immediate object or effect of

an outgoing will not suffice either to explain or to

characterize it. In such cases, indirect objects or motives

can assume a sometimes decisive importance."

24. In the present case, and implicit from all the evidence, is the fact that $125,000 was required by the partnership to enable it to carry on its business. Initially that money had been provided by the "original" five partners. In June 1984, a change occurred. To use a word commonly in use these days, the partnership business was restructured. Each of the five partners withdrew $25,000 capital. In order to enable it to carry on its business, the partnership borrowed $125,000 from the Bank. That loan remains outstanding and the partnership is paying interest on the amount of the loan. There can be no real doubt that if a partnership comes into existence and the partners borrow money to enable them to carry on that business, interest paid on the money so borrowed is deductible under s. 51(1) of the Act. This would be so even if the partners, individually, had sufficient capital to provide the same amount as that borrowed.

25. In the present case, the money borrowed from the Bank constitutes part of the capital of the partnership necessary for it to carry on its profession. The partnership pays commercial rates of interest on the loan. The loan is continuing. The partnership pays the interest on the loan. In my opinion the fact that as a result of the "restructuring" each of the five "original" partners receives a refund of capital does not prevent the characterization of the interest payments as having been incurred by the partnership in earning assessable income.

26. It follows, that the net income of the partnership which constitutes part of the assessable income of each of the applicants, is the assessable income of the partnership less the interest payments made on the Bank loan.

27. The essential submission on behalf of the Commissioner was that the $125,000 was borrowed by the five original partners for the object and purpose of enabling each of them to receive $25,000 for their own use. This had nothing to do with the business of the partnership and thus the loan, and the interest paid by the partnership were of a capital or private or domestic nature and thus not deductible under sub-section 51(1) of the Act. Counsel relied upon the views expressed in Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153. The question of characterization is always difficult. In Yeung v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1987-88) 19 ATR 1006 Davies J., on facts similar in many aspects to the present case but not so related to commercial reality, found in favour of an allowable deduction. For the reasons already given I do not accept the submission that the interest payments by the partnership were of a private or domestic nature.

28. In the result, in each case the appeal is allowed with costs. The decisions of the Tribunal and the Commissioner are set aside and the objection of each applicant is allowed. It is further ordered that each matter be referred to the Commissioner to be dealt with in accordance with these reasons.
