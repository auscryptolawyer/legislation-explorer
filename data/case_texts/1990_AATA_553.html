TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 13 July 1990

Place: Brisbane

These applications arise out of an audit of the applicant's affairs by the Taxation Office. The applications refer to the years of income ended 30 June 1976 to 30 June 1984 inclusive. Except for the 1984 year, the respondent has made an estimate of the applicant's taxable income for each year based upon an assets betterment assessment of the applicant's affairs. In respect of the 1984 year the respondent has made specific adjustments not involving an assets betterment assessment - in itself a matter of some contention by the applicant.

  1. The hearing of this application took 16 full sitting days over four weeks. The applicant conducted his own case and Mr J Gordon of counsel instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor appeared for the respondent.
  2. Because of alleged incidents outside the hearing room involving officers of the Australian Taxation Office improperly publishing the evidence before the Tribunal an order in terms of sub-section 35(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 was made. That order prohibits publication of the evidence in these applications.
  3. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, the auditor from the Taxation Office, two valuers, a former lecturer in tax law, and a stipendiary steward with the Queensland Turf Club. In view of the order preventing publication of the evidence it is not appropriate that I name the witnesses. There was also a large number of exhibits (161) tendered by the parties.
  4. It is not unfair to say that the hearing was a most difficult one because of the way the applicant conducted his case. I should record the Tribunal's appreciation of counsel's assistance throughout and I should also record that I can see absolutely no reason for the applicant's malevolent attack on the taxation auditor before the Tribunal. That the audit took a long time to complete is beyond doubt. Equally beyond doubt, in my view, is the fact that the audit took a long time to complete because of the tactics adopted by the applicant, in particular his failure to produce records. Even before this Tribunal the applicant was tendering documents not previously made available to the Taxation Office, but he failed to produce his accounting records to the Tribunal.
  5. Section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 provides inter alia:

"167. If-

(a) ...

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied with the return furnished by any person; or

(c) ...

the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his judgment income tax ought to be levied, and that amount shall be the taxable income of that person for the purpose of section 166."

Section 166 of the Act provides in effect that the Commissioner shall make an assessment of the amount of the taxable income of a taxpayer and the tax payable on that taxable income.

7. Section 185 of the Act, as it was at the relevant time, provided that a taxpayer dissatisfied with an assessment may within 60 days of any service of notice of that assessment, lodge with the respondent an objection in writing against the assessment stating fully and in detail the grounds on which the taxpayer relies. That is subject to a proviso to the effect that where the assessment is an amended assessment the right of objection was limited, in effect, to the fresh or increased liability imposed upon the taxpayer. Section 186 provided for the respondent to decide the objection and serve written notice of the decision. Section 187 of the Act as amended by the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act) 1986 relevantly provided that a taxpayer dissatisfied with a decision under section 186 may request that the decision be referred to this Tribunal. The applicant made such requests in respect of each year of income and the respondent complied with those requests on 10 April 1987.

  1. Section 190 of the Act provides that in proceedings on a review before this Tribunal the taxpayer shall, unless the Tribunal otherwise orders, be limited to the grounds stated in the objection and the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer.
  2. It will be apparent from this that it is essential that the taxpayer's notice of objection is before the Tribunal. In the present case the respondent referred copies of the notices of objection to the Tribunal in purported compliance with section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as modified by section 14ZG of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. Those documents were unreadable and at the interlocutory stages in these applications the respondent undertook to file readable copies. That was done by the respondent except that the readable copy filed disclosed that the notice of objection (the notices for each year are in the same terms) referred to a large number of attachments which were incorporated by reference in the notice of objection. It was only during the hearing when the Tribunal drew attention to the situation that tine omission was remedied. The original notices of objection were tendered to the Tribunal during the hearing so that the Tribunal became aware of the notices of objection as lodged by the applicant (Exhibit C) for the first time. Those documents are readable and repetitious. Attached to each notice of objection are a number of signed statements most of which are statutory declarations sworn before a Commissioner for Declarations on 21 June 1985 - the day the notices of objection were lodged in the Taxation Office.
  3. Those statutory declarations became an important part of the applicant's case because he adopted the position that the statutory declarations proved the facts asserted in those declarations and the respondent was then challenged to prove otherwise. The applicant did not see it as part of his case to produce evidence to corroborate his own evidence rejecting suggestions from the Tribunal that he should call evidence from persons able to corroborate his evidence.

11. Instead he placed considerable reliance upon a decision of the Supreme Court of New Zealand reported as Phillips v. C. l. R. (1959) NZLR 1357. As in the present case the Court was concerned with a taxpayer assessed by the Commissioner on the assets betterment method of estimating the taxable income. In that case the taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court on the basis that the court below had erred in its findings of fact by declining to find that:

(a) the taxpayer had a substantial sum of cash and foreign currency in a cash box on 1 April 1940;

(b) the living expenses of the taxpayer had been overestimated;

(c) a substantial sum of money employed by the taxpayer in purchasing shares had been derived from betting;

(d) substantial sums of money had been held by the taxpayer for others at call; and

(e) a specific adjustment had been incorrectly included.

As to (a), (c) and (d) the court below had not been convinced by the evidence of witnesses on the "preponderance of probability". The Supreme Court accepted the findings of fact on these matters and thereby rejected the taxpayer's case on those matters.

  1. As to (b) and (e) the Court held that the estimates of living expenses adopted by the Commissioner were excessive and it therefore followed that the assessments were excessive. As to (e) the amount is based upon payments made through the taxpayer's account with a company to a third party. The payments were made by the company. The evidence suggested that it was the company's responsibility to effect the payments but the taxpayer had guaranteed the payments and the payee looked to the taxpayer for satisfaction. That was said to be the reason for passing the payments through the taxpayer's personal account with the Company. The Commissioner asserted that there must be a quid pro quo coming back to the taxpayer for the payments but was unable to identify any asset.
  2. In relation to this supposed but denied asset of the taxpayer Shorland J had this to say regarding the onus on the taxpayer at page 1359:

“The onus is upon the taxpayer to show that the Commissioner's assessment is wrong; but whatever may be said about initial onus, I am of opinion that when the Commissioner seeks to apply the 'assets method' of calculating a taxpayer's income, he must, in the final resort, undertake the burden of proving the existence of the asset which he claims the taxpayer has accumulated if the taxpayer's denial on oath of the ownership an existence of any such asset, is to be displaced."

In the result his Honour held that as there was "not a title of evidence" as to the existence of the alleged asset and therefore the Commissioner's assessment was excessive.

  1. It will be apparent that the reported case does not support the much wider contention of the applicant in these proceedings. The proposition he sought to draw from the case was that if he gave evidence on a matter to a certain effect then the Tribunal was bound to find the facts in accordance with his evidence unless the respondent proved the contrary fact. Although the Tribunal directed the applicant's attention to section 190 of the Act he preferred to rely upon the decision in Phillips. Furthermore he was not interested in what weight, if any, should be given to the statutory declarations but stood by his submission that having given evidence as to the facts it was for the respondent to disprove his evidence. Furthermore he attacked the method by which the respondent had made the estimates of the taxable income again claiming that the onus was on the respondent to prove the correctness of the assessments.
  2. The applicant's submissions must be rejected in the light of the decision of the High Court in McAndrew v. FCT [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263 where the Court decided that the word "excessive" in paragraph 190(b) refers to the quantum of the assessment, not any incorrect or unauthorised step in the process of calculating the taxable income. It is therefore necessary for the taxpayer to show that the substantive liability in the assessment is excessive; he must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that the taxable income for the respective year of income was less than the taxable income as assessed. That statement of the law is consistent with the decision of the High Court reported as FCT v. Dalco 90 ATC 4088. I have discussed this matter at the start of these reasons because of the pre-eminence which the applicant gave the issue in the conduct of his case. It will be apparent that the decision in Phillips (which can only have persuasive authority in Australia) in fact only deals with a situation where the existence of an asset is in dispute and it cannot be used in any sense contrary to the binding decisions in McAndrew and Dalco.

16. I turn now to the facts of this case. Exhibit A is the income tax returns lodged by the applicant for the years of income ended 30 June 1976 through to 30 June 1984 but not including the return for the year ended 30 June 1978. A copy of the 1978 return tendered by the applicant is Exhibit B. It is relevant to note the applicant's occupation on each of those returns as follows:

1976-1979 Salesman/Accountant

1980 Salesman/Broker

1981 -1982 Salesman/Broker/Accountant

  1. Political Candidate
  2. Accountant/R.E. Consultant

17. Until 1980 the applicant was employed by a company I will call "Landco" which I understand to be a land development company. As will appear the applicant's employment with Landco is central to much that is in contention. For the 1981 to 1983 years of income the applicant was employed by a group of companies which I understand to be owners or operators of retail shopping complexes. From September 1982 the applicant was unemployed and receiving unemployment benefits. During this period of unemployment he was the endorsed (but unsuccessful) candidate for the Australian Labor Party for a Federal electorate in respect to the 1983 Federal election. The applicant rejoined the workforce during the 1984 year of income but also received unemployment benefits during periods of that year of income when he was unemployed.

Employment with Landco

18. The applicant's employment with Landco seems to have been as accountant cum office manager. The applicant is a professional accountant. So far as I can tell from the evidence the employment was unexceptional except for the way in which the applicant was remunerated. This method of remuneration resulted in the applicant taking proceedings in the Supreme Court for moneys owing from the contract of employment by both the applicant and Landco as cross claimant. The only oral evidence before this Tribunal in relation to the salary arrangements with Landco was given by the applicant. I have, however, had the advantage of reading the transcript of the proceedings in the Supreme Court and the Court's reasons for judgment. I am bound to say straight away that I do not think this Tribunal was given an accurate description of the employment arrangements in respect of remuneration of the applicant. The evidence about these arrangements came from the applicant, from Exhibit 3, and Exhibit AAA which is a copy of a ledger account in the accounts of Landco numbered Q3000.

19. From all the evidence before this Tribunal and the Supreme Court, there can be no doubt that the Q3000 account was nothing more than a record of an arrangement to evade the payment of income tax on the part of the applicant. My reasons for this conclusion are as follows. In negotiations with the managing director of Landco the applicant sought to increase his salary to an amount commensurate with his duties at the Company. That resulted in an arrangement whereby the applicant was allowed to charge certain types of goods for his private use to the company. Those transactions are recorded in the Q3000 ledger account. That account was the genesis of the litigation in Applicant v. Landco. The amounts charged to the Q3000 account for each of the relevant years of income are as follows:

Year ended $

30 June 1977 1,993

30 June 1978 6,312

30 June 1979 7,628

30 June 1980 2,454

The workings of this account were explained before the Supreme Court (Exhibit 3 pages 21, 22, 50).

  1. A further aspect of the applicant's employment relates to an overseas trip in January 1979. Although said to be working on the business of his employer the applicant was not paid salary during that period. He was, however, paid a travel allowance including the amount which otherwise would have been his salary. The evidence on this matter before the Supreme Court was quite clear and unequivocal (Exhibit 3 page 18. It is not at all clear as to whether the applicant was merely on a holiday or working for his employer.
  2. in addition, it was Landco's practice, it seems, to allow the applicant to charge the running costs of his motor vehicle to the company (Exhibit 3 page 25). Landco seems to have been content to pay the applicant's expenses where those expenses were of such a nature that they could be passed off as incurred in the course of Landco's business.
  3. There is no doubt in my mind that the applicant and Landco agreed that part of the applicant's remuneration would be paid in such a way that it was not disclosed on the group certificate issued each year by Landco. This is made clear in the Supreme Court proceedings (Exhibit 3 pages 50-4 and 81). For example, the applicant gave evidence that for the year ended 30 June 1977 his salary was $12,000 p.a. for the first three months and $15,000 p.a. for the remainder of the year.

23. The amounts (including allowances) shown in the income tax return (Exhibit A) total $11,578 compared with what should have been $14,250 on the applicant's own evidence in the Supreme Court. In the subsequent financial years the applicant was paid $15,000 p.a. in cash until he resigned from employment with Landco in January 1980. His evidence before the Supreme Court of payment of a further $15,000 p.a. plus bonuses over and above the cash payment confirms the rationale of the Q3000 account. However, I do not find that the applicant was paid $30,000 plus bonuses by Landco. On the evidence I am unable to find the actual amount of his agreed salary. His income tax returns disclosed the following salary and allowances from Landco (Exhibit 3 page 70):

Year Ended $

30 June 1978 15,289

30 June 1979 12,692

30 June 1984 8,654 (7 months)

It will be apparent that the amounts returned are only consistent with the evidence of a cash salary of $15,000. The 1979 year's figure as returned is down due to the non disclosure of salary paid as travel allowance while on the overseas trip. Cash bonuses were also paid in the 1977 and 1978 years but not disclosed for tax purposes.

Motor Vehicle Claims

24. Allowances for use of his private car were paid to the applicant by Landco as follows:

Year Ended $

30 June 1977 1,992

30 June 1978 2,920

30 June 1979 2,424

30 June 1980 1,652

With the exception of the 1977 year when deductions totalling $1,417 were claimed for motor vehicle expenses the taxpayer treated the car allowances as "fully expended". in view of the evidence in the Supreme Court to the effect that Landco paid the running costs and the fact that the applicant had an essentially "indoor" position with Landco I am not satisfied that the applicant incurred anything in the way of motor vehicle expenses which are allowable deductions. Nor am I satisfied that the motor vehicles were used for the purpose of producing assessable income. However, in view of the concession made by the respondent's counsel during the hearing I have not made adjustments for the amounts conceded.

  1. In the course of evidence before the Tribunal a somewhat bizarre situation arose in relation to the applicant's motor vehicles. It is fair to say that he was less than frank in his dealings with the respondent in relation to the motor vehicles. I instance some matters which, in my view, are illustrative of the general position in relation to the applicant's returns.
  2. The 1976 return disclosed a vehicle with an opening written down value of $900 sold for $850 with a loss of $50 on sale and a new vehicle purchased for $2,500. Depreciation at 22.5% was claimed for the full year. In the 1977 return six months depreciation was claimed in respect of the vehicle which was shown as being sold and a small loss on sale claimed. The applicant also claimed depreciation on a new vehicle said to cost $4,500 for the remaining six months.
  3. In evidence before the Tribunal the following facts were established. The vehicle with an opening written down value of $900 was in fact a flood damaged Subaru vehicle purchased by the applicant's wife from the Brisbane City Council for $20 and not disposed of until 1980 (Exhibits A and T). The car said to have been purchased for $2,500 was a Holden Kingswood. Exhibit U is a letter dated 29 May 1987 addressed to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation verifying the purchase of the Holden Kingswood by the applicant on 13 June 1975 for $1,710. The letter was written on letterhead of Valley (Windsor) Auction Rooms by a person describing himself as "Auctioneer". In a statutory declaration attached to his notices of objection the applicant deposed that in June 1975 a Holden sedan was purchased from the Valley Auctions rooms for $1,710 using a Credit Union cheque. That statutory declaration was made on 21 June 1985. Exhibit 45 is a letter addressed to the Australian Taxation Office in which the author of Exhibit U states that the 1987 letter was written on the "instructions" of the applicant without recourse to recollection or records of the auctioneer.
  4. If that was all it might be thought that the applicant's claims for depreciation might be explainable. However, when applying for finance from banks and other financiers the applicant developed the technique of inflating and misdescribing assets. That technique was also applied to the Australian Taxation Office in 1980 when the applicant answered a requisition for information in respect of his 1979 return (Exhibit 777). As already noted, in that return the applicant had claimed that the car allowance of $2,424 paid by Landco had been fully expended and that amount was allowable as a deduction. The vehicles identified were the Holden Kingswood and the Subaru (it was not suggested before me that this was a different Subaru to the one purchased for $20 and reported as sold). The Holden Kingswood was said to have been purchased for $4,000 in December 1976 and the Subaru for $3,000 in April 1977. In cross-examination the applicant acknowledged Exhibit ZZZ as his document.
  5. It will be apparent from the above that the applicant is adept at changing his story to suit the circumstances. In other words, he is an unreliable witness whose evidence should only be accepted when it is corroborated. So far as it is relevant I cannot be satisfied that the applicant used his motor vehicles for any purposes and in particular cannot be satisfied that the vehicles were used in the course of his employment or for any other relevant purpose.
  6. The respondent was incorrect in bringing two newly acquired motor vehicles into the asset betterment statement for the 1977 year at $3,000 and $4,500 respectively. Losses on sale of vehicles of $50 and $600 in the 1976 and 1977 years respectively should also be excluded from the assets betterment statement. Needless to say these adjustments are necessary because the respondent relied upon written statements of the applicant shown before the Tribunal to be false. Although the Holden was purchased for $1,710, I accept the applicant's evidence that he had to repair and register the vehicle before he could use it, thereby bringing the cost to approximately $2,500. It is highly probable that a second hand Holden Kingwood purchased for $1,710 at auction in 1975 would require additional outgoings to put it on the road. I have ignored the existence of the $20 Subaru (de minimis non curat lex), and the purchase and sale of a VW car.

The Family Trust

  1. The applicant's training in accountancy apparently made him aware of tax planning and the tools of the tax planner. One of those is the family discretionary trust. The applicant decided he would have a use for such a trust and a trust deed was prepared, executed and stamped in September 1978. According to the back sheet the deed was prepared by a reputable firm of solicitors. The applicant was the sole trustee of the trust for the benefit of his wife and four children (Exhibit FFFF).
  2. If I was required to interpret provisions of the deed I confess that would cause some difficulty because of the wording of the document itself. I do not need to go further than to find that the applicant intended that a trust be created for the benefit of his immediate family and the necessary steps were taken to put that intention into effect. There is no basis for saying that the trust deed is a sham.
  3. The relevance of the trust in the applicant's affairs is his claim that various investments in real property were held by the applicant as trust property and not for his own benefit. He claimed in evidence that each property was acquired for the benefit of his children jointly and not for the benefit of himself (or for that matter his wife - notwithstanding the deed of trust).
  4. The only factors supporting the claim are the trust deed and the applicant's evidence. Factors against the claim are the title registration of each property being in the names of the applicant and his wife as joint tenants, the absence of a trust bank account or other separate fund from the applicant's funds and the failure to file income tax returns for the trust. In relation to the filing of returns the applicant claimed that as the trust properties were, in effect, negatively geared, and the trust thereby showing a loss, there was no requirement to lodge income tax returns. Such a claim has no foundation in law (Income Tax Regulation 15) nor in common knowledge.
  5. The real situation, and I so find, is that the investment properties are registered in the names of the applicant and his wife, they have conducted the properties as their own using their own bank accounts, they have represented the properties as their own when applying for loans from banks and other financiers and there is simply no written record of the properties being held in trust. Furthermore the applicant did not lead any evidence other than his own to establish that the properties were held as trust property. The applicant's explanation for not calling his children (all adults at the time of hearing) was that he did not wish to concern them with the proceedings before the Tribunal. I have absolutely no doubt that the real reason was that he did not want his children (or his wife) to know that he was asserting that the various properties were held in trust for his children. The applicant sold his interest in these properties after 30 June 1984 to his children. No doubt they would be concerned to discover that interests in properties sold to them by their father were being represented as already beneficially owned by them.
  6. Although I am satisfied that the family trust was created I am not satisfied that there was any trust estate, because the trustee did not hold any property subject to the terms of trust. I am satisfied that at all relevant times the real estate was owned by the applicant and his wife as joint tenants for their own benefit. Taking into account the applicant's assertion that the properties were being run at a loss those findings of fact would be to the advantage of the applicant taxpayer. The applicant is an astute person well aware of the financial consequences of his actions. Prima facie it is unlikely that the applicant would argue against his financial interest so that it might be reasonable to expect that the inclusion of the rental properties as jointly owned assets of the applicant and his wife is not to his advantage.

Investment Properties

37. I turn now to the transactions regarding the investment properties.

  1. Real property on hand at 1 July 1975 was located at "Rapier" Street, "Shortland" Street, "Rudyard" Street and the family home which I will describe as such to maintain confidentiality.
  2. At 1 July 1975 the family home was in a partly constructed state (cf. Exhibit BBBB). It was, however, inhabited by the family without, it seems, any great inconvenience. Over the next couple of years the applicant undertook substantial building operations on the property resulting in a three storey multi occupancy house with substantial out buildings. This building work was financed in part through the Q3000 account. I also find that the applicant carried out some of the work himself thereby adding to the value of the property at minimal cost.
  3. In this regard the applicant gave evidence to the general effect that much of the building materials incorporated into the family home after 1 July 1975 were already on the site by that date. This evidence is corroborated to a limited extent by Statutory Declaration No 3 attached to the applicant's notices of objection, a letter addressed to the applicant and his wife by the Shire Council dated 18 September 1978 (Exhibit F) and a letter dated 12 October 1984 by G N Visser, a registered tax agent (Exhibit E).
  4. The true story is to be found in other exhibits. By an application dated 25 October 1976 to the Shire Council the applicant sought approval for addition of a third storey to the family home (Exhibit O). The estimated cost was shown as $15,000 with cavity brick construction for the external walls. Exhibit K is a delivery note for 3,600 medium buff bricks and is dated 24 November 1976. Exhibit G includes a copy of a receipt for cash dated 21 December 1976 in respect of payment by the applicant for 3,200 medium buff bricks. There is no evidence to suggest those were the only materials purchased for the third storey extension at that time.
  5. The applicant's evidence to the effect that the materials were on hand for the extension as at 1 July 1975 is, in my view, less than frank and disproved by the documentary evidence such as it is. I therefore reject the applicant's evidence regarding building materials and I will adopt the respondent's basis in the assets betterment statement in respect of the family home. I should add that the fact that the Shire Council served notice on the applicant about building materials on the site and adjoining Council land by letter dated 18 September 1978 (Exhibit F) does not in any the respondent's contention that those building operations, although commenced in 1976 continued for some considerable time.
  6. The applicant did not contend that the family home was trust property but he did contend that it was held for the benefit of his children. There is no evidence to corroborate the applicant's claim and I reject it.
  7. I turn now to the Rapier Street property. Considerable evidence was lead in respect of this property. That evidence shows, beyond reasonable doubt, that the applicant built the premises in contravention of the City Council town planning by-laws. As a consequence the premises were not connected by the sewerage and electricity authorities and were not used for any worthwhile purpose until leased in 1983. The applicant's evidence in respect of the use of this property is not corroborated by any other evidence. The respondent was correct in his treatment of the property in the assets betterment statement. Exhibit WW is a copy of valuation of the property made by the Australian Taxation Office in 1983 for the Department of Social Security. The state of the property and the department's interest therein are made clear by the following paragraph in the report:

"COMMENTS: Enquiries made of the Council indicate that about 3 years ago when the building was partially completed the owner made an application to have it registered as flats. When the application was refused work ceased on the building. Since then the owner has unsuccessfully tried to sell the project. In its present condition heavy expenditure would be required before the Council would allow the building to be occupied. The structure has been over-capitalised as a residential unit, and, although it could be adapted for use as a homeless persons hostel, it does not represent optimum design for that purpose."

Oral evidence before the Tribunal corroborated the valuer's comments in respect of the property. Notwithstanding those comments the Australian Government acquired the property in 1984.

45. Although the applicant asserted that he and his family undertook a substantial amount of the construction work on the property, and this is corroborated to some extent by the oral evidence of poor workmanship by two valuers who inspected the property, I am unable to come to any conclusion as to the cost of construction. To attempt to do so would amount to speculation on the part of the Tribunal. I note, however, that the respondent has already made a substantial allowance for the alleged fact that the applicant and his family did a lot of the work. How the respondent became satisfied that such allowance should be made is not apparent to the Tribunal.

  1. 1 am satisfied, however, that the Rapier Street property was not let until March 1984 when the applicant and his wife teased the property for $200 per week (less $50 allowed to the tenants for cleaning). Outgoings in respect of the property in toe nature of rates and interest would therefore only be allowable deductions from that time. I reject entirely the applicant's claim that the premises were previously let to his aunt (described on the 1978 return as a parent for dependant rebate purposes) for a peppercorn rent of $1 (Exhibit CC).
  2. The Shortland Street property previously used as the family home but let for the period 1975 to 1983, was sold in 1984 so that there is no adjustment necessary on the asset betterment statement for the family home, the Rapier Street property and the Shortland Street property. The property at Rudyard Street was vacant land purchased in 1966 and not developed during the relevant period so that no adjustment is necessary in respect of that property.
  3. The asset betterment statement shows the acquisition of a property in "Paling" Street as having been purchased in January 1976. The applicant gave evidence that the property was purchased for $16,500 in 1975. The cost included an option fee of $500.
  4. Although the evidence is less than satisfactory I am prepared to accept that the applicant and his wife paid a deposit of $3,000 in respect of the purchase of this property in April 1975. A consequential adjustment to the asset betterment statement will be necessary in respect of the 1975 year and the option fee of $500 should be added to the cost of Paling Street for subsequent years.
  5. The subsequent sale of Paling Street to the daughter of the applicant is not relevant.
  6. In respect of the remainder of the investment properties I need only deal with property located in Winter Street. That property was purchased from Landco and immediately resold. The property consisted of two shops, two flats and vacant land. The Asset Betterment Statement

  1. The applicant put in issue the methodology of the asset betterment statement also launching a quite unjustified attack on the Taxation Office auditor who compiled the statement. In so doing the applicant revealed a surprising ignorance for someone who claimed to be a professional accountant (when he was not claiming that he was merely a bookkeeper).
  2. I can do no better than quote dicta of Mr R R Gibson, Chairman, Taxation Board of Review No 1 in Case 110 3 CTBR (NS) 697 where the learned Chairman described the purpose and preparation of an assets betterment statement at pages 700-701:

'The purpose of the application of the 'betterment' or 'assets increase' basis is, by the ascertainment or estimation of the receipts and expenditure of a given year, to assess the taxpayer on the excess of his assessable receipts over his allowable expenditure, subject to allowances in respect of items (e.g. depreciation) which do not constitute expenditure made in the year. Let the case be supposed of a taxpayer who had no liabilities at the beginning or at the end of the given year but who, at the beginning of the year, owned assets (his only assets) the acquisition of which had cost him 2000 pounds in money actually expended and who, at the end of the year, owned not only those assets but also additional assets the acquisition of which had cost him 2000 pounds in money actually expended by him in the course of the year. In such a case the source of the additional expenditure of 2000 pounds during the year must have been moneys received by the taxpayer in the year in one way or another and such moneys be taken to have been so much of the taxpayer's total receipts for the year as had not been absorbed by his total expenditure for the year, not being expenditure made in the acquisition of the additional assets. The receipts absorbed by expense not allowable as deductions might be to some extent ascertainable but would usually be required to be estimated (as, e.g. in the case of expenses on subsistence). The receipts absorbed by expenses allowable as deductions would usually not be ascertainable, but that fact would be immaterial because the allowable expenses would necessarily be equal to the receipts which they absorbed. The end point of the Commissioner's calculations would be the exclusion from the total receipts, as thus ascertained, estimated or assumed, such of those receipts as he found (so far as he could find on the facts before him) not to be of the character of assessable income, e.g. a prize in a lottery. The amount of receipts absorbed by the expenditure during the year on the acquisition of the additional assets is seff-established. (In the supposed case it would be 2000 pounds). And the validity of the figure finally calculated by the Commissioner would depend upon the accuracy of his findings as to how much (if any) of the taxpayer's total receipts for the year (including the 2000 pounds) was in the nature of non-assessable income. The trouble which often arises in such a case is ascribable to the failure to distinguish between the Value' of an asset and the money expended in acquiring it."

See also per McGarvie J in L'Estrange v. FCT (1978) 9 ATR 410 at 431-3.

  1. It is clear beyond any shadow of doubt that the applicant failed to make a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary in respect of the returns of income before the Tribunal.   2. The question then arises as to whether paragraph (a) of sub-section 170(2) operates to authorise the amended assessments for the years ended 30 June 1976 to 30 June 1978 inclusive. In respect of the years ended 30 June 1977 and 1978 when the applicant fraudulently evaded tax by the use of the Q3000 account of Landco there was, in my opinion, both fraud and evasion and I so find.
  3. The understatements of income in the year ended 30 June 1976 are interest income of $22 and presumably undisclosed rental income represented by the calculated omissions. Those omissions have not been explained to the Tribunal and in particular the omission of the rental income cannot be explained by the creation of the trust. The trust deed is dated 8 September 1978 (Exhibit FFFF). There is no basis for a finding of fraud in respect of these understatements but they do represent, in my view, a deliberate avoidance of tax, there being no other reasonable explanation of the omissions before the Tribunal. A deliberate avoidance of tax amounts to evasion of tax and I find, as a matter of opinion, that there was evasion of tax in respect of the year ended 30 June 1976. I am conscious of the fact that the original 1976 assessment was non-taxable. No argument was put to me as to whether the assessment modified by notice datd 24 April 1985 was in law an amended assessment. I have taken the prudent course of assuming it was. An assumption consistent with the known facts.

Remission of Additional Tax

  1. The basis on which the respondent remitted the additional tax imposed by sub-section 226(2) of the Act was not made clear to the Tribunal although there is evidence of the remissions before me.
  2. The applicant says he has been persecuted by the Taxation Office because he stood for election as a candidate for the Australian Labor Party. The rationale of the applicant's allegation is not apparent to me, but I repeatedly declined the applicant's invitations to investigate the administration of the Taxation Office. I cannot, therefore, form any view of the allegation. That the applicant is excitable and becomes agitated by officials doing their duty was made abundantly clear before me. I think I can safely infer that the applicant was unco-operative in his dealings with the Taxation Office. Before this Tribunal he displayed personality traits which would make him difficult to deal with. He admitted such before me. However, I formed the view that these admitted facts are really a cover for a determination to camouflage the truth and seek to avoid liability under the taxation laws.
  3. The respondent reacted to this situation by generally remitting the additional tax imposed by sub-section 226(2) of the Act to amounts equalling or approximating 100% of the tax avoided. In doing so the officers apparently relied upon Taxation Ruling IT2012. The basis is set out in Exhibit 36 which makes it clear that some tempering of the original submission for remission of the additional tax took place in the upper levels of the Taxation Office.
  4. Because I have found it necessary to make adjustments to taxable incomes as assessed by the respondent I will also make adjustments to the remission of the additional tax. However, as will be clear from these reasons this is a case where subsection 226(2) should apply with full effect. The applicant has set out to deliberately hoodwink the respondent and this Tribunal as to his liability. He has been involved in blatant evasion in circumstances which do not evidence any mitigating circumstances.
  5. I have therefore decided that the additional tax imposed by sub-section 226(2) should be remitted to the following amounts of additional tax to be assessed:

  1. The Tribunal will set aside the objection decisions in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1976 through to 30 June 1983 inclusive and remit each matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with directions.
  2. Those directions will be in relation to the taxable income and the additional tax to be assessed. As a consequence of the Tribunal's findings some change in rebates allowable may result but no directions are made in respect of those rebates.
  3. In respect of the application relating to the year of income ended 30 June 1984 the objection decision under review will be affirmed.
