Donald Frederick Charles and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 244; (1993) 18 AAR 61 (1993) 30 ALD 417 (6 August 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DONALD FREDERICK CHARLES v. COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

No. W92/289

AAT No. 8897

Number of pages - 9

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 244; (1993) 18 AAR 61

[1993] AATA 244; (1993) 30 ALD 417 (extract)

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

T.E. BARNETT (Senior Member), J.G. BILLINGS (Member) AND K.J. TAYLOR (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Whether late election to preserve superannuation benefits on retirement should be treated as one within specified period under s.137(1) - applicant employed as a rigger at Telecom - working in isolated areas - prior to resignation received Form S2A with covering letter directing applicant to sign refund of contributions form - rejoined Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme - inadequate notice in subsequent circulars.

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.137(1), 157(1)

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s. 37

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456

Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 8553; 26 February 1993)

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315

Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 8609; 19 March 1993)

Foley and Commissioner of Superannuation (AAT Decision No. 8731; 25 May 1993)

HEARING

PERTH, 10 June 1993

6:8:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: Ms Nicholson

Solicitor for the Applicant: Dwyer Durack

Counsel for the Respondent: Ms Jolly (Departmental Advocate)

ORDER

WHEREAS the applicant elected by Notice in writing to the Commissioner that Division 3 of the Superannuation Act 1976 apply to him AND WHEREAS the said election was made outside the period allowed for by s.137 of the said Act AND WHEREAS the Tribunal is satisfied in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised.

IT IS HEREBY DIRECTED THAT:

1. The election be treated as if it had been made within the period

allowed by the Act.

2. The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the

applicant's entitlements in the light of this decision.

3. The parties are granted liberty to apply regarding the calculation

of entitlements.

DECISION

T.E. BARNETT, J.G. BILLINGS AND K.J. TAYLOR This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent dated 9 February 1992 refusing to direct, under s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), that the election made by the applicant on 25 July 1991 be treated as if it were an election made within the period specified under s.137(1) of the Act.

2. The Tribunal heard evidence from the applicant and from Mr Graham Watts and Mr Andrew Hemus and admitted into evidence the documents furnished pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T documents"), in addition to the following exhibits:

Exhibit R2 Statement of facts and contentions of the applicant

together with attachments

Exhibit R3(a) Administrative Circular dated 23 April 1976 and

Superannuation Board Circular

Exhibit R3(b) Administrative Circular dated 28 June 1976

Exhibit R3(c) Administrative Circular dated 18 September 1978

Exhibit R4(a) Information Statement for Contributors dated 1987

Exhibit R4(b) Information Statement for Contributors dated 1988

Exhibit A3 Copy of witness' statements of Mr Hemus, Mr McRedden

and Mr Watts

Exhibit R5 Copy of form S2A

3. The applicant in this matter was represented by Ms Nicholson, of Dwyer Durack; Ms Jolly, a Departmental Advocate, appeared for the respondent.

4. The applicant is a rigger employed by Telecom and a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme ("CSS") who resigned on 5 January 1979 and accepted a refund of his contributions to the scheme of $2,700. He did not elect to preserve his rights under the CSS which was provided for by s.137 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). In 1980 he rejoined Telecom in his old job and also rejoined the CSS. On 25 July 1991 he applied under s.157(1) of the Act to make a late election to preserve his superannuation rights.

5. The relevant sections are as follows:

"Section 137(1) A person who -

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph

(b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under

this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73;
or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee circumstances by virtue of which the

person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired

involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible

employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner that this

Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within

one month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has

effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an
eligible

employee.

(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section if of no

effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to

the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the

Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period that is the
prescribed

period in relation to him -

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed

period and, if so, the name and address of the employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a

member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that

employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."

6. Subsection 157(1) contains a power to recognise an election made after the prescribed time (of 21 days) in certain circumstances. It is in these terms:

"S.157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the

period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and
the

Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is

desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may

direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the

period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

7. The original decision of the delegate of the respondent determined on 9 February 1992 that, in all the circumstances, it was not desirable pursuant to s.157(1) of the Act that the election made by the applicant be recognised. This decision was affirmed by a Review Officer of the respondent by determination dated 12 October 1992. The Review Officer decided, with reference to Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456, that the applicant should have made further enquires in relation to his resignation benefits as the Commonwealth had adequately alerted the applicant to the preservation option by the terms of form S2A.

Findings of Fact

8. On the evidence and materials before it the Tribunal makes the following findings of fact.

(1) The applicant joined Telecom in 1965 as a technical officer and

became a member of the CSS on 19 March 1971. He spent most of his
working

life with Telecom as a rigger, in which job he was mostly employed in the

field in quite remote places, maintaining satellite dishes and radio

aerials. He is a man of limited formal education with no tertiary

qualifications.

(2) From 1965 to 1971 he was based in Carnarvon where he experienced

considerable difficulty and stress because of lack of accommodation for

himself and his family. During this time he had personal difficulties
with

one of his supervisors on this subject. This resulted in him being

transferred in 1971 to Gnangara, an outer suburb in Perth. The applicant

successfully applied for a position in Sydney and was transferred to

Doonside, an outer suburb in Sydney.

(3) After oral requests for him to return to Carnarvon, and after the

respondent undertook to provide adequate accommodation and an assistant
to

work with him, he agreed to return to Carnarvon and did so in 1977.

Accommodation was provided as promised but he was very frustrated to find

that he was not being provided with an assistant, despite the fact that
he

had to work at heights in his job.

(4) While suffering this frustration and stress he made a spur of the

moment decision to apply for a position advertised in the newspaper as a

rigger with the United States Navy in Exmouth. To his great surprise his

application was successful. The job carried higher pay and the
requirement

to join a superannuation scheme run by the United States Navy. He accepted

the position despite worries about his age (he was then 39 years old) and

the fact that he would have to work on high towers at heights of up to

1,200 feet. On the facts, this sudden resignation did not indicate a
firm

intention never to rejoin the Commonwealth Public Service, which was a

major user of the work skills which he possessed.

(5) In 1976 and 1978, administration circulars concerning the

superannuation scheme had been placed on the notice board in the

administration building in Carnarvon above the desk where the applicant
and

fellow employees had to sign on and off duty each day. The circulars

consisted of several pages of single space typing and the notice board
was

not maintained efficiently as new circulars were regularly placed over the

top of old information. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's evidence that

he did not notice or read the information provided by the circulars. The

circulars were tendered in evidence. The 1978 circular included the

following section:

"B. PERSONS LEAVING THE SUPERANNUATION SCHEME

An officer who ceases to be a contributor after five years membersh ip
and

has no entitlement for a pension or invalidity benefit, may elect during

the period one month before cessation to twenty-one days after cessation

that the superannuation rights be preserved. The following benefits are

available:-

(i) A Transfer Value

This is a lump sum including employee and employer components which may

be payable to an eligible superannuation scheme. These schemes would be

those so declared from time to time by the Minister concerned and would

include those provided by most of the Universities, by the Defence Force

Retirement and Death Benefits Act, and by the Commonwealth Hostels and
the

Australian Wool Corporation. The amount to be paid will be determined by

the Commissioner for Superannuation; or

(ii) A Deferred Benefit

Such benefits as are determined by the Commissioner for Superannuation

payable after the former contributor reaches sixty years of age.

Where a person does not qualify for a transfer value or a deferred
benefit,

a normal refund of accumulated employee contributions plus interest will

apply.

Contributors who consider that they may have an entitlement in accordance

with the foregoing, should address their initial inquiries to the

Conditions of Service Section, Administrative Branch.

Please ensure that this Circular is prominently displayed for reference
by

all staff members."

Personal copies of these circulars were not provided to members of the
CSS

"... due to the complexity of the procedures involved ... due to the

possibility of misinterpretation."

(6) The applicant signed a letter of resignation dated 4 December 1978 to

take effect on 5 January 1979. Shortly after posting his letter of

resignation he received a memorandum in the mail attached to a form S2A.

Form S2A was as follows:

The accompanying memorandum was in the following words:

"MEMORANDUM FOR

RIGGER GRADE 2

SUPERANNUATION

The memorandum and the Form S2A were initialled and handed on to the applicant by the local manager, Mr Watts, without comment.

9. After examining the circulars tendered in evidence the Tribunal is satisfied that it would have been difficult to read these unattractively presented documents while they were attached to a notice board. Only highly motivated and persistent readers would be likely to attempt (or to complete) this task and even then it does not convey the message, in simple language, that a resignee has an option to make an election to preserve his pension rights.

10. No other information was given to the applicant about his option to preserve his superannuation rights. The Tribunal accepts his evidence that he believed "the folklore" of that time that persons who resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service were limited to receiving a refund of their contributions to the CSS and that they would lose the benefit of the contributions paid by their employer. The Tribunal also accepts that the applicant was at the date of his resignation completely ignorant of his right to preserve his superannuation benefits and that if he had been aware he would probably have elected to do so, as it would have been very much to his advantage.

11. The applicant commenced work with the United States Navy at Exmouth a few days after resignation from Telecom. He found the job very unsatisfactory as he disliked working at heights of up to 1,200 feet with much younger employees, and he was experiencing trouble with his back from the climbing requirements. After several months he received a verbal invitation to return to work at Carnarvon for Telecom on the same conditions as before.

12. He then returned to Carnarvon in his old job after a break of just over twelve months. As he had broken his Commonwealth service for over twelve months he accepted the fact that he would suffer substantial loss of his sick pay and long service entitlements. On resumption he received no information at all about the CSS which he was rejoining. Had he been treated as a normal recruit he would have been given a considerable amount of information which would have set out all the terms, conditions and rights of members of the CSS, including the right of persons who resign from the CSS. The applicant made no inquiries about any of these matters as he believed there was no chance of regaining his various entitlements. Specifically, he made no inquiries about preservation of his former superannuation rights as he believed that all of these matters were dead issues.

13. In the years 1984, 1985 and 1986 the Commissioner sent information statements to the Departments to be handed to all members of the CSS. The Tribunal finds that the applicant would have received one of these statements in each year and notes that although many of the rights of members under the CSS were provided in the information statements, the right of people who resigned to retain their superannuation benefits was not included in any of these statements.

14. In the years 1987 and 1988 the applicant did receive new forms of annual statements headed "Information Statement for Contributors to the Superannuation Fund". On the back of the 1987 Information Statement under the heading "Preservation" were the words:

"Preservation. Certain contributors who resign may elect to preserve

their benefits in the Fund until retirement and thereby avoid forgoing
the

employer contribution of 2.5 times the employee's contributions

accumulated with interest. For further details of this option contact
your

personnel officer."

15. On the back of the 1988 information statement under the heading "Resignation Benefits" were the words:

"If you resign from Commonwealth employment before age 55 you should

consider the value of the preservation option. Briefly, if you become

entitled to a deferred benefit and so leave your equity in the fund your

equity will continue to grow at the rates of interest prescribed from
time

to time. In addition, you will have preserved an annual indexed pension

financed by your Commonwealth employer."

16. At the bottom of the statement there is a notice that further information about any retirement, resignation or invalidity benefits can be obtained from the personnel section or by writing to the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office and the address is supplied. The applicant admits that he may possibly have glanced through that information on the back of the information statements but does not remember doing so. He says that if he did so it would not have seemed relevant to his situation as, at that time he believed that the question of his rights at the date of his resignation in January 1979 were by then a dead issue. No information was provided on the statements or in any other way about the right to make late applications.

17. At a seminar run by the respondent to explain the new superannuation scheme to members in 1991, the applicant heard for the first time that he may have had a right in 1979 to preserve his superannuation entitlements when he resigned. After that he took prompt action by writing to the Retirement Benefits Office for copies of the relevant documents and when these were not forthcoming took further action when he again wrote to the Retirement Benefits Office on 11 March 1992. This was treated by the respondent as an application by the applicant to exercise his election to preserve his superannuation rights.

Application of the law

18. Section 157(1) allowed the Commissioner to accept a late election if:

"In all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election

should be recognised"

19. The word "desirable" was considered by Deputy President Forgie in Re Liddle:

"It could only be desirable to recognise a late election if there

were objectively good reasons which justified the person's not being

required to comply with the time limits otherwise clearly specified by

the Act."

20. The majority in Liddle (Deputy President Forgie dissenting) was influenced by the fact that the applicant was suffering from stress and found that it was desirable to allow the applicant to make a late election as he did not have knowledge of the option and because the Commissioner had not taken adequate steps to make him aware of it.

21. In Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8553; 26 February 1993) in a tightly reasoned decision Deputy President McMahon found that the applicant had an evidentiary burden to establish that there is an acceptable explanation for the delay in making the election but had failed to do so. He applied the principles propounded by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at 319-320. In that case the applicant was a very well educated librarian at the National Library in Canberra who wrote a letter of resignation requesting refund of her contributions to the superannuation fund because she felt she had been subjected to harassment by a superior officer. She was then sent form T5/26 and signed it to obtain her refund. The appropriate boxes on the form electing a refund rather than preservation of entitlements were marked. When she rejoined the Public Service in 1983 she could not remember signing any documents. She first heard of her right to elect to preserve her benefits years later at a seminar and then applied to make a late election. The Tribunal found that the principle reason she had resigned was because of role conflict between her job and the demands of caring for three young children. Mrs Lander made no inquiries about her superannuation rights at the time of resigning despite the fact that she signed the form in the presence of an experienced personnel officer who swore that she always explained the superannuation entitlements provisions and despite the fact that her husband was a senior executive officer in the Public Service. There was evidence that when Mrs Lander rejoined the Public Service in 1985 she attended an induction course directed at her then current superannuation rights.

22. In Lander each of the principles laid down by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley was considered. The Tribunal was particularly influenced by the fact that Mrs Lander had been located in Canberra, not in a remote location; that her rights had been carefully explained to her by a personnel officer; and that she had the ability and ample opportunity to make inquiries. In the result the Tribunal found that it was not desirable to allow her to make a late election.

23. Lander was followed in Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8609; 19 March 1993) where the applicant was a Telecom technician who also was unaware of his rights to preserve his superannuation rights at resignation. He was sent a similar memorandum and the same ambiguous S2A form as was given to the applicant in this present case. Having no knowledge of his rights he did not elect to preserve his superannuation entitlements. (Mr Secourable also had access to a book called "the yellow book", which was a Telecom guide to superannuation contributors, which was quite misleading in that it did not make any mention at all of this right to preserve benefits.) Mr Secourable left Telecom in order to go into a private business venture but resumed employment with Telecom in 1984. The Tribunal found that up until he resumed with Telecom he had no knowledge of his rights to preserve his entitlements and at the time of resumption, had he applied, it would have been desirable to allow him to elect to do so. When Mr Secourable rejoined the scheme, however, he was provided with full details of the rights of contributors, including clear instructions about how a contributor who resigned could elect to retain his superannuation rights. Then in the years 1987 and 1988 he was given, and read, the annual information statements for contributors, which gave him another opportunity to become aware that he had the potential for preserving his superannuation rights on resignation. The Tribunal found that, at that stage, it was reasonable to expect that he should have made inquiries about his position at the time of his earlier resignation. Specific mention was made of the fact that Mr Secourable was approaching retirement age and would have been alert to the question of his superannuation entitlements. The Tribunal in Secourable decided that the continued delay in making inquiries made it not desirable to allow him to make a late election.

24. Lander and Secourable were distinguished in the case of Foley and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8731; 25 May 1993) which was also decided by this Tribunal. In the special circumstances of Foley the applicant, who was a Senior Personnel Officer, had very detailed "hands on knowledge" of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act"), which gave a resignee only the option to obtain a refund of his contributions. Under the 1922 Act there was no possibility of preserving superannuation rights on resignation. Mr Foley was seconded to the United Nations in Montreal in 1967 and at that time he had this knowledge deeply entrenched in his consciousness, because supervising staff and personnel matters under the provisions of the 1922 Act had been the subject of his daily employment. After his secondment to the United Nations Authority he remained in Montreal for many years. During his time in Montreal he was completely out of touch with Australian public service affairs and did not become aware of important changes in the legislation. In 1972 he was obliged to resign and wrote his letter of resignation from his home in Montreal. He unsuccessfully sought information from his department about his rights including his rights to superannuation. Despite his frequent requests the department negligently failed to provide him with this information until his return to Perth about one month after his resignation. A senior personnel officer then gave him a form S2A to sign at a party in Perth. The document was already filled in so that it was limited to the option of claiming a refund and it was handed to him for immediate signature. Having signed the document Mr Foley then returned to Montreal where he continued in his employment with the United Nations Authority and continued to be out of touch with any information about Australian superannuation affairs.

25. When he finished with the United Nations and returned to Australia in 1989 he became aware of his rights which had existed at the date of resignation. He then made immediate inquiries and an early request for a late election to preserve his superannuation rights. The Tribunal held in those circumstances that it was desirable to allow him to make a late election.

26. Turning to Mr Charles, the applicant presently before the Tribunal: up to the date of his resignation the circumstances are quite similar to Secourable except there is no evidence that Mr Charles had access to the yellow book. Mr Charles was in an isolated area and he was under stress. He was personally given no information about his rights and he did not read the public service bulletin that was placed at Carnarvon on the notice board.

27. The only personal communication the applicant received regarding his superannuation rights prior to resignation was the memorandum dated 28 December 1978 signed for the Director Administration which was attached to the Form S2A. On the evidence the memorandum was probably received by the local manager just before the applicant departed for Exmouth. It said:

"enclosed is an application for refund of your accumulated

contributions. Please complete and return to the Superannuation Clerk"

28. The expert authority on superannuation, in effect, directed the applicant to sign the refund of contributions form and return it to Head Office. No alternative options were mentioned; no relevant information on entitlements was provided. The applicant clearly was given instruction from his superiors and the recognised authority on pension matters that fitted the common knowledge of long standing - that if you left the service, all you got was a refund of contributions. The memorandum was clearly misleading and directive. The Form S2A which was attached to the memorandum, despite Ms Jolly's submissions to the contrary, was ambiguous and it is confusing. The local manager who initialled and handed on the memorandum without comment gave evidence that he also was unaware of the right to elect to preserve benefits on resignation and that he shared a belief which was common at the time that the only available option was to claim a refund.

29. For these reasons the Tribunal finds that at the date of resignation not only did the applicant have no knowledge of his rights in this respect but he was not negligent in failing to inform himself of those rights. If fault exists it lay with the respondent which had not taken reasonable steps to advise the applicant adequately of his rights. Had he been so advised the Tribunal accepts his evidence that he would have elected to preserve his superannuation entitlements.

30. The circumstances of his resumption of duty are also quite different to those of Mr Secourable and Mrs Lander because, unlike them, the applicant was given no information at all about the CSS when he rejoined it. He then received information statements for three years in 1984, 1985 and 1986 which would not have alerted him to make inquiries as they made no mention of preservation of rights on resignation. The mere mention in general terms on the reverse side of a pamphlet in 1987 and 1988 that an option to preserve rights on resignation existed at that time was not sufficient to put a man in the situation of Mr Charles on the alert so that he should be expected to inquire about his rights way back in 1978 and 1979.

31. When he was made aware of those rights in a seminar in 1990/1991 he did take prompt action to make further inquiries and to apply for a late election to preserve his superannuation rights.

32. The Tribunal has found it helpful to consider recent decisions in similar cases but, obviously, each case must be considered in the light of its own special circumstances. In the circumstances of this case the Tribunal finds that it is desirable that the late election should be recognised and treated as if it had been made within the period allowed in s.137 of the Act.

Decision

33. WHEREAS the applicant elected by Notice in writing to the Commissioner that Division 3 of the Superannuation Act 1976 apply to him AND WHEREAS the said election was made outside the period allowed for by s.137 of the said Act AND WHEREAS the Tribunal is satisfied in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised.

34. IT IS HEREBY DIRECTED THAT:

1. The election be treated as if it had been made within the period

allowed by the Act.

2. The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the

applicant's entitlements in the light of this decision.

3. The parties are granted liberty to apply regarding the calculation

of entitlements.
