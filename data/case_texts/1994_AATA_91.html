Charles Duncan King and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales [1994] AATA 91; (1994) 28 ATR 1047 (8 April 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

CHARLES DUNCAN KING v. TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF NEW SOUTH

WALES

No. N93/505

AAT No. 9408

Number of pages - 6

Tax Agents' Board

[1994] AATA 91; (1994) 28 ATR 1047

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

M.D. ALLEN (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Tax Agents' Board - Whether Applicant who was a Chartered Accountant with experience of tax matters in United Kingdom and United States of America had substantial involvement in Australian income tax matters including preparing a broad range of income tax returns. Overseas experience help relevant in assessing competency to practice.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - S251JA, S251BC

Income Tax Regulations - Regulation 156

Webb v Tax Agents' Board Queensland 28 ALD 464

Commissioner for Superannuation v Scott 71 ALR 408

Civiti v Tax Agents' Board Victoria 90 ATC 2039

HEARING

SYDNEY, 16 March 1994

8:4:1994

Applicant appeared in person

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr G. Elliott

Solicitor for the Respondent: Ms I. Langan - Australian

Government Solicitor

ORDER

FOR the reasons given orally at the conclusion of the hearing in this matter the decision under review is SET ASIDE and the Tribunal substitutes in lieu its decision that the Applicant has satisfied the requirements for registration as a Tax Agent as set out in Sections 251 JA and 251 BC of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and Regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations.

DECISION

M.D. ALLEN At the conclusion of the hearing of the above matter the terms of the decision intended to be made and the reasons therefor were stated orally. After service upon the Respondent of a copy of the decision that was in fact made the Respondent, pursuant to Sub-section 43(2A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, requested the Tribunal to furnish to the Respondent a statement in writing of the reasons of the Tribunal for its decision.

2. The oral reasons for decision have been transcribed by Auscript, the Commonwealth Reporting Service. Whereas those oral reasons may reflect the inelegance of an extempore decision, they are in fact the reasons for the said decision.

3. The said transcript is annexed hereunto and furnished to the Respondent and to the Applicant as it is the reasons for the Tribunal's decision.

DRAFT JUDGMENT

MR ALLEN By notice lodged 29 July 1993 the applicant sought review by the tribunal of the decision of the respondent made 2 July 1993 not to grant him registration as a tax agent. The said application for review came on for hearing before me on 16 March 1994, the applicant representing himself and the respondent being represented by Mr Elliott of counsel. In its statement of issues to the tribunal the respondent put its case in the following terms, namely the issue in this matter is therefore whether Mr King's experience to the date of his application as represented by the number of returns which were either prepared or completed by him represents a substantial involvement in income tax matters as is required by regulation 156. Section 251JA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended states:

The board shall register the applicant as a tax agent if the

applicant satisfied the board that:

a) if the applicant is a natural person:

i) the applicant is a fit a proper person to prepare income

tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers

in income tax matters, and

ii) the applicant is not an undischarged bankrupt.

2. Section 251BC of the said act deals with those persons who are fit and proper persons to prepare income tax returns. It states:

Without limiting the generality of an expression used in this

part but subject to this section a person is not a fit and proper

person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf

of taxpayers in income tax matters as at a particular time if:

a) the person is not a natural person

b) both the following conditions are satisfied:

i) the person was not registered as a tax agent or as a

nominee for the purposes of this part immediately before the

commencement of section 39 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act Number

2 1988

ii) the person does not hold such qualifications whether

academic, by way of experience or otherwise as are prescribed

c) the person has not attained the age of 18 years.

d) the person is not of good faith, integrity and character.

e) the person has been convicted of a serious taxation offence during

the previous five years, or

f) the person is under sentence of imprisonment for a serious

taxation offence.

3. The only matter which really is contested in this matter is whether the applicant does hold qualifications as are prescribed. Regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations provides the criteria for the purposes of subparagraph 251BC(1)(b)(ii). It states:

1. For the purposes of subparagraph 251BC(1)(b)(ii) of the

following qualifications are prescribed:

a) The person:

i) shall have completed the academic requirements for the award of a

degree, diploma or other qualification from an Australian university,

college of advanced education or other tertiary institution of an

equivalent standard and have passed examinations in such subjects

under whatever name which an appropriate authority of the university,

college of advanced education or other tertiary institution certifies

to the board to represent a course of study in accountancy of not

less than 3 years duration and in commercial law of not less than 18

months duration or shall possess such other qualifications as the

board regards as equivalent to those qualifications

ii) shall have:

a) been engaged in relevant employment on a full time basis for not

less than a total of 12 months in the preceding five years

b) otherwise been engaged in relevant employment to an extent that

the board regards as equivalent to that referred to in subparagraph

(a) or

c) been engaged in such other employment and for such time as the

board regards as equivalent to being engaged in relevant employment

as referred to in subparagraph (a), and

iii) shall have by written examination successfully completed a

course of study in Australian Income Tax Law acceptable to the

board.

More importantly subregulation (2) of regulation 156 states:

In this regulation relevant employment means employment by a

person or a partnership or as a member of a partnership in the course

of which there has been substantial involvement in income tax matters

including:

a) the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax

returns

b) the preparation or examination of objections to assessments issued

in respect of such returns, and

c) the provision of advice in relation to income tax returns,

assessments or objections.

4. In March 1993 the applicant completed the course in Australian Taxation Law at the University of Sydney and this was accepted by the board as an acceptable course of study in Australian Income Tax Law. Various forms being from TAB130 were lodged by the applicant evidencing his experience in Australian Income Tax matters. For the reasons expressed in Re Webb v Tax Agents Board Queensland 28 ALD 464 I find that the 5 years referred to in paragraph 1A(2)(a) of regulation 156 refers to the 5 years immediately preceding this hearing. The applicant is however very experienced in accounting and income tax law. His curriculum vittae is exhibit F and it is sufficient to quote from it as follows:

The applicant is a chartered accountant being a Fellow of the

Institute of Chartered Accountants of England and Wales and a

Fellow of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Australia.

It would appear that he gained his qualifications after undergoing articles to a chartered accountant in the United Kingdom.

His employment commenced 1970 with Price Waterhouse and company

in London who are of course chartered accountants and there he was

auditing financial statements of 20 different companies of various

sizes and types. From 1972 to 1974 he was employed by Thomas Nelson

and Company of London, book publishers. From 1974 to 1979 he was

employed by Coopers and Lybrand, chartered accountants as a senior

auditor accountant. Part of that time, namely from 1974 to 1976, he

was in Perth and responsible for larger jobs including writing

financial reports on the operation of companies in the airline,

coffee and retail industries and supervising staff. In 1976-79 he

was at Goroka in Papua New. From 1980 to 1982, again at Goroka, he

was chief internal auditor of Telair Pty Limited.

In 1982 to 1984 he was self employed, being employed not only in

Sydney but also in California. During that employment he gained

experience in United States tax law and he also had experience in

Australia in preparing tax returns and accounts and giving tax

planning advice for companies, individuals, partnerships and trusts.

Again, in 1985 to 1987 he was employed by the Chesapeake Financial

Group in California. Returning to Australia in 1987, from 1987 to

1988 he was employed by John Moore and Partners, chartered

accountants, in North Sydney. Again, he was preparing tax returns

for trusts, individuals, companies and partnerships. During this

time he carried out an audit for the Australian Workers Union and

also prepared returns and audited that union's superannuation fund.

5. Then follows his experience leading to the current date. That experience is in evidence as the TAB 130s and is repeated here. Notwithstanding this wealth of experience, I agree with Mr Elliott's submission that the involvement in income tax matters referred to in sub-regulation 2 of regulation 156 must be involvement with Australian tax law - see Re Crowley in the Tax Agent's Board of New South Wales, Australian Tax Cases 2005 and 2010. In cross examination the applicant's experience during the last 5 years was extensively canvassed. The aim of that cross examination was to show that the applicant had not, in the words of sub-regulation 156(2) had substantial involvement in income tax matters, nor had he had involvement in the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax matters.

6. The word substantial was referred to by the majority of the full court of the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Scott, 71 Australian Law Reports 408 at 412. Fisher and Spender JJ said:

This court has on a number of occasions considered the adjective

substantial and even where it stands alone, uninfluenced by proximity

to the work "wholly" has adopted a meaning which accords with the

submission of the commissioner. I refer in particular to Tilman's

Butcheries Pty Limited v Australian Meat Industries Employees Union,

1980, 27 Australian Law Reports, 367. At 382 Dean J said: "The word

substantial is not only susceptible of ambiguity, it is a word

calculated to conceal a lack of precision. In the phrase

'substantial loss or damage' it can, in an appropriate context mean

real or of substance as distinct from ephemeral or phenomenal. It

can also mean large, weighty or big. It can be used in a relative

sense or it can indicate an absolute significance, quality or size."

The difficulties and uncertainties which the use of the word is

liable to cause are well illustrated by the guidelines given by

Viscount Simon in Pawsa v Grimley, 1948 Appeal cases 291 and 317

where after holding that in the context there under consideration the

meaning of the word was equivalent to considerable, solid or big, he

said: 'Applying the word in this sense it must be left to the

discretion of the judge of fact to decide as best he can according to

the circumstances of each case.'

7. In the contents of section 45D(1) of the Trade Practices Act 1974, Commonwealth, the word 'substantial' is used in a relative sense inthat regardless of whether it means large or weighty on the one hand or real or of substance as distinct from ephemeral or nominal on the other. It would be necessary to know something of the nature and scope of the relevant business before one could say that particular actual or potential loss or damage was substantial. As at present advised, I incline to the view that the phrase "substantial loss or damage" in section 45D(1) includes loss or damage that is in the circumstances real or of substance and not insubstantial or nominal.

8. It seems to me therefore that where the sub-regulation speaks of substantial involvement it means what it says, namely an involvement that is real or of substance and not insubstantial or nominal. What must be kept in mind, however, is that the sub-regulation speaks of substantial involvement in income tax matters without descending at that stage into particularity. The question of particular types of income tax matters is addressed in paragraphs A, B and C of sub-regulation 156(2). Paragraph A speaks of a broad range of income tax returns. The difference in language was referred to by Deputy President Forrest in Re Sevitti v the Tax Agents Board, Victoria, 90 Australian Tax Cases 2039 at 2043, namely:

The qualifying adjective 'broad' is a word of plain meaning and

common understanding. It has a similar meaning to 'wide'. I do not

accept the ambiguity of the argument or that it is open to put on the

word 'broad' the construction urged by the applicant. The word

'broad' is not one of similar meaning to 'substantial'. In the

context in which they are used, both terms are used as qualifying

adjectives. 'Broad range' does not mean the same thing as

substantial involvement and vice-versa. There maybe one without the

other. The respondent has to be satisfied the applicant has acquired

both in order to have been engaged in relevant employment within the

meaning of regulation 156.

Sub-regulation 156(2) cannot in my opinion be construed as applying

to a particular category of returns. It is not the experience within

a category of returns but rather the scope of the returns which is

the test to be applied.

9. Further, as Deputy President Forest pointed out in Re Civiti Supra registration as a tax agent is registration for all purposes and a limited right of practice cannot be given. The sub-regulation does, however, call for a substantial involvement in income tax matters and involvement in a broad range. Note: does not call for involvement in all categories of returns. In my opinion to concentrate on a mere numerical assessment of the number of returns prepared in any category is to make the mistake of confusing five years experience with one year's experience five times.

10. The applicant's experience in giving advice and preparing objection decisions was also attacked as insufficient. Although the relevant employment must relate to Australian tax law it is material in considering these factors to consider the applicant overall experience. Different approaches need to be taken to an experienced chartered accountant who has practised in tax accounting, not only in Australia but also in the United Kingdom, the United States and Papua New Guinea, to, say, for example, a recent university graduate with academic but little or no practical experience.

11. I am satisfied from the work which the applicant has done and his background that the applicant is perfectly capable of undertaking, without supervision and without risk to the public of all aspects of the practice of a tax agent. In particular, I find that the applicant has had a substantial involvement in income tax matters and that involvement includes the preparation and examination of a broad range of income tax returns, together with experience in the preparation of objections to assessment of the giving of advice in relation to income tax return assessments or objections.

12. I concede the applicant's involvement in the latter aspects is not of itself substantial but that is not the regulation requires. What I am satisfied of is that he has sufficient experience now in Australian tax law and those aspects that with his background he can be entrusted to undertake same. I find that the applicant has been engaged in such employment for such time as to be equivalent to being engaged in relevant employment as referred to in sub-paragraph (a) of regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations and that the decision under review should be set aside and the Tribunal substitutes in lieu thereof its decision, namely, that Charles Duncan King be registered as a tax agent pursuant to section 251JA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended.
