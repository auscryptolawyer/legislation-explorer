TAXATION - Income Tax - assessable income - applicant subject to amended assessments on the basis of undeclared income - explanation for source of income - question whether funds were taxable in Australia - applicant submitting that funds were derived from unrecorded transactions conducted from Vietnam - documentation unavailable in that particular environment - strength of evidence



Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) s.14ZZK(b)



REASONS FOR DECISION



12 February 1997 Deputy President G.L. McDonald

Mr D.L. Elsum, AM, Member

Mr W.G. McLean, Member



1. These are applications to review decisions of the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow an objection dated 13 April 1995 to assessments issued on 30 January 1995 in respect of the tax years ended 30 June 1988 to 1992 (inclusive). At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr M. Flynn, of counsel, and the respondent was represented by Ms J. Davies, of counsel. The applicant is married to Mr T who has also applied to the Tribunal for a review of the Commissioner’s decision to disallow the same objection to the same assessments over the same years as the applicant. While the hearings were not consolidated, it was agreed that, since the transactions which are disputed have been allegedly carried out in the joint names of the applicant and her husband and since both the applicant and her husband were giving evidence with respect to those transactions and that the results of these matters were dependent upon the credibility of the same witnesses, a decision of the Tribunal in the instant matter would equally apply to the appeals of the husband. The Tribunal had before it the documents filed for the purposes of s.37 (the “T” documents) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”) as well as statements of issues, facts and contentions filed by each of the parties, witness statements and other documents relevant to the proceedings. The Tribunal heard from a number of witnesses, some of whom gave their evidence assisted by an interpreter, including the applicant and her husband, Mr DNH, a friend of Mr T of 30 years, and his wife, TTH, Mr SPN and Mr NQQ, both of whom are married to sisters of Mrs T, and Mrs TT-HH, another sister of Mrs T, on behalf of the applicant, and Mr PM, an officer of the respondent, on behalf of the respondent.



2. There is no dispute other than that the applicant bears the onus of proof, i.e. the applicant must satisfy the Tribunal that the source of the disputed funding can be explained so that the sums in dispute could not be regarded as coming from a source of undeclared taxable income: Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) s.14ZZK(b). The degree of satisfaction is the normal civil standard, namely on the balance of probabilities.

In the respondent’s statement prepared for purposes of s.37(1)(a) of the AAT Act, it is said that “. . . the source and nature of the deposits is a real underlying issue in this case”. The parties’ presentation of their cases reflects this approach. The case is somewhat unusual because normally unexplained deposits are associated with undeclared income arising from the operation of a taxpayer’s business. In the instant case, the income returned by the taxpayers from the operation of their retail butcher shop is not challenged by the respondent. Further, the net profit compared to gross turnover, as set out in the tax returns at T3.1 et seq for each of the relevant years, is well within a range which could ordinarily be expected in the operation of such a business. No alternative source of returnable income is suggested by the respondent, nor evident from the material before the Tribunal. In paragraph 6 of the statement prepared for purposes of s.37 of the AAT Act the respondent states that certain of the disputed transactions “. . . were from the husband and wife money launderers Hinh To Nu and Dang Nguu.” No evidence was led from the respondent on this issue and the quoted statement has not played any part in the Tribunal’s considerations. It is against that background that the Tribunal has considered and reached conclusions with respect to the following facts.

5. The deposits which the respondent claims are unexplained are set out at T5.6 to T5.9 (inclusive) and relate to various accounts jointly in the names of the applicant and her husband at the Commonwealth Bank of Australia, Westpac, the Hong Kong Bank of Australia Ltd, St George Bank, ANZ Bank and the Capital Building Society. 6. Since it is to the telegraphic transfers into Westpac Bank, account 902616 - occurring between 26 May 1988 and 27 June 1990 which totalled $390,000, which is said to be the main source of unexplained deposits - it is to this issue the Tribunal will first address itself. The applicant claimed that these transfers resulted from the sale in Vietnam of gold being an asset of the estate of her late father-in-law. She told the Tribunal that her late father-in-law had, prior to the Communists taking over Vietnam, been a successful businessman and that he had been able to preserve some of his wealth in gold. She said that her husband (the other applicant in these proceedings) was the eldest son and it was understood that the money from his father’s estate would devolve to him for distribution amongst other members of the family. She said that her sister-in-law - TTH - wrote in September 1984, shortly after the death of her father-in-law, saying inter alia that –



“ Brother, with regard to our family, I am no longer willing to do anything and according to Dad’s wish, I will sent [sic] money to you gradually.” (T 9.94, 9.95 - as translated)



It was claimed that Ms TTH arranged for the gold to be sold in Vietnam and the proceeds transferred through Hong Kong to the applicants in Australia. Mr and Mrs T set out in paragraph 13 of their respective statements the dates and the amount of Australian dollars received via the transfers made by Ms TTH. Also in the same letter Ms TTH mentioned a friend of her late father as receiving money from the father which would be repaid. Also in the same letter Ms TTH mentioned a friend and the friend was not named in the letter.



7. Ms TTH lived in Vietnam until coming to Australia in 1995. She is married to Mr T’s long time friend DNH Ms TTH’s evidence confirmed the evidence of the applicant and her husband. She said that she organised for gold in her late father’s estate to be disposed of and the money arising from the disposition to be transferred to Australia via Hong Kong. Paragraph 12 of her statement reads:



“ To fulfil my father’s request and to arrange for his assets to be sent to TTh [her brother] in Australia I had to use unorthodox means and it took me some time to arrange the transaction.”



She said that she arranged for the money to be transmitted from Vietnam to Australia via Hong Kong. Given that her father died in 1984 and that the first transfer did not occur until 1988, there is a considerable lapse of time prior to the first transfer occurring. She said that, since it was illegal to transfer money out of Vietnam, it was first necessary to find someone she trusted to conduct the transactions. She said that this took some time to achieve. She told the Tribunal that, with the assistance of the applicant's sister (Mrs P), she found a woman known only to her as “sister 2” and who was said to be “reliable”. Ms TTH said that she carried out these transactions over a period of time to reduce the risk of losing the money if it was to be remitted as one lump sum. The gold, which was to be disposed of, was buried in the garden of her late father and/or deposited in his house. Ms TTH said she continued to live in the house after her fathers death. She said, at one point in her evidence, that “sister 2” came to her house to collect the gold and on another occasion that she took the gold in a bag to a meeting point with “sister 2” in Saigon. She said that she initially sent a small sum in order to check the reliability and that after her brother confirmed its arrival in Australia, she arranged for other sums to be sent. 8. Ms Davies accepted that Ms TTH had made arrangements for the transfer of the proceeds arising from the sale of her late father’s gold to be sent to Mr T in Australia. However, it was claimed, on behalf of the respondent, that the amounts set out in Mr and Mrs T’s statements and Ms TTH’s statement did not represent the amounts sent. By reference to the Reserve Bank of Australia price listings for gold which reflect the price of gold on the international market (set out in US dollar value), the exchange rate for US dollars into Australian dollars and factoring the difference in weight between a tael and an imperial ounce, Mr PM estimated that, for all transactions occurring on and after 5 October 1988, the transferee would have made a loss. The total loss for the person(s) arranging the transfer amounted on the basis of his calculations to $37,513. The respondent submitted that it would be extraordinary for a person in Vietnam to be undertaking such transactions, said to be illegal and therefore involving a risk, at a loss.



9. It was also suggested that the money transferred was to be divided amongst members of the family as attested by Ms TTH in her statement (exh E, para 10). Mr T told the Tribunal that there was a family dispute and he and his wife had kept all of the money and did not distribute it to any other members of his family. Support for this suggestion is, however, equivocal. In the letter from Ms TTH, of 20 September 1984 (the translation of which is set out at T9.95A), it is stated:



“ This amount is sent to you in accordance with dad’s wish and you have the ultimate right to use it to purchase houses or for the purposes of businesses. It’s totally up to you. You kept it over there so when mum and we arrive, we would have some capital to start our business.”



In her evidence Ms TTH told the Tribunal that it was Mr T’s decision, as the eldest son, to do what he wanted with the money from their late father’s estate. The Tribunal accepts Ms TTH’s evidence that it was Mr T’s decision whether or not to distribute the moneys devolving from their late father’s estate. The Tribunal is not persuaded that those parts of the evidence would suggest that the money is to be distributed amongst members of Mr T’s family are of sufficient weight to displace the view that the Tribunal has taken with respect to this evidence.



10. Mr Flynn submitted that, given Vietnam was a closed community, the price of gold may very well be different from that quoted on international markets and submitted that this may provide an explanation for the disparity of price. It would be speculative for the Tribunal to conclude that the price of gold in Vietnam was not the same price as that reflected on the international markets. It is equally speculative, in the absence of any evidence on this point, for the Tribunal to conclude that the price of gold in Vietnam is the same as that on the international market. Accordingly, the evidence on this point is equivocal. The Tribunal accepts the evidence of Ms TTH that she transferred gold belonging to her late father’s estate into currency which was transmitted from Hong Kong into her brother and sister-in-law’s Westpac Bank account in Australia. While the respondent accepts that evidence in part, it asserts that the sums outlined were not the sums sent by Ms TTH. The respondent can point to no reason why the Tribunal should not accept Ms TTH’s evidence other than a generalised submission that she must be “mistaken” about the amounts transferred. There is nothing in the material before the Tribunal which would suggest that other sums were transmitted by Ms TTH to Mr and Mrs T in Australia which would support the respondent’s assertion. While the respondent does not bear any onus of proof and therefore is not obliged to call evidence where it embarks on such a course then the evidence it calls must be capable of leaving the Tribunal satisfied as to the point(s) which it seeks to establish. In this case because there is no evidence that the price of gold in Vietnam is reflected in the price of gold on the international market the Tribunal is not able to conclude that those acting on behalf of Ms TTH in organising the transfer would necessarily have made a loss as suggested. Given that circumstance, there is no evidence, in the Tribunal's view, which would warrant the Tribunal not accepting Ms TTH’s evidence, which, on this point, corroborates the evidence of Mr and Mrs T. Given that evidence, there is nothing in the material before the Tribunal to suggest that the sums deposited are connected in any way with income returnable in Australia. Accordingly the Tribunal is satisfied that the telegraphic transfers into the applicant’s Westpac Bank, account 092616 between May 1988 and June 1990 are satisfactorily explained.



11. The respondent has also identified a further telegraphic transfer occurring on 16 September 1987 for $50,000 and which was paid into a joint account of the applicant and her husband, held at the ANZ Bank (account 7891-33846). The circumstances surrounding how this deposit came to be made are set out in paragraph 13 of Mrs T’s statement and paragraphs 14 and 15 of Mr T’s statement. The money was said to be a repayment made by a Mr G in Hong Kong of a debt owing to Mr T’s late father. Mr T said that he did not know Mr G and did not know whether the loan had been made to Mr G or whether Mr G was organising the payment for somebody else. In the letter written by Ms TTH to Mr T of 20 September 1984 mention is made of the likelihood of this debt being repaid as follows:



“ Brother, dad has a friend living in Hong Kong. A few months prior to dad’s passing away, dad had contacted him and he promised to dad that he would come to Australia to see you and pay you the debt he had borrowed from dad before the day of liberation. He has not been aware of dad’s death but I strongly believe that he will come to see you and pay it to you. He is quite wealthy now and as it is the money for which he should have felt grateful he will have to return it to you. If he pays you all, approximately 40 trees, get them and write to notify me of this.”



Ms TTH said that the reference to “40 trees” was to be interpreted as a reference to 40 taels of gold. Whatever may be the situation as to the value of gold in Vietnam, it is hard to conceive, having regard to the evidence of Ms TTH, as to the value she received from Sister 2 for taels of gold to equate 40 tael to a value of $A50,000. In the absence of something more substantial, it would be speculative for the Tribunal to conclude that there may have been an interest component in the sum paid or that the person repaying the amount may have included a component in gratitude for the loan originally made. The Tribunal is also troubled by the explanation given by Mr T that he was contacted by a person unknown to him who asked for his bank account details saying that a sum was to be deposited. Mr T made no further inquiry. Mr T said that he was not worried because it was a deposit to be made rather than a payment demanded and that, in those circumstances, he was not concerned to enquire to ascertain further details. The Tribunal finds it difficult to accept that a person would give his/her bank account number to an unknown person on the basis that an undefined sum of money was to be deposited into that account for repayment of a debt without making some further basic enquiries, e.g. was the amount to be repaid in full satisfaction of the debt incurred or only in part satisfaction? While there was some evidence that “honour” played a considerable part in the way in which Vietnamese people conducted financial transactions, the Tribunal finds that that general evidence on this point is not of such weight that it leaves the Tribunal satisfied that the $50,000 transferred into the joint account of the applicant and her husband on 16 September 1987 is satisfactorily explained. The Tribunal has reached this conclusion having considered the transfer was of a capital sum from outside Australia without there being evidence of any apparent connection to it being sourced from income returnable in Australia. However, the Tribunal remains satisfied that, particularly in the absence of any corroborative detail, Mr T’s evidence was unsatisfactory and accordingly the applicant has not discharged the onus of proof with respect to satisfying the Tribunal as to the source of this sum.



12. Mr H, in his statement and confirmed in his evidence, told the Tribunal that he had known the applicant's husband for approximately 30 years and was now married to the applicant's sister-in-law. He said that in 1978 he wished to leave Vietnam because of the then oppressive conditions but did not have the money to secure a passage on a boat. He said that the applicant's father-in-law, because of his friendship with the applicant's husband, lent him 80 tael of gold to secure a place on a boat. While this was a gift from the applicant's father-in-law to Mr H, Mr H said that he wanted to repay the amount and that he paid $45,000 to the applicant's husband in relatively small amounts of cash over a number of years. There is no reason for the Tribunal not to accept Mr H’s evidence. However, there was no attempt to identify any of the amounts said to be repaid by Mr H to any of the unexplained deposits. Mr H arrived in Australia in 1978. He said that he found a job and that it took him a number of years to repay the amounts. There was nothing in the evidence to suggest other than the amounts were repaid at some stage prior to the disputed transactions occurring. Accordingly the Tribunal is satisfied that none of the disputed transactions can be explained satisfactorily by Mr H’s evidence.



13. In her evidence the applicant said that some of the unexplained deposits came from Hui banking arrangements (see in particular exh 8.2). The system of Hui banking amounts to a mutual financing association in which a group of individuals each pledge the same sum of money to be paid at set intervals, in this case monthly intervals. The aggregate total per month is able to be accessed once by each of the contributors who, during the period of the Hui, pays back the total (less his or her share). Within the group, the person who is to receive the total each month is chosen either by ballot or by a system of bidding. It is the responsibility of the organiser of the Hui to ensure that all participants honour their commitment to make monthly payments for the period of Hui. In the period March 1987 to September 1991, Mr and Mrs T were involved in 28 Huis, with monthly contributions from as low as $300 to as high as $5000. It may also have been the case that they were involved in making ongoing contributions to Huis, commenced prior to March 1987 and extending into the relevant tax period, but since the Tribunal has no specific evidence on this point, the Tribunal is not able to take it into account. The applicant claims that amounts from the Huis were used to acquire the equity in the purchases of the land made and in some cases meet outgoings in relation to those purchases. It is plain from the calculations contained in Schedule E of the respondent's submission that the Hui contributions extremely high in some years (e.g. 1989, $254,200 - 1990, $312,700 - 1991, $273,000). However, the highest deficient, i.e. the amount paid by way of contribution by the applicant and her husband in excess of lump sums received in any one of the relevant years was only $25,700 (for 1990). In those circumstances, the Tribunal accepts that the years in which expenditure exceeded return did not show exceptional deficit. The applicants means were, having regard to their returned income, well able to cope with the Hui outgoings. In the summary of unspecified deposits (T5.6) the respondents states it has “allocated 50% of unexplained cash” to be accounted for as the result of the applicants participation in Hui banking. The calculation was applied for the years 1988, 1991 and 1992 but not for 1989 and 1990. The calculation was presumably based on the fact that in the latter years the applicants expended in contributions more than they received. In the Tribunal’s view the correct approach to take is to apply the surplus for those years in which the applicants have achieved a surplus over contributions, and for those sums to be deducted from the total of the unexplained deposits for the year in question, i.e. $20,200 for the 1988 year, $3000 for the 1991 year and $25,000 for the 1992 year.

14. The credibility of the applicant and her husband was adversely affected by virtue of the evidence they gave in relation to the so-called “Mondimex” transaction in their attempt to explain the source of a deposit $27,547 to their Commonwealth Bank of Australia in 1990. Mr T told the Tribunal that he had been involved in partnership with two other people in a grocery business in Richmond. In paragraph 18 of his statement he says:



“ I was involved in a business known as Mondimex in partnership with a number of other people in the 1980”s. This business was unsuccessful, and was ultimately sold at a loss. I took out a personal loan to meet my share of the shortfall. The interest rate on this loan was very high. In about May 1990, my wife and I sold our house in Springvale and we used the money to repay this loan. On 23 May 1990 I made a payment of $27,547 to finalise the loan.”



Mrs T provided a similar explanation in paragraph 17 of her first statement. Despite both saying that the payment to finalise the loan was made on 23 May 1990, it is not disputed by either that the sale of the house in Springvale (being Westall Road and described in exhibit 1 as “Clayton South”) occurred in May 1991, i.e. approximately one year later so that there can be no correlation between the two events. When asked about the disparity, Mrs T said that she did not know that she would need to consult with her husband. When Mr T was asked, he said that he was unsure of the source, since the deposit was made by his wife. In his summing up Mr Flynn conceded that the deposit presented a difficulty to the applicant and that the explanations given were not satisfactory. There was no attempt to produce a deposit slip on behalf of the applicant nor any other attempt to trace the source of the money. The Tribunal is, accordingly, not satisfied on the evidence presented that there is any sufficient explanation as to the source of the $27,547 deposit to the Commonwealth Bank account.



15. In paragraph 31 of her second statement, Mrs T says that a deposit to Capital Building Society account 7321805, made on 20 July 1987, for $20,684 arose at the time she and her husband purchased the property at Buckingham Avenue, Springvale. She said that she needed to borrow approximately $75,000 in order to complete the purchase of the property and that to do this she approached the Hong Kong Bank which was able to provide her with a favourable rate of interest. She said that the minimum sum which she could borrow from that Bank was $100,000 and that accordingly there was money left from the purchase of the property and it was this that was the source of the $20,684. However, it was agreed that the details set out in exhibit 1 concerning the properties were correct. That shows that Buckingham Avenue, Springvale, was mortgaged to the Hong Kong Bank on 9 September 1987, i.e. well after the deposit to the Capital Building Society was made. The Tribunal accepts that it is unlikely the bank would release the money without having the security in place. Accordingly, the Tribunal remains unsatisfied as to the source of the deposit of the $20,684.



16. There are number of cash deposits made into the applicants’ Westpac Bank, account 601839 occurring on 11, 12 and 13 April 1989 which together total $320,000. The applicant claimed that the $320,000 was the balance of the cash component paid following the deposit of $80,000 to complete the amount to be provided by the parties to allow the purchase of shop B, Springvale Road, Springvale, to proceed (the balance being made up by a $400,000 mortgage). At T9.48 there is a handwritten note (the author of which is unknown) identifying some at least of the payments as “Marrickville Dep” (presumably referring to the purchase of the property at Illawarra Road, Marrickville). Since the Marrickville property was purchased in 1986 this reference appears to be incorrect. In her statement (exh A2) the applicant attributes the deposits as being part of the cash contributions made by her sisters and their husbands to the purchase of shop B, Springvale Road. Mrs TT-HH, who lives in Sydney, said that she made three trips to Melbourne, on each occasion bringing approximately $22,000 in cash with a further payment of $21,000 on 11 April and $24,500 on 12 April. Mrs TT-HH said the money came from a withdrawal made from her bank savings account. Mr NQQ said that he arranged for $81,000 to be deposited to the applicants’ Westpac account in April 1989. He was able to produce evidence of withdrawing two amounts, one for $71,000 and one for $10,000, to confirm the transfers as occurring. Mr SPN confirmed that he and his wife paid the applicant and her husband $80,000 in April 1989.



17. The applicant claimed that the $90,000 deposit made on 11 April 1989 constituted the contribution to be made by her and her husband to the shop B, Springvale Road, and was sourced from Hui payments ($39,000 on 25 March 1989 and $45,000 on 5 April 1989 with possible further earlier payments of Hui’s otherwise due on 23 April 1989 ($12,000) and 5 May 1989 ($45,000)). A handwritten note at T9.48 identifies the $90,000 as coming from the Westpac Bank account into which the overseas transfers arranged by Ms TTH had been deposited. The latter would accord with two payments transferred from Hong Kong, totalling $90,000 in February 1989 at the instigation of Ms TTH and seems the more likely explanation and, despite there being no attempt to confirm that the $90,000 came from the Westpac Bank account, the Tribunal accepts this as being the source. Despite some apparently slight variations in the amounts deposited in the sense that not all of the sums were exactly equal from each of the participating couples, the total of $320,000, the short period in which the deposits occurred, the date occurring at the time of the purchase of shop B, Springvale Road, the confirmatory evidence given by Mrs TT-HH, Mr NQQ and Mr SPN leave the Tribunal satisfied that the source of deposits was as stated and the fact that they are consistent with the dates surrounding the purchase of shop B, Springvale Road, and that accordingly the deposits are satisfactorily explained.



18. It was further said that the deposits made into Westpac Bank, account 601839 for the period of 1 May 1989 to 6 February 1990 were provided by the parties other than the applicant and her husband involved in the purchase of shop B, Springvale Road, in order to meet a shortfall between rent received for the property and the interest charged by the Bank. There was oral evidence given to the Tribunal from the applicant's brothers-in-law and sister that loans were made to the applicant and her husband to meet interest and expense shortfall relating to the purchase of shop B, Springvale Road. Despite the lack of any paper confirmatory details, which perhaps could be traced through bank records, but having regard to the fact that 1989/1990 years were a period of high interest rates and of the oral evidence given by the applicant's brothers-in-law and sister, the Tribunal accepts this as a reasonable explanation.



19. There are number of deposits made in 1987-1988, 1990-1992 to Hong Kong Bank, account 002-019560-001. In her statement - exhibit A2 - the applicant says that she is unable to recall the source of all of the deposits. Her statement reveals that she cannot be certain about any one of the deposits and her evidence with respect to them is speculative. In paragraph 20 she even attributes three deposits totalling $21,200, made on 5 July, to a Hui arrangement, “Although my records do not show any Hui which was payable at that time . . .”. The applicant states that she believed that a deposit of $30,000 made on 12 June 1990 was to meet exchange losses that she and her husband incurred from the borrowing of Hong Kong dollars and that the deposit arose from a Hui of $45,000 received on 24 May 1990. The applicant makes no attempt to confirm any exchange loss or to further source the money from a Hui deposit. Deposits of $9800 and $13,000, both occurring on 2 September 1991, were identified by the applicant in her evidence to the Tribunal as being part of the proceeds from a Hui receipt of $75,000, received on 1 September 1991. Since this was a Hui the applicant organised (see paras 4, 5 of her statement, exh A2) given the proximity of the deposit to the Hui, the Tribunal accepts the source of this deposit as being from the Hui. The deposits occurring in January 1992 of $9800 and $6942 are claimed to be part of the proceeds from a deposit of $21,000 paid in December 1991 in relation to the sale of the Buckingham Street property. Again, no attempt has been made by the applicant to source the funds - which would normally be expected to be paid by way of bank cheque, but rather the applicant has sought to rely on a belief. In circumstances where it could be expected that there is more certain evidence available than memory and where the applicant bears the onus of proof, the Tribunal could be expected to be provided with some more concrete evidence. In the absence of any concrete evidence with respect to any of the deposits made into the Hong Kong Bank account and the explanations which the Tribunal regards as vague with respect to those deposits, the Tribunal remains unsatisfied as to the explanations provided, except to the extent where otherwise indicated.



20. With respect to Commonwealth Savings Bank account numbers 2199-00238217 and 2285-5000581, the applicant claims that these are accounts of her sister TNQ. There are three deposits, the first consisting of two deposits each of $9500 made on 13 December 1991 to account 2199-00238217, with a further deposit of $9500 to account 2285-5000581 on 16 December 1991. Mrs NQQ was not called to give evidence but her husband in his statement (exh D, para 5) describes money being lent by the applicant to his wife and the accounts into which they were deposited as being “ours” (i.e. Mr and Mrs NQQ’s). A copy of savings investment account 2285-5000581 (p.5 of attachment to exh D) shows two amounts each of $9500 being deposited on 16 December 1991 and the bank account in the name of “Mrs TNQ” (Mrs NQQ’s maiden name) at the Erskineville Branch of the Commonwealth Savings Bank of Australia. The entry on the second page (also marked 5) is unclear but a handwritten note albeit by an unidentified person indicates two deposits of $9500 occurring on 13 December 1991. Mr NQQ and Mrs NQQ live in Earlwood, New South Wales. Earlwood and Marrickville are both inner western Sydney suburbs. Given the applicant and her husband live in Melbourne, it would seem more likely than not that there has been a mistake made with respect to identifying in whose name the bank accounts exist. In view of the confirmation contained in Mr NQQ’s evidence to the Tribunal, the Tribunal is satisfied that all three disputed deposits are satisfactorily explained.



21. There are disputed deposits for three accounts with the Capital Building Society. In her statement (exh A2) at paragraph 29 the applicant is able to recall why the deposits were made (to meet ongoing interest payments on a loan) but is unable to recall the source of the deposits. No attempt has been made to trace any possible sources of this money and the Tribunal is, accordingly, not satisfied as to the explanation given. A further sum of $45,000 was deposited into the Building Society account 7322109 on 25 November 1987, in relation to which the applicant believed the source as being a transfer from ANZ account occurring on 16 September 1987. She claimed that the money was transferred in order to derive a higher rate of interest. There has been no attempt to produce any records from the ANZ account in order to confirm the withdrawal, nor any other material which would confirm the applicant’s belief. In the absence of that material the Tribunal is not satisfied to the explanation given by the applicant. Finally, there is a deposit of $20,684 made to account 7321805, which issue has been addressed in paragraph 15 of this Decision.



22. There is one further matter in relation to which the Tribunal should draw attention. In her evidence to the Tribunal the applicant admitted she had taken money from the till of the butcher’s business to cover the family’s living expenses and this had not been declared as income. She initially said that this was in the vicinity of $200 to $300 a week but later amended this to $100 to $200, saying that the family’s living expenses were kept to a minimum. Mr T confirmed in his evidence that living expenses were taken from the till. He said that the family worked and living expenditure was minimal. The Tribunal notes in a record of interview with an officer of the respondent, the applicant fixed the figure for living expenses at $100 per week. The Tribunal notes that the applicant and her husband have two children. It is plain that the sums so taken should be included in the taxable income of the applicant and her husband. Doing the best the Tribunal can on the figures presented by the applicant and bearing in mind that she bears the burden of proof, the Tribunal is satisfied that the sum of $200 per week should be included as part of the taxable income of the applicant and her husband (being $100 per week each) for each of the years under review.



23. The decision under review is varied as follows:



(a) The adjustment to the applicant's income tax returns for the years 1988-1992 (inclusive) are varied as follows:



(i) the deposits to the applicant's Westpac Bank account 902616 for the years 1988-1990, totalling $390,000, are set aside and a nil figure substituted;



(ii) the adjustment to the applicant's returns relating to Hui banking system to be set aside and the following adjustments by way of deduction to be substituted:



1988 - $20,200

1991 - $3000

1992 - $25,000



and for the other relevant years a nil deduction to be substituted;



(iii) the adjustments relating to unexplained deposits in Westpac Bank account 601839 to be set aside and a nil figure substituted;



(iv) the adjustments relating to Commonwealth Bank accounts 2199-00238217 and 2285-5000581 to be set aside and a nil figure substituted;



(v) the adjustments to the Hong Kong Bank account 002-019560-001 for the sums of $9800 and $13,000 made on 2 September 1991 be set aside and a nil figure substituted;



(vi) the sum of $5200 per annum to be included in the adjustments for the applicant's share of daily living expenses taken from the undeclared profits of the operation of the applicant's business; and



(vii) the applicant's share of the capital profit arising from the sale of the Buckingham Avenue property to be calculated and included in the applicant's returnable income.



(b) The Tribunal is unable to make an exact calculation but, for the years in which the adjusted income exceeds the income returned by the applicants in their tax returns, then the penalties and interest to be applied as per the original decision.



(c) The same variations as set out in paragraphs (a) and (b) above to be applied to adjustments made to the tax returns for the same years for Mr T.



(d) Otherwise the decisions under review are affirmed.
