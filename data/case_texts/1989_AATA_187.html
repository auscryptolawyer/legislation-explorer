ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Partnership formed for gold mining activities -third party paid to conduct mining - chain of financiers linked to partners - third party paid partner "procuration fee" -partnership loss - whether partnership engaged in deriving income from gold mining - sham.

REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. This Tribunal is asked to review a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation in which the Commissioner disallowed an objection made by a taxpayer in respect of his assessment of income tax by the respondent for the income tax year ended 30 June 1980. The notice of assessment was issued on 4 May 1981 and it states that the taxpayer's taxable income is $96,301. The adjustment sheet shows that the taxable income as returned was $36,239.
  2. The Commissioner increased the taxable income to the larger of the two sums referred to above by disallowing a claim for a tax deduction made by the taxpayer. The claimed deduction was said to represent the taxpayer's share in the loss incurred by a partnership known as Q. This partnership was a limited partnership established under the provisions of the Mercantile Act 1867 (Qld). A question which is raised in this case is whether a fee paid by the partnership to a company known as R Pty Ltd was paid to enable R Pty Ltd, as the partnership’s agent, to carry on gold mining operations on a lease numbered XXXX over the last three days of the financial year ending 30 June 1980. Part; of the respondent's case is that the fee was in reality a device to provide persons connected with R Pty Ltd with a source of funds for their own purposes. It was submitted that this was not an expenditure by R Pty Ltd in respect of which the taxpayer could claim an allowable deduction.
    1. For the taxpayer it was argued that the loss suffered b the partnership was deductible on a number of grounds. First, as the partnership of Q was engaged in a gold mining venture any income produced through this activity would be exempt income pursuant to section 23(o) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, (Cth) (hereafter referred to as the "ITAA"). consequently, any loss incurred in the year of income in carrying on the exempt business was deductible under sub-section 77(1) of the ITAA. It was submitted that if the business carried on was not a business of that sort, then the loss claimed by the partnership was deductible under section 51(1) of the Act. Finally, it was submitted that the loss was really an outgoing of a capital nature and was therefore deductible under section 122DB of the Act. (This was not relied upon by the applicant's counsel in his closing submissions in support he deductibility of the expenditure).
    2. The Commissioner contended that the arrangement between the partnership and R Pty Ltd was a sham. The provision of the fee was part of an elaborate scheme which provided funds for certain individuals to carry out mineral exploration and which purported to confer on investors in the scheme the right to claim a tax deduction. The Commissioner's submission was that even if the arrangement pursuant to which the fee was paid was not a sham no deduction was allowable under either section 77(1) or section 51(1) of the ITAA as no business of any sort was being carried on on behalf of the partnership in the relevant year of income. In addition the Commissioner relied upon the provisions of section 260 of the ITAA. Finally, and formally, the Commissioner raised the doctrine of fiscal nullity.

5. The circumstances which led to the situation in which the partnership loss was said to have been incurred are quite involved so I will set them out in some detail. Much of the background evidence was not contested and much comes from the documents which were tendered to the Tribunal.

The Taxpayer

6. The taxpayer is a welder by occupation. In the year in question the taxpayer also had earnt considerable income from share trading. The taxpayer at that time had an accountant acting for him. The taxpayer's accountant arranged for the taxpayer to meet A of s Pty Ltd. The taxpayer in evidence said that he discussed a project with A. According to the taxpayer's evidence A told him that the project had some land which was probably good for gold mining. The taxpayer was told by A that if the taxpayer put some money into this project the taxpayer would eventually get some benefit out of it as it was a new mine. The taxpayer's evidence was that he understood the benefit to be a tax benefit. The taxpayer paid $9120 to enter into the project. This payment was made by cheque. The taxpayer considered, and I use his words, that "gold mines that I have been in their maiden dividend mostly is tax free."

7. At the time the taxpayer had spoken to his accountant and A, the taxpayer had earnings for the year of about $15,000 from his work as a welder and some $81,000 from share trading. The taxpayer was asked this question in cross-examination:

"And you claimed a loss from this partnership of $60,000? —— Well there again I would not know anything about that. That had been arranged between my tax accountant and "A" I presume."

8. The taxpayer said that his accountant said that the taxpayer could see a friend of the accountant (A):

"and we can help you put down some of this, or get rid of some of this tax---Yes?---- by putting you into a gold, or into a whatever it was that these people do, a project where you could save either on films or in this case a gold mine." (see transcript at p. 173)

9. The taxpayer said that he understood that what was suggested to him by A was a way in which the taxpayer might reduce tax to which he might otherwise be liable in the particular year. The taxpayer candidly agreed that what he thought he was doing was paying some money to the project to help reduce the tax problem which, his accountant told him, he, the taxpayer, had in 1980. I accept this evidence of the taxpayer. I accept generally the evidence of the taxpayer as to how he came to participate in the project that I shall describe below.

Formation of Limited Partnership - Q

  1. The taxpayer became a member of a partnership known as Q - which was a limited partnership established under the Mercantile Act (supra). A partnership established pursuant to this legislation possesses some unusual features. For example, the Act provides for a division between general partners and special partners. General partners have the total management of the business vested in them and so have the unlimited liability of a normal partner in an ordinary partnership. The function of special partners in such a partnership is to contribute capital for the business. They take no part in the management of the business. The liability of the special partners is limited to the extent of the capital that each contributes. A limited partnership may only exist for a maximum of seven years. The Q partnership was the means by which the taxpayer was to became involved in gold mining.
  2. Q came into existence in the following way. On 27 June 1980, B, a solicitor who had just prior to this time been employed in a firm of solicitors, one of the principals of which was C who played a major role in the facts considered by the Tribunal in this case, travelled from Melbourne to Brisbane to attend a meeting which gave birth to the partnership. B went to that meeting armed with a power of attorney (Ex. 33) from, inter alia, the taxpayer in these proceedings which empowered B to perform various acts. B also held powers of attorney from other people who had been recruited to join the partnership. B, though no longer employed in the firm of solicitors, came to be at the Brisbane meeting because, he said, other partnerships had been "done" by the firm whilst he was an employee there and since he had been involved in those other partnerships it was convenient that he continue his involvement with the formation of the Q partnership.

12. Various resolutions were passed by the meeting, the principal ones being that:

  1. those present, together with those represented by their attorney, enter into a limited partnership to be registered under the Mercantile Act to carry on the business of mining and extracting minerals, including gold, in any part of Australia,

b) the partnership be carried on under the name of Q,

  1. the draft limited partnership agreement tabled at the meeting be executed, (Ex. 35), and
  1. it was also resolved that the partnership enter into an agency agreement, a licence agreement and a loan agreement, these agreements having been tabled at the meeting in draft form.
  1. I accept the evidence of B that he acted under a power of attorney for the taxpayer and others at the meeting held in Brisbane on 27 June 1980. I accept the evidence concerning the passage of the resolutions set out above.
  2. There is no dispute, and I accept, that on 27 June 1980 Q was established as a limited partnership under the Mercantile Act (supra) (see Ex. 2) and that the taxpayer was a special partner in the limited partnership.

The Agency Agreement

  1. The profit and loss statement in the partnership's tax return for the income year in question refers to a "fee paid as per contract". That fee was said to have been paid pursuant to a contract with the company called R Pty Ltd. Pursuant to that contact R Pty Ltd was engaged by the partnership to carry on the mining of gold on Lease XXXX at Coolgardie in Western Australia. The fee payable was a yearly fee payable in advance.
  2. One of the resolutions passed at the partnership meeting on 27 June 1980 was that the partnership enter into a licence agreement with a company called T Pty Ltd. T Pty Ltd held the mining rights over Lease XXXX. One of the directors of T Pty Ltd was D who was also a solicitor in C's firm at this time. His evidence was, and I accept this evidence, that T Pty Ltd entered into a licence agreement with U Holdings Pty Ltd (U Holdings). The licence agreement (Ex 3) says in clause 1 that the grantor (T Pty Ltd) confers upon the grantee (U Holdings in its capacity as general partner for the partnership of Q) authority to enter any part of the land referred to in the Second Schedule to this agreement (namely Lease XXXX) to "search for and prove, dig, win, work and carry away for its own use all such substances referred to in the Third Schedule hereto" (being gold and gold bearing ore). Clause 2 provided that these rights were conditional upon the partnership forthwith commencing and continuing to do the things specified in clause 1. If the partnership entered into an agreement in the form set out in the Fourth Schedule to the Licence Agreement and referred to as the Agency Agreement, then clause 2 would be deemed to have been satisfied.

17. I accept C's evidence that the Agency Agreement was entered into on 30 June 1980. The agreement is part of Ex. 3. The agreement was executed on Norfolk Island by two persons who possessed powers of attorney on behalf of U Holdings, the general partner. The agreement is one between R Pty Ltd and U Holdings as the agent of the partnership. Clause 1 describes R Pty Ltd as the agent of the partnership. By clause 2 R Pty Ltd agreed to, inter alia, search for and do other relevant things and carry away for the benefit of the partnership all gold and gold ore found. By clause 3(a) the partnership agreed to pay R Pty Ltd a fee set out in the Fourth Schedule, namely $3,250,000. By clause 3{b) this fee had to be paid by 30 June of each year.

Background to formation of Partnership

18. In 1979 C, a solicitor, decided to become involved in gold mining activities through his company, R Pty Ltd. He made contact with persons who had gold mining leases in Western Australia, near Coolgardie. C entered into a course of dealing with these persons who were represented by a company called V Pty. Ltd. V Pty Ltd, was at this time anxious to use C's company, R Pty Ltd, to mine their leases. As a result of this R Pty Ltd agreed to mine a number of leases held by V Pty Ltd.

  1. C gave evidence to the effect that by May of 1980 gold production had not reached the levels hoped for. The re-commissioning of a larger plant was seen as the way to increase production levels. The larger plant and the smaller plant then said to be in operation both operated on a gravitational process, ore would be fed into the top of a hopper and then crushed. The crushed ore would then travel down a sluice and the gold ore, being heavier than the ordinary dirt, would sink to the bottom of the sluice from where it would be collected. The difference between the two plants was that the larger one could process greater quantities of ore. It was agreed V Pty Ltd would allocate a new lease and R Pty Ltd would enter into another joint venture in relation to that lease. I accept the evidence of C that relates to the circumstances leading up to the creation of the Q partnership. I accept C's evidence that the sum of $200,000 was required in order to re-commission the larger plant and I note particularly his comment at p. 68 of the transcript where he agrees that in order to obtain the $200,000 a partnership was required to be established. That partnership came to be known as the Q Partnership to which I have already referred. This was the partnership the taxpayer came to invest in.
  2. D who, it will be remembered, was a solicitor in C's firm, was a director of T Pty Ltd. His evidence was that a re-structuring of the entire mining venture occurred, the re-structuring commenced in October 1980 and was completed in May 1981. The purpose of the re-structuring, according to D was to obviate the necessity for the Q partners to have to contribute further capital to the partnership in the second year of its operation. D said it had become apparent by October 1980 that the lease would not be as productive as anticipated and that this was the reason for the re-structuring. This evidence was not challenged. I accept the evidence of D to which I have referred. D also gave evidence about the mechanics of the re-structuring, however, for the purposes of this decision it is unnecessary to consider this evidence any further.

21. D said that he had dealings with a number of those people who entered into a series of limited partnerships, of which Q was only one.

A Joint Venture

22. I accept D's evidence that on 27 June 1980 an agreement was entered into between V Pty Ltd and T Pty Ltd. (Ex. According to the terms of the agreement V Pty. Ltd and T Pty Ltd agreed to explore and mine for gold ore on leases held by V Pty Ltd. This agreement was referred to as the joint venture. D said, and I accept this evidence also, that the joint venture entered into an agreement with R Pty Ltd by which R Pty Ltd was made the agent of the joint venture and it had the responsibility of carrying out the mining. R Pty Ltd, under its agreement with T Pty Ltd and V Pty Ltd, was obliged to spend a certain amount of money in operating the mine. R Pty Ltd, in order to find this money to satisfy its obligations to the joint venturers, entered into agreements with a number of limited partnerships one of which was Q and agreed with various limited partnerships to operate leases held by the limited partnerships in return for an annual fee.

  1. The joint venture agreement, (Exhibit 4), executed on 27 June 1960 provided, inter alia, for a committee of management to oversee mining operations and as far as T Pty Ltd was concerned C and D represented T Pty Ltd on the joint venture management committee.
  2. I accept that on 30 June 1980 T Pty Ltd entered an agreement with V Pty Ltd and R Pty Ltd. (Ex. 5) I do accept D's evidence in that regard. C executed the agreement on behalf of R Pty Ltd. By clause 2(b) of that agreement R Pty Ltd agreed to, inter alia, conduct mining on Lease XXXX. R Pty Ltd agreed that it would act on behalf of other persons in relation to its activities and account for that part of nett profits other than those owing to V Pty Ltd to such persons as it saw fit.
  3. Clause 4(a) of the agreement between V Pty Ltd, T Pty Ltd and R Pty Ltd (Exhibit 5) imposed an obligation on R Pty Ltd in the following terms:

"Within seven days of the date hereof R Pty Ltd shall pay to Messrs. Sly & Russell, Solicitors, 68 Pitt Street, Sydney, the sum set out in part five of the Schedule hereto to be held by Messrs. Sly and Russell as stakeholder to be applied towards the expenditure for which R Pty Ltd is liable pursuant to this agreement AND IT IS HEREBY AGREED AND ACKNOWLEDGED that the whole of the said sum set out in part five of the Schedule hereto is to be applied towards the said expenditure for which R Pty Ltd is liable as aforesaid and that no part of the said sum shall be refundable to R Pty Ltd under any circumstances whatsoever."

26. D said, and I accept his evidence on this point that the amount of money set aside for mining activities on Lease XXXX was limited to the amount of money R Pty Ltd was obliged to spend pursuant to its agreement with T Pty Ltd. I take this reference to an agreement with T Pty Ltd to be a reference to clause 4(a) of the agreement between V Pty Ltd, T Pty Ltd and R Pty Ltd. (Exhibit 5)

27. Part Five of the schedule to Exhibit 5 provided that the sum should be $200,000. By clause 5(a) this sum had to be spent within two years. By clause 6(a) R Pty Ltd agreed to carry on mining as agent for V Pty Ltd and for such other persons as it shall from time to time see fit.

Fee paid by Q - $3,250,000

28. Pursuant to clause 3(a) of Ex. 16, the Agency Agreement, made between U Holdings as general partner and R Pty Ltd, U Holdings agreed to pay the fee set out in Part A of the For Schedule, however, Part A of the Fourth Schedule is not attached to this exhibit, but exhibit 3, in part a copy of the Agency Agreement, says the fee is $3,250,000. That sum was not in dispute before me. So far as the taxpayer is concerned, Ex. 17, identified by C, is a receipt recording that $9,120 was received from the taxpayer, this figure representing the taxpayer's capital contribution to Q. The money was deposited into a trust account held by C for Q.

29. Exhibit 19 is a copy of an entry in C's trust account for Q. On 30 June 1980 a deposit of $95,280 was paid into the trust account in favour of Q. This figure, C said, represented an advance made by S Pty Ltd on behalf of some partners whose cheques had not cleared by the time the partnership was to have commenced operations. Another entry of 30 June 1960 shows that a company called W Pty Ltd deposited $2,762,500 in favour of Q and then there was a payment out of the account to R Pty Ltd in the sum of $3.25m. This was paid out on 30 June 1980. I accept C's evidence concerning the effect of the transactions set out in Exhibit 19.

30. It was submitted by Mr. Shaw QC, who appeared for the Commissioner, that there was no provision contained in Ex. 16, the agency agreement, which obliged R Pty Ltd to spend the $3,250,000 received from the partnership on gold mining activities on Lease XXXX. D's evidence was that R Pty Ltd only had to spend $200,000 and this obligation was imposed by the joint venture agreement. Mr. Shaw QC submitted that if one assumed that a business was being carried on at all, and the Commissioner did not agree that this was the case, then the most that Q could claim as a deduction was $200,000. Mr. Shaw submitted that all those involved in these arrangements knew that the $3.25m, less $200,000, was going to be paid solely to S Pty Ltd as a "procurement fee". The Commissioner's principal submission was that the agreement between R Pty Ltd and Q was a sham.

Provision of Funds - W Pty Ltd

31. I must now set out in still more detail the financing arrangements whereby W Pty Ltd provided $2.7m towards the fee Q paid to R Pty Ltd.

32. In his evidence C said it was proposed that Q take a loan from W Pty Ltd and that this loan would represent 85% of the fee payable to R Pty Ltd. W Pty Ltd in turn received money from X Finance, a Norfolk Island company, through a Leverage Loan Agreement.

Procuration Fee

33. At the same time as Q agreed to borrow from W Pty Ltd, Pty Ltd, according to the evidence before me, entered into a "procuration agreement", dated 27 June 1980, (Ex. 23) with S Pty Ltd. Under this Agreement S Pty Ltd agreed to procure people to enter into an agreement with the mining company, R Pty Ltd. By clause 2 of the procuration agreement it was provided that in consideration of S Pty Ltd procuring these people R Pty Ltd would pay to S Pty Ltd a commission on all fees paid by those who entered into the partnership. The figure in the procuration agreement (Ex. 23) states the commission to be 3% of the fees received, but C said in evidence that this was an error, it was actually 93%. He said R Pty Ltd did pay a procuration fee to S Pty Ltd and it was the total figure R Pty Ltd received from Q less $200,000. This should be $3,050,000. C did agree with Mr. Shaw that he could not point to evidence of a documentary nature to support his oral evidence that the fee was paid.

34. At this time S Pty Ltd entered an agreement with X Finance which was executed on 27 June 1960 and styled "Contract to Procure Loan" (Ex. 24). C referred to both this agreement and the attached leverage loan agreement. By clause 3 X Finance agreed to lend a sum set out in the First Schedule to W Pty Ltd upon the terms and conditions set out in the Second Schedule to exhibit 24 which was entitled "Leverage Loan Agreement". By clause 3 of the Leverage Loan Agreement X Finance agreed to lend W Pty Ltd the "principal sum" namely $2,762,500 on 30 June 1980. (see the First Schedule to the Leverage loan Agreement) By clause 5 W Pty Ltd agreed to pay interest at the rate set out in the Fifth Schedule (namely 11%).

  1. Under clause 5 as well, W Pty Ltd agreed to pay X Finance, by way of a premium, an amount equal to all royalty payments received by it under clause 11 of a Loan Agreement which was said to be attached. C, in his evidence, said that he understood the reference to the loan agreement to be a reference to a loan agreement entered into between W Pty Ltd and the partners.
  2. Clause 4 of Ex. 24, the "Contract To Procure Loan", states that upon X Finance making the loan to W Pty Ltd (under the attached Leverage Loan Agreement) S Pty Ltd would pay to X Finance an amount by way of a procuration fee and it would purchase all of X Finance's rights under the leverage loan agreement. C said the procuration fee which S Pty Ltd was to pay plus the price for the purchase of X Finance's rights would be $2,000 greater than the sum of money lent by X Finance to W Pty Ltd. He believed that S Pty Ltd paid the money. Again no documents were put into evidence in this regard.
  3. By clause 5 of the contract between S Pty Ltd and X Finance (Ex. 24) it was agreed that when S Pty Ltd made all the payments referred to in clause 4, X Finance would, when requested, transfer all its rights under the Leverage Loan Agreement to 5 Pty Ltd or its nominee. X Finance did eventually assign to Y Corp, a Norfolk Island company according to C's evidence.
  4. Extensive cross-examination of C concerning his understanding of the financial arrangments of the scheme to place but before considering the matters arising out of that I should refer back to the taxpayer. The applicant's tax return for the year ending 30 June 1980 (Ex. 37) shows that he earnt $15,650 from his employment as a welder and $81,213 from share trading activities. He claimed as a deduction $60,062, this being his share as partner in the loss for that income year claimed by the partnership.

39. The evidence of the taxpayer left no doubt in my mind that he knew very little about what arrangements had been made for the provision of funds for the mining activities both because he did not bother to enquire and also because he was not told a great deal by those who organised the scheme. Notwithstanding all of this it seems to me that he is bound by what has occurred in that he is a partner and he is bound by the actions of the general partner, U Holdings, and also by the actions of the partnership's agent, R Pty Ltd, this company in reality being under the control of C. Indeed Mr. Shaw QC submitted that there was very little that the taxpayer could do in relation to the partnership as he was a special partner, under section 54 of the Mercantile Act (supra) the management of a limited partnership is carried on by the general partner. The taxpayer signed his tax return for the income year ending 30 June 1980 in which he claimed a partnership loss of $60,062. I am of the view that the taxpayer was desirous of obtaining a tax deduction in order to reduce his taxable income and not greatly concerned with the manner in which the organisers of the scheme went about obtaining that tax deduction for him. In Davies v. FCT 89 ATC 4377 Hill J. at 4,409 raised the question of whether it is necessary for the operation of section 260 that the taxpayer know of or participate in the scheme. He said (at 4,409):

"In many cases it may well be critical that a taxpayer either alone or through an agent participate in the scheme as a whole albeit that it will quite often be the case that a taxpayer is not conscious of each and every step in the scheme. There is, in the language of sec. 260, no requirement that a taxpayer necessarily be a party to the scheme provided that it is correct to characterise the scheme as a whole as one having a purpose or effect of avoiding the tax of the taxpayer."

40. Hill J. said that it was not necessary to resolve the question in the case before him. In the case before me the taxpayer placed his affairs in the hands of his accountant and A, knowing generally that he was entering into a gold mining scheme and that this may have the effect of reducing his taxable income. His reliance on the actions of U Holdings and R Pty Ltd as well as the other matters to which I have referred in the preceding paragraphs of this decision lead me to the conclusion that the taxpayer is sufficiently a party to the arrangement sought to be impugned for the purposes of section 260.

The Evidence of C

41. Having now set out the arrangements for the operation of the scheme from the point of view of its establishment and from the point of view of financing it would be instructive to refer to some of the questions and answers from the cross—examination of C. C gave these answers to questions put by Mr. Shaw QC.

"Well R Pty Ltd's obligations to the partnership did not involve spending any particular amount of money at all? No, that is correct. No precise amount.

.......Well then R Pty Ltd received $3,250,000 from the partnership, did it not? Yes.

if you had the documents, they would show that R Pty Ltd paid of the moneys it received $3,050,000 to S Pty Ltd.....Yes.

None of that money was ever spent on mining, was it? No.

Well does R Pty Ltd have any records showing how the $3,050,000 was spent, otherwise than to show that it went to S Pty Ltd? No.

Now that sum of $200,000, or whatever it was, as I understood you to say, the reason for the setting up on this particular partnership was to fund renovation ox the large plant....? That was an important objective

....

... is there some record showing how much the recommissioning of the large plant cost? I do not have a record. I understand it to be about $75,000 that was actually expended on - directly on that plant."

42. D was unaware of the manner in which the $200,000 supposedly paid to Sly and Russell was spent. A comment like this from a member of the committee of management on whose behalf R Pty Ltd was required to spend the money causes suspicion to be aroused as to whether any of the $200,000 was spent at all in the manner alleged by C in his evidence.

  1. Cross-examination established that some money was spent in respect of a number of leases. The records, if they were available, C said, would certainly indicate that some money was spent in work on Lease XXXX. C said he was advised by phone that work had commenced on Lease XXXX and that by 30 June 1980 material from this lease was being fed through the smaller plant. I shall come back to this at a later point.
  2. Exhibit 28 is a list of the agreements entered into between T Pty Ltd and V Pty Ltd and between T Pty Ltd, V Pty Ltd and R Pty Ltd and the payments that were to be made under these agreements. The first partnership agreement was entered into at the end of June 1979 and the document purports to show that later a whole series of agreements were entered into with different partnerships from November 1979 to June 1980. C agreed that by June 1980 none of these agreements had resulted in the production of anything other than small amounts of gold.
  3. C agreed (at p. 130) that when the Q Partnership was formed there was no prospect of a substantial amount of gold being mined before 30 June 1980. He said that prior to this time it had been hoped that quite a substantial amount of mining might be carried out as the small plant had been operating since January 1980. Exhibit 29, a bundle of income tax returns for R Pty Ltd, contains a Profit and Loss Statement for the year ending 30 June 1980. In that statement an entry appears in the following terms "Mining costs - Coolgardie Venture 755,000". C's evidence was that this sum had been spent over the entire range of leases held by the partnerships set out in Exhibit 28 and not just on Lease XXXX.
  4. Mr. Shaw tendered as evidence Ex's 28, 29 and 30, the latter being copies of income tax returns for the partnerships listed in Exhibit 28, in order to demonstrate that the financial arrangements under which Q operated were almost identical to those under which a number of other gold mining partnerships operated. The documents showed, and C agreed with this, that ea partnership took a loan from W Pty Ltd in order to pay the fee required to be paid to R Pty Ltd. The amount of the loan in each case represented 85% of the fee that had to be paid to R Pty Ltd. Again R Pty Ltd had to pay a procuration fee to S Pty Ltd which was the total of all the fees paid to it by the partnerships less certain smaller sums of money. These smaller amounts can be found listed in Ex. 28. The profit and loss statement ending 30 June 1980 for R Pty Ltd shows total fees received as being $26,250,000 and procuration fees paid as being $25,296,100. (see Ex. 29).
  5. The following answers were given by C to Hr. Shaw QC (at 134-5):

"Well in your belief the reason why the partnerships were structured in the way they were with the loans payable in the manner they were, was to enable a claim to be made by the partnership for a partnership loss for taxation purposes, so that the individual partners could claim losses which stood as to their contributions 85 per cent and 15 per cent; that is right, is it not? ---- My objective was to encourage them to enter the partnership.

Yes, and your belief was that unless such a taxation loss was available to them they would not enter into the partnership? I think that is right."

  1. C said, and I accept this evidence, that in order for the scheme to be self-funding, that is to not require further capital contributions in the second or later years of operation, the partnership would have to produce enough gold to generate an income of $1.1 to $1.2m by 30 June 1961. Any positive return to the partners was remote bearing in mind the fact that the loan to T Pty Ltd had to be paid off within five years and that the partnership was only to last for five years. C would not agree that the chance of a profit was remote if the larger plant had been able to be brought into operation as hoped for. Mr. Shaw referred the Tribunal to p. 11 of Ex. 31, a prospectus published on 11 November 1983, in which this larger plant is referred to. C agreed that as at 1983 the plant had still not been made operational.
  2. C signed the prospectus as a director of the company concerned. Oh page 3 of the prospectus there is a statement that between June 1979 and Hay 1981 the company entered into a series of joint venture agreements with T Pty Ltd pursuant to which, inter alia, T Pty Ltd paid a total of $1,010,000 to V Pty Ltd's solicitors, to be held in trust for the joint ventures and to be expended on behalf of V Pty Ltd and T Pty Ltd in the exploration and development of the relevant mining tenements. C agreed that some of the money spent in this process came from contributions made by the partnerships when they paid their fee to R Pty Ltd. The figure of $1,010,000 is in fact the sum of the figures appearing in Ex. 28 in the column entitled "Payment Project". In the case of Q which is listed as G.8 in Ex. 28 the figure of $200,000 appears which was the sum remaining after R Pty Ltd paid its procurement fee to S Pty Ltd out of the fee it received from the Q partners. $200,000 was also the figure R Pty Ltd was required to pay to Sly and Russell to satisfy its obligations as agent to the joint venturers.

50. On page 5 of Ex. 31 there is a statement that the object of the issue is to raise funds to conduct further exploration and testing. At page 7 of Ex. 31 there appears this statement:

"The Directors believe that the exploration and testing programmes that have been carried out---- have identified and brought to an advanced state a very worthwhile exploration prospect and one which well warrants the further exploration and testing expenditure recommended by "

C agreed that the principal continuing advantage from the series of partnerships in fact had been derived by S Pty Ltd. This was a company which C described as being in the habit of responding to his requests.

51. C claimed mining work was being carried on on Lease XXXX. He was told of this in a telephone call. C provided to the Tribunal three telegrams, (Ex. 38). They are all dated subsequent to 30 June 1980 and the first, received in Sydney on 14 July 1980, says: "Production 2nd July to 10 July inclusive ...... 912 tons from XXXX...." However, the statements in Ex. 31 that I have quoted speak of exploration work only.

52. The evidence of C was unsatisfactory in many respects. As I have already indicated I have accepted some of his evidence. I am not prepared to accept his evidence generally unless it is supported by other reliable evidence or where the evidence is not contentious. Whenever C's assertions concerning different aspects of the scheme were challenged by Mr. Shaw QC I formed the view that C became very evasive. He was reluctant to answer some questions. So far as C's evidence concerning the work which he said took place on Lease XXXX was concerned I do not accept that evidence. Various photographs (Exhibit 11) of plant and apparent mining sites were placed in evidence. I am not satisfied that they depict any relevant activity on Lease XXXX during the income year in question. Various reports of persons said to be mining experts were produced by C. None of the reports assist me, even if I accept them at face value, to conclude that relevant activity occurred on Lease XXXX. I do accept C's comment, at page 130 of the transcript, where he said there was no hope of a substantial amount of gold being produced on Lease XXXX before 30 June, 1980. I note also his evidence that only small amounts of gold were produced from other leases which had been worked from November 1979 (transcript at p 130).

Shan

53. One of the principal attacks on the deduction claimed in this case is that the arrangements giving rise to the claimed deduction are a sham. It would be helpful to define what is meant by the term. No better definition can be found than that found in the well known passage by Diplock LJ in Snook v. London and West Riding Investments Ltd [1967] 2 QB 786 at 802 where His Lordship stated that:

" ... if it has any meaning in law, it means acts done or documents executed by the parties to the "sham" which are intended by them to give to third parties or to the court the appearance of creating between the parties; legal rights and obligations different from the actual legal rights and obligations (if any) which the parties intend to create...... all the parties thereto must have a common intention that the acts or documents are not to create the legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating."

54. Again considerable assistance in identifying the concept in question can be gained by what has been said in Australian cases by Windeyer J. and Lockhart J. In Scott v. Commissioner of Taxation (1966) 40 ALJR 265 Windeyer J. said at 278-9:

"I shall assume that a fund set up in the manner and for the purpose that the deed in this case apparently contemplates could be a superannuation fund for the benefit of employees. I make that assumption, notwithstanding that the extent to which an employee would benefit could, it seems, depend very much upon the wishes, perhaps on the whims, of the employer. But it is not enough to say that a fund governed by the provisions of a deed such as that we have here could be a superannuation fund within the meaning of the Act. For it to be so in fact the parties concerned must have intended that the deed should take effect and operate according to its tenor; that a fund should be set up subjected to the trusts of the deed; and that Associated Provident Funds should as trustee be bound to carry out those trusts. On the other hand, if the scheme including the deed, was intended to be a mere facade behind which activities which were not to be really directed to the stated purposes but to other ends, then the words of the deed should be disregarded. It was urged for the appellant Associated Provident Funds that it is a real company and that the deed was really executed by it; and that, it was said, is the end of the question. But it is not. A disguise is a real thing; it may be an elaborate and a carefully prepared thing; but it is nevertheless a disguise. The difficult and debatable philisophic questions of the meaning and relationship of reality, substance and form are for the purposes of our law generally resolved by asking did the parties who entered the ostensible transaction mean it to be in truth their transaction, or did they mean it to be, and in fact use it as, merely a disguise, a facade, a sham, a false front - all these words have been metaphorically used - concealing their real transaction..."

55. In Sharrment Pty Ltd v. official Trustee in Bankruptcy [1988] FCA 179; (1988) 82 ALR 530 at 537 Lockhart J. said that:

"A "sham" is therefore, for the purposes of Australian law, something that is intended to be mistaken for something else or that is not really what it purports to be. It is a spurious imitation, a counterfeit, a disguise or a false front. It is not genuine or true, but something made in imitation of something else or made to appear to be something which it is not. It is something which is false or deceptive."

  1. In the present case the Commissioner does not dispute that the sum of $3,250,000 was paid by Q to R Pty Ltd. The evidence to support the payment is to be found in Ex. 19, an extract from the trust accounts of C's firm. There was an account in the name of Q and Ex. 19 shows a payment from the account to R Pty Ltd on 30 June 1980 to the extent of $3,250,000. One may perhaps be excused for thinking that a payment of this magnitude could be evidenced by bank statements, however, the respondent did not question the making of the payment so I am prepared to find that $3,250,000 was paid by the partnership to R Pty Ltd. At this point I might also add that I have found that the taxpayer contributed $9,120 to the partnership, (see Ex.17).
  2. Mr Davies, the taxpayer's counsel, submitted that the agency agreement was not a sham. He said R Pty Ltd and U Holdings intended to reflect the terms of the agreement between R Pty Ltd and the partnership. Mr. Davies said the whole scheme involved three groups all at arms length with each other namely the partners, S Pty Ltd and V Pty Ltd. He said the only prospect for profit for V Pty Ltd came from the terms of the joint venture agreement which, he said, conferred on V Pty Ltd the right to fifty percent of the nett profits from R Pty Ltd's gold mining operations. The partners were at arms length from V Pty Ltd. S Pty Ltd was at arms length from R Pty Ltd. However, R Pty Ltd, on; my view of the evidence, played an important part in the channelling of funds through to S Pty Ltd.

58. I find that the partnership, Q, entered into an arrangement with the company, R Pty Ltd, pursuant to which a sum of money was paid over by the partnership to the company ostensibly for the purpose of R Pty Ltd conducting gold mining operations on behalf of the partnership. In reality, however, I find that the payment of the fee by the partnership was a device to provide the promoters of the scheme with funds to spend on exploration activities over a number of leases. I find that the purpose behind the establishment of the partnership was to provide the promoters of the scheme with a source of funds from which they could draw for the purposes of other gold mining operations in which they had an interest. C thought that about $75,000 was spent upgrading the larger plant though there is no documentary evidence to support this claim. In the absence or that I am skeptical about the size of the sum actually spent on the plant, if indeed, anything was spent at all. I accept that at least some of the capital contributions of the partners in the Q partnership were used by V Pty Ltd and T Pty Ltd, along with the capital contributions of members of other partnerships, in the exploration work that was carried out over a number of leases between June 1979 and Hay 1981, in acquiring mining tenements and in the acquisition and recommissioning of plant and equipment. In fact C agreed with this assessment, (see transcript at p 145).

  1. I am satisfied that the fee of $3,250,000 paid to R Pty Ltd was never intended to be spent on mining operations on Lease XXXX during the income year in question. A reading of the agency agreement (Ex. 16) shows that clause 3 required the general partner, on behalf of the partnership, to pay a fee of $3,250,000 to R Pty Ltd. However, no provision of the agency agreement required R Pty Ltd to spend any sum of money on Lease XXXX. This stands in contrast to the agreement made between the joint venture and R Pty Ltd wherein R Pty Ltd was obliged to spend certain amounts of money.(see Ex. 5 clause 4(a)).
  2. So one may wonder what R Pty Ltd did with this $3,250,000. C said in evidence that if he had the relevant papers before him he could show that R Pty Ltd paid $3,050,000 to S Pty Ltd. Be agreed that none of this money was spent on mining operations, though the payment to an expert for his report may have been made from this sum, however, he had no records to show that this in fact happened and anyway this payment, if it was made, would have been a payment on account of S Pty Ltd. The remaining $200,000 was paid to Sly and Russell to satisfy R Pty Ltd's obligations to the joint venture. (see Ex. 5, c1 4(a)).
  3. As I have indicated I do not accept the evidence that there was material being fed through the smaller plant from Lease XXXX by 30 June 1980.
  1. In regard to the question of sham or not I have had regard to the manner in which the partnership was financed. In order to fund the payment of the fee to R Pty Ltd the partnership took a loan from W Pty Ltd. Before W Pty Ltd could lend to the partnership it had to borrow money itself. So pursuant to Ex. 24 which was an agreement between S Pty Ltd and X Finance the latter agreed to lend W Pty Ltd $2.762m, the same sum w Pty Ltd lent to the partnership. X Finance lent this sum to w Pty Ltd pursuant to the attached Leverage Loan Agreement. Clause 4 of Ex. 24 said that S Pty Ltd would procure all of X Finance's rights under the Leverage Loan Agreement. S Pty Ltd acquired X Finance's rights under the agreement for a sum $2,000 greater than to total of the loan, that is, for $2,764,500.
  2. C agreed (transcript at p 111) that the difference between the $3,050,000 received from R Pty Ltd and the $2.764m it paid to X Finance remained with S Pty Ltd as gross receipts. Mr. Davies, in arguing that the agreement and the payment of the fee were not sham transactions pointed out that S Pty Ltd, which he described as a party independent of the partners and V Pty Ltd, intended the arrangements to be honoured as S Pty Ltd was owed about $2,760,000 by the partnership.
  3. However, C's evidence referred to above demonstrates the true extent of S Pty Ltd's interest in the scheme. So on 30 June 1980 X Finance lent W Pty Ltd $2,762,500, W Pty Ltd lent Q $2,762,500, Q paid R Pty Ltd $3,250,000, R Pty Ltd paid S Pty Ltd $3,050,000 and then S Pty Ltd paid X Finance $2,764,500. C recalled in his evidence that his office company, z Pty Ltd, in turn financed X Finance. I am satisfied that a company, whatever its name, associated with C fared well out of these arrangements in that the flow of money described above started with one his companies and ended with a company associated with him.
  4. I consider that the arrangements which have been described above are surrounded by a number of matters which heighten suspicion as to the bona fide nature of the arrangement. For example, many of the agreements admitted into evidence are undated or unsigned, there is only the evidence of C to support the claim that they were executed.
  5. A close reading of some of the agreements reveal unusual discrepencies between one agreement and another. For example, Ex. 3, the licence agreement between T Pty Ltd and U Holdings is to run for a period of five years and three months whereas under the agency agreement (Ex. 16) R Pty Ltd is appointed agent for five years. The commission payable by R Pty Ltd to S is said to be 3% of total fees received by R Pty Ltd according to the attachment to Ex. 23. However, C tells us that this was a mistake, the commission was actually 93%. Yet Ex. 23 appears to be duly signed and dated despite a most fundamental and obvious error. Perhaps the existence of the error did not matter in the least in so far as the real arrangements were concerned.
  6. I find on the balance of probabilities that a scheme was set up to provide funds enough to carry out the necessary exploration for those interested in having that done, these funds being purchased by a scheme which manufactured tax losses. I find that the agreement between the partnership, into which the taxpayer entered, and R Pty Ltd was a scheme designed to provide a cloak for a sham payment under it. This payment was not made to enable relevant activity to take place on Lease XXXX in the last days of June 1980. In reality it was paid for the purpose or purposes which I have set out above. I am not satisfied that the taxpayer has made out the deduction claimed.

Deductions

68. The respondent's arguments in relation to sections 77 and 51 are closely related to the considerations which in this case are relevant to the finding of sham described above.

Section 77(1)

69. Section 77(1) of the ITAA provides:

"Where a loss is incurred in in the year of income by a taxpayer in carrying on an exempt business in Australi that loss is an allowable deduction."

70. Mr. Shaw Q.C. submitted that the Tribunal must consider two questions, was an exempt business being carried on and was the loss incurred in carrying on that business.

71. Mr. Davies submitted that a business was being carried on. The following were factors, he said, which indicated the carrying on of a business: (a) the partnership engaged a company to carry on mining, (b) the company so engaged had access to a supply of water which is important for the processing of ore, (c) the smaller plant was in operation, (d) it was hoped that eventually the company would have access to a larger plant, and; (e) the company was obliged to spend $200,000 on the partnership's lease.

  1. None of these factors, either of themselves or considered together, persuade me to take the view that a gold mining business was being carried on.
  2. In relation to the first question, it must be shown that Q was carrying on a gold mining business in the year of income that is on or before 30 June 1980.
  3. Mr. Davies referred to a passage in the judgement of the High Court in Ronpibon Tin No Liability v. FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 57:

"In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income."

75. Mr. Davies said it was not relevant whether one defined the activities on Lease XXXX as exploration or mining as the former led to the latter and was a necessary first step in carrying on a gold mining business. Mr. Davies' submission that a preliminary expense may be deductable if it represents an action that is in the end productive of assessable income has no merit in the circumstances of this case. I am not satisfied that the money paid to R Pty Ltd was paid for the purposes even of exploration on Lease XXXX, let alone to enable a gold mining business to be carried on. I am not satisfied that the occasion for the expenditure in question is to be found in regard to things productive of assessable income in regard to activities on Lease XXXX. The evidence, to which I have already referred, shows that no more than $200,000 of the $3,250,000 was spent on exploratory activity and it was spent over a whole series of leases located in Coolgardie and it was spent over a number of years. I am not satisfied that any money was spent in regard to Lease XXXX or at all in the last days of June 1980, that is the year of income in question.

76. Any business could only have been carried on from the 27 June 1980. The point of time at which the partnership was established and the period of time during which it was said to be carrying on business amount to four days. I am not satisfied that a business was being conducted over the 27th, 28th, 29th and 30th of June 1989. As I have said the evidence of C, the mining reports, the photographs and telegrams and the other matters referred to by Mr. Davies do not establish on the balance or probabilities that any business was being carried on. It must also appear that a sum of money, here it is claimed to be $3,250,000, was an expenditure incurred in the carrying on of the business. I am not satisfied on the evidence before me that the sum of $3,250,000 was expended in the carrying on of any business. I am satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the expenditure came about to enable a tax loss to be incurred and not made in carrying on any business. I am not satisfied that any lesser sum was expended in the carrying on of any business during the relevant period on behalf of the partnership.

  1. The arrangements in question here possess an air of commercial unreality. The arrangements include a set of six similar transactions none of them returning in reality anything at all. When the procuration fees paid by R Pty Ltd are considered along with the matters disclosed by prospectus (exhibit 31) and the arrangements which have been found to exist between V Pty Ltd and T Pty Ltd I am satisfied that the expenditure of $3,250,000 was not incurred by the partnership in carrying on any relevant business.
  2. The taxpayer has failed to satisfy me that any loss falls to be deductible under Section 77 of the ITAA.

Section 51

79. Section 51(1) of the ITAA provides:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

80. Mr. Davies submitted that the business of mining commenced when R Pty Ltd incurred its obligation to perform mining operations, that is, on 30 June 1980. (See submission at p.201 of transcript). Mr. Davies said the partners could only claim the tax deduction under section 51(1) if a business was being carried on. I have already set out Mr. Davies submissions in regard to the question of whether a business was being carried on.

  1. Mr. Davies also made submissions in relation to the applicability of the second limb of section 51(1). However, I find it unnecessary to refer to these submissions. I have concluded that no mining activity, and no business, was carried on on Lease XXXX on the 27th, 28th, 29th and 30th of June 1980. As I have said, I find myself unable to accept the evidence relied upon by the taxpayer in this regard. The taxpayer bear the onus of satisfying me that some sort of relevant activity was carried on in respect of Lease XXXX over the last days of the financial year ending June 1980 and he has failed to discharge that onus. Any loss or expenditure incurred by Q in the income year in question was not gained in producing assessable income or incurred in carrying on a business. I am not satisfied that a mining business or any other business was being carried on on behalf of Q in the income year in question.
  2. I am of the view that the outgoing in question is not deductible under section 51(1) of the ITAA. I am of the view that the taxpayer's share in the partnership loss should not be regarded as the cost of a step taken in the process of gaining or producing the partnership's assessable income; cf John v. Commissioner of Taxation [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 83 ALR 606. The taxpayer's share of the partnership loss is not a deduction allowable to the taxpayer as a member of the partnership.

Section 260

  1. Section 260 of the ITAA was relied upon by the respondent. If I am wrong in the conclusions I have reached above as to sham and whether or not any business was being carried on I should, and do, go on to set out my findings in regard to Section 260 and its application in this case on an assumption for that purpose that the documents in this case and the arrangement which I have held to be a sham do have the meaning and effect which is contended for by the taxpayer. Section 260 was in operation during the tax year in question. In relation to schemes entered into from and after 27 May 1981, Part IVA of the ITAA applies (sub section 260(2) (effective from 24 June 1981).
  2. Section 260 of the ITAA at the relevant time provided as follows:

"260 Every contract, agreement or arrangement entered into, orally or in writing, whether before or after the commencement of this Act, shall so far as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly -

(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;

(b) relieving any person from liability to pay any income tax or make any return;

(c) defeating, evading or avoiding any duty or liability imposed on any person by this Act; or

(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any respect, be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, or in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but without prejudice to such validity as it may have in any other respect or for any other purpose".
  1. Mr. Davies referred to a passage from the judgement of Lockhart J. in FCT v. Janmor Nominees Pty. Ltd. 87 ATC 4813 at 4824 where his Honour referred to opinions to the effect that the application of s.260 was of some difficulty in a case where a deduction was otherwise properly allowable under s.51 of the ITAA. Of course, Mr. Davies had earlier submitted that a deduction was properly allowable under s.51(1).

86. The words of Menzies J. in Franklin's Self Serve Pty. Ltd. v. FCT 70 ATC 4079 at 4092 are worth repeating here.

"...I do not regard Cecil Bros. Pty. Ltd. v. FCT [1964] HCA 82; (1962-4) 111 CLR 430 as deciding that the operation of s.260 cannot destroy, as against the commissioner, the leases upon which a tax payer claims a deduction ....: here the taxpayer and the other parties to the agreement, after hard bargaining, re-opened a long since concluded transaction to arrange for the avoidance of the operation of a tax subsequently imposed. The elaborate arrangement here made to re-open a past transaction and re-organise the shareholding in the taxpayer and those party thereto seems to me to fall pecisely within the words and intent of s.260."

87. In approaching the application of s.260 I must bear in mind what Gibbs C.J. said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55 {at 65):

"Section 260 creates many difficulties of interpretation, but some matters may now be taken as settled. The section does not refer to the motives of the taxpayer or other person who entered into the arrangement which it is sought to impugn; the purpose on effect of the arrangement must be ascertained from the terms of the arrangement itself and by the overt acts by which it was carried into effect. Not every arrangement which results in a saving of tax will be struck down by the section."

88. His Honour (at 66) in referring to the test expounded by Lord Denning in Newton v. Federal Comissioner of Taxation [1937] HCA 37; (1958) 58 CLR 1 AT 8, described it as a practical test that was useful and often applied but it does not provide a guide to the decision of every case. The test referred to is the well known one that you must be able to predicate by looking at the overt acts by which the arrangement was implemented that it was implemented in that particular way to avoid tax - if you cannot so predicate but the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax, then the arrangement is not within s.260.

89. Gibbs C.J. went on to say (at 66):

"A line of decisions illustrates that if the Act offers to the taxpayer a choice of alternative tax consequences, either of which he is free to choose, or offers certain tax benefits to taxpayers who adopt a particular course of conduct, the choice of the advantageous alternative or the adoption of the beneficial course does not mean that s.260 is attracted."

90. Hill J. in Davis and Anor. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4377 has identified propositions which are now clearly established in relation to the interpretation of s.260. These include the following (see Hill J. at 4400 and 4401):

  1. It is necessary to identify the contract, agreement or arrangement which the commissioner seeks to render absolutely void under the section;
  2. the contract, agreement or arrangement must be at the very least bilateral;
  3. where the section refers to purpose it is not concerned with the subjective motivation of a particular taxpayer. The purpose of which the section speaks is the purpose of the contract, agreement or arrangement and not the purpose of some person;
  4. the tax avoidance purpose (which is a reference to the purpose that falls within one of the four alphabetical paragraphs of s.260) need not be the sole purpose nor indeed, even the principal purpose, it need only be a purpose at least so long as it was not merely an incidental or an unessential purpose;
  5. section 260 directs attention alternatively to the purpose and the effect of the arrangement;
  6. the section is an annihilating one only and does not permit the commissioner to reconstruct a new and fictitious set of facts creating a liability for a taxpayer.

91. In this case the agreements such as the partnership agreement, the agency agreement and the licence agreement all happened in reality simultaneously. I find that the purpose of the agency agreement was to set up a claim for a deduction. As C conceded, much of the money went around in a circle. Much of the money was not destined for use in mining operations of any sort. indeed so far as the overall arrangement is concerned which involves the partnership agreement and then the further agreements and action taken which are said to give rise to the claim for the deduction sought in this case leads me to conclude that at least a purpose of the arrangement was to avoid tax and that this purpose was not an incidental nor was it an unessential purpose of the arrangement.

92. As a result the arrangement is annihilated so far as the Commissioner is concerned. So far as the agency agreement i concerned that agreement is void as against the Commissioner. In the result the taxpayer is liable to be assessed without a deduction being allowed in respect of the amount claimed as being expended by the partnership in the production of gold or another business expense. I find that by looking at the overt acts by which the arrangement was implemented the Tribunal is able to predicate that the purpose of the arrangement as implemented in that way, was to avoid tax. The transactions in question here are not capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing. This is not a case where, after the arrangement is struck down so far as the Commissioner is concerned, the taxpayer can point to any section of the Act which allows the deduction claimed by him.

Fiscal nullity

93. Finally, the effect of the doctrine of fiscal nullity was relied upon. Following the decision in John's case [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 83 ALR 606) that doctrine does not apply in Australia to cases of this sort and I do not consider that matter any further.

Conclusion

  1. I am of the view that the applicant has not discharged the onus which rests upon a taxpayer by virtue of s190(b) of showing on the balance of probabilities that the assessment in question was wrong.
  2. Accordingly the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
