ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECSISION

INCOME TAX - Overseas Student Charge - whether allowable deduction as self-education expenses - interior design student - earning income as assistant designer - decision under review affirmed.

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

27 June 1991 Mrs J. R. Dwyer, Senior Member

  1. This is an application to review a decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Melbourne, disallowing an objection to a taxation assessment in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1989. The applicant was represented by Mr Petty of Combined Student's Legal Service and the respondent by a departmental advocate. The taxpayer in her taxation return showed a total income of $10,714.00, being principally the result of earnings from employment as a design assistant. During the relevant income year the taxpayer was studying for a degree course in interior design. She had come to Australia to undertake that course. As she was not a citizen or resident of Australia she was required to pay a charge under the Overseas Student Charge Act 1979 in respect of her enrolment in that course.
  2. In her taxation return the taxpayer requested a ruling as to whether the overseas student charge of $3,753.00 was an allowable deduction, as an expense of self-education, under s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The Commissioner decided that the amount was not deductible.

3. Sub-section 51(1) of the Act reads as follows:

“ 51. (1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

4. The statement lodged pursuant to s. 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 contained the findings of fact which provided the basis for the disallowance of the taxpayer's claim. Paragraph 4 reads as follows:

"The expense incurred by the taxpayer is not incurred in the production of assessable income but is incurred in her need to study and to remain in Australia, it is a payment that provides a visa to remain in Australia. This is not a once and for all payment but is a yearly charge payable by March of the calendar year that study is being commenced or continued. Other requirements are that the person must be in fulltime education or be undertaking the maximum course-load of the education, they may also work up to 20 hours per week."

5. The parties prepared a statement of agreed facts and the respondent also presented to the Tribunal a written statement of argument. The statement of agreed facts contained the following paragraphs:

"The applicant has attended . . . as a full time student for the following years commencing March 1987, 1988 and 1989 and as a part time student in 1990.

As a pre-requisite to enrolment to study, the applicant has been required to pay to the Commonwealth Department of Employment Education and Training an Overseas Student Charge which entitles the applicant for a twelve month period to:

  1. Reside In Australia on a Student Visa.
  2. Study the Course at . . ..
  3. To be employed up to 20 hours per week in employment.
  4. Exemption from the Higher Education Contribution Scheme."

6. The respondent's first submission was that the Overseas Student Charge was "not a payment for education, or self education, but a payment for a right for the applicant to enter and reside in Australia for a specific period and to receive a higher education whilst in Australia.

7． This argument required that the Tribunal consider the provisions of Overseas Students Charge Act 1979, and the Overseas Student Charge Collection Act 1979. The respondent's representative suggested that as a preliminary matter I refer to the Minister's second reading speech in Hansard "to give an education of what the actual student charge is intended to do". I refused to do so unless, after looking at the words of the relevant statutory provisions, I found that there was some difficulty about interpreting and applying those provisions. The Federal Court has recently referred to the limited circumstances in which such extrinsic materials are to be used in Secretary, Department of Social Security v Clear. (unreported, Heeney J, 27 March 1991) and Secretary Department of Social Security v Sword (unreported. Gray J, 22 April 1991). They may be used where there is some ambiguity or difficulty about interpreting or applying the words of an Act. They may not be used as preliminary reading to give the flavour of the object of the Act, so that its express words will be read with that object in mind.

8. Section 5 of the Overseas Students Charge Act 1979 reads as follows:

"Where an overseas student is enrolled in a prescribed course for a year, then, subject to this Act, a charge is imposed on the student in respect of that enrolment. (emphasis added)

The Overseas Students Charge Collection Act 1979 provides in s.4(1) :

4. (1) Subject to section 7, the charge in respect of the enrolment of an overseas student in a prescribed course for a vear is due for payment by the student-fa) where the enrolment commences on a day in that year - on that day, or on 15 March in that year, whichever is the later; or

(b) where the enrolment commences on a day before that year - on 15 March in that year." (emphasis added)

The respondent's representative referred the Tribunal also to s.6(1) which reads as follows:

6. (1) Notwithstanding anything in the Migration Act 1958, an officer shall not grant under that Act a temporary entry permit to a person who proposes to undertake or continue a prescribed course in a year in which the permit is to be in force unless the officer is satisfied that -

(a) there has been paid to the Commonwealth, in accordance with the regulations, an amount equal to the amount of the charge (if any) to which the person is or will be liable in respect of his enrolment in that course for that year;

(b) any liability of the person to the charge in respect of his enrolment in that course for that year will be discharged under sub-section 7(1) or an arrangement has been entered into under sub-section 7(2) in relation to that charge; or

(c) the Commonwealth has accepted the responsibility for the payment of any charge in respect of his enrolment in that course for that year (emphasis added)
  1. The respondent's representative submitted that the charge is a payment for a visa rather than a self education expense because s.6 of the Overseas Students Charge Collection Act provides that a temporary entry permit shall not be granted, unless the charge due under the Overseas Students Charge Act has been paid, or arrangements have been made for it to be paid. The submission depends on a very superficial reading of s.6(1) in that paragraphs (a), (b) and (c) each refer specifically to the charge as a charge in respect of enrolment in a course. Even if this were not so, I am satisfied that the character of the charge is better gleaned from the Act imposing the charge, than from the Act providing for its collection. Section 5 of the Overseas Students Charge Act establishes, to my satisfaction, that the charge is a charge paid in respect of enrolment in a course by an overseas student and not a charge paid to obtain a visa. This is confirmed by s.6(1) of the Overseas Students Charge Act. I therefore reject the respondent's first submission and find that the sum of $3,753.00 was an expense of self-education.
  2. The Act does not however provide that expenses of self-education are deductible as such. Section 51(1) allows deduction of outgoings only "to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income". Section 82A(1) does not provide any additional allowable deduction for self education expenses. It only applies to self-education expenses which are deductible under s.51. It provides that the first $250 of such expenses of self-education are not allowable as a deduction.
  3. The taxpayer herself did not give evidence. In support of the argument that her self-education expenses, including the Overseas Student Charge, were deductible under s.51(1) of the Act, her representative called evidence from an employee of the company where the taxpayer earned the major part of her income as a design assistant.
  4. The witness said that the taxpayer used the draughting skills that she was learning in her course, in performing her duties for her employer. She said that draughting cannot be learnt without formal tuition, such as was given to the taxpayer in her course. The witness said that it would be almost impossible to gain employment in the area of design, without having done a course of one sort or another. She said that the taxpayer had good design flair and had learnt draughting skills in her course. Her work was good and so she was invited to stay on as a designer on completion of her course. She did not do so as she moved to Sydney for personal reasons.
  5. The witness said that she was not herself involved with the hiring of the taxpayer, but she understood that the taxpayer had phoned the design director a number of times asking for part-time work. She said the design director spoke to the taxpayer's teachers and was sufficiently impressed by what they told him to engage her to help the firm at busy times on a part-time basis. The witness said that she doubted if the company would have employed the taxpayer, if she had not been doing a relevant course, because there were so many applicants for such positions.

14. I asked the witness what would have happened about the taxpayer's employment if she had decided not to go on with her course, but wished to retain her part-time employment. The witness replied:

"I do not know whether that would have worried　him,　because she was good at organising things. She　was　liaising with the client the whole thing　and　designing the house for the client. I mean,they　were - they were quite happy with the job that　she　performed, and they were both very impressed by　her　organisation skills, liaising with the client,　and　just sitting down and doing her draughting work when　required. So, I doubt whether that would have　made much difference."

15. I asked the respondent's representative what his attitude would be to the deductibility of the applicant's expenses of enrolment in her course, if she had been a local rather than an overseas student. He replied:

"If she was a local student, there are a number of courses that can be taken. If she pays the - I think in regard to certain courses you can pay a full fee-paying amount which is tax-deductible. There is a reduced amount which is approximately half of that amount which is not deductible, which becomes the higher education contribution scheme which is known as the HECS.

MRS DWYER: Why would it be tax deductible?

I am not - there is - I think most of that is probably related to post-graduate courses, but they are full fee-paying courses. I am not exactly sure in regard to this, but there are courses that are full fee-paying courses that are tax deductible."

16. I asked the respondent's representative to state the principle, which he submitted should be applied, to decide whether or not a local design student's tuition fees would be allowed as a deduction from income earned as a design assistant, he suggested that it was hypothetical. When he was pressed for an answer he replied:

"Well, with the Commissioner's position at the moment, I mean everything is from virtually 1986 to 1987, whatever the taxpayer wants to put in their return they may claim under self assessment. If it is a question, well, they put in a 169A request, otherwise it is left to audit. Wow, it may have been that if the taxpayer had been audited, if she had been a resident in Australia, the expenses may have been disallowed."

17. When I suggested that cases should be decided on principles rather than on the fluke as to whether or not a return had been audited, it was the applicant's representative who finally correctly analysed the situation. He said:

"The answer to your question may well lie in the hurdle that both local students and overseas students must leap, and that is to create this real nexus between the payment of the fee, whether it be an overseas student charge or fee relating to any study. There still must be the nexus established between the payment of that fee and the incurring (sic) of the assessable income, and I suggest that this is how individual cases will either float or sink as to whether the nexus has been established, which is a separate issue for the tribunal to decide today."

18. Unfortunately the cases do not provide any clear guidelines as to what education expenses are to be accepted as having the necessary nexus to be regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income. Before Federal Commission of Taxation v Hatchett (1971) 71 ATC 4184, where a person embarked on a course, with a view to equipping him or herself to earn income at some future date, such expenditure was regarded as capital and therefore not accepted as deductible. However, Menzies J in Hatchett's case said, at p.4186:

"It seems to me that it would be wrong to consider any of the expenditure here under consideration as an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature. An outlay is of that character when it is expended to obtain what can properly be described as capital in the economic sense. . . . In the field of taxation, as in the field of business, 'capital' is used in contrast with 'revenue'; it has no reference to a man's body, mind, or capacity. Section 51 does recognise that there will be outgoings incurred in gaining or producing income, or in carrying on a business to do so, that are of capital or of a capital nature. Expenditure of this sort is commonly made. Thus, for instance, a taxpayer who is a baker, who buys a van to deliver bread to his customers, makes such an outlay,"

His Honour concluded:

"The moneys expended by the taxpayer cannot, I am satisfied, be denied the quality of deductibility because they are 'outgoings of capital' or 'outgoings ... of a capital...nature' ."

  1. Mr Hatchett was a teacher who had incurred outgoings in obtaining a Teacher's Higher certificate, which enabled him to be classified at a higher level, and hence to be paid additional income. His Honour was satisfied that outgoings necessary to obtain that certificate should be regarded as outgoings incurred in gaining assessable income. He said that it followed from the fact that the outgoings were incurred in gaining assessable income, that the expenditure was not of a private nature.
  2. His Honour however dealt differently with fees for University subjects which the taxpayer had studied in the Faculty of Education. He accepted that the University fees were paid with the encouragement of the Department of Education, as shown by the fact that it contributed towards them. He did not however accept that those fees were incurred in gaining assessable income. He said at p.4186:

"The payment of university fees, however, seems to me to fall into a different category. Any relationship between any assessable income of the taxpayer and the payment of university fees is problematical and remote. Having regard to the taxpayer's lack of success in passing university examinations it is not possible to find affirmatively that there exists any connection between the payment of university fees in 1967 and the earning of assessable income at any time in the future. The prospects of the taxpayer obtaining a university degree leading to his promotion to positions in the service for which a university degree is prerequisite affords no ground for concluding that the Commissioner was in error in refusing to allow the fees paid as deductions. If these fees are deductible it must be on a simpler footing, namely that expenditure upon university study, which the department encourages teachers to undertake, is, without more, incurred in gaining assessable income as a teacher."

He continued at p.4187:

"Enlightened employers often encourage employees to improve their bodies and their minds, and assist them to do so. Such encouragement is not, of itself, enough to warrant the deduction of outgoings for these purposes. The test to be applied is a more stringent one, namely were the outgoings incurred in gaining assessable income?

21. His Honour concluded on the evidence, that in that particular case he was not able to find any connection between the payment of fees and the assessable income of the taxpayer. He accepted that a teacher who has pursued University studies, is likely to be a better teacher than if he had not done so, and therefore is more likely to obtain promotion within the department, but he said that general consideration was not enough to make the fees deductible. He considered that "there must be a perceived connection between the outgoing and assessable income". In this matter the taxpayer passed all her subjects and they were directly relevant to her employment. On completion of her studies she was offered full-time employment by her employer. The question is whether those facts are sufficient to distinguish this matter from Hatchett's case.

22. Menzies J regarded his conclusions as consistent with Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60. I turn therefore to that case to see if it provides clear guidance on this issue. The taxpayer, Mr Finn, was an architect employed by the Public Works Department of the State of Western Australia. He sought to deduct expenses of an overseas trip to study architecture in order to better fit himself to perform the work required of him. The High Court allowed the deduction. Kitto J said at p.69:

"[T]he office was of a kind which by its nature made incumbent upon the occupant much more than the performance of set duties at set times. Its professional status implied an obligation of progressive acquaintance with a living and developing art. It was therefore, I think, plainly incidental to the office that the respondent should avail himself of such opportunities as might arise to add, in the interests of the State even if also in his own interests, to his knowledge and understanding of architectural achievements and trends overseas, both in design and in construction, and of endeavours made and being made to solve architectural problems such as might from time to time confront his Government.

In my judgment the respondent, in making the investigations and studies which he pursued during his period of leave, was acting within the scope of his office, and therefore in the gaining of his salary. I would hold that the expenditure which he incurred in the process was an allowable deduction under s.51 in the assessment of his income tax."

23. Those comments seem to apply equally to a design assistant hoping for employment as a designer. I confess that I have some difficulty in reconciling Finn's case with Hatchett's case, in that it seems to me that Mr Finn would have continued to be paid his salary as a senior design architect in the Public Works Department of Western Australia, whether or not he had spent his leave travelling overseas to study architecture. The possiblity of him gaining a promotion, as a result of study on his overseas trip, on the evidence appears no more likely than the possiblity of Mr Hatchett gaining a promotion as a teacher, because he had completed university studies, or of the taxpayer before me being offered a full-time position on completion of her course. This latter event did indeed happen as appears from the evidence before me.

24. In Finn's case Windeyer J said at p.70:

"Generally speaking, it seems to me, a taxpayer who gains income by the exercise of his skill in some profession or calling and who incurs expenses in maintaining or increasing his learning, knowledge, experience and ability in that profession or calling necessarily incurs those expenses in carrying on his profession or calling."

That passage appears to apply equally to the taxpayer in this matter. On the evidence she gained her income by the exercise of skill in her calling as a designer, and she incurred expenses in increasing her learning, knowledge, experience and ability in that calling. Thus applying Windeyer J's phraseology or that of Kitto J, it would appear that the taxpayer's expenses were incurred in carrying on her calling.

25. However, there are cases of persuasive authority which have purportedly applied Hatchett's case and Finn's case and which have rejected stronger claims than that presently before me. One such case is Federal Commissioner of Taxation v White (1975) 75 ATC 4018. In that case a clerk with a firm of accountants sought to deduct his travelling expenses, in pursuing his night-time accountancy studies. The evidence established that his employer expected him to complete his accountancy course, and that he would not have been expected to maintain his position in the firm, if he had given up the course. It was not a condition of his employment that he undertake such studies, nor was there any agreement that when he completed the course he would obtain any salary increase. The senior partner of the firm, however, said that if he did not continue with relevant studies he probably would not have been of sufficient use to the firm to warrant expected salary increases in the future.

26. In spite of that evidence, Helshem J of the Supreme Court of New South Wales, rejected the claim saying at p.4022:

"...expenses incurred in pursuing studies associated with employment will qualify as allowable deductions under sec.51 when it can be said that those studies are part and parcel of the employment, which means that the expenditure is incurred in the process of carrying out the employee's duties, or, even if they are not such, they can be seen to have a direct effect on income. Where that cannot be seen, I think it will be difficult to establish the necessary connection between the study activity and the employment so as to give study expenses the character of outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the income derived from the employment."

27. Thus Helsham J required "a direct effect on income". The taxpayer in this matter would not qualify under Helshem J's test as there is no evidence that her studies had a direct effect on income, but such a direct effect was not required in Finn. It is possible that it was what distinguished the Higher Teachers Certificate expenses in Hatchett from those in respect of University studies, but that is not clear.

28. Waddell J, also of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kropp (1976) 76.ATC 4406, did not regard Hatchett's case as requiring an increase in salary as a necessary or direct consequence of the studies. He allowed a claim by an accountant for expenses of overseas travel, to work with the Canadian equivalent of the Australian firm for which he had worked, even though he had resigned his employment with the Australian firm before travelling and had no guarantee as to re-employment on his return. He said at p.4409:

"[T]he taxpayer's evidence establishes that overseas experience of the kind which he set out to gain in Canada greatly enhanced his prospects of promotion and earning a higher income with the Australian firm. It is, I think, to be inferred from the evidence that his re-engagement in a higher position at an increased salary by the Australian firm and his selection to serve in the staff exchange scheme are to be attributed to the experience which he had gained in Canada as well as to his professional capacity."

And at page 4410:

"I think that it may be concluded from the differing reasons given by the members of the Court in F.C. of T. v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60, that where a taxpayer incurs expense in maintaining or improving his qualifications in a way which is of distinct advantage to his work in the eyes of his employer (p.67), or pursuant to an implied obligation of progressive acquaintance with a living and developing art (p. 69) the expenditure is incurred in gaining the assessable income."

29. Waddell J referred to Hatchett's case, Finn's case and White's case. As to White's case he simply said that Helsham J took the view on the evidence that there was no sufficient association between the study activities and the taxpayer's employment. Waddell J however was satisfied on the facts before him that the expenditure did have sufficient association with employment. He said at p.4411:

"The expenditure was part of a plan pursued by the taxpayer to increase his income from his employment as an accountant by the acquisition of two years' overseas experience with a national firm associated with his Australian employer, at the conclusion of which it could have been anticipated with considerable confidence that he would be re-employed in Australia at an increased salary and that the rate of increase of his salary in his remaining professional life would be accelerated. These considerations provide, to my mind, the necessary 'perceived connection' between the outgoing and the assessable income so that it can fairly be said that the outgoing was 'incurred in gaining the assessable income' of future years in Australia. In reaching this conclusion I rely upon the fact that in April 1974 the taxpayer was promoted and given an increased salary only as a subsequent event confirming that the taxpayer's plan when put into action was reasonably calculated to achieve its object."

30. The difficulty of reconciling the cases on this point is again demonstrated by reference to Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Klan (1985) 85 ATC 4060. The facts in that case were very similar to those in Kropp's case, of the Supreme Court of Victoria. Yet Ormiston J rejected a claim by a teacher for a deduction in respect of overseas travel expenses incurred in taking up a teaching position in Wales, after resigning a position in Australia. After his year of teaching experience in Wales the taxpayer returned to Australia and obtained a position as head of a history department at a salary higher than any he had previously received. Ormiston J accepted that Mr Klan's "proposed and actual experience was both desirable and necessary for advancement in the education system of the independent schools". Nonetheless he rejected the claimed deduction, characterising the expenditure as incurred in obtaining new employment rather than in gaining or producing assessable income.

31. Ormiston J said that a number of the earlier cases had rested on the finding of a "perceived connection" between the expenditure and the earning of assessable income generally, purportedly following the reasoning of Menzies J in Hatchett's case. Ormiston J referred to Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 84 ATC 4513, as authority for the view that the perceived connection was not sufficient by itself for a finding that the expenses were incurred in gaining the income. In Martin's case the Full Federal Court said at p.4516:

"To begin with, we do not believe that Menzies J. was purporting to substitute some new test for the principles well established in earlier authorities. In the passage in which the words quoted appear, his Honour was at pains to make it clear that he was not dealing with some general question of the deductibility of university fees but 'the particular question whether the fees paid by the taxpayer in the circumstances already stated are deductible'. His Honour went on to say that he was unable to find any connection beyond the circumstance that a teacher who has pursued university studies is likely to be a better teacher than if he had not done so and is more likely thereby to obtain promotion. He Honour then said:

'In my opinion this general consideration is not enough to make the fees deductible; there must be a perceived connection between the outgoing and assessable income'

We agree with the statement of the learned primary Judge in the present appeal that his Honour was not saying that such a connection was sufficient by itself and that, in the language of the Act, the expenditure must be "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income."

0rmiston J went on to suggest that Kropp's case was one of a number of cases which might need to be reconsidered in the light of the decision of the Full Federal Court in Martin's case.

32. Ormiston J concluded on this point at p.4068:

"In the end, therefore, it is a question of characterising the acts of the taxpayer as being either incidental and relevant to operations and activities carried on for the earning of assessable income or as a means of obtaining a contract of employment with a new employer. I have little doubt that Mr Klan's plans both as originally envisaged and as carried out were directed to the latter end. They were not mere incidents to his employment."

If those passages set out the appropriate test it appears that the taxpayer's expenses in the case before the Tribunal could have been directed towards obtaining new and better employment with the same employer. They were clearly relevant to operations and activities carried on by the taxpayer for the earning of assessable income, but I am not sure that they were incidental to that employment.

33. Mr Petty, in his final address submitted that the cases allow two ways of showing that education expenses are deductible under s.51(1) of the Act. He submitted that the applicant satisfied both tests in that, as the offer of full-time employment at the conclusion of the course showed, her studies, "had a direct and immediate result on obtaining income", and further, the fees for studies were incurred, "in the process of carrying out the duties which gave rise to the assessable income", in that the witness said that the applicant's draughting skills were required in her work. While the evidence does show that this was so I am not confident that it shows that the expenditure was incidental and relevant in the sense that the "essential character" of the fees was expenditure incurred in gaining the assessable income. That explanation of the test has been referred to by Lockhart J as well established, in the very recent case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper [1991] FCA 164; (1991) 99 ALR 703 at p.707:

"For expenditure to be an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end: Ronpibon at 56. This test of deductibility has been explained in subsequent judgments of the High Court, so that to be deductible the expenditure must be incidental and relevant in the sense of having the essential character of expenditure incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income. This formulation of the test has its origins in the joint judgment of Dixon CJ, Williams, Webb, Fullagar and Kitto JJ in Charles Moore & Co (WA) Pty Ltd v FCT [1956] HCA 77; (1956) 95 CLR 344 at 351. It has been applied subsequently in cases which include Lunney v FCT (1958) 100 CLR 478 at 497; Handley v FCT [1981] HCA 16; (1981) 148 CLR 182 at 194; 34 ALR 274; FCT V Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1981) 148 CLR 203 at 210; [1981] HCA 15; 34 ALR 263; John v FCT [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 166 CLR 417; 83 ALR 606. The essential character test is also applied to determine if the expenditure is of a capital, private or domestic nature as these cases illustrate."

34. The written submission of the respondent, set out four submissions as to why, even if the respondent failed in its submission that the Overseas Student Charge was not an education expense, that charge should not be regarded as an expense incurred in the earning of assessable income. The first submission was:

"1. It is an initial qualification".

This is clearly so, and prior to Hatchett's case such an expenditure would have been treated as a capital expenditure and therefore not deductible under s.51(1). No authorities of which I am aware since Hatchett's case specifically state that fees incurred to obtain an initial qualification are not deductible, but it may well be that an initial qualification, commenced prior to entering the workforce, is more likely to be an expenditure of a private nature than an expenditure incurred in gaining assessable income.

35. The second submission was:

"2. It is not sure that the applicant will stay with the employer that she was employed with in the year in dispute and therefore the employment is not consistent. The employment that the applicant had undertaken in the year in dispute was on a part-time basis and the completion of the qualification would not necessarily mean that the applicant receive full time employment."

This submission derives some support from Klan's case but the facts there were distinguishable. In Klan's case, and in Kropp's case where the deduction was allowed, the taxpayer had resigned one position when the expenses were incurred and had not yet obtained other income-producing employment. In this matter the taxpayer was gaining the assessable income when the expenditure was incurred. Dixon C.J in Finn's case rejected the suggestion that the productive effect of expenditure on study must be within the current year of income, saying, at p.68:

"[I] is impossible to suppose that an expenditure directed to gaining future income cannot be allowed as a deduction, unless its productive effect within the current year is seen or expected."

Ormiston J in Klan's case suggested that the words of Dixon C.J should not be given a wider effect than they themselves require, but he did not suggest that expenditure incurred in one year, which is incidental and relevant to the earning of assessable income in that year, would lose its deductible character if there were no certainty that the taxpayer would remain with the same employer in the future.

36. As to the second aspect of this submission, the evidence satisfied me that the completion of the qualification in interior design did render it more likely that the taxpayer would be offered full-time employment by her employer. The fact that that is what happened, is corroborative evidence of this likelihood. In Hatchett's case Menzies J was prepared to regard the taxpayer's lack of success in passing University exams as a circumstance making it impossible to find that there existed any connection between the payment of University fees and the earning of assessable income. Waddell J in Kropp's case (at p.4411) expressed some doubt as to whether it is relevant, in characterising an item of expenditure, to enquire whether its object was achieved. The fact that the studies in this matter did lead to the offer of a full-time job as a designer may not be relevant, but if it is relevant it assists the taxpayer's case.

37. The third submission was:

"3. The qualification, or the commencement of the qualification preceded the employment."

In support of this submission the respondent relied on Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Maddalena (1971) 71 ATC 4161 J said at p.4163:

"Had the taxpayer claimed as a deduction the expenses of changing from one job to another as an employee electrician his outlay would not have been an allowable deduction. The expenditure would have been incurred in getting, not in doing work as an employee. It would come at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income."

The respondent claimed that because the taxpayer had started her course in 1987 and had completed two full-time years before commencing her work as a part-time designer in late 1988, the expenditure as a whole was incurred "at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income". That is clearly correct in regard to the 1987 and 1988 charges, but does not apply to the year to which this claim relates. The 1989 charge was paid after the taxpayer had commenced the relevant employment.

38. There is, however, a related point which I consider is very important in determining this matter. It is that the essential character of the expenditure on fees for the course has not changed since the taxpayer commenced the course. When she commenced the course, so far as the evidence establishes, she was not employed. She commenced her studies to provide herself with further education and training so that she could obtain work of her choice as a qualified person. The essential nature of the expenditure as at that time was private. It was clearly not incurred in gaining any assessable income as there was no assessable income. I am not satisfied on the evidence that its essential character changed merely because the taxpayer, while completing her studies, obtained part-time employment to which the studies were relevant and incidental.

39. The fourth submission was:

"4. The payment to the Department of Employment, Education and Training was first entered into, prior to any employment, and in fact to the applicant entering Australia, and the payment made to the Department in March of each year is an instalment to a contract that the applicant made when entering Australia and this amount wi11 be due whi1e the applicant is not a permanent resident of Australia."

I do not accept that the taxpayer entered into a contract with an unspecified person to complete her course when she first entered Australia The payment of the charge for each year is in my view a separate payment giving rise to rights to enter or continue to be in Australia in order to study the specified course during that year. However, as already stated, I do consider that the fact that the studies were commenced prior to the employment being obtained points to the studies themselves being the prime objective of the taxpayer rather than them being undertaken as incidental to the gaining of the assessable income.

40. I consider that the cases provide so little assistance in determining whether or not self education expenses are "incurred in gaining . . . the assessable income" that the Tribunal is thrown back on a simple interpretation of the words of s.51(1) of the Act, together with a realistic analysis of the evidence. On the evidence I conclude that the study expenses were not incurred in gaining the assessable income. The study, which was the third year of the course, was the prime object, and was indeed the reason why the taxpayer came to Australia. The employment provided income for the support of the taxpayer during the course and also provided her with relevant experience and enhanced her future prospects of finding employment in her field. But I cannot find that the expenditure for the studies was incidental and relevant, in the sense of having the essential character of expenditure incurred in gaining the assessable income. If the taxpayer had worked as a waitress while studying she could not have deducted her self-education expenses from her earnings. The fact that she found work as a design assistant to support herself during her studies, even though it was relevant to the studies, does not, in my view make the fees for those studies deductible under s51(1) of the Act. The decision under review will be affirmed.
