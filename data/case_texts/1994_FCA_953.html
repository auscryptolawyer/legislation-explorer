Kevin Trevor Pollock v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia, Official Trustee In Bankruptcy, Sgio Insurance Ltd and Tyre Marketers (Australia) Limited [1994] FCA 953 (4 March 1994)

ORDER

1. The application is dismissed.

2. The applicant pay the first, second and third respondents' costs

DECISION

Introduction CARR J This is an application to annul a sequestration order made, in the absence of the applicant, by the District Registrar on 7 September 1993 in respect of a creditor's petition presented by the first respondent. The application is primarily based on s.153B of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth) ("the Act") but the inherent jurisdiction of the court is also relied upon and incidental relief is sought under s.37(1) of the Act.

Factual Background

2. On 24 September 1992 the first respondent sued the applicant in the Supreme Court of Western Australia for income tax assessed in respect of the four years ended 30th June 1985, 1986, 1987 and 1988. However, by reason of certain credits the income tax in issue in those proceedings was that assessed on the applicant's income in only the first three of those years.

3. On 24 November 1992 the first respondent obtained judgment in those proceedings, in default of appearance, in the amount of $147,133.26 and $349.70 in respect of costs. A writ of fieri facias which was issued very shortly thereafter was returned unsatisfied on 28 January 1993.

4. On 25 May 1993 the first respondent applied for the issue of a bankruptcy notice based on the Supreme Court judgment. The bankruptcy notice was issued two days later and served on the applicant on 15 June 1993. It was common ground that by reason of non-compliance with that bankruptcy notice the applicant committed a further act of bankruptcy.

5. Either shortly before or shortly after receiving the bankruptcy notice (the applicant swore conflicting affidavits on this point) the applicant approached a Mr Bernard John Hoey, an accountant. The applicant had seen a newspaper advertisement advertising Mr Hoey's services. Mr Hoey agreed to contact the first respondent on the applicant's behalf to try and come to some form of repayment arrangement. Mr Hoey wrote to the first respondent on 15 July 1993 enclosing objections against each of the three income tax assessments and requesting an extension of time in which those objections could be lodged. Mr Hoey also prepared a statutory declaration which the applicant declared and filed at the Federal Court of Australia on 20 July 1993. That statutory declaration was not a document which complied with any of the bankruptcy rules or forms prescribed in those rules, but in it the applicant:

. acknowledged receipt of the bankruptcy notice;

. stated that objections against income tax assessments

totalling $139,489.99 had been lodged with the first

respondent;

. asserted that "the above amount" represented a counterclaim

or set-off to the net amount claimed by the first respondent

in the Supreme Court proceedings;

. claimed that as a result of those objections the applicant

was entitled to further credits from the first respondent;

and

. stated that accordingly "most if not all of the amounts

referred to" in the bankruptcy notice were subject to

dispute.

6. On the same date, 20 July 1993, the first respondent filed a petition for the making of a sequestration order against the estate of the applicant. The petition was returnable on 7 September 1993.

7. In late July 1993 Mr Hoey lodged a request under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth) to obtain access to documents on the first respondent's files with respect to the three income tax assessments issued to the applicant and referred to above ("the income tax assessments").

8. Although under s.15 of the Freedom of Information Act the first respondent was required to provide copies of the relevant documents by 29 August 1993, the documents were not made available until 3 September 1993.

9. In the meantime, on 2 August 1993 Mr Hoey telephoned a Ms Nola Rice, an officer in the first respondent's Debt Collection Section who was instructing the Australian Government Solicitor on the first respondent's behalf in relation to the petition.

10. In his affidavit sworn on 16 September 1993 Mr Hoey (who was not cross-examined on any of his affidavits) stated his recollection of that telephone conversation in the following terms:

"7. On 2 August 1993 I had extensive

discussions with Ms Nola Rice of the ATO. I had

telephoned Ms Rice to ensure that the First

Respondent would not apply for a sequestration

order whilst the taxation assessment, the

subject of the petition, was still in dispute.

I confirmed with Ms Rice that objections to the

assessment had been lodged with the ATO and that

if the decision was adverse the Applicant would

almost certainly seek a review through the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the AAT").

During the course of that telephone

conversation, Ms Rice confirmed that it was the

First Respondent's policy not to proceed with a

sequestration order whilst any tax which formed

the basis of the petition was in dispute. Ms

Rice further advised me that if necessary she

would advise the court of the dispute on the

hearing day of the petition and would have the

matter adjourned until the dispute was resolved.

I emphasised to Ms Rice that if there was any

chance that the First Respondent wished to

proceed and obtain a sequestration order, I

ought to be contacted. Ms Rice confirmed that

she would contact me in that case."

11. Ms Rice (who was also not cross-examined on her affidavit) set out her recollection of that telephone conversation as follows:

"31. On 2 August 1993, Mr Hoey telephoned and

informed me that the Applicant had contacted him

to advise that he had been served with the

petition. I advised Mr Hoey that the ATO would

seek an adjournment of the petition unless the

objections were resolved by the hearing date. I

advised further that if the objections were

disallowed, a sequestration order would be

sought. Mr Hoey said that he would ring me on

Friday 3 September 1993."

12. On 24 August 1993 Mr Hoey had a telephone conversation with a Mr Frank Maloney of the first respondent's Appeals Branch. Mr Hoey, in his affidavit sworn on 16 September 1993, described what took place during that telephone conversation in the following terms:

"8. On 24 August 1993 I was telephoned by Mr

Frank Maloney of the Appeals Branch of the ATO.

Mr Maloney advised me that he had considered the

Applicant's application for an extension of time

to lodge objections and had decided that that

application would be refused. That advice was

confirmed by way of a letter to Roper Hoey from

the ATO dated 25 August 1993. Annexed hereto

and marked "BJH11" is a true copy of that

letter. I advised Mr Maloney that the Applicant

would be lodging an appeal to the AAT against

the First Respondent's refusal of an extension

of time. However, I also advised Mr Maloney

that an appeal to the AAT could not be lodged

until I had received information requested under

the Freedom of Information Act relating to the

issues underlying the objection against the

taxation assessment. I pointed out to Mr

Maloney that I had been advised by Mr Ray White

of his office that the information requested

under the Freedom of Information Act would not

become available until 3 September 1993."

13. Mr Maloney's version, again in an affidavit on which he was not cross-examined was in these terms:

"3. I refer to paragraph 8 of the affidavit

sworn by Bernard John Hoey on 16 September 1993

and confirm that I spoke to Mr Hoey on or about

24 August 1993 and advised him that the

Applicant's request would be refused. Mr Hoey

did not advise me that an appeal would

definitely be lodged although he indicated that

this was likely. He advised me that a decision

whether or not to appeal would be made after he

had examined the documents which he had

requested from the Australian Taxation Office

under the Freedom of Information Act."

14. On 25 August 1993 Mr Maloney told Ms Rice that he had decided to refuse the applicant's request to deal with the objections against the income tax assessments as if they had been lodged within the prescribed time and that a letter would be sent to the applicant that day advising him of that decision.

15. On 3 September 1993 (a Friday) the first respondent provided Mr Hoey the documents which he had requested pursuant to the Freedom of Information Act ("the F.O.I. Documents").

16. On the following Tuesday (7 September 1993) the petition came on for hearing before the District Registrar, there was no appearance by or for the applicant and the sequestration order was made.

17. On 8 September 1993 Mr Hoey, on behalf of the applicant, lodged an appeal with the Administrative Appeals Tribunal against the first respondent's decision to refuse extensions of time for the lodgment of the objections against the income tax assessments.

18. On 10 September 1993 the applicant filed this application to annul the sequestration order made three days previously.

19. The first respondent filed an objection in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to the jurisdiction of that Tribunal to hear the applicant's appeal against the first respondent's refusal to extend the time for lodging the objections.

20. On 17 December 1993, immediately before the hearing of this application, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ruled that it had jurisdiction to hear that appeal. It is likely that the Administrative Appeals Tribunal will hear that appeal early in 1994.

21. The application to annul the sequestration order is opposed by the first respondent and the third respondents who also are judgment creditors of the applicant. The second respondent indicated through counsel (who also appeared for the first respondent) that he did not wish to present any submissions to the Court but would simply abide by the Court's decision.

The Statutory Provisions

22. Sections 153B and 37(1) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 relevantly provide:-

"153B If the Court is satisfied that a

sequestration order ought not to have been made

... the Court may make an order annulling the

bankruptcy."

"37. (1) Subject to subsection (2), the

Court may rescind, vary or discharge an order

made by it under this Act or may suspend the

operation of such an order.

(2) The Court does not have power to

rescind or discharge, or to suspend the

operation of:

(a) a sequestration order; or

(b) an order for the administration of the

estate of a deceased person under Part

XI."

The Legal Principles Applicable

23. The leading case in point is the decision of the High Court of Australia in Cameron v. Cole [1944] HCA 5; (1944) 68 CLR 571. In that case the debtor had, through inadvertence, not been given notice of the adjourned hearing date for the petition and a sequestration order was made in his absence. Subsequently the Federal Court of Bankruptcy annulled the sequestration order, directed a rehearing of the petition and at that rehearing a second sequestration order was made. The matter went on appeal to the High Court principally on the question of whether the Federal Court of Bankruptcy, having annulled the petition, had jurisdiction to make the second sequestration order. The Court was concerned with the application of sections 26 and 124 of the Bankruptcy Act 1924 being the equivalent of sections 37 and 153B of the current Act but also held by majority, that the Federal Court of Bankruptcy had inherent power to set aside the first sequestration order. The applicant relies on Cameron v. Cole for the proposition, developed in argument, that in exercising the inherent jurisdiction of the Court to set aside orders made in the absence of a party I should not be concerned about the strength of what were described as the applicant's tax appeals. Reference was made to the observations of Rich J at p.589 and McTiernan J at p.600. The observations made by Rich J at p.589 were in the context of an objection by the debtor that the Court which annulled the first sequestration order had no jurisdiction to direct a rehearing of the petition. Even acknowledging that Rich J used the words "ex debito justitiae" in describing a person's right to have set aside a determination made without that person having been given a reasonable opportunity to appear and present his case, his Honour was not considering whether, in a case where there was no substance to the debtor's dispute, the Court should exercise its inherent jurisdiction to set aside the first petition. Furthermore, at pp.590-591 his Honour clearly regarded the original sequestration order as being voidable, not void. Similarly at p.600 McTiernan J was not dealing with the matter of whether the debtor needed to show that there was a genuine dispute and in any event was referring to the exercise of the statutory jurisdiction under s.124 of the Bankruptcy Act 1924 which his Honour regarded (apart from s.71(18)) as completely regulating the annulment of any sequestration order made under the Act. In my view, the exercise of the Court's inherent power to set aside a sequestration order is a discretionary one exercisable on similar principles to the discretion in s.153B of the present Act.

24. To accept the applicant's submission to the contrary would mean that in the exercise of its inherent jurisdiction the Court might have to set aside a sequestration order in circumstances where it knows that there is no real question to be tried. As I see it, in circumstances where there has been a denial of natural justice in relation to the hearing of a bankruptcy petition both the inherent jurisdiction and s.153B work in tandem in the same manner. Proof of the fundamental irregularity triggers the right to an immediate consideration of what should have happened had that irregularity not occurred. If the Court on all the evidence before it finds that there is nothing genuinely in dispute then there is no logical basis for annulling the sequestration order, because the inevitable consequence will be that on a rehearing of the petition a further sequestration order will be made. More importantly justice is served by requiring only that the applicant show that there was a real question to be tried.

25. As Rich J explained in Cameron v. Cole at p 589:

"Assuming that the latter section (s.124 of the

Bankruptcy Act 1924) should now be regarded as

the governing section when the setting aside of

a sequestration order is concerned, the effect

of any particular setting aside necessarily

depends on the circumstances. If the order is

set aside on the merits, that is no doubt an end

of the petition. But if it is set aside because

the hearing of the petition was invalid for some

reason which prevents it in law from being

regarded as ever having been heard at all, there

is nothing in s.124 which prevents the Court

from hearing, or absolves it from its duty of

proceeding to hear, the petition in due course

of law."

26. Although in Cameron v. Cole Williams J held that the Federal Court of Bankruptcy had no power to annul other than the express power conferred by s.124(1)(a) of the Bankruptcy Act 1924 in my view the Court whether exercising inherent or statutory jurisdiction should approach the matter in the manner his Honour outlined at p.608:-

"When it was brought to his Honour's notice that

the sequestration order of 22nd December 1942

had been obtained without notice of the

adjourned hearing having been given to Cameron,

it was his duty to inquire whether, in all the

circumstances, that order should not have been

made. In order to determine that question he

could have reviewed its propriety in the light,

inter alia, of the objections which Cameron

alleged that he could have raised against the

making of that order if he had had an

opportunity of being heard. If his Honour had

considered that there was no substance in these

or any subsequent objections, he could have

refused to annul the order. If, on the other

hand, he had considered that, if these

objections had been raised, he would not have

made the order, he could have annulled it."

27. Cameron v. Cole was referred to again in a jurisdictional context by Gibbs J (as he then was) in Taylor v. Taylor [1979] HCA 38; (1979) 143 CLR 1 at pp 8-9 (a case where a decree nisi for dissolution of a marriage had been made in the absence of the husband, such absence being through no fault of his own) in these terms:

"It is clear that the majority of the Court in

Cameron v. Cole accepted that a court, whether

superior or inferior, has inherent power to set

aside an order made against a person who did not

have a reasonable opportunity to appear and

present his case.

. . . .

Since service of the petition was properly

effected upon the appellant, it is not possible

to regard the order made by Woodward J as a

nullity, whatever view is taken of the questions

discussed in Cameron v. Cole. However, since

the appellant in fact had no notice of the

hearing, the court had inherent power to set the

order aside. This power was a discretionary

one, but there was no reason to refuse the

appellant's application. The appellant had

filed affidavit material which showed that the

facts of the case were seriously in contest.

The proper course was to set aside the order of

Woodward J and to reconsider the matter, as

Hogan J did.

. . . . .

In such a case, assuming that there is a real

question to be tried, justice requires that the

order, having been made in breach of a

fundamental principle of natural justice, should

be set aside, and that the matter should be

reconsidered on its merits."

28. These two cases were followed by Burchett J in Re Anasis; Ex parte Total Australia Ltd [1985] FCA 458; (1985) 63 ALR 493, another case in which a debtor did not receive notice of an adjourned hearing date for a petition upon which a sequestration order was made in his absence. His Honour, having held that he had jurisdiction to set aside the sequestration order and order a rehearing of the petition, turned to the exercise of the discretion whether to make such order. Referring to the above passage from Taylor v. Taylor, Burchett J said (at p 499):-

"Although I think the applicant's case would

satisfy the test of a "defence on the merits",

as the test has been understood in relation to

applications to set aside default judgments, I

do not understand the expression "a real

question to be tried" as, in this context,

demanding so much. Even an unwitting denial of

natural justice is so fundamental a fault in the

structure of a decision of a dispute that a

party who had not been heard is, in my opinion,

entitled to a hearing unless there is shown to

be in fact nothing genuinely in dispute"

(further authorities cited).

29. In Re Anasis Burchett J ordered that the petition be reheard. What should a Court do where in addition to the adducing of evidence of the circumstances whereby the applicant was not represented at the hearing of the petition the application for annulment has been conducted in such a manner (with, for example as in this matter a full day of hearing including cross-examination of the bankrupt and extensive submissions on the merits) that the Court is in as good a position to assess the merits of the petition as if there had been a rehearing?

30. In my view, the course to be adopted is that foreshadowed by Rich J in Cameron v. Cole (at p 589) and outlined by Riley J in Re Calderon (unreported Federal Court of Bankruptcy, 31 May 1977 No. NSW 573 of 1976), where his Honour after reviewing the authorities, set out a convenient summary of the relevant principles in the form of five propositions which I respectfully adopt. With one factual reference deleted, they were as follows:-

1. It is the applicant for annulment who alleges,

and it is therefore for him to bring himself

within the section and satisfy the Court, that

the sequestration order ought not to have been

made.

2. The Court to whom the application is made seeks

to ascertain the actual state of affairs at the

time when the sequestration order is made.

3. In order to ascertain that actual state of

affairs the Court hearing the application for

annulment looks at the facts that were before

the Court which made the sequestration order and

at any other facts that were not before that

Court but are shown on the hearing of the

application for annulment to have been in

existence when the sequestration order was made.

4. Having considered all the facts so looked at,

the Court determines whether on those facts the

applicant has satisfied it that the

sequestration order ought not to have been made.

5. If it is so satisfied, the Court is not bound to

annul the sequestration order but must consider

in all the circumstances of the case whether it

ought to be annulled.

31. Einfeld J also reviewed the authorities in Re Piccardi (unreported 3 August 1988 Judgment No. 424/88) and observed that there seemed to be a conflict between Re Scott (1975) 6 ALR 558, Re Calderon, Re Hatcher (French J unreported 6 November 1987, Judgment No. 608/87) and Re Johnson (Jackson J unreported 5 February 1987, Judgment No. 57/87) on the one hand and Re Bond [1978] FCA 42; (1978) 22 ALR 287 on the other hand as to the extent to which matters occurring since the sequestration order may be taken into account. The approach taken by Einfeld J in Re Piccardi seems to me to be entirely consistent with Riley J's five propositions set out above.

32. In Re Bond [1978] FCA 42; (1978) 22 ALR 287 at p 289 Sweeney J annulled a sequestration order relying in part on evidence which became available subsequently and cited a passage from the judgment of Williams J in Cameron v. Cole [1944] HCA 5; (1944) 68 CLR 571 at p 608:

"It (the Court) can decide, therefore, at a

properly constituted hearing whether the order

should have been made on the merits in the light

not only of the evidence which was available at

the date the sequestration order was made, but

also of any evidence that has subsequently

become available before the date of the

application to annul."

33. Finally, on this point in Re Frank (1987) 77 ALR 511 Fisher J at pp 517-518 observed:

"Alternatively it can be established that an

order "ought" not to have been made because

subsequent evidence discloses that all of the

true facts were not before the Court when the

order was made: Re Cook (1946) 13 ABC 245 at

249."

Whether the sequestration order "ought not to have been made"?

34. The applicant, through no fault of his own or of his agent, Mr Hoey, was not present or represented when the petition was heard. As none of the deponents to the relevant affidavits on this aspect (extracts from which are set out above) was cross-examined, the resolution of the factual position concerning the proposed adjournment of the petition cannot be determined on credibility but by trying, as best one can, to ascertain what transpired in a manner which is consistent with the contents of the affidavits.

35. It seems clear from the affidavits of Mr Hoey and Ms Rice that on 2 August 1993 it was agreed between them that the first respondent would arrange for the hearing of the petition to be adjourned until the disputes over the income tax assessments had been resolved. There is a slight difference in the two accounts of what took place on 24 August 1993 when Mr Maloney informed Mr Hoey that the first respondent had refused the applications for extension of time to object to the income tax assessments. Mr Hoey says that he told Mr Maloney that appeals would be lodged when he received the FOI Documents. Mr Maloney says that Mr Hoey was not so definite but had said that an appeal would be likely and that a decision would be made after receipt of the FOI Documents. I do not think it is appropriate or necessary to try and resolve this slight factual dispute in the affidavits. The evidence discloses that the disputes over the applicant's income tax had not been resolved even though Ms Rice may have thought that they had been resolved. If, for example, the time for appealing to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal against the first respondent's refusal to extend time had expired without such an appeal having been lodged, the situation would have been different. But that was not the case. In the circumstances referred to above the applicant was, in my opinion, entitled to expect that the first respondent would, as agreed, apply for an adjournment of the hearing of the petition. In my view there was a genuine misunderstanding of the situation by both parties without fault on either side as a result of which the applicant was unrepresented at the hearing of the petition.

36. In those circumstances, on the basis of the authorities referred to above, in my view the next step to take is to ascertain whether upon a consideration of all the evidence now adduced a genuine dispute exists over the debts which arose out of the income tax assessments, the total of which became the subject of the Supreme Court default judgment, which judgment in turn founded the bankruptcy notice, non-compliance with which was the ground for the first respondent's petition. If a genuine dispute exists as a result of which the conclusion is reached that the sequestration order ought not to have been made, the question of whether annulment should be ordered is then one of discretion. As Gibbs J said in Re Deriu (1970) 16 FLR 420 at p 421

"Under this section there are two matters which

the Court has to consider, first whether a

sequestration order ought not to have been made,

and then, if the Court is satisfied of that,

whether in the exercise of the Court's

discretion the order should be annulled (Re

Williams (1968) 13 FLR 10 at p.23."

Is there a genuine dispute?

37. It is clear that in deciding this question the Court may go behind the judgment debt in the same manner and to the same extent as if the Court were hearing the petition for a sequestration order: Re Piccardi and the cases cited in that judgment at pages 26-27. This is particularly true where the judgment is one obtained by default: Corney v. Brien [1951] HCA 31; (1951) 84 CLR 343.

38. As the Full Court of this Court noted in Ahern v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1987] FCA 312; (1987) 76 ALR 137 at p 148:-

"It is also well established that in general a

Court exercising jurisdiction in bankruptcy

should not proceed to sequestrate the estate of

a debtor where an appeal is pending against the

judgment relied on as the foundation of the

bankruptcy proceedings provided that the appeal

is based on genuine and arguable grounds

(authorities cited)."

39. The applicant has initiated the first stage in the appropriate appellate procedure by asking the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to extend the time in which to lodge objections against these three assessments.

40. The applicant also has an application pending before the Supreme Court for leave to intervene and to set aside the first respondent's Supreme Court default judgment. He had made a previous application (heard on 1 October 1993) to set aside that default judgment. That previous application was based on two grounds, namely that the judgment was irregular and should be set aside and also that the applicant had an arguable defence on the merits. On 12 October 1993 Master Adams held that the judgment was not irregular and, in response to an objection by the first respondent to that effect, further held that the applicant, then being a bankrupt had no standing to apply to set aside the judgment. Hence the applicant's application for leave to intervene (as part of which he seeks to amend the name of the judgment debtor to "The Official Trustee in Bankruptcy as trustee for the Estate of Kevin Trevor Pollock (a bankrupt)") so that the question of an arguable defence on the merits can be considered.

41. In view of the provisions of s.177(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) and the relevant case law, the applicant's prospects of having an arguable defence on the merits in the context of those Supreme Court proceedings would seem to be somewhat limited: F.J Bloemen Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 CLR 360. No evidence was led by the applicant to suggest that despite the issue of the notices the income tax assessments had never been made, see for example Reg. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation; Ex parte Briggs (1986) 13 FCR 389. Such evidence as the applicant led on the matter in these proceedings was directed to whether the income on which the assessments were based was that of the applicant or of a company called Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd. The forum for that dispute is no longer the Supreme Court of Western Australia.

42. I see my task at this stage of the matter as being to decide whether a genuine dispute exists, which if resolved in favour of the applicant will result in the debt upon which the petition was based (indirectly via the bankruptcy notice) ceasing to exist. In other words - does the applicant have a reasonably arguable case to get the three income tax assessments set aside? As part of making that judgment it seems to me that as Mr MacLiver, counsel for the second respondent submitted, I have to consider whether the applicant has a reasonably arguable case to obtain any extension of time in which to lodge objections.

43. In the particular context of disputed income tax assessments and a bankruptcy petition the taxpayer bears an onus of showing the existence of a genuine dispute based on substantial grounds by adducing evidence establishing the substantial nature of the grounds of that challenge and may not merely rely on assertions contained in the notice of objections: Re Verma [1984] FCA 340; (1984) 4 FCR 181 at p 187 Re Baker 89 ATC 4652.

44. The income tax assessments arise out of an audit of the applicant's affairs and those of the companies in which he had either a direct or indirect interest. That audit started in 1984 and recommenced in 1991. One of the issues which was examined in the audit of the K.T. Pollock Family Trust ("the Trust") was whether certain fees amounting to $197,212.00 ("the commission") paid by a company called Romel Pty Ltd to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd as trustee for the Trust was the applicant's personal income and should have been included in his income tax returns. The total comprised amounts of $32,427.00, $121,799.00 and $42,986.00 paid by Romel Pty Ltd to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd as trustee for the Trust during the three years ended 30 June 1985, 30 June 1986 and 30 June 1987 respectively. The major part and possibly all of those payments were made pursuant to an agreement ("the commission agreement"). An unexecuted copy of the commission agreement was exhibited to the applicant's affidavit sworn on 20 September 1993 and also to Mr Hoey's affidavit sworn on 30 September 1993. The applicant in his affidavits and orally at the hearing of this application gave evidence as to the circumstances leading to these payments. It is necessary, for a proper understanding of this matter that I summarise those circumstances.

45. Prior to 1 January 1985 Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd and another company (in which the applicant did not appear to have any interest) called T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd held shares in Romel Pty Ltd and also units in a unit trust of which Romel Pty Ltd was trustee. Romel Pty Ltd as trustee traded under the style "Soils Ain't Soils" and had a contract to carry out earthworks for the construction of the Burswood Casino near Perth. On 1 January 1985 Pollock Nominees and T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd sold their units in the unit trust of which Romel Pty Ltd was trustee. The applicant, in his affidavit sworn 30 September 1993, swore that in addition to the monetary component of the purchase price and the issue of certain shares the consideration payable by the purchaser included an amount of 10% of the net profit (if any) which might be earned upon carrying out the earthworks contract for the Burswood Casino. Half that commission was payable to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd and the other half to T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd. The commission agreement was drawn and executed to provide for the payment of this share in the net profits.

46. The applicant further swore in that affidavit that one of the conditions of the sale was that he, on behalf of Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd, would provide ongoing management for Romel Pty Ltd after the sale referred to above. The applicant's evidence was that a service agreement was drawn up between the purchaser and Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd to reflect this. That service agreement was not put into evidence before me. The applicant swore in the same affidavit that Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd received a management fee for the services which he provided to Romel Pty Ltd and that Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd returned that management fee as income. The applicant's evidence was that the management fee was calculated at the rate of 5% of what he described as "the day to day trading profits" by way of distinguishing them from the profits derived by carrying out the contract for the earthworks for the Burswood Casino. Accordingly there were two sets of commissions payable to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd. The first (pursuant to the commission agreement) was 5% of any net profit earned on the earthworks for the Burswood Casino and the second was 5% of "the day to day trading profits" by way of a management fee for providing the applicant's services.

47. The sum of $197,212.00 referred to above includes the commission paid by Romel Pty Ltd to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd pursuant to the commission agreement. It is not entirely clear from the evidence whether that sum also included the management fee.

48. On 12 September 1989 the applicant and his accountant Mr Phillip Metcalf (who was also the applicant's tax agent) were interviewed by officers of the first respondent. A note of that interview was put into evidence which recorded that the management fee was a consultancy fee paid by Romel Pty Ltd for the applicant's expertise in reclaiming, recycling and transporting sand for the Burswood Casino project. Reference was then made in that note to the commission agreement being based on a 3 year contract with Vox Adeon (the purchaser of the units in the Trust). The note concluded: "By Pollock's description of the working arrangement, he was informed that the income was from personal exertion as it was his own personal service and not that of the family trust or other beneficiaries."

49. On 9 July 1991 the first respondent wrote to Mr Metcalf expressing the opinion that commission fees of $32,427.00, $121,799.00 and $42,986.00 paid by Romel Pty Ltd to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd in the years ended 30 June 1985, 1986 and 1987 and totalling $197,212.00 should be classified as personal exertion income and included in the applicant's personal income tax returns.

50. By letter dated 19 August 1991 Mr Metcalf replied to the first respondent's letter dated 9 July 1991 and specifically in response to the opinion expressed by the first respondent in respect of these commission fees stated:

"Your decision to have the commission included

in Mr Pollock's personal returns is accepted.

We also confirm that your office will amend all

necessary returns for Mr Pollock, the Family

Trust and for all beneficiaries as follows:

1985 Mr Pollocks (sic) return to include

commission of $32,427.00

1986 Mr Pollocks (sic) return to include

commission of $121,799.00 . . .

1987 Mr Pollock's return to include commission

of $42,986.00."

The amounts coincide precisely with the amounts referred to in the first respondent's letter of 9 July 1991. Mr Metcalf's letter concluded with the observation that it was critical to consider the time to which penalties would apply.

51. On 7 February 1992 the applicant signed a short document evidencing the agreement reached on penalties. That read as follows:

"PENALTIES ARE CALCULATED:

1. On the net tax avoided by Mr Pollock,

2. After taking into account the tax already

paid by Mrs Pollock,.

3. At the rate of 20% on the net tax avoided.

4. The `per annum' component of the penalty

was calculated to 6 October 1989, not on

the date the audit was completed."

It was on that basis that the first respondent issued amended income tax assessments in respect of the applicant's income for the financial years ended 30 June 1985, 1986 and 1987 which included the commission referred to above and penalties calculated in accordance with the short agreement set out above.

52. Although the applicant swore and filed five affidavits in support of his application, he made no reference in them to the agreement made by Mr Metcalf on his behalf and outlined above. In cross-examination the applicant stated that he had not seen Mr Metcalf's letter until recently when looking at the affidavits. He stated that his understanding was that the agreement with the Australian Taxation Office only concerned the second category of commissions referred to above and that "... we were talking in amounts of 20 sort of thousand dollars was what I understood that he was agreeing to for commissions that I'd made on behalf of the company's trading." The applicant was emphatic that he had not agreed to these additional amounts being included in his income and that he had not instructed Mr Metcalf to make the above response to the Australian Taxation Office.

53. I do not think that it affects the outcome of this application but I did not believe the applicant's evidence. My disbelief was only partly based on his demeanour in the witness box in response to cross-examination on this point although I thought he gave many of his answers somewhat glibly. I was also influenced by at least two other matters. The first was that in response to an earlier question, the applicant stated that he could not even "hazard a guess" whether he had had a discussion with Mr Metcalf about the letter dated 9 July 1991 from the Australian Taxation Office.

54. The second matter is that in my opinion it is of some significance that no affidavit from Mr Metcalf was filed either on this point or in relation to the question why no objections were lodged when the notices of assessment were issued. The evidence was that Mr Metcalf had assisted the applicant to prepare his Statement of Affairs filed on 19 October 1993 and no explanation was given as to why Mr Metcalf did not provide an affidavit on these matters.

55. The applicant's evidence was that when the assessments were issued he wanted to lodge objections but that Mr Metcalf and also an officer of the Australian Taxation Office told him that he could not lodge objections unless he first paid the tax which formed the subject matter of the assessments. Consequently, as he was not in a position to make payment, he did not lodge any objections.

56. On this aspect a finding of credibility may be relevant to the disposal of the application because it goes to the question of whether the applicant has a reasonably arguable case to obtain (via the Administrative Appeals Tribunal proceedings) an extension of time in which to lodge objections.

57. Whilst I do not believe the applicant's evidence on this point (particularly as there was no evidence from Mr Metcalf), it may be that the Administrative Appeals Tribunal will form a different opinion on the applicant's credibility. If so, the Tribunal may decide that the applicant should have an extension of time in which to lodge objections. Where credibility is thus a major factor it is inappropriate to hold that the applicant does not have a reasonably arguable case to obtain from the Tribunal an extension of time in which to lodge objections.

58. On that assumption, the next question is whether the applicant has adduced evidence establishing the substantial nature of the grounds of objection to the extent that it could be said he has a reasonably arguable case to get the three income tax assessments set aside?

59. In my opinion the applicant has not done so. As the authorities indicate, it is not sufficient merely to rely on assertions. Mr Hoey's evidence on this point can only be described as assertions. In paragraph 11 of his affidavit sworn 16 September 1993, Mr Hoey states:

"I verily believe that the amended assessments do not

appear correct and I believe that it would be a gross

injustice if the assessments are allowed to stand".

In paragraph 2 of his affidavit sworn 30 September 1993 Mr Hoey states:

"I have now conducted a full review of all papers

forwarded to the applicant pursuant to the Freedom of

Information Act request .... On the basis of the

documents which I have perused from the ATO's files I

have formed the conclusion that the ATO has levied tax

against the Applicant in respect of income that is not

properly assessable."

Mr Hoey exhibited the commission agreement to that affidavit and made adjustments excluding the amount of $197,212.00 from the applicant's assessable income for the years in question and then stated:

"... the ATO has taken the view that the work

performed by the Applicant should be classified as

personal exertion income and therefore properly

included in the personal income tax returns of the

Applicant rather than in the tax returns of Pollock

Nominees Pty Ltd. In my opinion the ATO has taken an

incorrect view of the commission agreement and thereby

assessed tax on $197,212.00 as against the Applicant

when tax should have been assessed on that figure as

against Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd."

60. The only evidence bearing on this point which was adduced was the unexecuted copy of the commission agreement and some advice given by Messrs Parker and Parker that the commissions earned from the "casino profits" (which I took to be a reference to the profits made on the earthworks for the Burswood Casino) would at worst be regarded as taxable in the hands of T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd and Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd.

61. The commission agreement describes the work which T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd and Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd was required to do in the following terms:

. advise the Owner (Romel Pty Ltd) in respect of all preliminary

steps the Owner should take to enable it to promptly prepare

(sic) and submit a tender for the Project when tenders are

called;

. assist the Owner in the preparation of the tender documents and

all other supporting documentation;

. at the request of the Owner negotiate the terms of any tender

or the terms of any contract arising out of the tender to the

best advantage of the Owner; and

. use their best endeavours and their contacts and negotiating

skills to procure the acceptance of the Owner's tender or the

award of the contract for the Project in favour of the Owner.

62. The documents annexed to Ms Rice's affidavit sworn 2 November 1993 make it abundantly clear that the first respondent based its income tax assessments on his opinion that all of the commission paid to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd was in fact income derived by him from personal exertion.

63. If the applicant had any evidence upon which to attack this basis for the assertion he should have adduced it in support of this application to annul the sequestration order. For example, if it had been necessary for Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd to have engaged other employees or consultants or to have used equipment to enable it or the applicant to carry out the work necessary to earn the commission then this evidence should have been led. In my opinion the evidence led by the applicant did not demonstrate, even on a prima facie basis, that there was a substantial ground of objection to the income tax assessments. There was simply insufficient evidence for that judgment to be made. Accordingly I propose to dismiss the application to annul the sequestration order.

Discretion

64. Having reached that conclusion, it is strictly not necessary for me to consider the question whether, if I am wrong in that conclusion and accordingly the sequestration order ought not to have been made, as a matter of discretion that order should be annulled.

65. However, it may be of some assistance if I consider the various submissions which were made in support of the proposition that even if the sequestration order ought not to have been made, I should exercise my discretion against annulling that order. I deal with those submissions in turn.

1. That the applicant is hopelessly insolvent

66. This submission was made on behalf of the first respondent and the third respondents.

67. The first respondent points to the Statement of Affairs filed by the applicant on 19 October 1993. That statement shows liabilities of $19,682,000.00 and assets totalling $6,300.00. The applicant responds by saying that aside from his liabilities to the first and third respondents his debts are largely what was described as "guarantee debts". It was submitted that the likelihood of the principal obligations in respect of those guarantees being called up was unknown, that any inability on the part of the principal debtors to satisfy the financiers was also unknown and that none of the financiers has opposed the application to annul and that one of them (Custom Credit Corporation Ltd) has been prepared to say so in writing. What Custom Credit in fact said in writing was contained in an annexure to the applicant's affidavit sworn on 17 November 1993 and was as follows:

"We believe that Custom Credit Corporation Ltd is the

largest creditor of Mr Pollock, however, we have

played no part in the bankruptcy proceedings nor have

we been officially contacted at any stage regarding

same.

Custom Credit has not in the past advocated the

bankruptcy of Mr Pollock nor does it presently support

such proceedings. Notwithstanding, the Corporation

reserves it rights to take any action it deems fit in

future under its security documentation."

68. The Statement of Affairs indicates that the liability of the applicant to Custom Credit Corporation Ltd in respect of moneys owing by Kinham Pty Ltd and in respect of personal guarantees for equipment purchased by various companies is in excess of $11 million.

69. The next largest single amount shown as owing to an unsecured creditor was an amount of $1,200,000.00 in respect of a guarantee. The creditor's name was shown as T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd and the nature of debt described as "guarantee Shell". In view of the applicant's involvement with transport companies I have taken that debt to arise out of a guarantee of moneys owing by T.K.J Nominees Pty Ltd in the sum of $1,200,000.00 for petroleum products. No evidence was given as to the applicant's ability to meet that obligation. Another unsecured debt of $260,000.00 was shown in the applicant's Statement of Affairs as owing to "NCH Hydraulics" and Peat Resources of Australia Pty Ltd. A further amount of $110,000.00 was shown as owing in respect of a guarantee for rent owing by Peat Resources of Australia Pty Ltd. An amount of $2,000.00 was shown in the same statement as owing to Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd. The applicant exhibited three letters in identical terms from Peat Resources of Australia Pty Ltd, NCH Hydraulic Systems (Australia) Pty Ltd and Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd which included the statement "we wish to confirm our support to have the sequestration order annulled". Those letters were signed by P.W. Pollock, N.J Pollock and J Pollock. The evidence was that these signatories were Mr Pollock's brothers.

70. The applicant's Statement of Affairs also shows a sum owing of $700,000 which in evidence was explained as being in respect of a personal guarantee to the Rural and Industries Bank of Western Australia Ltd ("the RandI Bank") of certain indebtedness of Kinham Pty Ltd.

71. Over objection from the applicant, I admitted some evidence in relation to the RandI Bank debt. That evidence included a paragraph in an affidavit sworn by a Mr Thomason on 14 December 1993 to the effect that on or about 1 November 1993 Mr Thomason had spoken to Mr Frank Romanin of the RandI Bank who stated that the R and I Bank had issued a notice of default pursuant to a guarantee signed by the applicant in favour of the RandI Bank against the applicant in the sum of $760,000.00. Objection was also taken to the annexure to Mr Romanin's affidavit of a copy letter from the applicant's solicitors to the RandI Bank which was addressed to The Chief Bank Solicitor and was dated 14 October 1993, a month after the present application to annul had been filed. Omitting formal parts that letter reads as follows:

"We act for Mr Kevin Pollock, Mrs Frances Pollock and

Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd.

We have to hand your notices of demand dated 6

September 1993 to each of the above parties. Those

notices of demand appear to be based upon a Deed of

Guarantee and Indemnity dated 10 August 1988 to secure

financial accommodation granted by the R and I Bank to

Kinham Pty Ltd.

Upon our reading of that document, Pollock Nominees

Pty Ltd is not a signatory to the guarantee and

therefore is not bound as a guarantor. In respect of

Mr Kevin Pollock we advise that he was made bankrupt

pursuant to a sequestration order made in the Federal

Court of Australia on 7 September 1993. We are also

instructed that Mrs Pollock has no personal assets.

In respect of Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd could you

please confirm that you shall not be proceeding

against that company in purported reliance on the Deed

of Guarantee and Indemnity dated 10 August 1988.

Could you also please advise us of your intentions

with respect to Mr and Mrs Pollock in the light of their

financial circumstances.

We look forward to hearing from you."

72. In so far as the objection to the admission of this evidence was based on the fact that it was hearsay I rely on Rule 141A of the Bankruptcy Rules to admit it and I consider that the letter referred to above is relevant to the question of Mr Pollock's insolvency and also to his conduct since the sequestration order was made. It would seem to me that the applicant, through his solicitors, was seeking to use the fact that a sequestration order had been made to deter the RandI Bank from pursuing the applicant. No mention is made in that letter of the application to annul the sequestration order. It is to be noted that the RandI Bank was not amongst the creditors to whom a copy of this application was sent - see Mr Burvill's affidavit sworn 8 November 1993.

73. I also admitted into evidence an affidavit from Mr Romanin sworn 20 December 1993. In summary, Mr Romanin deposes to the fact that he is a solicitor employed by the RandI Bank having control of the litigation department within that Bank, exhibits a copy of a deed of guarantee and indemnity guarantee dated 10 August 1988 with the RandI Bank whereby the applicant guaranteed to the RandI Bank on demand all moneys due and owing by Kinham Pty Ltd. Mr Romanin also exhibits a notice of demand dated 6 September 1993 sent to the applicant and the applicant's solicitor's letter acknowledging service of that notice. Mr Romanin concludes his affidavit by stating that in the event the sequestration order is set aside the Bank intends to proceed against the bankrupt and that the Bank has not issued proceedings as it awaiting the outcome of this application.

74. The applicant's counsel objected to this evidence (and the evidence adduced through Mr Thomason referred to above) on the basis that the disclosures comprised within that evidence involved a breach of the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth). The breach was said to be with respect to information concerning the applicant, the principal debtor, Kinham Pty Ltd and the other guarantors. Even if, for the purposes of considering this point, one were to assume that the passing on of the information referred to above between Mr Romanin and Mr Thomason and the passing on of the information contained in Mr Romanin's affidavit constituted an act or practice which was an interference with the privacy of an individual, nothing in the Privacy Act suggests that such evidence thereby becomes inadmissible in a court of law. The sanctions for interference with privacy are limited and do not expressly nor in my opinion impliedly extend to requiring the rejection of evidence relevant to the disposal of this matter. I was certainly not cited any authority for that proposition. If it were necessary, before admitting such evidence for this Court to exercise the type of discretion referred to in Bunning v. Cross [1978] HCA 22; (1978) 141 CLR 54 I would have no hesitation in doing so in this matter. I can see no relevant unfairness to any person in taking such a course.

75. I consider that the matter of the RandI Bank debt is an important one. The applicant and his wife and two others executed the deed of guarantee and indemnity by which, in summary, the guarantors irrevocably and unconditionally guarantee to the RandI Bank payment by Kinham Pty Ltd of all moneys which the RandI Bank had at the time of the deed advanced to Kinham Pty Ltd or which the RandI Bank subsequently lent to that company. The limit of the guarantee and indemnity is expressed as being $700,000.00.

76. The notice of demand, a copy of which was exhibited to Mr Romanin's affidavit, states that Kinham Pty Ltd as at 6 September 1993 owed the RandI Bank $614,901.79 in respect of current account number 497718-4 with daily interest accruing at the rate of $212.93 and a further amount of $102,684.09 in respect of an In-Reduction Loan account number 89-497719-2 with daily interest accruing at the rate of $35.56. The amount specified in the notice of demand served on the applicant was the maximum provided for in the deed namely $700,000.00.

77. In cross-examination the applicant said that he believed that the principal debt was secured over property and that he would only be liable under the guarantee if that property were sold at a loss. It is quite clear from clause 4 of the deed that the RandI Bank has the right to recover from the guarantors without being obliged to take any such course. The applicant further denied in cross-examination that he was liable as guarantor as a matter of law but his evidence (not surprisingly) was somewhat vague in that regard. Nevertheless, I think that it is significant that when the applicant passed the notice of demand to his solicitors and they responded to the RandI Bank by the letter dated 14 October 1993 referred to above there was no denial of liability on the applicant's part. Counsel for the applicant submitted that there was a dearth of evidence as to the financial arrangements between Kinham Pty Ltd and the RandI Bank, the value of the security and the value of any claims against the co-guarantors. In the light of the exhibits to Mr Romanin's affidavit and specifically the particulars contained in the notice of demand, I think that sufficient evidence is before the Court to form a prima facie assessment of the position especially as there is no evidence led from the applicant which puts in doubt the RandI Bank's claim.

78. There is also a Supreme Court judgment in favour of the third respondents against the applicant in the amount of $264,949.08 entered (after a trial) on 17 August 1993. I have read the reasons for judgment in that matter and the notice of appeal and I do not think that it can be said that the notice of appeal is without substance as it raises at least one serious question of law. If that judgment debt had been the basis for a petition and given the circumstances of the appeal having been lodged, I think a petition based on the judgment would have been adjourned.

79. Nevertheless on a review of all of the evidence I consider that the first and third respondents have made good their submission that the applicant is insolvent. This is a ground relevant to the exercise of the discretion in this matter: Re Dennehy; Ex parte Dennehy (1895) 16 LR (NSW) BandP 40.

The conduct of the applicant during his bankruptcy

80. The third respondent submitted that the cumulative effect of certain of the applicant's conduct during his bankruptcy should also be considered as weighing against the exercise of any discretion to annul the sequestration order: Re Gray (1961) 19 ABC 29.

Failure to disclose certain shareholdings

81. The first aspect of such conduct was the applicant's failure to disclose what were described as "beneficial interests in property". The evidence was that this comprised shareholdings in the following companies:

(a) Zone Holdings Pty Ltd. The applicant was shown to be the holder of

one ordinary fully paid $1 share in this company in which a total of six

ordinary $1 shares have been issued. The latest annual return for that

company put into evidence shows a shareholder's deficiency of

$382,830.00.

(b) Pollock Nominees Pty Ltd. The applicant holds one of the two fully

paid $1 ordinary shares issued in this company. The latest annual

return shows a total shareholder's equity in this company of $2.

(c) Metro Sands Supplies Pty Ltd. The applicant holds one fully paid

$1 ordinary share out of a total of 1002 shares. The latest annual

return shows shareholders equity in this company as totalling $1002.

82. The applicant in cross-examination explained that he regarded the expression "shares" as being shares that were "... readily tradeable, ... something that you buy and sell".

83. Whilst it is true that the applicant did not disclose these interests in his Statement of Affairs, I would not regard this non-disclosure as being significant were I exercising a discretion to annul the sequestration order.

84. The next matter raised by the third respondents was the fact that the applicant failed to disclose to a creditor, namely the RandI Bank that he was making an application to annul his bankruptcy. The evidence relating to this is set out above and in summary related to the fact that whilst the applicant served other creditors with a copy of the amended application in this matter he did not so serve the RandI Bank and by letter dated 14 October 1993 his solicitors sought to use the applicant's bankruptcy to prevent the RandI Bank pursuing the applicant and furthermore made no mention in that letter to this pending application.

85. I would regard that particular conduct as weighing against the exercise of a discretion to annul the sequestration order but not particularly heavily.

Failure to disclose directorships

86. The evidence led by the third respondents showed that the applicant had within five years of the date of his Statement of Affairs been a director of Boronga Pty Ltd, Kinham Pty Ltd, Peat Resources of Australia Pty Ltd, Zone Holdings Pty Ltd and Multi-Transport Holdings Ltd and had not disclosed this information in that statement. The applicant disclosed directorships in other companies. Once again whilst I would regard these non-disclosures as being relevant and weighing against the exercise of any discretion to annul the sequestration order, I would not regard them as being on their own determinative in respect of that discretion.

87. Nonetheless, on a cumulative basis I feel that the conduct of the applicant during the relatively short period of his bankruptcy fell short of that required and should be taken into account as part of the exercise of any discretion to annul the sequestration order.

Whether the bankrupt would be entitled to an early discharge from bankruptcy

88. The third respondent referred to Re Keat (1905) 2 KB 666 at p 671 as authority for the proposition that "in the absence of special circumstances it is not a good exercise of discretion to make an order of annulment where, if the bankrupt were applying for his order of discharge, an order of discharge would not be granted." That was a case in which the bulk of the bankrupt's creditors executed a deed releasing him absolutely from his debts without any consideration and agreed to withdraw their proofs. The bankrupt paid the remaining creditors in full in cash and then applied for an annulment on the grounds that his debts had been "paid in full". The Court of Appeal held that there had been no "payment in full" and Stirling LJ made the above comment in relation to the exercise of discretion on the question of annulment. No subsequent cases either English or Australian were cited for this proposition. In the absence of any binding or more persuasive authority, it seems to me to be inconsistent to condition the exercise of a discretion to annul under s.153B of the Bankruptcy Act on whether an applicant would be barred from obtaining an early discharge under the provisions relied upon by the third respondents in this matter namely sections 149Z and 149Y of the Bankruptcy Act.

89. In terms of the application of s.149Z it is true that the applicant failed to disclose his beneficial interest in the shares referred to above. In terms of s.149Y the evidence shows that the applicant's income is $37,000 per annum and his unsecured liabilities are well in excess of 150% of that amount.

90. I have expressed my view in relation to the weight to be given to the first of these factors. Although it should not be counted twice in the exercise, I consider that both these factors are simply ones that should be taken into account when exercising the discretion under s.153B.

91. On weighing the cumulative effect of all of the abovementioned factors I consider that any discretion should be exercised against annulling the petition. The factor which I regard as of greatest importance in that discretionary judgment is the insolvency of the applicant.

Conclusion

92. For the above reasons I would dismiss the application because I do not think that it has been shown that the sequestration order "ought not to have been made". If that conclusion is wrong then, as a matter of discretion, I would not consider that it would be appropriate to annul the sequestration order. Accordingly the application will be dismissed with costs.
