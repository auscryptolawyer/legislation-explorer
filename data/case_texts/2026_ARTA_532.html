Brighton-Stangstins and Australian Information Commissioner [2026] ARTA 532 (30 March 2026)

Applicant: Jason Brighton-Stangstins

Respondent: Australian Information Commissioner

Other Parties: ANZ Banking Group

Tribunal Number: 2023/2417

Tribunal: Deputy President P Britten-Jones

Place: Brisbane

Date: 30 March 2026

Decision: The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

Catchwords

PRIVACY – Australian Privacy Principle 12 – waiver of legal professional privilege – interference of privacy by failing to give access to personal information - obligation to give access to information in the manner requested – obligation to give access in a way that meets the needs of the entity and the individual - consideration of what is information ‘about’ an individual – consideration of what is a serious interference with privacy – reimbursement of expenses under the Privacy Act 1988 – decision under review affirmed

Legislation

Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)

Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth)

Cases

Jason Brighton-Stangstins and ANZ Bank Ltd (Privacy) [2023] AICmr 19

Mann v Carnell [1999] HCA 66; (1999) 201 CLR 1

Osland v Secretary, Department of Justice (2008) 234 CLR 275

Privacy Commissioner v Telstra Corporation [2017] FCAFC 4; (2017) 249 FCR 24

Statement of Reasons

  1. This is an application for review of a decision (the Decision) made by the delegate of the Respondent on 31 March 2023: Jason Brighton-Stangstins and ANZ Bank Ltd (Privacy) [2023] AICmr 19.
  2. On 24 March 2019, the Applicant lodged a privacy complaint with the Respondent under s 36 of the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) (Privacy Act). He alleged that the other party (ANZ) interfered with his privacy by failing to provide access to his personal information as required by the Australian Privacy Principles (APP). Prior to making his complaint the Applicant had made three requests to access his personal information from ANZ. For the purposes of this application, the Tribunal need only consider the second access request which, in effect, includes the third access request.
  3. The second access request was for documents in ANZ’s possession regarding the Applicant’s dispute with ANZ including documents relating to a ‘systemic issues’ investigation by the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS) and a FOS file. ANZ refused the request on 20 March 2019.
  4. On 2 October 2019, the Respondent advised the Applicant and the ANZ that a formal investigation into the Applicant’s privacy complaint would be opened. The Respondent issued a preliminary view with respect to the complaint on 5 August 2022. On 31 March 2023, the delegate of the Respondent made a decision under s 52 of the Privacy Act. This is the decision which is reviewable by the Tribunal.

The decision under review

  1. The delegate of the Respondent relevantly found or declared as follows:
    (a) ANZ did not comply with APP 12.1 or APP 12.3 in relation to 14 documents marked “without prejudice” and 14 documents marked as “strictly confidential”.

    (b) Documents subject to legal professional privilege were subject to an exception at APP 12.3, and the ANZ has sufficiently established that the exception applied.

    (c) ANZ failed to provide access to the Applicant within a reasonable period following the receipt of the access request.

    (d) ANZ has interfered with the Applicant’s privacy by:

    (i) failing to provide access to the Applicant within a reasonable period following receipt of the second access request, and

    (ii) refusing to provide access to the Applicant’s personal information contained in the Without Prejudice Documents and Confidential Documents in breach of a PP 12.1.

    (e) ANZ must not repeat or continue this conduct.

    (f) ANZ must:

    (i) provide the Applicant with their personal information contained in the Without Prejudice Documents and Confidential Documents, and

    (ii) send this information to an address nominated by the Applicant within 30 days.

  2. ANZ does not contest the Decision and says that it has complied with it. In particular, ANZ says that it complied with the requirement of the Decision to provide the Applicant with his personal information contained in the Without Prejudice Documents and the Confidential Documents by giving him extracts of that information and in some cases the documents themselves.

Issues

  1. The first issue is whether ANZ has waived legal professional privilege.
  2. The second issue is whether ANZ has interfered with the privacy of the Applicant by breaching the APP and whether there is any further personal information held by ANZ to which access should be given. This would include the Applicant’s contention that the Tribunal should inspect documents held by ANZ for the purpose of determining whether ANZ has complied with the APP.
  3. The third issue is whether the Applicant is entitled to be reimbursed for any expenses or costs.

Legal professional privilege

  1. If an APP entity holds personal information about an individual, the entity must give access to the information pursuant to APP 12.1 unless an exception to access applies. Numerous exceptions are set out in APP 12.3 including that an entity is not required to give access to personal information to the extent that giving access would be unlawful pursuant to APP 12.3(f) or denying access is required or authorised by or under an Australian law, pursuant to APP 12.3(g). There is some overlap between APP 12.3 (f) and (g). Unlawful activity is not defined in the Privacy Act but would include a breach of legal professional privilege.[1] Australian law is defined to include a rule of common law which would include the common law doctrine of legal professional privilege.[2]
  2. The Applicant accepted that if the personal information was covered by legal professional privilege, then an exception to the APP 12.1 applied. The Applicant contended that legal professional privilege does not apply because it had been waived by the conduct of the ANZ. The particular conduct of the ANZ relied upon as waiver is the following statement in a letter from a case manager in the office of the customer advocate in the ANZ to the Applicant dated 23 December 2019:

When ANZ identifies or is notified of potential systemic issues impacting its customers, ANZ is required to take steps to remediate the issues. ANZ has processes and procedures in place in such instances to identify customers who may have been impacted by the issue, and to compensate them for the loss incurred.

ANZ was under no obligation to act on David Robertson QC’s legal advice to FOS or remediate customers in line with his suggested resolution. As you will appreciate, legal opinions vary on legal questions raised that are yet to be resolved definitively. ANZ was entitled to seek its own counsel with respect to the matter to consider its position, and remediate customers accordingly.

  1. The relevant background to the ANZ letter is as follows.
  2. On 20 August 2011, the Applicant lodged a complaint with FOS about ANZ. The complaint related to ANZ’s fees, charges and related practices. I will refer to this complaint as the Applicant’s FOS dispute.
  3. David Robertson QC provided a legal opinion (the Robertson opinion) to FOS on 16 November 2012. A copy of the Robertson opinion was provided to the ANZ on 14 December 2012 but was not provided to the Applicant until much later.
  4. In December 2012, FOS initiated a “systemic issues investigation” (hereinafter referred to as the FOS systemic issues investigation.
  5. On 25 February 2013, FOS suspended consideration of the Applicant’s FOS dispute while they considered whether it could be a systemic issue.
  6. On 9 October 2013 the Applicant commenced proceedings in the County Court of Victoria against the ANZ.
  7. On 22 October 2013, FOS discontinued its consideration of the Applicant’s FOS dispute due to the commencement of the proceedings in the County Court of Victoria which it regarded as relating to the Applicant’s FOS dispute.
  8. The County Court proceedings settled out of court in about 2014.
  9. On 22 February 2019, the Applicant wrote to the ANZ complaining that the Robertson opinion had been withheld from him and that the ANZ had not refunded interest to cardholders in accordance with the Robertson opinion.
  10. It was in the context of the Applicant’s complaint relating to ANZ not remediating customers in line with the Robertson opinion that the office of the customer advocate of the ANZ wrote to the Applicant on 23 December 2019. In that letter, ANZ explained it was under no obligation to act on the Robertson opinion and said that ‘legal opinions vary on legal questions raised’ and that ‘ANZ was entitled to seek its own counsel ... and remediate customers accordingly.’ Whilst there was no express statement that ANZ had sought and obtained legal advice, it could be inferred from the letter that ANZ had obtained its own legal advice as to whether to remediate customers and that the advice received differed from the advice given to FOS in the Robertson opinion. The question for the Tribunal is whether ANZ impliedly waived its privilege by making those statements.
  11. In Mann v Carnell,[3] the majority said:

Legal professional privilege exists to protect the confidentiality of communications between lawyer and client. It is the client who is entitled to the benefit of such confidentiality, and who may relinquish that entitlement. It is inconsistency between the conduct of the client and maintenance of the confidentiality which effects a waiver of the privilege. ...

Waiver may be express or implied. Disputes as to implied waiver usually arise from the need to decide whether particular conduct is inconsistent with the maintenance of confidentiality which the privilege is intended to protect. ... What brings about the waiver is the inconsistency, courts, where necessary informed by considerations of fairness, perceive, between the conduct of the client and maintenance of the confidentiality; not some overriding principle of fairness operating at large.

  1. The High Court decision of Osland v Secretary, Department of Justice is instructive because it involved partial disclosure of advice for the purpose of explaining or justifying certain action. [4] It is apposite to this application for review that the Court considered that the relevant context included the nature of the matter in respect of which the advice was received, the evident purpose in making the disclosure that was made, and the legal and practical consequences of limited rather than complete disclosure.[5]
  2. First, with respect to the nature of the matter in respect of which advice was received, the statutory declaration of ANZ’s Belinda Thompson dated 18 February 2022 provides details of confidential communications prepared in relation to the County Court proceedings and the Applicant’s FOS dispute and FOS systemic issues investigation. Ms Thompson provides her opinion that the categories of documents identified meet the elements of legal professional privilege because they are confidential communications prepared for the dominant purpose of providing legal advice to ANZ (or ANZ seeking legal advice) in relation to the County Court proceedings or the FOS issues, or providing legal services to ANZ (or ANZ seeking legal services) in relation to the County Court proceedings or anticipated litigation arising from the FOS issues.[6]
  3. It is relevant that at the time of the alleged waiver in December 2019 there was no investigation or litigation on foot. This was not a case of a party to litigation deploying a partial disclosure for forensic advantage, while seeking unfairly to deny the other party an opportunity to see the full text of the privileged communication.[7]
  4. Second, with respect to purpose, the ANZ was explaining and justifying its conduct in response to an issue raised by the Applicant, namely that the ANZ had not provided the Robertson opinion to him and that the ANZ had not acted in accordance with that advice. ANZ’s response was that the Robertson opinion was ‘legal advice to FOS’ which ANZ was under no obligation to follow and that ANZ was entitled to seek its own advice and to remediate customers accordingly. The ANZ’s conduct, viewed objectively, was not to obtain some forensic advantage. The objective purpose of the response was to explain the issue raised by the Applicant. In providing the explanation, the ANZ did not disclose the substance or even the gist of the advice. The letter does not disclose the content of any legal advice. At most it could be said that ANZ impliedly disclosed the fact of obtaining advice but not even the conclusion of that advice, except that it was different to the advice in the Robertson opinion. A statement that ‘legal opinions vary’ discloses nothing of substance about the content or conclusion of advice. This did not involve ‘inconsistency’; and it involved no unfairness to the Applicant.
  5. Third, with respect to limited rather than complete disclosure, the High Court said in Osland that whether, in a given context, a limited disclosure of the existence, and the effect, of legal advice is inconsistent with maintaining confidentiality in the terms of advice will depend upon the circumstances of the case.[8] In this case, there was not even a limited disclosure of the effect of advice and therefore ANZ has not acted inconsistently with the maintenance of privilege. Simply referring to the existence of legal advice in a communication does not mean that the privilege holder has acted inconsistently with the maintenance of privilege in that advice.[9]
  6. I conclude that there was no waiver of legal professional privilege and that the Decision under review was the correct or preferable decision in that regard.

The issue relating to giving access to Personal information

  1. The Applicant contends that there is personal information of his to which he has not been given access. Further, he says that the Respondent has failed to give him access to his personal information in the manner requested by him.
  2. The Applicant seeks access to his personal information in the documents relating to the systemic issues investigation (hereinafter referred to as the systemic issues documents).[10] ANZ contends that the FOS systemic issues investigation was not specific to the Applicant’s circumstance and, as such, information relating to that investigation is not, of itself, information or opinion about the Applicant.
  3. The Applicant wants the Decision set aside and an order that he be provided with this further personal information in the manner requested by him.
  4. The Applicant objects to being provided with his personal information in the form of extracts from the documents in which his personal information is contained. Rather, the Applicant wants a copy of the document containing his personal information with any other information redacted. The Applicant contends that the extracts do not meet his needs because he cannot tell from those extracts if there has been any inappropriate redactions. He wants a copy of the documents containing the personal information because then he will be able to determine the extent of the redactions and identify any incorrect redactions.
  5. APP 12 obliges an entity to give an individual access to personal information. The information may be recorded in a material form (ie in a document) or not. [11] Unlike Part 3 of the Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth) which provides for access to documents, APP 12 is clearly directed to providing access to personal information. However, the Applicant relies on APP 12.4(b) which obliges an entity to give access to the information in the manner requested by the individual, if it is reasonable and practicable to do so. The Applicant requested documents in his email dated 4 June 2018. He maintains an entitlement to be provided with the personal information by way of documents because that is the manner of access requested by him and it is reasonable and practicable for him to be given access to the personal information in that manner.
  6. The obligation in APP 12.4 should be read in conjunction with APP 12.5 which provides relevantly that if the entity refuses to give access in the manner requested, the entity must take such steps (if any) as are reasonable to give access in a way that meets the needs of the entity and the individual. It follows that if it is reasonable and practicable to do so, the entity must give access to the information in the manner requested by the individual. But if it is not reasonable and practicable, then the entity may refuse to give access in the manner requested but must take such steps (if any) as are reasonable to give access in a way that meets the needs of the entity and the individual.
  7. ANZ contends that it was not reasonable and practicable to give access to the information by way of redacting documents because:[12]

it would require redaction of... almost all the document, except to the extent that the document includes the complainant’s name, on the basis that the redacted parts of the document do not contain personal information about the complainant.

  1. The approach taken by ANZ, as reflected in the letter dated 28 February 2022, was to describe and give the date of the document which contained personal information of the Applicant. Instead of providing a redacted document, ANZ provided an extract of the personal information in Schedule 3 to the 28 February 2022 letter.[13] ANZ explained that Schedule 3 sets out the personal information about the Applicant that is held by ANZ in documents that primarily relate to the FOS systemic issues investigation. The extracts in Schedule 3 included references to the Applicant’s FOS case number, his name and sentences in which the Applicant was named. I note that the personal information in the extracts is consistent with the common examples of personal information given in the APP Guidelines at B.89 namely:

an individual’s name, signature, address, telephone number, date of birth, medical records, bank account details, employment details and commentary or opinion about a person.

  1. In addition to the extracts provided in Schedule 3 of the 28 February 2022 letter, ANZ provided in Schedule 1 a copy of complete documents, being communications between it and FOS and internal file notes. Further, in Schedule 2, ANZ provided a copy of documents redacted for legal professional privilege.
  2. It is apparent from the schedules to the 28 February 2022 letter that ANZ has provided to the Applicant some complete documents and some partially redacted documents. In addition, where there is personal information in a document limited to a name, file number or short phrase, ANZ has extracted this personal information and provided it to the Applicant. The systemic issues documents were voluminous and the personal information contained therein was relatively minor and therefore I consider that it was not reasonable and practicable for ANZ to provide those documents in a redacted form. There is no utility in the Applicant being provided with a large amount of blank paper. Further, I consider that it was reasonable in the circumstances, for the purposes of APP 12.5(b), for ANZ to provide extracts of the personal information because that was a way to meet the needs of both the ANZ and the Applicant. It met the needs of ANZ because it was able to avoid the cost and impracticability of redacting voluminous pages of documents and it met the needs of the Applicant because he was provided with access to the personal information to which he was entitled under the APP.
  3. ANZ contends that there is no basis for release of further information to the Applicant. The issue for the Tribunal is whether there is any personal information held by ANZ to which the Applicant has not been given access. The Commissioner in the Decision under review decided that ANZ had sufficiently provided access to the Applicant’s personal information from the systemic issues documents by giving the Applicant extracts from those documents in the letter dated 28 February 2022.[14]
  4. ANZ says that the Applicant’s second request relates to personal information from two separate procedures. First is the Applicant’s FOS dispute. Second is the FOS systemic issues investigation. In a letter[15] dated 20 February 2019 from the Australian Financial Complaints Authority which succeeded FOS, there is the following explanation with respect to a systemic issue investigation:

A systemic issue investigation happens independently of any individual case. That is, if it is identified that an issue affecting a particular customer may also affect other customers, we will investigate with the financial firm involved whether or not other customers have been affected, and if so, take steps to ensure the issue is rectified. ...

The individual customers that have raised their concerns with us are not kept informed of the progress of the broader systemic issue investigation.

  1. FOS began its systemic issues investigation in about December 2012 and ceased consideration of the Applicant’s FOS dispute in October 2013 after having suspended it from February 2013. This is important because the focus of the investigation turned from the individual to a class of persons potentially affected by a systemic issue.
  2. The Applicant contends that given the systemic issue investigation was commenced because of the Applicant’s own complaint, any information that describes personal circumstances of members of the class will be reasonably identifiable as personal information of the Applicant. I reject that contention because the information being considered relates to whether there is a systemic issue rather than whether an individual has a valid complaint. In other words, the information contained within the systemic issues documents is not ‘about’ the Applicant for the purposes of the definition of personal information.
  3. Personal information has the same meaning as in the Privacy Act which relevantly provides that personal information means information or an opinion about an identified individual or an individual who is reasonably identifiable.
  4. The Full Court of the Federal Court in Privacy Commissioner v Telstra Corporation[16] said that the concept of personal information is very broad but that it was constrained by the requirements that:

(i) it must be held by the organisation;

(ii) it must be “about” the individual who requested access; and

(iii) it must be about an individual whose identity is apparent, or can reasonably be ascertained, from the information or opinion.

  1. It follows that if the information in the systemic issues documents is not about the Applicant, then it will not be personal information. Further guidance is provided by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Privacy Commissioner v Telstra Corporation[17] who said that the words “about an individual” direct attention to the need for the individual to be a subject matter of the information or opinion. It is clear from the description of the systemic issues investigation that the Applicant is not the subject matter of the information. That is not to say that the information in the FOS systemic issues investigation does not relate to the Applicant or does not have a connection with him, but it is not “about” him.[18]
  2. The Applicant drew my attention to some specific documents from which extracts were provided but which the Applicant claimed should have been fully disclosed because he considered that the whole of the document was personal information. The Applicant was able to make this argument because the FOS had provided him with a full copy of some of the documents in issue.
  3. The first document is the FOS letter to ANZ dated 14 December 2012,[19] the extracts from which were provided to the Applicant in Schedule 3 to the ANZ’s letter dated 28 February 2022.[20] The subject of the letter is “Possible Systemic Issue”. Where there is reference to FOS’s investigation of the Applicant’s complaints regarding the ANZ (defined herein as the Applicant’s FOS dispute), those references have been extracted from the letter as personal information of the Applicant. Contrary to the contention of the Applicant, I consider that this is an example of ANZ correctly identifying personal information held by it and providing access to the Applicant.[21] The balance of the letter not extracted[22] was not personal information of the Applicant because it was general information about a systemic issues investigation.
  4. The second document is the FOS letter to David Robertson dated 5 February 2013,[23] the extracts from which were provided to the Applicant in Schedule 3 to the ANZ’s letter dated 28 February 2022.[24] Similar to the first document,[25] there are significant passages of information which are not about the Applicant.
  5. The third document is a letter from FOS to ANZ dated 11 December 2013,[26] the extracts from which were provided to the Applicant in Schedule 3 to the ANZ’s letter dated 28 February 2022.[27] The subject of the letter is “Definite systemic issue” but the references to FOS’s investigation of the Applicant’s complaints regarding the ANZ (defined herein as the Applicant’s FOS dispute) have been extracted from the letter as personal information of the Applicant. Contrary to the contention of the Applicant, I consider that this is an example of ANZ correctly identifying personal information held by it and providing access to the Applicant. The balance of the letter not extracted was not personal information of the Applicant because it was general information about a systemic issues investigation.
  6. The Applicant gave further examples of documents in annexure JBS-15 to the Applicant’s amended statement of facts, issues and contentions. The Applicant objected to only being provided with extracts of personal information as opposed to the whole document. In my view the Applicant’s objection is not supported by APP 12 and the definition of personal information. ANZ has correctly identified from the information held by it the information that is about the Applicant whose identity is apparent from that information. The balance of the information (not extracted) is not personal information because it is not about the Applicant and the Applicant cannot be identified from the balance of the information.
  7. The Applicant referred to the above documents as examples of ANZ failing to provide appropriate access to his personal information and, further, as a reason for the Tribunal inspecting all of the relevant documents including the systemic issues documents. The Applicant also claimed that ANZ made misrepresentations with respect to contents of the personal information held by it. Given my findings that ANZ adopted the appropriate approach to identifying personal information, I reject the claims of misrepresentations and I do not consider there is any need for the Tribunal to carry out an inspection. In my view there is nothing to suggest that there is personal information requested by the Applicant to which he has not been given access.
  8. Whilst I am satisfied that the ANZ provided adequate access to the Applicant’s personal information, it remains the case that ANZ failed to provide access to the Applicant within a reasonable period following receipt of the access request. This was a finding made in the Decision[28] which is not contested by ANZ. It follows that there was a breach by ANZ of the APP and therefore an interference with the privacy of an individual pursuant to s 13(1) of the Privacy Act. The Applicant contends that this was a serious interference with privacy and that a civil penalty should be imposed under s 13G of the Privacy Act.
  9. It was noted in the Decision[29] under review that ANZ had failed to provide access to the Applicant within a reasonable period following receipt of the access request. However, since the Decision was made, ANZ has provided access to the Applicant in accordance with the Decision. Further, I have found that other breaches of the APP alleged by the Applicant on the review application have not been established. The phrase ‘serious interference with privacy’ is not defined in the Privacy Act but it would involve a failure to take privacy obligations seriously or a blatant disregard of privacy obligations. No such findings can be made against ANZ. I have considered the factors in s 13G(1B) of the Privacy Act that may be taken into account in determining if the interference with privacy is serious and none of them suggest that the interference was serious. Viewed objectively, the personal information was not of a sensitive nature and the breaches of the APP found by the Commissioner in the Decision did not have significant adverse consequences to the Applicant. Much of the personal information held by ANZ was contained in documents[30] which had been previously provided to the Applicant by FOS.[31] Whilst the Applicant was entitled to this information from ANZ, it can hardly be said that ANZ’s failure to provide it caused the Applicant any real detriment given that he was already in possession of it and he was aware of most of the personal information held by ANZ.

Costs/Reimbursement

  1. Finally, I reject the Applicant’s request for costs or reimbursement of expenses as claimed under various subsections of s 52 of the Privacy Act. The Applicant has not identified the particulars or the quantum of the expenses incurred. In any event, given my findings to the effect that appropriate disclosure of personal information has been made, I would not exercise my discretion to declare that the Applicant is entitled to any reimbursement.

Conclusion

  1. The Applicant has not been successful. The Tribunal affirms the Decision under review.
