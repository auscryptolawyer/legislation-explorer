ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - assessable income - applicant's funds misappropriated - legal expenses incurred to effectuate recovery of moneys - whether legal costs are deductible under s 51(1) expenditure incurred in protection of fixed capital not expenditure of a revenue nature : Pve followed - apportionment

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 51(1), 64A (2), (3)

Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 75; 85 CLR 423

Pye v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1959) 33 ALJR 337

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 July 1988 Mr B J McMahon (Senior Member)

1. The applicant and the respondent agreed on the following Statement of Facts -

"1. [Taxpayer] had an original investment of $30,000.

  1. This $30,000 was in the form of a term deposit.
  2. At the end of each term the interest was added to the original investment and re-invested.
  3. The bank had authority to re-invest the deposit plus interest accrued at the current interest rate unless otherwise instructed in writing.
  4. As at 23rd May, 1983, the sum of the original deposit plus all interest accrued thereto re-invested was $35,201-45.
  5. The amount re-invested on 23rd May, 1983 was re-invested for a 3 month period.
  6. The maturity date was 21st August, 1983.
  7. Interest of $1,012-04 accrued to this account on the maturity date.

9. On 22nd August 1983, the total of the re-invested deposit plus the interest accrued to that deposit was transferred to another account by an unknown person and thereafter withdrawn in the same month.

  1. [Taxpayer's] only other income was derived from deposits with savings institutions and rental income from a home unit at [address].
  2. [Taxpayer's] personal involvement on a day to day basis was minimal and in fact the investments were managed by either the savings institution ie held on deposit or in the case of the rental property, by a real estate agent.
  3. On the 13th March, 1984 the Commonwealth Bank paid to [taxpayer] the sum of $38,585-18 being the return of the principal deposit as at 22nd August, 1983 together with interest accrued up until the 13th March, 1984 being the amount of $2,371-69.
  4. The Commonwealth Bank declined to pay [taxpayer's legal costs in her dispute with that bank."
  5. It will be seen from the above that on or about the time that the applicant's funds were misappropriated, the deposit had matured. In accordance with standing instructions given to the bank, which were tendered in evidence, interest was to be added to the principal upon maturity of the deposit. The lump sum misappropriated therefore was $36,213-49.
  6. In order to seek recovery of this amount, the applicant engaged a solicitor. It became clear that the bank did not intend to take any action concerning recovery until such time as police enquiries had been concluded. Correspondence between the solicitor and the bank was tendered in evidence, together with a copy of an account rendered by that solicitor for $1,575. The account itemises correspondence, telephone calls and attendances with and upon the bank and its solicitors and with the police and reports that the solicitors were ultimately successful in finally obtaining reimbursement from the bank. On 13 March 1984, some 7 months after the misappropriation, the bank forwarded its cheque for $38,585-18 representing $36,213-49 (the amount misappropriated) and interest of $2,371-69 from the date of misappropriation until the date of payment.

4. In her return of income for the year ended 30 June 1984, the applicant claimed to be entitled to a deduction for legal costs of $1,575. A note attached to her return is in the following terms -

"ITEM 16

In September 1983, a thief fraudulently imitated the taxpayer's signature and obtained withdrawal of the taxpayer's $30,000 term deposit with the Commonwealth Bank, Randwick. At first, the Bank did not admit the mistake and conducted an investigation of the taxpayer and her family whether in fact they had committed the fraud themselves. Knowing that the Bank made no firm attempts to repay, the taxpayer appointed Enamel a solicitor, to take over the dealings with the Bank.

Finally, in March 1984, the Bank repaid the taxpayer in full, both the principal and almost two years' interest amounting to $6,603-68, which is included as income this year.

The taxpayer paid the solicitor for his services regarding the above matter, the sum of $1,575 which is an allowable deduction, being the cost of recovery, of possible loss of principal and interest income. The legal fee incurred was necessary expense for protection of capital and income. The taxpayer advised that if it was not for the solicitor's persistent pressing and threat of legal action, the Bank may not have met the taxpayer's satisfaction. Enclosed herewith are some of the correspondence written by the solicitor regarding this matter for the Commisioner's reference."

5. The claim was disallowed and a formal objection was taken by the applicant's tax agent in the following terms -

"We have been instructed by the abovenamed client to object against income tax assessment issued by the Commissioner dated 2nd April, 1985, in respect of tax year ended 30th June, 1984, in which the Commissioner disallowed legal fees incurred against interest income of $1,575. The Commissioner alleged that the said expenses were of a capital expenditure. The taxpayer claimed that the Commissioner has made a mistake of law and a mistake of fact and as a result the taxpayer's 1984 taxable income was over assessed. The taxpayer contended that the assessment should now be cancelled and withdrawn.

Grounds of objection upon which reliance is placed are as follows :-

  1. That the said legal expenses were incurred as necessary outgoings in gaining of assessable income, they were not of a domestic, private or capital nature.
  2. That the said expenses incurred were solely for revenue purposes and they were allowable deduction under Section 51 and other Sections of the Income Tax Assessment Act.
  3. That in preparing her 1984 tax return, the taxpayer has made extensive notes to explain to the Commissioner in order to justify her claim for legal expenses incurred. The taxpayer made a full and true disclosure of all material facts and the Commissioner was not authorised to make adjustments to the claims made.
  4. That from the wealth of information supplied by the taxpayer in her income tax return, the Commissioner should have been aware that the expenses incurred were for recovery of interest income that the bank did not want to pay. Such expenses incurred were a form of debt collection expenses and are allowable deductions under Section 51 and other Sections of the said Act.

5) That part of the legal expenses were incurred for recovery of $30,000 principal and the cost incurred in recovery of the principal and are allowable deductions under Section 51 and other Sections of the said Act. As the purpose of recovery of the principal was for maintaining future assessable incomes.

6) That the Commissioner has erred by claiming that the full amount of $1,575 incurred in legal expenses was of a capital expenditure whereas the full amount expended was for the purpose of recovery of revenue.

7) That alternatively, if the Commissioner was of a view that legal fees of $1,575 were excessive, which is not admitted, the Commissioner should not have disallowed the full amount claimed."

  1. The objection was disallowed. In accordance with the applicant's request the matter was then referred to this Tribunal for review.
  2. In order to obviate the distinction between capital and income payments of legal costs to which I will later refer, subsections (2) and (3) of s 64A have been inserted into the Income Tax Assessment Act to deal with small amounts claimed. Those 2 sub-sections are in the following terms -

"64A(2) Legal expenses incurred in the year of income in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income shall, except to the extent to which they are expenses of a private or domestic nature, be allowable deductions.

(3) The deduction allowable under this section shall not be greater than the amount (if any) by which $50 exceeds any amount allowable as a deduction under sub-section 51(1) in respect of legal expenses."

8. It is clear however, from the Agreed Statement of Facts that in the year of income the applicant was not "carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income". It is not possible therefore to regard $50 of the amount claimed as deductible under this section. For the same reason, it is not possible to regard the claim as falling within the second limb of sub-section (1) of s 51. The terms of both limbs are as follows -

"51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. The claim falls squarely for determination under the first limb of the sub-section.
  2. The amount in question was paid to the solicitors in order to effectuate recovery of moneys to which the applicant believed she was rightfully entitled. Of the total of $38,585-18 that was finally recovered, $36,213-49 must be regarded as capital. The note on her return and the terms of the objection refer variously to recovery of income and of capital when describing the purpose of expenditure of the moneys claimed. In my view however, the principal sum, being the amount misappropriated, must be regarded as capital. The accrual of interest to the deposit and the consequent imparting of the character of capital to the sum involved, comes about as a result of the contractual relationship between the bank and the applicant. The note to the applicant's tax return refers to a sum of $6,603-68 as "2 years interest". So far as the nature of the deposit and the contractual relationship between the bank and the applicant are concerned, that money would have acquired the character of capital well prior to the date of misappropriation. The bank has certainly treated the sum misappropriated as capital, having calculated the additional amount of interest of $2,371-69, at the appropriate rate, as a proportion of the enlarged capital. The question then is whether legal costs paid in order to recover an amalgam of the 2 sums is deductible under s 51(1) of the Act.
  3. The applicant's representative made very few submissions beyond those appearing in the formal objection. Even in that document however, paragraphs (5), (6) and (7) indicate an appreciation of the applicant's difficulties in maintaining this claim.
  4. The fact is that the legal costs were paid in order to assert title to or to protect a capital asset. Legal costs applied for that purpose, themselves take on the character of a capital payment. If any authority is required to support this proposition, I would refer to Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 75; 85 CLR 423. It is further settled, in Australia at any rate, that expenditure incurred in the protection or preservation of fixed capital is not expenditure of a revenue nature (see for example Pye v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1959) 33 ALJR 337). The argument advanced by the applicant's representative that if the capital had not been recovered, then income would have been foregone in future years, is not of great relevance in the light of these 2 High Court decisions. Moneys paid for the protection of capital assets, be they in the form of legal costs or otherwise, are simply not characterised as revenue payments and in terms of s 51(1) must be regarded as outgoings of a capital nature.

13. The use of the phrase "to the extent" in s 51 indicates that in certain cases an apportionment may be appropriate. I consider this to be such a case. Of the total sum of $38,585-18 recovered partly as the result of the solicitor's endeavours, $2,371-69 is, unquestionably, interest. I consider therefore that a deduction should be allowed which bears to the sum of the legal costs, the same proportion that the interest bears to the capital. That amount is $96-81.

14. The objection decision is therefore set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that the applicant's claim for a deduction be allowed to the extent of $96-81.
