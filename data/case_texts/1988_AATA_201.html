Income Tax - assessable income - whether business established

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 June 1988 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. These applications for review, which were heard together, were heard on separate days some months apart, followed by written submissions. I have decided that the applicants have, in the light of the whole of the evidence, satisfied me affirmatively on the balance of probabilities that the relevant assessments are excessive.
  2. The applications raise the assessability of the proceeds of sale of certain properties under s.26(a) or s.25A(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA"). Section 26(a) has subsequently been replaced by s. 25A (1), the terms of which are however identical. Section 25A has additional provisions, but the whole section is now subject to a "sunset" clause. It is s. 26(a) that would appear to apply here, and I shall for convenience refer to it rather than to s 25A (1) in these Reasons. If the better view were after all that s.25A (1) applies, it would be of no moment.

3. Despite the number of cases that have made reference to the problems of the onus referred to in s. 190(b), culminating in the famous series of cases represented by Gauci and Others v F.C. of T 75 ATC 4257; McCormack v F.C. of T 79 ATC 4111; and Macmine Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T 79 ATC 4133, I am not sure that much attention has ever been directed to the form of the onus. It is not expressed as an onus of proof of fact, but as a "mixed" onus of fact and law, thus: "... the onus of proving that the assessment is excessive". A similar form of onus has been adopted in s.61 of the Freedom of Information Act 1982. Section 190(b) is of course of general application in the ITAA, but it seems to have raised particular problems under s. 26(a). Having regard to the phasing out of s.25A, the full conceptual nature of the onus in this particular area may never be fully expounded. Notwithstanding that the thrust of the comment contained in the Australian Tax Reporter at paragraph 81-663 appears to have been supported in the Explanatory Memorandum to Act No.118 of 1986 (amending s. 190(b) of the ITAA), I am at a loss to know how an onus of proof provision can apply in an appeal from the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to the Federal Court. It was made clear beyond measure in F.C. of T v Brixius 87 ATC 4963 that, compared with the position under the former s.196 where the whole decision of the Board of Review was subject to an appeal if the decision involved a question of law, under s. 44(1) of the AAT Act the existence of a question of law is not merely a qualifying condition requisite in order to ground an appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. That question of law and it alone, is the subject matter of the appeal, and the ambit of the appeal is confined to that question. How an onus of proving anything can be relevant in relation to an appeal on a question of law escapes me. But the point is not material here. It is enough in this case to say that to speak of an onus of proof is essentially to speak of the proof of facts. The facts of each and every case will differ, and it is the facts of each particular case that will be determinative. It is on the very particular facts of this case that the decision must turn. The determinative facts here are in the long run a family tragedy that I have had to be careful leads not to a decision based on sympathy, but rather to one based on giving credence and force to the event that happened as an explanation of what occurred. I gratefully refer to and adopt the passage in the Reasons for Decision given by Mr. P.M. Roach (Senior Member) which found favour with M.L. Foster, J. in the decision of the Federal Court of F.C. of T. v Cainero (No. G457 of 1987, 10 May 1988). Mr. Roach's reasons encapsulate in economical language the problems of substance and onus that arise in cases such as this, in which issues arise in relation to both limbs of s. 26(a) of the ITAA.

  1. This is not the place to expand on it, but I believe that there was an increasing tendency down the years in the Boards of Review, one that I consider should not be perpetuated by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, to retreat in this area to an application of s.190(b) simply because there was evidence both ways. The onus of proof provision does not mean that a taxpayer applicant has to prove his, her or its case beyond all reasonable doubt. The taxpayer has to satisfy the administrator (the Commissioner or on review this Tribunal) on the balance of probabilities: See per Fullagar J in Pascoe v F.C. of T (1956) 11 ATD 108 at 114: "The question ... I have to ask myself is - Am I convinced - not beyond reasonable doubt but as a matter of belief - that the partners, in making the acquisition, were not actuated by a dominant purpose of profit-making by sale?"
  2. I now set out the facts as I find them to be having considered all of the evidence presented before me. That evidence is to be found in the documents marked as exhibits and in the oral evidence which is set out in the transcript. The applicants were husband and wife ("H" and "W") when the events in question occurred. They are now divorced. H was a plumber in a capital city. In the early 1970s he met X, an investor. H and X were an interesting combination. H was an Australian tradesman apparently of drive and ability, and with a will to get on. I do not know when X came to Australia, but it was at least 40 years ago. Those years have not dimmed the qualities of gracious manners that his European background afforded him. These two, quite different, personalities formed a friendship in which each readily acquired an appreciation of the other's qualities. H was in the building trade and had the capacity to organise and supervise rather than confine himself to work as a plumber. X, on the other hand, was an acquirer, an investor. They obviously trusted each other implicitly and I doubt that they ever had a written agreement. X was trusted in another quarter too. He had business relationships with a firm of suburban solicitors which gave him ready access to funds. If he had an investment in mind, usually the purchase of a property for rental purposes, he could go to these solicitors and obtain an "interest-only" loan almost at will. These secured loans would be at current ruling rates of interest, and as things developed it became the situation that X received loans the principal whereof amounted to around one third of the total assets offered by him as security. These assets were in this sense effectively pooled, but the loans were tied to specific mortgages to different clients of the solicitors. The loans were usually for three years and were then "rolled over". X's evidence as a whole satisfied me that he has essentially been an investor interested in rental properties rather than a man interested in profiting from the purchase and sale of real estate. That of course does not mean that he has not sold properties and profited thereby. Some of such sales, as in this case, no doubt occurred soon after purchase, but it was not made to appear that such early sales have been an emphatic part of his activities. If that is not correct, it was certainly not put to him when he was giving evidence that it was not.

6. Both H and X had an interest in the snow and skiing. H, who had acquired his builder's licence in 1972, liked a particular area near the snow and in 1974 he became aware of a particular property for sale in the applicants' favoured area for this activity, which I shall call A. H and X, with their wives, purchased this property, vacant land known as Lot 34 I Street. X contributed to the purchase and to the provision of funds for construction of three units and thereafter the two men and their wives appear to have conducted their acquisition of properties in partnership. Partnership returns lodged in respect of the years in question in these proceedings disclosed these sales. The Commissioner determined that the profits arising therefrom were assessable and therefore included the same as assessable income of the partnership. The profits were assessed on an individual basis and were not treated as the trading stock of a business. H and W were assessed for their respective shares of the profits in the years in question. H and his family moved to A to live in about 1976. They in fact went to live in one of the three units, the one occupied by them being essentially an attic unit. In 1977 or 1978 another lot was acquired in I Street, Lot 14, on which two townhouses were built. Again X was involved, as he was in each of the subsequent transactions. There was some difference between the evidence of H and X on the purpose of this acquisition. X said that Lot 14 was acquired for resale, whereas H said that the purpose was "To build flats on it ... At the stage we purchased the block it was for rental". It is unnecessary to make any finding in relation to this difference. In fact one of the units which was in due course built was sold in April 1979 and the other in June 1980.

7. H, W and family were not comfortable in the attic unit, and they purchased a block of land on which to build a home for themselves, in J Drive. A loan assisted in this purchase. At this time H continued to work as a plumber and as a supervisor of building work generally, as well as in relation to land purchased by himself, W and Mr and Mrs X. W also interested herself actively in relation to the units on the letting and managing side, and in keeping an eye out around the town for further acquisitions to enable a rental stock to be built up. It should be observed that A was not only a town with access to snow sports. It offered virtually year-round holiday facilities including boating. Nevertheless the fact that people resorted to it for holiday purposes, including snow sports, meant that lettings were uneven, with peak, "shoulder" and quiet times accordingly, with large rental differentials according to season.

  1. In May and June 1979 Lots 11 and 12 C Street were bought. H said that the intention was to build flats for rental purposes, but this property was held only until August 1979. W had found a property known as Lot 21 T Street. This was a lakeside property which they wished to acquire, and, when an unsolicited offer to purchase Lots 11 and 12 C Street was made soon afterwards the offer was accepted. The offer was such that, as W said in evidence, they "would have been fools" not to accept it. A was now in a real property "boom", and a profit of about $76,000 was made. This was of course assessable under S.26AAA of the ITAA, and tax was assessed and paid accordingly.
  2. Cashed-up by this transaction H, W, and Mr and Mrs X proceeded to purchase Lot 21 T Street, a waterfront block and the first of the two properties here in dispute, in October 1979. Three units were built, building taking place in the second half of 1980. At the beginning of 1980, when H was involved in building his own home in J Drive, W found out that another block was available in C Street, Lot 14, and the contract of purchase was entered into in February 1980. H was adamant that the purpose of this acquisition was to build on it and to let the properties so built indefinitely. This block had greater potential, it transpiring that 8 units could be built. This was to be the project after the building of the units at Lot 21 T Street which, having been built, were effectively divided between H, X and their wives in this way: Unit 1 was to be owned jointly by H, X and their wives and the rentals shared. Unit 3 was for H and W; and Unit 2 was for Mr and Mrs X. In that behalf the units were later "strata titled" by an application lodged in May 1982. But before all this, on 18 July 1980, a catastrophe struck the H family.
  3. On that very day, H, W and their family of three boys aged 14, 12 and 4, moved from their attic unit to their new home in J Drive. The house was finished in builder's terms, but not carpeted. On that very day W became extremely ill. She was well one moment, and the next she was stricken. A virus was diagnosed, but it was not. It was an aneurysm. For three weeks the misdiagnosis continued. W went out once to try to do some shopping with her sister. H said: "... they do not know how she ever did not die then; that is what they were concerned about". W was bedridden. She could not assist H in moving into the house, or in looking after the children or with anything, and he effectively had to nurse her.
  4. After three weeks W was moved to a hospital two to two and a half hour's drive from A. Here the aneurysm was diagnosed. There she remained till early 1981, or just after Christmas 1980, hopelessly stricken. For three weeks H, who had to look after the children and the new house, cooking and cleaning, getting them to school, collecting them or arranging for their collection, drove up and back to see W nightly for three weeks. After that he went two or three times a week. Bookings for the units, previously managed by W, had to be passed on to other people. H was also at this time doing plumbing and supervision on another house. There was some shortage of funds at this time, and he and X borrowed from a bank on the security of H's house.
  5. It was not known at this stage what was the prognosis for W, but there appears to have been a general assumption, at least by H, that she would recover. In November 1980, while W was still in hospital, H pressed on with obtaining development approval for the building of the eight units on Lot 14 C Street. For this purpose a plan had to be drawn up showing general design features, car-parking, landscaping, height restrictions and so on. The development plans were approved on 28 November 1980.
  6. When, just after this, W came home, the realisation dawned that things would never be the same again. She was in a wheelchair, she had one eye closed, she could not do anything for herself. She could only speak a little, and she had to be helped in the shower. She had nursing assistance once a week and some physiotherapy, but H said that after her return the demands made on him in fact increased. After some weeks H said that "she was just getting worse and - [scil. everything] just fell in". He said that he "just wanted to pay off my debts and just sort of cool it, you know". I think that he probably had some sort of offer for Lot 14 C Street in April or even March 1981. He said that he had decided that he wanted to sell it "not long after we got home from hospital". H said that he told W "look I will just sell it and I told [X] and X said 'No, I do not want to sell it'. We had - not heated words over it, he is not that type of person, he finally understood and he said, 'Well, sell it,' because he never wanted to sell it". In the event Lot 14 C Street, which had been purchased for $48,000 was sold in June 1981 for $275,000 with the development approval. The buyer has since developed it in accordance with that approval and with the plans that H had submitted.

14. The three units at Lot 21 T Street began to be let for the snow season of 1981, that is to say the "joint" unit and H and W's unit. That unit was sold for $150,000 in December 1983, and H and W sold their half of the shared unit in July 1982. By this time there had been more personal disaster. In August 1981, the son of Mr and Mrs X, described by H as a brilliant medical student, died tragically. The bottom fell out of their world, and, relevantly for present purposes, their association with the snow-fields became a painful one and in turn their partnership with H and W quietly came to an end. Then in 1983 H and W separated The marriage had not survived the strain of what had occurred. Divorce followed in 1985. W never did recover. She is better than she was after coming home. She is mentally in good shape, as could be seen when she gave evidence, but she tires easily and cannot move readily. She has to use walking aids and is quite disabled. She described in evidence the active role which she had displayed in managing and servicing the rental properties and arranging their interior decoration, which role of course came entirely to an end the day she became Ill. She could not cope physically, and she had lost all interest.

15. The only difference in the evidence of H and X was in relation to Lot 14 I Street, which as previously stated X, contrary to H's evidence, said had been bought for resale. The sale of this property is not of course the subject of these proceedings. The sworn evidence of H and W was that Lot 21 T Street and Lot 14 C Street had been bought to be developed as rental properties and held indefinitely. What evidence is there that should lead to that evidence being rejected, or to it being held that in the light of all the evidence the applicants have failed to discharge the onus of proof? The evidence pointed to by the Commissioner was -

(i) The comparatively short time for which the properties were held.

(ii) The previous history of purchases and sales.

(iii) What were said to be vague estimates of likely net returns from the properties in question.

(iv) The fact that no one property was still held.

(v) As far as Lot 14 C Street was concerned, while W was still ill when H began the process of selling this property, by the time it was actually sold in July 1981 W was "recuperating"

(vi) The borrowing done was short-term borrowing of an "interest only" variety.

(vii) C Street was the scene of earlier profit-making.

(viii) Whatever the position as to C Street, the sale of the T Street properties was further distanced from W's illness and not explicable by it.

16. Of these points, (i), (ii), (iv) and (vii) are all entitled to some weight, but need no further comment. I make the following comments however about (iii), (v), (vi) and (viii):

As to (iii): This point has some weight, but on the second day of the hearing X said that he had worked out the figures again and that they indicated a return per annum of 12 to 16 per cent gross. The rental "industry" at A appears to have been as volatile as the capital side of the property market. It seems to me that H and X backed their own judgment that the rental business would boom and did not consider it necessary to make detailed estimates of returns at the time of purchase.

As to (v) : This point involves in my opinion a fundamental misapprehension. To speak of W "recuperating" is simply flat contrary to the situation. She never has "recuperated", and her condition at the time of sale of Lot 14 C Street was such that the family was in a state of dissolution. The whole case is in my opinion dominated by that fact.

As to (vi): While this is true, the evidence of X was that this had been how he had done all of his borrowing. One client of the solicitors had been a source of funds for over 20 years under that system. H always just went along with what X said and did in relation to obtaining finance.

As to (viii): H had found his financial situation getting tighter. To build on Lot 21 T Street he had had to borrow on the security of his home. The shared unit was sold at the end of December 1982 as financial difficulty mounted. H and W separated in mid 1983. The important point is that up to this time there had on my view of the evidence been no let-up whatsoever in the strains placed on H, W and their family, strains which culminated in the separation. From July 1980 onwards, I do not think that hard-nosed financial considerations were or could have been expected to be dominant.

  1. I now turn to consider my findings in relation to the three possible bases of assessability that were advanced.
  2. Section 25(1) of the ITAA

There are two ways in which the general concept of income could be applied here.

(i) First, it could be argued that as H was a plumber turned builder, who built on land which he acquired with others, all of his endeavours were linked in one income-earning operation. The Commissioner's case was not however structured around such a proposition and there was no investigation of H's other activities. They were simply referred to by him in passing. In addition, his other income appears from the returns to have been derived from a company formed by H and W, and those other activities could not be linked for this purpose to those of H and W in relation to the transactions the subject of the proceedings. I put this on one side.

(ii) Secondly, it could be argued that H and W conducted a business represented by the purchase, development (in some cases) and sale of all of the properties referred to in the history set out above, or alternatively of the two subject properties. This argument has close links to the argument under the second limb of s. 26(a), and I defer consideration of it until I have dealt with the question of "profit-making undertaking or scheme".

19. The first limb of s. 26(a)

I have found above the facts that are relevant to this potential head of assessability. I have acknowledged the force of the points made as set out in paragraph 15 above, but my finding is that the personal situation which overwhelmed H and W in 1980 and continued thereafter is such that it would be unsafe to reject the sworn evidence that the subject properties were bought other than for the purpose of resale at a profit, namely for the purpose of their being held for rental purposes. W did not recover, and the situation therefore did not improve but rather got worse. Eventually they both left the area, and their world fell apart. Were it not for these factors contra-indications might well, as they often have in past cases, led to a contrary conclusion, but in this case I find otherwise and accept the evidence of H and W.

  1. Profit-making undertaking or scheme" - the second limb of s. 26(a)

There are a number of propositions in relation to the second limb of s. 26(a). They are conveniently stated in the Australian Federal Tax Reporter. The following are those that need to be considered in this case:

(i) "The mere realisation of a capital asset which was not acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale does not constitute a profit-making undertaking or scheme within the meaning of the second limb" - see Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v F.C. of T [1950] HCA 16; (1950) 81 CLR 188 and the other cases referred to in Australian Federal Tax Reporter paragraph 12-755 (4). See also now in particular the analysis of F.C. of T. v The Myer Emporium Ltd. 87 ATC 4363 by M.L. Foster J. in Cainero.

(ii) Section 26(a) has no operation in relation to a transaction which forms part of a more extensive business, but is intended to deal "with transactions which are entire in themselves ...": See the cases referred to in Australian Federal Tax Reporter at paragraph 12-625 esp. Australasian Catholic Assurance Co Ltd v F.C. of T [1959] HCA 26; (1959) 100 CLR 502.

(iii) "Although the second limb of s.26(a) does not contain a reference to the word 'purpose', in order that an undertaking or scheme should fall within the scope of that provision it must have been carried out with the purpose of making a profit": Australian Federal Tax Reporter at paragraph 12-755(7) See F.C. of T. v Becker [1952] HCA 77; (1952) 87 CLR 456 at 460; XCO Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1971] HCA 37; (1971) 124 CLR 343 at 350; Geo. W. Cheverton Pty Ltd v F.C. of T (1962) 12 ATD 461 at 470.

(iv) A capital asset may however be ventured into a profit-making undertaking or scheme: Australian Federal Tax Reporter paragraph 12-755(5); see the cases referred to in Australian Federal Tax Reporter at paragraph 12-765.

(v) More controversially, to be a profit-making undertaking or scheme it must produce income and not a capital gain - it must have "the character of a business deal": McClelland v F.C. of T [1970] HCA 39; (1970) 120 CLR 487: See however the effect of later decisions of the High Court as discussed in Australian Federal Tax Reporter paragraphs 12-630 and 12-785.

21. Of the four propositions mentioned, (i) and (ii) above hold no terrors for the present applicants. Indeed, standing unqualified proposition (i) must be at the forefront of their case. It is propositions (iii) (iv) and (v) that must be considered further.

  1. It was put for the Commissioner that a global undertaking or scheme should be found to have existed from the time when H first met X, after which, it was said, there was a persistent pattern of moving from one deal to another, alternatively there were "sub-schemes" relating to each individual transaction. The preferred position was said to be that there was a sequence of transactions bordering on such a degree of repetition and similarity of procedure as to represent "in a low-key way" the carrying on of a business of buying and selling land sometimes developed and sometimes not, depending on the mood of the moment and the availability of purchases. There was said to be little difficulty factually in branding these transactions, and all of them, with what was described in McClelland's Case as the character of a business deal.
  2. The Commissioner's submission was not without some force, perhaps more so in the case of Lot 21 T Street, where vacant land was bought and then units built before the sale. A reading however of the cases in which property bought without any purpose of profit-making by sale has been sold profitably and the profit thereby accruing has been held to be assessable under the second limb indicates that they have by and large been cases in which there has been adaptation or change in the ownership of the property (as by company formation etc.) or of the physical presentation of the property. There needs it seems to me to be some change of course in the holding, management or development of the property, some change in the nature or quality of the property, in order that a new venture be able to be discerned.
  3. In relation to Lot 14 C Street, no more had been accomplished than the bare obtaining of development approval, application for which had gone ahead during W's hospitalisation when it was not known that she would not recover to anywhere near her former state of health. In relation to Lot 21 T Street, the division by the applicants and Mr and Mrs X of the three units between them, only later followed by conversion into strata titles, was consistent with their evidence that their original purpose was development for income production, and does not in my opinion give adequate ground for holding that the building of the units, and the later obtaining of strata titles, constituted a change in the nature or quality of the holding of the property, nor on balance did anything else appear that should lead to a conclusion that the property had been ventured into a profit-making undertaking or scheme.
  4. Having given this aspect of the matter as much earnest consideration as I can, I conclude that the case for the applicants should be preferred. Having accepted in relation to the first limb of s. 26(a) that their purpose was to develop the properties in question into investment properties, it is a consistent finding under the second limb that on the balance of probabilities the applicants' plan was development for income production derived from rents rather than development for profitable resale. The unremitted illness of W having dictated sale, as I have previously found, it is hardly surprising that the opportunity to sell very advantageously was seized. There was, I find, no profit-making undertaking or scheme but rather a propitious realisation of assets dictated by the circumstances of the applicants. In all the circumstances therefore I do not consider that the second limb of s. 26(a) is applicable in respect of either of the two sales.
  5. I may add that I agree with the submission put by Mr. Dodson of counsel for the applicants that a parallel is to be found in the case of Eisner v F.C. of T (1971) 45 ALJR 110, and I respectfully adopt the line of reasoning expounded by Walsh J. at 113-114:

"My conclusion is that the land was not acquired by the appellant for the purpose of profit making by sale. The respondent contends that even if that be so the appellant derived a profit which was income within the meaning of s.25 without resort to s. 26(a), or alternatively a profit which was taxable under the second limb of s. 26(a). As to s.25, it was submitted that the appellant bought the land as part of his business and it was intended to be used as part of his business and in some way turned to profit. On that argument it does not matter whether the appellant had decided upon any particular way in which the land would be developed. It was a business venture of a man whose companies were in the building business and who was likely to be able to use in the course of his business a first-class residential building site. Counsel acknowledged that this way of putting the case did not differ much from one of the submissions which he made as to the application of the second limb of s. 26(a). That submission was that at the time when the land was bought there was a profit-making undertaking or scheme. There was a plan to buy it and afterwards to use it to make a profit in some way, whether this would be by resale in the general market or by resale to a company to be formed which would itself derive profit from the venture or by providing a profitable contract for an Arcos building company. But I am of opinion that these submissions should not be accepted. The decision in favour of the appellant upon the main issue of fact litigated in the appeal means that his dominant purpose was not the making of profit by resale of the land, either to the highest bidder in the open market or to a company intended to be formed to buy it. The finding means also that in spite of the difficulties in his way, his claim that his purpose was to retain the land and make it into an asset which would produce income from rents has been accepted."

27. This leaves only the question held over from paragraph 18 above of the application of s. 25(1) of the ITAA. This is dealt with by Walsh J. in the passage cited above. I do not consider that the previous transactions of the applicants should be treated as leading to a conclusion of assessability in relation to the two subject transactions when all are taken together, and there is in my opinion no way in which the sales of the two subject transactions should in the circumstances of this case be found to be caught by s.25(1).on the footing of the carrying on of a business when I have found that they are not a profit-making undertaking or scheme within the second limb of s.26(a).

28. The question of additional tax that was imposed disappears accordingly, and the applicants' claims succeed. Each of the objection decisions under review must be set aside and the objections of the applicants allowed in full. In my opinion the profits on the sale of Lot 14 C Street; and of Unit 1, 21 T Street and Unit 3, 21 T Street were not, and none of them was, assessable income of either of the applicants under s.2 5(), s.26(a) or S.25A of the ITAA.

29. I should mention one point of evidence on which I ruled at the hearing. Under cross-examination H strongly denied that he had made a certain statement to a Bank Manager in 1981. Counsel for the Commissioner later sought to tender a statement made by the Bank manager and held in the Bank's records. The tender was sought to be made under the equivalent of the Bankers' Books Evidence Act. Whether or not a statement purporting to record such a conversation falls within such provisions may be open to question, but in any event the statement would be admissible in the discretion of the Tribunal under s. 33(1) of the AAT Act which governs admissibility of evidence before the Tribunal. That section provides:

"33.(1) In a proceeding before the Tribunal -

(a) the procedure of the Tribunal is, subject to this Act and the regulations and to any other enactment, within the discretion of the Tribunal;

(b) the proceeding shall be conducted with as little formality and technicality, and with as much expedition, as the requirements of this Act and of every other relevant enactment and a proper consideration of the matters before the Tribunal permit; and

(c) the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence but may inform itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate."

30. Having regard to this provision, I ruled at the hearing that, H having given sworn evidence denying the relevant part of the conversation, and the respondent indicating that it was not proposed to call the maker of the statement (who is now living in a distant State), that in fairness and justice the statement should not be received in evidence, it not being subject to cross-examination. It was suggested that if the Bank Manager were able to be called he would be permitted to refresh his memory from his own contemporaneous note and that, other things being equal, he would give exactly the same oral evidence. I am afraid that after no small experience in these cases I am not prepared to make any such assumption and having had time to reflect on the ruling which I gave I have no hesitation in confirming that ruling.
