Statement Of Reasons

BACKGROUND TO THESE PROCEEDINGS

  1. In these proceedings, Mr Peter Day (the Applicant) appeals, pursuant to s14ZZ(1)(a)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA), from an objection decision made by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) on 23 May 2023.[1] That decision concerned the Applicant’s objections to amended assessments made pursuant to s 166 and s167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (ITAA36) in respect of the income years ended 30 June 2016, 30 June 2017, 30 June 2018, 30 June 2019 and 30 June 2020 (the relevant years).
  2. The Respondent allowed the Applicant’s objections in part and determined that alterations ought to be made to reduce the Applicant’s taxable income, as set out in the original assessments by $1,143,006.00.[2] However, the objections were otherwise refused, such that the amended assessments remained to the effect that the Applicant had understated his assessable income in each of the relevant years.
  3. In these proceedings, the Applicant contended the Respondent’s amended assessments of his taxable income in each of the relevant years was excessive. The Applicant further submitted he had not committed a “blameworthy act or omission” in the preparation of his income tax returns for the relevant years and the finding of evasion should not have been made. The Applicant asked the Tribunal to set aside the Respondent’s evasion opinion,[3] and the amended assessments,[4] including the penalty assessments for the relevant years.
  4. The Respondent submitted the Applicant had failed to furnish sufficient evidence to discharge his onus of proving that the ‘unidentified deposits’ which had been assessed to him were not income by reference to 10 broad categories, recorded in Appendix A to the objection decision.[5] The categories were set out in the Respondents opening submissions under the following headings: Salary and Wages, Original Source of Funds from Rental, Miscellaneous / Split payments, Original Source IMB xxx1231,Errors, Loans, Loan Facility the subject of New South Wales Supreme Court proceedings, Director Loans, Business related Transactions and Not Disputed.
  5. Originally, Appendix A prepared by the Respondent, that from part of the Respondent’s reasons for decision, had no line items against the original bank statement, making identifying the relevant transaction and tracing the flow of funds extremely difficult. Appendix A in its original format was contained in the Tribunal documents at TB 164- 230.

Adjournment after commencement of the hearing

  1. It became clear on the first day of the hearing that the Applicant had not appreciated the difference between putting his evidence into a witness statement (statutory declaration) to allow cross examination by the Respondent’s counsel, or why the Respondent’s counsel asked the Tribunal to draw a line “in terms of what is the extent of the Applicants evidence” between evidence and submissions made by the Applicant “from the bar table”.[6]
  2. To deal with the procedural fairness issue raised by the Respondent’s counsel, the Tribunal explained to the Applicant that he would need to take the Tribunal to the page numbers in the Tribunal documents and his Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentious to corroborate his oral submissions. Having prepared a detailed colour coded list of transactions that identified the flow of funds, the Applicant was content to take the Tribunal through each line item in the various bank statements that supported his oral submissions. To avoid any later dispute the Tribunal Book (TB) together with any other evidence filed in the Tribunal pursuant to section 23 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024(Cth) (ART Act) was tendered as evidence.
  3. The Tribunal explained to the Applicant that as his evidence was closed, he would not be allowed to file further evidence. The Tribunal made it clear that in order for the Applicant to discharge the statutory burden of proof, he would need to take the Tribunal through each line item in the bank statements identified in Appendix A to demonstrate the deposit was not an “unexplained deposit”.
  4. The Applicant opened by identifying a number of not insignificant errors made by the Respondent in the tables that appeared in the Respondent’s outline of opening submissions at paragraphs [5] [6] and [7].[7]
  5. By way of example only, the Applicant referred to a Pepper Home Loan ending 8771 that appeared in the table at [7] and told the Tribunal there was no income in that account in 2016 and 2017, stating “that would be impossible for the first two figures, because that account was only opened in 2018”...”In that home loan account - in that particular account there's none. That particular account that property was purchased in 2018 and the account was not opened”. When asked by the Tribunal to take the Tribunal to the relevant document in the Tribunal Book the Applicant told the Tribunal “because that account's not opened, there's no document for it”.[8]
  6. The Applicant also appeared to have legitimate concerns about adjustments made by the Respondent to the Applicant’s declared assessable income in income tax year ended 30 June 2019 and the penalties that flowed from two adjustments made by the Respondent.
  7. The Applicant explained the covert audit report relied on an income tax return that had not been lodged by the Applicant, rather it was an extract from a return amended by the Respondent during pre – issue compliance action for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020.[9] The issue concerned the Applicants PAYG Withholdings, a matter the Tribunal has no jurisdiction over, and significant interest deduction and other associated deductions relating to the Applicant’s rental properties. Though the pre issue compliance audit did not form part of these proceedings, the issue partially explained a more fundamental problem. The Applicant took the Tribunal to the Respondent's document at TB 285, the Respondent’s reasons for decision. In table 5 at [68] it recorded the taxable income in the original return the Applicant had lodged. In that return, the Applicant had declared assessable income of $312,850. The Applicant told the Tribunal the return had included a PAYG Summary, “I included it originally. It got audited, and then when they audited again, they re -included it”... “So they didn't include it, they audited it, removed 272,850 on it and when they redid the COVID-19 audit (covert audit), they re-added the 272,850 amount, not as a group certificate but as income, no matter where (what) you declared and I think if you look at the TB 285, it's the ATO's own document where they've made a table up and in that table it says 2019 “Taxable Income Reported in return” and it shows I reported 312,850, not the 40,000 figure. .. That would confirm that the 272, 850 plus the 40,000 is the $312,850 figure so that was in my original one that I submitted is actually 312850. If you look at the 2019 year, it's got reported that I did declare $312,850”. In essence, it appeared that the pre-issue compliance audit had reduced the Applicant’s reported income to $40,000 not the $312,850 declared by the Applicant.
  8. The Tribunal then asked the Respondent’s counsel if they could provide an explanation, understandably he could not, other than to explain “I don't have any material in respect of that audit. I know of its existence because it's referred to in the various material and I think that's the root cause of why the numbers are (different) ....” “and less straightforward than it would otherwise be”.[10] The Respondent’s counsel’s assessment that this was not straight forward was an apt description of the “Gordian knot” the Tribunal faced.
  9. During the Applicant’s opening, the Applicant took the Tribunal to line items in the Applicant’s bank accounts. It soon became apparent to the Tribunal and the Respondent’s counsel that many of the unidentified deposits had in fact been included twice or possibly three times in the Respondent’s calculation of the total amount of unexplained deposits during the relevant years. This was an understandable error on the Respondent’s part because for reasons that later became apparent, the Applicant had opened a separate bank account for each of the companies and ventures he was associated with. At the time of the audit the Applicant had nine active bank accounts.
  10. To demonstrate the double and possibly triple counting of bank deposits, the Applicant explained that when the Respondent reviewed the bank accounts the Applicant was operating “for some random reason, ATO didn't audit all nine accounts. They only audited 3 accounts, or in some years 4 accounts and in different years they audited different things. For home loans in one year they audited that bank account and then another year they audited the account number. The issue with this is that a lot of the transactions were Internet account transfers so if you audited all my accounts you’ll clearly see they went to a main account. So my main business account that I did all my transactions through is this one IMB ended 1231. Most of my salary went into that account. Everything went into that account, like my personal income. Then I transferred from that account to another account of mine, which would be my home loan account for example. When I've transferred it from my main account to my home loan account, they've counted it as income again”.[11]
  11. When the Tribunal put to the Applicant that the Respondent had probably removed the double counting the Applicant was adamant the Respondent had not done so, telling the Tribunal “no they didn't. Because what happened is, if it went from one directly they removed it. But if it went through two accounts if I transferred it from 1231 to account ending xxx 482 for example and 482 didn't get audited so when they [replay] on - when I picked it up on the 4871 account we [they] had not audited it - this other account. But if you follow it through you'll see---“. The Applicant then took the Tribunal to a colour-coded and extremely detailed spreadsheet the Applicant had prepared by financial years to demonstrate the point. However, as the Respondent had prepared Appendix A by bank statement it was difficult to reconcile the entries in the 2 documents.
  12. To complicate matters further, it appeared a number of the items the Applicant was referring to had not been disputed at the objection stage by his then representatives. The Applicant told the Tribunal “I honestly thought it had been disputed. It clearly states that it's from one account to another account on the document so that document does prove that it's from my other account”.[12] The Applicant then took the Tribunal to various entries by line item for October 2019. It was apparent from the transaction details that the amount in the example picked by the Applicant of $88,050 had been transferred on 10 October from the account ending 1231, into account ending 4871 and the statement had the narration of the transfer to account ending 4871.
  13. It was at this stage of the hearing that counsel for the Respondent helpfully offered to seek further instructions.
  14. As the hearing progressed during the day, it was apparent to the Tribunal that in pursuing the Tribunal’s objectives set out on s.9 of the ART Act, to ensure an independent mechanism of review was provided to the Applicant in a just and fair way and to resolve as quickly, and with as little formality and expense, as a proper consideration of the matters before it permitted, the hearing should be adjourned in order for the Applicant to refine his spreadsheet by including document numbers for each of the transactions to demonstrate the flow of funds. The Respondent’s counsel helpfully undertook to obtain further information from the Respondent’s audit team to ascertain if the issues in dispute could be narrowed.
  15. On 25 February 2025, the Tribunal made orders that Appendix A was to be amended. An additional column was to be added for the Applicant to summarise the money the Applicant submitted he transferred to and from related bank accounts, with the relevant account numbers for each bank account, attaching a copy of the relevant pages with the credits and debits highlighted. The hearing was adjourned until the 16 and 17 June 2025.
  16. On 17 March 2025, the Applicant provided the Amended Appendix A to the Respondent. In the date column the Applicant had included a line-item reference on each bank statement that clearly identified the transaction in question and a reference to the Tribunal document. On 12 May 2025, the Respondent added a column setting out its response, that is, whether the Respondent maintained its position or accepted the Applicant’s explanation of the deposit. Appendix A was 467 pages long and provided a detailed narration of the transaction details and both parties contentions in relation to each transaction, the subject of these proceedings.
  17. On 4th June 2025 the Tribunal made further directions that the Respondent file and serve:
(a) An amended Statement of Facts Issues and Contentions identifying the amounts that remained in dispute and any concession the Respondent had made in relation to the objection decision, amended assessments or penalties; and

(b) updated submissions written in plain English which clearly identified for the benefit of the Applicant which items in Appendix A remained in dispute.

Concession made by the Respondent

  1. The Respondent, in its Statement of Facts Issues and Contentions had agreed to confine the issues for determination by the Tribunal to whether the amounts recorded in Appendix A of the objection decision were assessable to the Applicant.[13] The Respondent’s closing submissions reiterated that concession stating “the Respondent has confined the dispute to whether the deposits in Appendix A formed part of the Applicant’s assessable income. The Applicant will have discharged his onus under s14ZZK if he can establish that the deposits were not assessable income to him. The Applicant does not need to prove the actual amount of his taxable income for the relevant years” (emphasis added).
  2. For the reasons that follow, the Applicant has established, on the balance of probabilities, that the deposits assessed to him were not assessable income to him in the relevant years and that the Respondent’s amended assessments were excessive. Further, the Commissioner’s evasion opinion should not have been made. The Tribunal also found on the facts before it that the s 167 (a) assessments made for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020 should not have been made. Finally, on the evidence before the Tribunal, had the Applicant been required to prove his actual income for the relevant years, the Tribunal believes the Applicant would have been able to do so.
  3. In very general terms, although the Applicant’s evidence in relation to certain loans fell short of what would ordinarily be expected for a proceeding of this nature, the Applicant’s evidence was detailed and specific.
  4. Firstly, the Applicant’s submissions were supported by copies of bank statements with sufficient narration for the Tribunal to follow the flow of funds. Secondly, the Applicant’s evidence was supported by a detailed draft report prepared by Slaven Torline, a forensic accountancy firm on 23 September 2022. The forensic accountant who prepared the report was able to use the available material provided by the Applicant, that included the Respondent’s objection decision, to re-calculate the ‘unexplained deposits’ identified by the Respondent in their audit and set out in the Respondent’s reasons for decision. The report of Slaven Torline was detailed, it attached supporting documentation and ran to 540 pages.[14] The forensic accountants report was provided to the Respondent, prior to the Respondent’s decision on objection. The Slaven Torline report was able to show that a large number of the unexplained deposits in the Respondent’s transaction list were actually internal transfers between accounts owned by the Applicant, or loans advanced to the Applicant, or loan repayments received by the Applicant or business related transactions, or expenses, or other refunds.[15]
  5. Thirdly, during the course of the hearing the Applicant, as a consequence of the amended Appendix A, was able to establish to the satisfaction of the Respondent that numerous “unidentified deposits” should not have been included in the Respondent’s Appendix A that formed part of the objection decision. The Respondent conceded further deposits were not unidentified deposits and would be excluded from the amounts assessed to the Applicant.
  6. Fourthly, the Tribunal found the Applicant had furnished income tax returns for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 30 June 2020 on 4 May 2021 and accepted the Applicants explanation for the late filing for the 2019 and 2020 ‘income tax return.
  7. Fifthly, the Tribunal found the Applicant to be a reliable and credible witness, as were his parents Mrs Cathie Day and Mr Glennon Day. The Tribunal did not find their evidence to be self-serving.
  8. Sixth, the Tribunal found the Applicant’s explanation for the lack of many of the Applicant’s business records and copies of the Citibank accounts was entirely plausible.
  9. Seventhly, the Tribunal found the Applicant’s failure to obtain witness statements from his previous accountant RSM Australia (RSM) or the forensic accountant Mr Torline of Slaven Torline (forensic accountant) and to call them to give evidence was more likely than not due to his financial constraints given the freezing orders obtained by the Respondent in 2021 that remained in place and the garnishee notices issued by the Respondent shortly after the Respondent issued the first amended assessments on 19 August 2021.
  10. Eighthly, the Applicant was prepared to concede amounts that had occurred “so long ago... There is no possible way for me to prove the originating source of this cash or the flow of it”. The amount the Applicant did not dispute was $71,404.57. Given the Applicant’s attention to detail, mathematical abilities, memory and the fact he had opened separate bank accounts for each of the companies or ventures associated with the Applicant, the Tribunal was left in no doubt that if the Applicant had the relevant supporting documentation the amount conceded would be significantly further reduced or eliminated entirely.

THE APPLICANT

  1. The Applicant suffered from a condition known as developmental dyspraxia, which affected his ability to express his arguments both orally and in writing. On occasion he appeared not to listen as attentively as he might have and to rush through his often extremely detailed and highly relevant points. The Applicant gave comprehensive and nuanced answers.
  2. It was apparent from the Applicant’s reconstruction of Appendix A that he was extremely mathematically inclined, retained a great deal of detail about transactions and his business operations in his head, and was able to recall specific details about events when taken to a line item on a specific bank statement which had a notation against the entry, and given the opportunity to explain matters of detail to the Tribunal .
  3. The Applicant also demonstrated to the satisfaction of the Tribunal that he had established and run his mobile phone retail and wholesale businesses on very fine margins. The Tribunal's own research indicated that from around 2014 margins for small independent distributors of mobile phones declined significantly because of the major Telcos and the phone manufacturers increasingly dealing directly with their customers. The lockdowns during Covid 19 witness a seismic shift from local retailers to platforms like Amazon, which resulted in the closure of many small retailers, Mobile Phone Shack being one such casualty.
  4. At the time of the audit, the Applicant owned 3 residential properties on Casula Road, Casula, that were rented. The managing property agent was Domain Plus Real Estate. The Applicant had financed the purchase of these properties through loans obtained from Pepper Home Loans, La Trobe Financial, and Resimac Home Loans and form 3 private lenders Mr Mohamed Cheikh and Mr Bilal Chocair under a loan agreement dated 19 July 2020 prepared by the law form Joseph G Capogreco and Associates and Cash Stash Pty Ltd under a loan agreement prepared by Concisus Legal Pty Ltd.
  5. For the income tax years ended 30 June 2016, 2017, and 2018 the Applicant had engaged the services of a registered tax agent to prepare and lodge his returns. The Applicant had given his tax agent access to all his bank accounts through a platform called Banklink. The accounts prepared by the registered accountant were lodged by that accountant on time. The Applicant’s previous advisors RSM informed the Respondent in correspondence dated 27 September 2021 that the registered tax agent had become ill in around 2019 and was not able to prepare the 2019 and 2020 income tax returns. It was common ground the Applicant had prepared these returns, possibly somewhat hurriedly, as he told the Tribunal he was conscious of the fact that he was already late with respect to filing these returns.
  6. The Applicant, at least at the time of the hearing, was living with his parents and the Applicant’s father had at the Applicants request and on the Applicant’s instructions, prepared the Applicant’s application to the Tribunal. In the opening paragraph of Section 3 of the application the Applicant’s father Mr Glennon Day requested the Tribunal note “the taxpayer is limited in reading and more so writing as he suffers from a condition known as developmental dyspraxia. As his father, Glennon Day, at [the Applicant’s] request and expressing information [the Applicant] has made known to me and directed me to state in this AAT application, I am writing these reasons for the application. I am not [the Applicant’s] representative at this AAT matter. He did have accountants representing him for the review by the ATO and while their submission was detailed and well supported the review officer allowed only minor changes to the ATO assessment. Due to financial constraints [the Applicant] will be representing himself in this matter. I should add that despite [the Applicant’s] disability he is very capable of understanding and clearly answering any questions that are likely to arise in the process of reaching a conclusion to this appeal.[16]

THE RESPONDENT’S’ AUDITS AND ASSESSMENTS

Pre Issue Compliance Audit

  1. The Applicant was subjected to 2 audits by the Respondent. An audit of the Applicant’s tax returns prepared and filed by the Applicant for the 2019 and 2020 years, referred to as “pre-issue compliance action” (the audit) and an audit without notice (covert audit) for the relevant years. Regrettably, the Respondent’s decision in relation to both audits were provided to the Applicant within approximately three weeks of each other. It was apparent during the hearing that the findings of the auditor conducting the pre-issue compliance action were quite different from the findings of the auditor conducting the covert audit in relation to the 2019 year.
  2. It was common ground that on 4 May 2021, the Applicant filed his income tax return for the year ended 30 June 2019 which the Applicant had prepared. From the Respondent’s reasons for decision at [86] it appeared the Applicant’s tax return filed in that year reported taxable income of $312,850.[17] A copy of the original return did not appear to be included in the Tribunal Documents. It appeared from the answers given by the Applicant during his cross examination that after various deductions, primarily associated with the Applicant’s rental properties, the Applicant’s taxable income on the notice of assessment issued on 29 July 2021, was stated as $26,459.[18] The calculation of the assessable income remained something of a mystery throughout the hearing because in none of the Respondent’s five iterations of their Statement of Facts Issues and Contentions was the original return identified, copies of the 2019 tax return that were included all had the comment “Audit Adjusted” or ”adjusted at audit” on the copies of the return.
  3. Following the audit of the 2019 income tax year the assessable income allegedly reported by the Applicant was adjusted by the auditor (not by the Applicant) to $40,000 and tax payable was determined as $1,569.21.[19] Curiously, the notation in the reasons for decision dated 17 August 2021 states “adjusted as a result of pre-issue compliance action”.[20] This error on the part of the Respondent had a snowballing effect. Firstly, the Applicant was subject to administrative penalties of $43,119.35 under section 284-90 item 2 of the TAA because a “shortfall amount” resulted from the Applicant’s recklessness as to the operation of a taxation law. As improbable as that sounded and notwithstanding the Tribunal’s scepticism, the reasons for decision included in the Tribunal documents recorded an administrative penalty of $43,119.35 as did the Account Statement prepared by the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) that clearly showed a shortfall penalty relating to recklessness for the income tax year ended 30 June 2019 of $43,119.35 was debited on 22 July 2021.[21]
  4. The Applicant’s evidence, that the Tribunal accepts, was he attempted to object to the audit of the 2019 and 2020 years but was informed by the Australian Taxation Office that he was unable to do so because the results of a second audit had already been issued.[22] The second audit, the covert audit, unsurprisingly adjusted the Applicant’s amended assessable income from $40,000 to $440,326. The Respondent glossed over this error and simply struck out the $40,000 entry that appeared in the table at [6] of the Respondent’s fourth further amended Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions with a double Asterix beside the figure and under the table, a notation “**adjusted as a result of pre-issue compliance action”.
  5. As a result of the covert audit, the Respondent imposed a base penalty amount on the shortfall amount for intentional disregard of a taxation law at 75% that was calculated on the difference between the pre-issue compliance action adjusted income of $40,000 and the amended taxable income of $440,000. The Respondent imposed a shortfall penalty for the 2019 year of $161,422.80[23] that was in addition to the shortfall penalty for reckless already imposed for the 2019 income tax year.
  6. At the conclusion of the hearing the Tribunal made an order that the Respondent provide an explanation for the outcome of the pre-issue audit. The Respondent made it very clear the initial penalty for recklessness did not form part of the reviewable objection decision and therefore did not from part of these proceedings. Although undoubtably correct, the findings of the pre-issue compliance action are not the subject of these proceedings, the reasons for decision of the audit are contained in the Tribunal Documents as are the Respondent’s requests for further information in relation to Applicant’s income tax returns for the 2019 and 2020 years[24] (The penalty for the 2019 year debited on 22 July 2021, appears not to have been reversed and is highly relevant to these proceedings.
  7. The explanation provided by the Respondent in the “Statement of Facts pursuant of the Tribunal’s directions received on 7 July 2025”, left the Tribunal none the wiser as to what had occurred. Those submissions stated that:
    [2] “on 4 May 2021 the applicant filed his income tax return, for the year ended 30 June 2019 which disclosed a taxable income of $139,189. This income tax return also indicated that there was $38,996.93 tax payable on the taxable income. A copy of the income tax return is enclosed at tab A (page 4)”.

    Unfortunately, the return behind tab A was a return that had been adjusted as a result of audit.

  8. The Respondent sent a letter on 20 May 2021 notifying the Applicant that the processing of his income tax return year ended 30 June 2019 had been delayed and he was required to provide further information. Apparently, the notification letters issued on 20 May 2021 for the 2019 income tax return and in June 2021 for the income tax year ended 30 June 2020 commenced the pre-issue audit. The covert audit having been commenced on 14 February 2020.
  9. The Respondent issued the notice of assessment for the income year ended 30 June 2019 on 29 July 2021, which indicated a taxable income of $26,459[25]. On the same day the Respondent issued a notice of assessment for shortfall penalty for that year for $43,119.35[26].
  10. As a result of the pre-issue audit, it was determined that the taxable income for the income year ended 30 June 2019 was $1,569.21.
  11. On finalisation of the covert audit on 17 August 202, the Respondent issued a notice of amended assessment for the income year ended 30 June 2019 which was dated 19 August 2021[27] and “indicated a taxable income of $440,326.00”... “A notice of assessment shortfall penalty was also issued on 19 August 2021, imposing a base penalty amount 75% of the shortfall amount for intentional disregard of a taxation law pursuant to item 1 section 284 – 90 (1) of schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 resulting in a penalty $161,422.80” [28](emphasis added).
  12. The Respondent stated at [13] of its 7 July Statement of Facts that “as a result of the covert audit it was determined that the tax payable for the income year ended 30 June 2019 was $171,243.70” and at “[14] the Respondent accepts that the shortfall amount for the purposes of penalties should be recalculated by reference to the taxable income included in the Applicant’s tax return for the income year ended 30 June 2019 and the tax thereon (i.e. $38,996.93).”[29] The tax assessed was $26,459 and penalties were imposed at the 50% rate of $12,537.93, so the amount owing was $38,996.
  13. The Tribunal finds the imposition of both penalties extremely odd for the following reasons:

Firstly, there is no rational explanation for the imposition of penalties on an amount greater than the Respondent had determined was the Applicant’s adjusted income for the 2019 year.

Secondly, in respect of the penalties imposed for the same year following the covert audit, as the Applicant was told he could not object to the amended assessments issued as a result of the pre issue compliance audit for the 2019 and 2020 years because amended assessments and penalty assessments had already been issued, it appeared the amended assessments and penalty assessments were clearly intended to replace the assessments issued after the audit of the 2019 and 2020 income years. It was not apparent on the face of the penalty assessments that the penalties were imposed for two separate shortfall amounts.

  1. In response to the Tribunals queries sent to the parties on the 28 of January 2026, the Respondent replied on the 16 February 2026, advising the Tribunal that, among other things, “the Respondent considered the two penalties to be separate incidences of two distinct behaviours and believes they should be maintained”. “The initial penalty for recklessness does not form part of the reviewable objection decision and is therefore not under review in these proceedings”.
  2. The Tribunal gleaned from this somewhat cryptic explanation that the penalty in relation to the pre issue audit was determined “on reckless” for those behaviours set out in the response “due to the scale of the false statements made”. The penalty imposed under the second audit was imposed because the assessments had been made under section 167 (a) in relation to the 2019 year”. Obviously, the base penalty amount of 75% is automatically imposed by section 284-90 item 7 of the TAA if subsection 284-75 (3) ITAA applies. However, that explanation is at odds with the explanation provided in the Respondent’s Statement of 7 July 2025 that the penalty of 75% was imposed under item 1 of s 284-90 Sch 1 TAA not under item 7 of that section. In other words, the penalty imposed after the pre-issue audit and the covert audit relate to the same shortfall amount not “separate incidences of two distinct behaviours” in the same income tax year.
  3. Putting to one side the Tribunal’s findings that the section 167 (a) assessments should not have been made for the income years ending 30 June 2019 and 2020, in light of the Respondent’s statement of 7 July 2025, it is difficult to conclude that the 75% penalty was indeed imposed for a separate and distinct behaviour. The Respondent’s response to the Tribunal’s queries dated 28 January 2026, in relation to the penalty issue is extracted below:
“Until May 2021, the Applicant had not lodged his 2019 and 2020 income tax returns. Upon filing his 2019 and 2020 income tax returns, the Applicant was due for a refund of over $90,000 resulting from significant amounts claimed with a large portion of the Applicants tax liabilities being reduced by the negatively geared properties owned by the Applicant. A pre-issue audit was undertaken in relation to these years and the refunds were retained under s 8AAZLGA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) after the Commissioner determined that:

a) there was no commercial rent arrangement in place for the properties available for rent at the time; and

b) the PAYG withholding values declared were from associated companies controlled (or formerly controlled) by the Applicant have not declared or paid the requisite W2 values.”

Taking the above into consideration, the Commissioner removed the rental deductions and income and removed the income from the entities where the W2 amount had not been paid. Accordingly, a penalty based on recklessness was applied for those behaviours due to the scale of the false statement made.

Around the same time, the Applicant was subject to a second audit for the income years ended 30 June 2016 to 30 June 2020. The audit issued assessments as follows:

  1. The Respondent is correct the objection decision following the covert audit is before the Tribunal. However, the Tribunal is charged with the responsibility of arriving at the correct or preferable decision for the relevant years which includes the penalty assessments for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020. On the facts before the Tribunal, it is apparent the shortfall penalty imposed in 2019 was calculated on the figure of $40,000 a figure arrived at by the auditor conducting the pre-issue compliance audit and not a figure that appeared in the original return filed by the Applicant.
  2. It is difficult to see how the two penalties can be maintained, because on the facts and the Respondent’s submissions the two penalties appear to deal with the same shortfall amounts in both the 2019, and 2020 income tax year not “two and distinct behaviours”.
  3. In Ziegler v Commissioner of Taxation[31] the Court (Bromwich, Thawley and Jackmann JJ) held:
    “Assessments of administrative penalties flow from assessments of primary liabilities to tax, including assessments under section 166 and 167 of the ITAA 36”.

    “It is the amended assessments that lead to the “shortfall amount” or change in shortfall amount arising from an amended assessment of the primary tax on which the administrative penalty is calculated”.

    ...”given the statutory context and practical operation of the assessment and objection provisions, s.298-30(1) must be construed as requiring the Commissioner to make an assessment if he considers the earlier assessment incorrectly reflects the operation of the provisions imposing the penalty. The Commissioner must then notify the increased penalty liability under section 298 – 10. The taxpayer has the right to object to the underlying assessment under section 298-30 (2)”.

    “The Applicant subjected to the increased penalty assessments has the right to object to as they were entitled to do by s 298-30(2). The Tribunal had jurisdiction to review the relevant objection decision”.[32]

  4. Based on the Tribunal’s findings, the notices of administrative penalty issued after the audit of the 2019 and 2020 income tax years must have been superseded by the notices of administrative penalty following the covert audit and again replaced following the notices of amended assessment issued on 1 June 2023 for the 2019 and 2020 years.
  5. Contrary to the Commissioner’s position[33] that both penalties should be maintained because they are “separate incidences of two distinct behaviours”, the Respondent categorically advised the Tribunal the penalty of 75% was imposed for recklessness under item 1 s 284 – 90 (1) sch 1 TAA for intentional disregard of a taxation law. That penalty surely must have replaced the penalty for recklessness imposed under item 2, s284-90.
  6. Maintaining two penalties for the same year of income when the taxpayer was not entitled to object to the first penalty assessment because the second penalty assessment had already been issued is not consistent with the statutory context and practical operation of the assessment and objection provisions in s 298-30 schedule 1 TAA53.
  7. Further, the Commissioner’s submission that the penalty for recklessness does not form part of the reviewable objection decision and therefore not under review in these proceedings[34] cannot be correct. The Tribunal’s role is to determine how the amount of the penalty for recklessness was calculated to arrive at the correct or preferable decision with respect to penalties imposed in relation to the 2019 and 2020 income tax years. If there are two distinct shortfall amounts, it is not apparent on the face of the documents filed in these proceedings.

Evidence and comments on credibility of witness

  1. The Tribunal’s duty is not to speculate about evidence that might possibly have been called but rather to consider the evidence of the Applicant and any other evidence that forms part of the Tribunal documents and then decide whether, having regard to the whole of the evidence the Applicant has discharged the duty imposed on the Applicant under s14ZZK(1)(b)(i) TAA.[49]
  2. As Justice Logan stated in Commissioner of Taxation v Cassaniti[50] (Cassaniti) “In determining whether a case has been proved to the civil standard, demonstration that other evidence could have been called but was not called could , in certain circumstances, of which Jones v Dunkel offers an example, be relevant”.[51] Like Cassaniti, this is not such a case, for the reasons set out below it was not opened for the Tribunal to draw any adverse inference against the Applicant because he failed to file witness statements from various people, in deciding whether the Applicant had proved the case to the required standard. The Applicant’s documentary evidence did not need to be corroborated by third parties.
  3. In respect of the evaluation of the Applicant’s credibility, after considering in detail the entirety of the evidence filed in these proceedings, including the documents produced to the Respondent by the nine financial institutions the Applicant held accounts with pursuant to section 353-10 Schedule 1TAA, the opportunity to examine and reflect upon the 4888 pages of evidence that comprised the Tribunal Documents and Supplementary Tribunal Documents and to draw conclusions from them, by reason of the inferences available from the evidence as a whole, the Tribunal believed the evidence of the Applicant.
  4. The Tribunal found the Applicant’s evidence to be truthful. The documentary evidence that was available was consistent with the facts that were ultimately agreed by the Respondent and with the report of the forensic accountant and the bank statements and receipts that formed part of that report.
  5. The detailed amended Appendix A prepared by Applicant with references to the relevant line in each of the relevant bank accounts allowed the Tribunal to follow the debits and credits into and out of the various bank accounts.
  6. The Respondent relied on the insufficiency of the evidence before the Tribunal to raise doubts as to the credibility of the Applicant’s evidence. In relation to the brief narrations that appeared next to various transactions entered in the original bank statements, counsel for the Respondent contended the Applicant couldn’t possibly remember what the transaction was for and the brevity of the narration was not of assistance putting to the Applicant during cross examination “you would have no idea sitting here in the absence of keeping any proper ledger or any proper set of accounts whether these amounts were loans or some other cash deposit, be it a deposit from your parents, a deposit from your friend, a deposit from your business.” However, when taken to a particular bank[52] transaction with a narration beside it, the Applicant’s memory was prompted, and he was able to recall what the transaction related to with sufficient detail to satisfy the Tribunal that he could in fact recall the transaction in question. By way of example only, the Applicant responded to the above question as follow: “ I can tell you by that (indistinct) [entry] that I know I was stuck in Victoria and couldn’t pay the registration fee and you would see the rego fee, [referring to an entry below in the account] then you would see the accommodation fee, and looking at it you can logically paint a picture that – like, you reflect back to the time and go, ‘Okay, I remember why that happened, like, I couldn’t pay the rego. I needed money; what did I do? Okay, I borrowed it from the company’. The Applicant having previously answered “So for example if there is a cash deposit for argument’s sake of $1,000 and the next line afterwards is that I’ve gone and paid registration to VicRoads for example – well, I can recall that. I was short the money to pay the registration fee, so I took a director’s loan from the company. As in, I got the staff member running the shop to put the money into my account so it would show as a cash deposit of $1,000 or $1,500, whatever it is, then I’ve used that money to pay the, like rego to VicRoads. Then when I’ve come back, I’ve then paid it back to the company. So as far as cash deposits like that, I can reconcile it in my head to the fact that – by remembering, like, what lines underneath it when you look at it in the contents of the following lines”.[53] There was nothing in the Applicant’s demeanour to suggest he was making his answers up haphazardly.
  7. The authenticity of the various statement’s provided by the financial institutions with whom the Applicant banked was not in doubt. As the Applicant’s narrations were on the statements produced under the compulsory notices to the Respondent, the narrations were clearly not a recent invention of the Applicant’s. Further, there did not appear from an examination of the various bank statements to be a general intermingling of funds, primarily because the Applicant maintained separate bank accounts for each of his ventures or companies. Finally, the Applicant’s method of keeping track of the income and expenses for each property investment or company meant the Applicant’s personal financial records and business records could be recreated, as the report prepared by the forensic accountant clearly demonstrated. Having had the opportunity to observe the Applicant over the course of the hearing, the Tribunal found no inconsistencies with the evidence given by the Applicant and the various narration against certain entries in the bank statements. The statements produced by the various financial institutions, and the Applicant’s entries against certain transactions were relevant to the questions for consideration before the Tribunal.
  8. The Applicant’s explanation for the loss of his records due to a flood for which he received government compensation was entirely feasible and the Respondent’s counsel did not seek to challenge it.
  9. Section 262A of ITAA36 imposes a requirement on a taxpayer carrying on a business to keep records that record and explain all transactions and other acts engaged in that are relevant for the purposes of ascertaining a taxpayer’s income and expenditure. The Tribunal found the Applicant had kept records that were sufficient to explain all transactions, and those records were included in the Tribunal Documents.
  10. Section 1305 of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) (Corporations Act) provides that a book kept by a body corporate under a requirement of the Corporation’s Act is admissible in evidence in any proceeding and is prima facie evidence of any matter stated or recorded in the book. “Book” is defined in s 9 of the Corporation’s Act to include “financial records”. “Financial records” is in turn defined to include the following:
    (g) “invoices, receipts, orders for the payment of money, bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes and vouchers...”
  11. In Australian Securities and Investments Commission v Rich, Austin J[54] held that ‘financial records’ extended beyond documents of prime entry, such as cash books and journal, to derivative documents which include documents that record a company’s financial position or facilitate the preparation or auditing of annual or half yearly financial statements. As Justice Steward held in Cassaniti “In my view, the payslips and the PAYG payment summaries are probably ‘financial records’ which are required to be kept by s 286A”. The same is true in these proceedings, the PAYG payment summaries, the Bank and other financial institution statements, the formal ledger prepared by Domain Plus Real Estate, and the various invoices prepared on the letterheads of the respective companies, fell within the category of documents a company must keep under the Corporations Act and for the purposes of the Income Tax Act. These documents are written financial records that correctly record and explain the transactions and financial position of a company, or in this case allowed the Applicant, to prepare true and fair financial statements. The Applicant’s records were clearly sufficient to enable the forensic accountant to trace the flow of funds and prepare a detailed report, and it was opened to the Tribunal to examine that report to see what may be reasonably inferred from it. The Tribunal found the forensic accountants report was a financial record.
  12. Counsel for the Respondent did not put to the Applicant that the narrations in the bank statements or the invoices from Mohammadi Enterprises or Jakka Electronics or Mobile Phone Shack Pty Ltd or Domain Plus Real Estate were false. It was opened for the Respondent’s counsel to make that submission because the native spread sheets prepared by the Respondent’s auditor and filed by the Respondent with the Tribunal’s documents implied that was the case.
  13. The various invoices appear to have been written on the letterhead of the respective companies, addressed to the Applicant’s company, Mobile Phone Shack Pty Ltd (Mobile Phone Shack) or issued by the Applicant on Mobile Phone Shack’s letterhead. There was no evidence to suggest that the invoices did not constitute a correct record of the terms of the supply and purchase of the mobile phones.
  14. While the Applicant’s record keeping was less than perfect, the Applicant’s evidence was sufficient to support his contentions that he had not evaded tax in the relevant years and to establish that amounts included by the Respondent as assessable income in the Relevant years were not income of the Applicant.
  15. The question of “insufficiency of evidence’ was considered by the Full Federal Court in Commissioner of Taxation v Casmatti[55] Justice Steward, with whom Greenwood and Logan JJ agreed, stated a taxpayer does not bear a special burden of proof:
    “..., other than the necessity to scrutinise evidence given by the taxpayer him or herself with care, no such special burden exists.”
  16. His honour went on to summarise 5 requirements, derived from the judgement of Hunt J in in Allied Pastoral Holdings Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation,[56]that his Honour noted applied to all forms of revenue proceedings:
    “(1) first, where the onus is on the taxpayer (whether pursuant to s 14ZZO of the TAA or otherwise) the degree or standard of proof required is that which ordinarily applies in civil proceedings. The direction given to a jury in civil cases aptly describes that onus by reference to a pair of scales and to the arguments of each party being placed at each end as Hunt J said in Allied Pastoral:

    ...if the plaintiff succeeds... in weighing down those scales ever so slightly in his favour then he has discharged the burden he carries...(emphasis added).

    (2) secondly, for that purpose it is not obligatory for a taxpayer, in order to discharge his burden of proof, to call all material witnesses and to produce all material documents which support her or his or its position;

    (3) fourthly, there is no requirement that evidence can only be accepted as admissible and probative if it is corroborated;

    (4) fifthly, the Tribunal of fact is free to accept the evidence of the taxpayer alone if it finds the taxpayer to be truthful;

    (5) finally, it would usually be prudent to corroborate the evidence of a taxpayer. it is also prudent to adduce contemporaneous objective evidence, but prudence should not be confused with the requirements of the law.”

  17. In these proceedings the Tribunal Documents included a detailed report prepared by a forensic accountant, whose independence was not called in to question by the Respondent. Included as part of that report were all banking records the Applicant was able to obtain which had narrations, even though brief, on the copies of the original statements, copies of the Applicant’s PAYG payment summaries, Domain Plus Real Estate’s (the managing real estate agent) rental records, the original tax returns for the income tax years ended 30 June 2016, 2017 and 2018 that had been prepared and lodged by a registered tax agent, copies of the tax returns prepared and lodged by the Applicant for the 2019 and 2020 years, and copies of invoices that had not been destroyed in a flood. These records were sufficient to enable the forensic accountant to identify deposits that were not income of the Applicant.
  18. Although the Respondent did not accept all the forensic accountants’ findings, the Respondent subsequently removed $1,143,006.00 from the Amended Assessments issued in August 2021 following the receipt of that report. Further, the detail inserted into Appendix A by the Applicant allowed deposits and payments going back almost 10 years to be identified against each of the entries, resulting in the Respondent conceding, during the hearing, that a further amount of $1,060,753.63 should not have been included as part of the Applicant’s allegedly undeclared and assessable income.

The categories of unexplained deposits included as income in the Applicant’s Amended Notices of Assessment that remained in dispute.

The Loans

  1. The Respondent’s position in respect of the various loans that remained in dispute was essentially that the Applicant made submissions that had no evidential foundation. Counsel for the Respondent in closing submissions, said:
    “I have made it very clear from the start of the case and its duration, that there’s got to be parameters around when someone is making submissions on when someone is giving evidence. Much of what you’ve heard today is submission that lacked any evidential foundation. By way of witness statements from each of Mr Day’s parents, a letter from Mr Madawala, who was available for cross examination, and a letter from Mr Zay Aung, who was not available for cross examination. Beyond the documentary record, that is the totality of the witness evidence that you have in this case.

    In my submission, you wouldn’t give much weight to those narration is in the bank statements in circumstances where you have no evidence about the terms of the loan, for example, Mr Aung was not available to be cross examined about it. You simply have assertions and a narration of ’’loan’, in circumstances where there are a series of highly unusual and complex movements of money and transactions”.

  2. The Tribunal proposes to deal with each of the loan transactions below and the reasons why the documentary evidence, coupled with the Tribunal’s findings that the Applicant was honest in his answers, was sufficient to satisfy the Tribunal that the unidentified deposits included in the Applicant’s Amended Assessment and during the hearing characterised as Loans, were indeed loans.

Repayment of a loan made to the Applicant by a Mr Zay Aung in the amount of $26,630.

  1. The Tribunal’s evidence included a signed letter from Mr Zay Aung[57] The Applicant’s bank statements showed 14 entries that the Applicant had identified by the relevant line item on each bank statement in Appendix A. The Applicant’s evidence was “Now, if I take you to say line 19 which is on TB827, you’ll see next to the $5000 deposit, it clearly states “cash deposit $5000” then the notation on the bank statement is “Loan repayment Zay”,... I can go through line by line here, but every single line on there, on the original bank statement, they all indicate, Loan repayments Zay”.[58] The deposit of $5,000 was the second loan payment which was made on 4 July 2016, the first repayment for $1,875 was made on the 24 December 2015. The following 12 repayments all had the same narration but varied in amounts from $1000 to $2,500. The last repayment by Zay was made on 18 July 2017,[59] well before the start of the covert audit.
  2. The Applicant told the Tribunal “Zay was one of my friends, he’s since moved back to Burma, but he was living here he borrowed money when he was buying a house for a deposit and then paying back over a period of the year”.[60] When asked by counsel for the Respondent why there was no loan document and it was put to the Applicant that there was no loan facility, the Applicant’s evidence was “he was staying here he was one of my best friends. He was living in Australia at the time. So no, there is no loan facility document done up. You’ll see that one of the transactions, however, there is where I supposedly loaned him so where he transferred me about 28 grand I think it was. It was a deposit for his house at 87 Alfred Street, which is referred to in one of the transactions when he transferred me the money then I transferred it to the bank for him because he didn’t at the time you couldn’t do bank transfers instantly. So he was with the same bank as me, then I transferred it with the NAB connect at that time, to pay for his home loan deposit. And then you can see that I paid the repayments for him and he paid it back to me.” There was nothing evasive or general in that answer. When asked why there was no correspondence, the Applicant’s evidence was “I would have had text messages at the time but were talking about an event of like seven years ago. My phone doesn’t go back seven years. I don’t know if yours does”.[61] In fact the first loan was made on the 24 December 2015 almost 10 years earlier.
  3. The Tribunal asked the Applicant to explain the circumstances in which Mr Zay prepared the letter. The Applicant told the Tribunal “I phoned Zay and told him that I needed a letter or something to state that I (presumably he) had borrowed the money from him (presumably me) over that period of time. Like, I (presumably he) had made the repayments over that period of a period of time”. The Tribunal accepts the errors in the above statement were probably the result of the Applicant’s dyspraxia, because the Applicant went on to say “So it is the second entry from top, which is the first entry for 4 July 2016. It is a cash deposit of $5000 and underneath that in brackets you’ll see Loan repayment Zay. That’s an original bank statement from back in 2016. There is no way I could go back and edit the original bank statement, so I’d say that the very fact that it’s got the notation on there would give some right to fact there was obviously a loan repayment from Zay there. That’s why it’s actually written on the bank statement. And that’s the fact for every single of these loan Zay’s that I got in my page 8 of my final submissions document do you want me to go line by line?” ... “It’s from line 19 to line 25 in Appendix A or on TB 827 document”.[62] The Applicant then took the Tribunal to each of the line items in TB documents. The Applicant concluded his submissions in relation to the loan repayments received by Zay by telling the Tribunal “so basically all of the transactions that I’ve said are for my friend Zay- relate to my loan from my friend Zay, which are referred to on page 8 my final submissions document, every single one of those transactions have the comment on there of ‘ Loan repayment Zay’ of an actual original bank statement. So when I asked how we can relate it back so many years, what cash deposits relate to Zay, what bank statement loan repayment Zay. So that’s how I can (go) back it at 10 years later or seven years later or whatever it is and be able to confirm that it is part of that loan that I lent to my friend Zay.”[63]
  4. The Respondent’s position was the letter from Mr Aung was not a witness statement or affidavit, and Mr Aung was not available for cross examination. Counsel for the Respondent submitted:
    “you wouldn’t give much weight to those notations in the bank statements in circumstances where you have no evidence about the terms of the loan, for example, Mr Aung was not available to be cross-examined about it. You simply have assertions and a narration of ‘loan” in circumstances where there are a series of highly unusual and complex movements of money in transactions so even in those instances where there is a reference to loan, in the absence of other evidence, that wouldn’t be sufficient, on the facts of this case, for the Applicant to discharge his onus in respect of those amounts.. ”[64]
  5. On the contrary, and consistent with the five principles summarised by Steward J in Cassaniti, the Tribunal finds the contemporaneous objective evidence comprising copies of the original the bank statements, was sufficient to corroborate the Applicant’s submission that all the deposits with a narration “Loan repayment Zay” were exactly that.

Loan Repayment to the Applicant by a Miss Naomi Croak in the amount of $500.00

  1. A similar exchange occurred between counsel for the Respondent and the Applicant in relation to a deposit of $500 made on the 19 October 2016. Counsel’s focus was the lack of a loan agreement between Ms Croak and the Applicant, and a failure to file any evidence such as text messages or emails referring to the loan. The Applicant’s evidence was “like I said before, were going back seven years, so it would be impossible to have a text message. The only evidence I have is what’s on my bank statement which says ‘repayment loan Naomi.’ Naomi was an ex-partner of mine. She borrowed money off me of $500 or $530 and paid it back.”
  2. In the Amended Appendix A, the Applicant had identified the line item in the relevant bank account that had the narration ‘repayment Namoi.’ For the same reasons set out above, the Tribunal was taken to the Bankwest account of the Applicant and the relevant line item on that statement and the narration against a deposit of $500 stated ‘repayment Namoi’. A sufficient contemporaneous record existed, there was nothing vague about the Applicant’s explanation of the deposit, and the Tribunal is satisfied the deposit of $500 made on 19 October 2016 was the repayment of a loan.

Loan repayment to the Applicant by Raj of $2500

  1. A similar exchange occurred between counsel for the Respondent and the Applicant in relation to a cash deposit of $2,500 made on 11 July 2017, where the narration underneath the cash deposit stated ‘Repayment loan Raj’. The Applicant’s evidence was Raj was a friend whom he had lent money to when he was planning to move overseas. Raj had repaid the amount into the Applicant’s account before he departed Australia. The Applicant told the Tribunal he had had no contact with Raj since he left Australia.
  2. Notwithstanding the only identification for the borrower in this case was a first name Raj, the narration on the original bank statement against a deposit on the 11 July 2017 was a sufficient record to satisfy the Tribunal that the cash deposit of $2,500 was the repayment of the loan previously made to a friend.

Loans made to the Applicant by his father Mr Glennon James Day between 2017-2019 in the amount of $31,850.

  1. Mr Glennon Day provided a statutory declaration in relation to loans he had made to his son in 2017, 2018 and 2019. The statutory declaration was sworn on 26 March 2024. Although brief, it reads as an honest statement by a father attempting to recall the amounts and the dates that he lent money to his son. Mr Glennon Day in the statutory declaration stated he recalled lending money to his son “in an amount of approximately $6,000 to help enable him to pay his home loan in March 2017”. He recalled lending approximately $10,000 to his son over the period April to June 2017, but “after being questioned by Peter quite possibly $11,400 given to him in about four lots of $3000 or $2000 between those months”. [65] Mr G Day stated he recalled [the Applicant] paying this money back to him “a week or two later.” Mr G Day stated he recalled lending his son “approximately $14,000 around the middle of 2018, most likely in early July”. Mr G Day stated he recalled lending “approximately $1,500 in May 2019”.
  2. During cross-examination by the Respondent’s counsel, Mr G Day presented as a truthful witness. There was nothing in Mr G Day’s evidence that suggested he had manufactured his evidence to assist his son. Mr G Day stated he discussed his evidence with his wife and his son, but he “hadn’t really assisted him in too much of a way.”[66] The truth of that statement was apparent from his evidence.” When counsel for the Respondent put to Mr G Day that he had no recollection of the amounts or dates the money was lent and that the amount in issues was at odds with his sons, Mr G Day’s evidence was “I know there was amounts of 3000, 2000, 2000 and another 3000 that’s what I was aware of so that would have been 10,000 I was aware of. There could well have been other times he would come to me at certain times for certain amounts of money.”[67] When counsel for the Respondent put to Mr G Day that he had an “unreliable recollection of these events.” Mr G Day was very clear stating “events in 2017, the first ones, I certainly remember the $6000 and I remember a $10,000-$11,000- it says 11,400 over the next few months the following year, I certainly remember the $14,000 being repaid yes I do remember”. When questioned about the repayment of the $14,000, Mr G Day’s evidence was “no, the payment was in July, that I made and he would have repaid it in August approximately August, September, it was pretty soon afterwards a couple of weeks after”.[68] When counsel for the Respondent questioned Mr G Day about the deposits into his son’s account, Mr G Day quite honestly told the Tribunal he wouldn’t have any idea because he did not make the deposit, but he then went on to say in his evidence “but that doesn’t mean I didn’t lend it to him before that, he’s chosen to put it in on that particular day”. When counsel for the Respondent asked why the Applicant had made deposits in random amounts on random days with narratives as brief as cash deposit, Mr Day’s evidence was “I can only assume that that’s when [the Applicant] needed to pay the money for some reason or other, for purchasing something. I don’t know but that’s up to him not me.” Finally, when counsel for the Respondent put it to Mr G Day that he couldn’t differentiate one cash deposit in the Applicant’s bank account from another Mr G Day quite truthfully answered “no, why would I, no.”
  3. Mr G Day was a retired senior public servant with the Commonwealth Government, having worked for the Australian Government “for over 35 years, including 15 years as an onshore protection case officer in the Department of Home Affairs.”[69] Mr G Day’s evidence was matter of fact, and direct. Mr G Day thought there might have been a loan agreement, but when it was put to him that there was no evidence before the Tribunal “of any communication between your wife and yourself referring to the existence of a loan,”[70] he agreed.
  4. In answer to the questions put to him addressing repayment of the loans, apart from the $14,000, Mr G Day in his evidence said had been repaid by his son in 2018, Mr G Day agreed there had been no other repayments. It was apparent from his evidence that when his son needed money for a reason, he would give him the money in cash which he had at his home, the cash coming out of “a punting bank that I have.”[71] That evidence was consistent with the Applicant’s evidence given during his cross examination. When counsel for the Respondent put to Applicant that he had not included in his evidence “any bank statements from your father to show or explain deposits made into your bank account.” The Applicant answered “because if I borrowed money from my father, he would give it to me in cash. My father was a punter or bookmaker so to speak, at one stage. So I’d get it in cash because he had it in cash. It was not income from an account as such it would have been written down, like, in his little ledger book or whatever how he keeps his record”.[72] When counsel for the Respondent put to the Applicant that “he had no way of knowing seven years later that the cash deposits came from any loans from your father”, the Applicant responded “I have no way of proving it no,” but went on to explain that (emphasis added):
    “I took money from my father in a lump-sum amount and then would have used it over the next few days to pay whatever needed to be paid. When you asked me why I would have put 900 and then 400, why wouldn’t I have just put a set amount in, my answer to that question is, I put the money in that was required to pay a particular bill, which money has gone in and been transferred out to pay the required bill”.
  5. There is nothing unusual in a family situation for a loan between a parent and a child to be undocumented. It would have been unusual given the random nature of the above loans if there had been a loan document drawn up. The same is true of text messages or emails between a parent and a child, even an adult child, when they are living under the same roof. The Tribunal accepts Mr G Day’s evidence that he made loans in cash to his son during the 2017 and 2018 and 2019 years to assist his son meet various expenses from time to time. The Tribunal found nothing self-serving in any of the evidence given by Mr G Day during his cross examination.
  6. On the evidence before the Tribunal, which included references in Appendix A to the relevant line items in the Applicant’s bank accounts that were included in the Tribunal documents, the Tribunal finds the amount of $31,850 was lent to the Applicant by his father, and it was not assessable income of the Applicant.

A Loan made to the Applicant by Mrs Cathie Day on 30 July 2018 in the amount of $9,000

  1. Mrs Day provided a second statutory declaration that she had lent the Applicant $9,000 on 30 July 2018. The deposit was made into the Applicant’s IMB account and under the deposit is a notation Cathie.[77]
  2. Counsel for the Respondent put to Mrs Day that because there was no loan agreement, no communication in evidence between the Applicant and his mother, or Mrs Day and her husband about this loan and there was no evidence in of any cash withdrawal that Mrs Day had “mistaken having lent her son $9000 on 30 July 2018”. Mrs Day replied in clear and unequivocal terms “no because I had the cash at home. I went to the bank, and that’s me putting it in. Did you see it say, Cathie next to it? But it’s annotated on here with my name I deposited the money at Sylvania.”
  3. During his cross-examination about the loans from his parents, when it was put to him by counsel for the Respondent that there had been no communication between the Applicant and his parents about the loan, the Applicant answered
    “there was a phone call to my mum at the time saying look mum, I’m desperate for this property. I need some money. I’m short on it. Can you go to the bank and organise it for me? And my mum went and deposited in at the bank which wasn’t on whatever date it was that you say, states loan or deposit Cathie Day, because it was my mum that went and did it and the bank filled out the form or whatever it is put her name on it”.[78]
  4. The Respondent’s counsel, in closing submissions, stated that the evidence of Mr Glennon Day and Mrs Cathie Day should not be accepted as evidence. The Respondent’s counsel contended that Mr and Mrs Day were “unreliable witnesses” who were “clearly motivated to assist him in these proceedings” and “intimately involved in the preparation of [the Applicant’s] evidence”. The Respondent put to the Tribunal that “while parents would not ordinarily be considered to be independent witnesses, the Applicant’s parents are much more involved in their son’s litigation than what would ordinarily be the case for most parents”...[79]
  5. With respect to the above comments by the Respondent’s counsel, the observations of Justice Hill in Imperial Bottle Shops v Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 276; (1991) 22 ATR 148 are relevant:
    “A taxpayer who does not keep records of his deductible outgoings faces a very difficult task. If he goes into the witness box and swears that he has incurred the outgoings he is making a self-serving statement. That does not necessarily mean that he is not to be believed. Such a statement, like statements of purpose, or object or state of mind must, however, be "tested most closely, and received with the greatest caution": Pascoe v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 11 ATD 108 at 111. It would, of necessity, be a rare case indeed where a taxpayer, claiming to have expended a very large sum of money on trading stock and other business expenses, would succeed in satisfying the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive. Some other corroborative evidence would normally be required which makes it more probable than not that his sworn testimony is to be believed. It must, however, be borne in mind that the evidence of a taxpayer is not to be regarded as "prima facie unacceptable” (McCormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; (1978-9) 143 CLR 284 at 302 per Gibbs J)
  6. Although the above observations of Justice Hill relate to a taxpayer, the same is true of any family member or close friend who gives evidence on behalf of a taxpayer. Just because the witness has a close relationship with the taxpayer does not necessarily mean that they are not a credible witnesses. Their statements must be “tested most closely and received with the greatest caution” and “some other cooperative evidence would normally be required which makes it more probable than not” that the sworn testimony is to be believed. It must, however, be borne in mind that the evidence of a taxpayer is not to be regarded as prima facie unacceptable.”[80]
  7. Having had the opportunity to observe Mr Glennon Day and Mrs Cathie Day during cross-examination, the Tribunal could discern no bias toward the Applicant in any of their testimony. Both Mr and Mrs Day presented as honest and straightforward individuals who though motivated to assist the Applicant with the necessary preparation for the proceeding before the Tribunal, and clearly worried about their sons mental health, which was made apparent in correspondence sent by Mr Day to the Respondent on 18 May 2023, had not colluded in respect of their evidence.[81] There was nothing that suggested either parent had manufactured their evidence in order to assist their son. Further, there was corroborative evidence that Mr and Mrs Day had withdrawn a significant amount of money from their joint bank account and Mrs Day’s individual bank account to help their son purchase another Casual Road property.
  8. Counsel for the Respondent made much of the fact that there was no contemporaneous document making any reference to the existence of a loan between Mr and Mrs Day and the Applicant. Frankly, the Tribunal would be more likely to find the evidence “self-serving” if there had been a formal loan agreement between Mr and Mrs Day and the Applicant setting out a drawdown schedule backed up by text messages and emails when the Applicant’s evidence was he was living under the same roof as his parents.[82] The fact that the loans had not been repaid, is not relevant to the question of whether the money was lent. There was no evidence before the Tribunal that suggested Mr and Mrs Day could not afford to lend their son the funds they advanced, that submission made by counsel for the Respondent lacked foundation. It was clear from Mr and Mrs Day’s bank accounts that they had withdrawn $45,000 mostly from ATMs in the space of a few days, which appeared from a precursory review of their bank statements, an uncharacteristic method of transaction. Mrs Day’s explanation for the method of transfer was entirely plausible. Having had the opportunity to observe the Applicant during the hearing, the Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence that he rang his mother and said words to the effect of “look I’m desperate for this property. I need some money can you go to the bank and organise it for me.”[83] Finally, counsel for the Respondent’s submission that failing to include the loans from his parents as a liability in the various applications the Applicant’s mortgage broker made on behalf of the Applicant to obtain finance to purchase various properties, was not sufficient to establish the loans did not exist. It would be a rare event if informal loan arrangements between a parent and a child were included on an application to obtain finance, particularly one prepared by a mortgage broker.
  9. On the totality of the evidence before the Tribunal and having the opportunity to observe Mr and Mrs Day during cross-examination, the Tribunal finds the amount of $64,000 was a loan from the Applicant’s parents and that amount was not assessable income to the Applicant.

The Applicant’s evidence was the accountant used BankLink for the purpose of preparing these returns and he believed the returns were correctly lodged. BankLink is an automated service designed to streamline bookkeeping for small businesses and their accountants. It is a secure link between the bank and the accountant, that automatically codes transactions allowing accountants to prepare financial reports and tax compliance documents efficiently and without the need to rely on the taxpayer’s description of a transaction. It was designed to eliminate manual data entry for small businesses and deliver accurate up-to-date data for tax compliance. If the accountant used Banklink to prepare the Applicant’s income tax returns for the financial years ending 30 June 2016, 2017 and 2018 there would be no reason for the Applicant’s tax returns to be anything other than accurate. Any discrepancy between the tax returns prepared and lodged by the registered tax agent may be the result of the Applicant initially accepting that amounts ‘unverified” by the forensic accountant should be included in his assessable income because at the time he made that concession he was not able to prove otherwise.

The Tribunal finds, on the balance of probabilities, that the Applicant has demonstrated the amount of $463,657.78 was made up of directors’ loans and should not have been included in the Amended Assessments.

If the Applicant’s notice of objection failed to object to the deposits referable to these loans or lines of credit, the Respondent should not have been taken by surprise that the Applicant sought to raise them before the Tribunal, and the Tribunal finds the Commissioner suffered no prejudice.

CONCLUSIONS ON THE ASSESSMENTS -THE RESPONDENTS ISSUE 1

“Whether the Applicant has discharged his onus under s 14ZZK of the TAA 1953 of establishing that the amounts recorded in appendix a to the objection decision are not assessable to him in the relevant years.”

  1. Notwithstanding the Applicant’s less than perfect record keeping, the source material, being the original bank statements produced under compulsory notices to the Respondent, the draft forensic accountants report prepared in 2022, that broke down the transactions identified by the Respondent into various categories for each of the financial years under review, undertook detailed calculations divided into verified and unverified columns, annexed bank statements, business transaction reconciliation and stock invoices, a detailed Re-performance of bank transactions workpaper, the Domain Plus Real Estate Rental Income Summary Records for the relevant years and the fact that the 2 loans made by private lenders were the subject of New South Wales Supreme Court Proceedings that the Respondent was a party to, were sufficient to satisfy the Tribunal that documentary evidence existed to substantiate the Applicant’s submissions.
  2. The Tribunal accepted the Applicant’s sworn testimony, his answers were detailed and he was plainly truthful. There were few inconsistencies between the written submissions and the other documentary evidence before the Tribunal and the Applicant’s oral evidence given during his cross examination. The Applicant’s parents, although clearly supportive of their son, gave credible and consistent evidence of the circumstances surrounding the loans they had made to their son. Mr and Mrs Day were cross-examined by the Respondent’s counsel, and the Tribunal could discern nothing to suggest they had colluded with the Applicant or manufactured any part of their evidence.
  3. It was unfortunate that the Applicant had not arranged to obtain witness statements from material witnesses. However, as Steward J stated in Cassaniti[127] as a general proposition it is not necessary for a party to call all material witnesses and produce all material documents to support their position. Although it may have been helpful if Mr Hillier from RSM or the forensic accountant Mr Torline of Slaven Torline had provided witness statements and been available for cross examination, the material they produced contained in the Tribunal Bundle spoke for itself. Further, it was apparent from the letter RSM sent to the Respondent in September 2022 that many affidavits potentially relevant to these proceedings had been filed on behalf of the Applicant in the Supreme Court proceedings. It may have assisted the Applicant if arrangements had been made for the affidavits relating to the loans from the private lenders Mr Choucair and Mr Cheikh to be made available to the Tribunal and well as any orders made by the Supreme Court and on a confidential basis the settlement arrangements between the parties to those proceedings. It may also have assisted the Applicant if Mr Aung had prepared a witness statement, however the annotation on the original bank statements was sufficient for the Tribunal to be satisfied that the Applicant had made loans to Mr Aung, among other of the Applicant’s friends. It may have been helpful if Mr Mustafa Madarwala of Mohammadi Enterprises had prepared a witness statements however the annotation in the original bank statements that identified the electronic transfers from Mohammadi Enterprises with an invoice number and the reconciliation done by the forensic accountant was sufficient for the Tribunal to be satisfied that deposits made by Mohammadi Enterprises were payments for stock supplied by Mobile Phone Shack.
  4. Having carefully considered the information contained in Amended Appendix A, which runs to 451 pages and cross referenced that material against the 4888 pages of Tribunal documents, primarily made up of the Applicant’s Bank statements, and the opportunity to reflect on the oral evidence of the Applicant and his parents Mr Glennon Day and Mrs Cathie Day, the Tribunal finds on the civil standard, that the Applicant has discharged the statutory onus imposed on him under 14ZZK TAA. Other than the $71,404.57 conceded by the Applicant, the Tribunal finds all other amounts recorded in Amended Appendix A to the objection decision that which the Respondent maintained during the hearing were not assessable income of the Applicant.

ONUS OF PROOF - FRAUD AND EVASION OPINION

  1. Under s 170 ITAA36 the Respondent may amend an assessment at any time if the Respondent is of the opinion that there has been “fraud or evasion”.
  2. In this case the Respondent has exercised this power in respect of the assessments for financial years ended 30 June 2016, 2017, and 2018.
  3. Under s 56 of the ART Act, for the purpose of reviewing a decision, the Tribunal may exercise all the powers and discretions conferred by any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision and under s108. The decision of the Tribunal, where there has been a variation or substitution of the decision under the review, is deemed to be that of the decision-maker whose decision is under review.
  4. It is well settled that under the ART Act the Tribunal is placed in the same position as the administrative decision-maker at first instance under s 108 and s 108 generally excludes the introduction of the concept of onus of proof in making decisions on review. As Deputy President the Hon Brain Tamberlin QC held in Adam Bennett, Molly Bennett and the Estate of Edwin Bennet and Commissioner of Taxation[128] “No onus of proof in the legal sense generally arises under the AAT Act: see Catena v Australian Securities and Investments Commission[129], a decision of the Full Federal Court.”
  5. Section 14ZZK of the Administration Act relates to grounds of objection and burden of proof. It provides that on an application in the Tribunal for review of a reviewable objection decision, the Applicant has the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive.
  6. Under s 350-10 item 2 of the TAA the production of a notice of assessment is considered evidence that the assessment was properly made and of its correctness.
  7. It is also well settled that the provisions of s 170 ITAA36 form part of the substantive liability under an amended assessment:[130] The Applicant bears the onus of disproving the Respondents finding of evasion.
  8. In the present circumstances it is worth setting out in full the much-cited statement of Dixon.J in Denver Chemicals Manufacturing Co v Commissioner of Taxation[131]
    “I think it is unwise to attempt to define the word “evasion”. The context of s.210 (2) shows that it means more than avoid and also more than a mere withholding of information or the mere furnishing of misleading information. It is probably safe to say that some blameworthy act or omission on the part of the taxpayer or those for whom he is responsible is contemplated. An intention to withhold information less the Commissioner should consider the taxpayer liable to a greater extent than the taxpayer is prepared to concede, is conduct which if the result is to avoid tax would justify finding evasion” (emphasis added).
  9. As the Applicant conceded amounts in the financial year ended 30 June 2016, 2017 and 2018 only because there was “no possible way for me to prove the originating source of this cash or the flow of it’ (emphasis added). The Tribunal cannot make any findings about those amounts.
  10. To the extent the amended assessments for the income years ended 30 June 2016 – 30 June 2018 were grounded in a finding of evasion given the number of concessions made by the Respondent during the hearing and the Tribunal’s findings in relation to the amounts not conceded by the Respondent, the Tribunal is not persuaded on the evidence before it that the Applicant’s conduct was conduct to avoid tax and therefore justify a finding of evasion.
  11. Furthermore, the Applicant used a registered tax agent to prepare and lodge his income tax returns for those years and provided the tax agent with access to a platform called Bank Link so if there were any errors in the preparation of those accounts, they were probably the result of inadvertence rather than a deliberate withholding of information. There was no evidence before the Tribunal to suggest the Applicant had not given the tax agent all relevant taxation information to prepare the tax returns. Based on the material before the Tribunal, the Tribunal finds any error in those returns was not the result of intentional disregard of a taxation law or recklessness by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law. The provisions of s 184-75 (6) should have been applied.
  12. The Tribunal finds on the facts before it that the Respondent erred in forming an opinion of evasion on 20 August 2021 and was not authorised under section 170 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1953 (Cth) (ITAA36) to issue notices of amended assessments to the Applicant for the income tax years ended 30 June 2016, 30 June 2017 and 30 June 2018.
  13. In respect of the financial years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020, for the following reasons the Tribunal finds the Applicant has discharged the onus that the opinion of evasion should not have been formed.
  14. Firstly, the Applicant conceded the amount of $71,404.57, only on the basis he could no longer prove “the originating source of the cash or the flow of it”. As a percentage of the alleged unexplained deposits of $6,863,098.96 that is 1.04%. In respect of the amounts conceded in the 2019 year of $4,918.28 and in the 2020 year of $6,087.78, in total $11,005.78, as a percentage of the amended assessments for the 2019 year of $308,326 and for the 2020 year of $1,250,948 in total $1,559,274, that is a percentage of 0.070%, by any standard a de minimis amount. There is a legal doctrine at common law that prevents the law from intervening where matters are trivial. The de minimis rule applies in both civil and criminal matters. Relevantly, for present purposes, in criminal matters the rule may prevent an offence from being proven where it is technically made out but its effect is insignificant.
  15. In relation to the Copyright Act the High Court in Ice TV Pty Ltd v Nine Network Australia Pty Ltd[132] affirmed that the breach must be substantial for a breach of copyright to be found to have occurred.
  16. With respect to taxation matters in Taxation Determination TD 2005/54: the Commissioner applied the principle that the law is not concerned with trifles (the minimis non curat lex), citing with approval the decision of Hill J in Farnell Electronics Components Pty Ltd.[133] In that case Justice Hill confirmed the de minimis principle is a principle of statutory interpretation and quoted with approval the following passage from Halsbury’s Laws of England, 4Th ed, vol 44 (1), at paragraph [1441]:
    “The de minimis principle. Unless the contrary intention appears, an enactment by implication imports the principle of legal policy expressed in the maximum de minimis curat lex (the law does not concern itself with trifling matters): so if an enactment is expressed to apply to matters of a certain description it will not apply where the description is satisfied only to a very small extent.“
  17. At [6] “the Commissioner does not consider that there is any intention to exclude the de minimis principle in the interpretation of sections 705- 35 and 104 - 515. What is de minimis in any particular case would depend on all the facts and circumstances including the relative cost and the value of the relative asset compared with the CA of the joining entity.” The same reasoning applies in this case. Having addressed all of the evidence and considered the matter very carefully, the Tribunal determines the concessions made by the Applicant are the minimis and finds on the facts before it that the Applicant did not deliberately evade his taxation obligations in the financial years ended 2019 and 2020. Further, the errors made by the Respondent were significant. This is not a case of chipping away at the Respondent’s assessments, these errors are material and formed part of the Respondent’s reasons for decision.
  18. In the table reproduced below the Applicant quite rightly pointed out that the cash withdrawals from his personal bank accounts in 2019 did not add up to $241,873 they added to $63,724. Similarly, there was only one entry in 2020 for an amount of $127,271 but the total for that year is recorded as $148,756. Further, the Applicant drew the Tribunal’s attention to the fact that the Applicant had made no withdrawals in any year from the IMB account ended 2623, a review of that account demonstrated that was correct. The withdrawals from that account recorded in the Respondent’s table amounted to $203,730. The relevant bank statements were produced by IMB to the Respondent on 31 March 2020 and they form part of the Tribunal documents at TB 10832 TB 1100. Finally, a review of the bank statement compulsorily produced to the Respondent for the Bankwest account ended 7009, do not reveal any deposits that were “unexplained” and certainly not an amount totaling $82,602.64.
  19. The Tribunal found the Applicant to be truthful in his oral evidence, he conceded he may have made errors, but from the records that were before it, the Tribunal did not find “the taxpayer committed a blameworthy act by omitting from his tax returns the unexplained deposits which he used to fund his lifestyle without any credible explanation for his conduct. The deposits were substantially more than the taxpayer’s declared income and the taxpayer knew, or ought to have known, that he had omitted reporting the income in his income tax returns for each of the relevant years”,[134] as alleged by the Respondent in the Evasion Opinion. A review of the bank accounts produced under compulsory notice to the Respondent do not reveal deposits totaling $28.3 million were made to the Applicant’s personal bank accounts during the income tax years ended 30 June 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2020. It is impossible for the Tribunal to ascertain where this figure originated from or conclude that this amount was used to fund the Applicant’s lifestyle.
  20. In respect of the financial years ended 2016-2018, the Applicant engaged a registered tax agent to prepare and lodge his income tax returns for the 2016- 2018 income tax years. The Applicant gave his tax agent access to all his accounts via the Bank Link platform. The Tribunal found nothing to suggest the Applicant failed to provide his tax agent with all necessary information to correctly prepare his income tax returns and if there was any error, which on the evidence before the Tribunal is doubtful s 284-75 (the so-called safe harbor provisions) should have applied.
  21. In respect of the income tax years ended 2019 and 2020, the Respondent advised the Tribunal in the statement provided to the Tribunal on 16 February 2026 that the default assessments for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020 were issued under s 167(a) ITAA36. The Applicant did not default in lodging his tax returns for those years albeit the 2019 income year was lodged late. Late lodgment is not ordinarily a sufficient reason for issuing a default assessment. The Applicant accepted he had made errors in those returns, however on the evidence before the Tribunal the errors appear to have been caused by the Applicant’s inexperience in preparing his own returns rather than an intention to evade his tax obligations or furnish misleading information to the Respondent.

PENALTIES AND SHORTFALL INTEREST CHARGE

  1. The notices of penalty assessments for the financial years ended 30 June 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, made pursuant to section 284-75 (1) and 284 -75 (3) are set aside. To the extent the base penalty amount was imposed under section 284 -90 (1) under item 7 that imposes a penalty of 75% when section 284 -75 (3) applies those penalties are set aside. As the Respondent stated the base penalty amount was imposed under section 284 - 90 item 1 for Recklessness the penalty assessments are set aside.
  2. The Tribunal exercises its discretion to remit the shortfall interest charge as on the facts before it, the Tribunal finds no evasion.

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal finds the Applicant did not omit a total of $6,863,098.96 of assessable income from his income tax returns in the financial years ending 30 June 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, and 2020.
  2. The Respondent erred in forming an opinion of evasion on 20 August 2021 and was not authorized under section 170 ITAA 36 to issue the notices of amended assessments to the Applicant for the income tax years ended 30 June 2016, 30 June 2017 and 30 June 2018.
  3. The Applicant discharged the onus imposed on him under s 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 in respect of the amended assessments issued by the Respondent under s 166 and 167 of the ITAA36 for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020.
  4. The Applicant did not fail to furnish an income tax return for the income tax years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020. The default assessments made under section 167(a) in respect of those years should not have been made. It was therefore not appropriate for the Respondent to work out the base penalty amount by applying s 284-90 item 7.
  5. Given the Applicant’s concession for the finical years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020 to the extent there was an underreporting of income by the Applicant in the financial years ended 30 June 2019 and 2020 and any short fall interest charge is more than 20% the shortfall interest charge is remitted.
