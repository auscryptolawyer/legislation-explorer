Last Updated: 25 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - expense's of overseas study tour by high school teacher - whether deductible - whether payments for trip in previous year deductible in year in which tour took place.

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 (Ss. 51(1)

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 203; 81 ATC 4157

Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 CLR 458

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 CLR 494; 71 ATC 4184

REASONS FOR DECISION

3 May, 1988 Mr. J.R. Gibson (Senior Member)

1. The applicant is a school teacher and during the year ended 30 June 1984 and for some years before was employed by the Department of Education as a teacher of French, German, Latin and Ancient History at a high school in a large country town. In her return of income for year ended 30 June 1984 she claimed a deduction of $6,402.72 for travelling expenses of a tour of England and Europe which she and her husband made in December 1983 and January 1984. In assessing the applicant to income tax for the year ended 30 June 1984 the Commissioner of Taxation disallowed the deduction and, accordingly, increased the taxable income returned. An objection to the assessment by the applicant was disallowed by the Commissioner on 18 August 1985 and the application now before the Tribunal is for review of that decision.

  1. It was not contended by the Commissioner at the hearing of the application that the amount claimed had not actually been expended and it was common ground that the question to be determined by the Tribunal was whether all or any of the expenditure was deductible under s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as being an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the applicant's assessable income.
  2. The evidence at the hearing comprised the documents lodged with the Tribunal by the Commissioner, a copy of the relevant return of income and copies of receipts for expenditure totalling $8,003.40 for the costs of travelling to and from England, for a guided tour of Europe and for a tour of England, Scotland and Wales.
  3. The applicant was an impressive and truthful witness and the Tribunal accepts her evidence.
  4. The applicant, a graduate in arts from the University of New England, taught French, German, Latin and Ancient History at the school for some years prior to and during the year ended 30 June 1984 and had final year classes in French and Ancient History. By 1984 there had developed a considerable emphasis on teaching oral French and German as used in contemporary society in those countries and some understanding of the setting in which French and German people live; in assessing a candidate for the Higher School Certificate in French and German the percentage applicable to the oral skill was 45% and 40% respectively. In the Ancient History and Latin courses students were required to know and understand something of the institutions and places of importance as they existed in historical times and of archeological evidence relating to them. In this area, the applicant said, her pupils in the country town did not have the assistance of libraries and museums on the scale of those in the city. Nor were there any fluent French or German speakers in the town.

6. The applicant said that she had decided to apply for a position of Head Teacher of Languages at the high school or, in the event of her husband being transferred to another city or town, at a high school in that place. A Head Teacher does not receive any greater remuneration than a teacher but, by virtue of additional responsibility, has slightly less lessons to give. The position of Head Teacher can be a stepping stone to appointment as a Master, a position which does carry a higher salary, and the applicant said that it was in the hope of some such promotion at a future time that she had set her mind on becoming a Head Teacher. When a teacher applies to be a Head Teacher the whole range of his or her activities and qualifications is examined by a Board of Inspectors. The applicant considered that if she did a study tour overseas, it would be considerably to her advantage when she applied to be appointed Head Teacher. In fact, at the time of her overseas tour, no position of Head Teacher was available at the high school but this would not have prevented her applying for and being accepted as qualified for appointment as a Head Teacher there or elsewhere.

  1. The applicant testified that she also had in mind eventually studying for a Master's degree in arts and said that, if successful, she would have good prospects of promotion. She believed that the information derived from a study tour would be of great value to her in a Master's course.
  2. The applicant stated that she would have preferred to travel to England and Europe in the warmer months and not in school holiday time so that she and her husband could have hired a car and gone to those places she felt would be of most value to her. However, she was aware that if she chose to take leave without pay and go in school term time her position at the school might not be open to her upon her return. She and her husband therefore decided to make their tour in the Australian summer school holidays and, having regard to driving hazards in the European winter, travel on an organised tour. They studied a great deal of travel literature and eventually chose tours which most closely approached what would have have been her own ideal tour.
  3. The applicant said that she had been advised by a travel agent that, because of troubled conditions in parts of Europe, she would be less secure if she travelled alone. She therefore persuaded her husband to accompany her, at her expense, and she said in evidence at the hearing, that she would not have gone if he had not accompanied her. The total expenditure of $8003.40, evidenced by receipts, did not include the cost of meals other than breakfast or other incidentals. In making the claim for a deduction that amount was reduced by one-fifth on the basis that basis that the double accommodation for the couple was only one-fifth dearer than single accommodation would have been.
  4. The applicant did not notify the Department of Education of her proposed study tour or seek its approval. She had been told by an officer employed in the department as an adviser on French that she should do a tour. She notified the Headmaster of her school of the proposed tour as she wanted to have it noted on the records relating to her experience.
  5. Although, as the Commissioner's representative pointed out, the tours undertaken by the applicant were not designated as study tours and were open to tourists generally, the Tribunal has no doubt on the applicant's evidence that it was indeed a study tour for her. She concentrated on applying as much time as she had available in getting the atmosphere of the places visited traversing historic places on foot, visiting museums, public institutions and archeological sites. She made use of every opportunity to improve her fluency in French and German and observe the language of ordinary citizens. On the European tour bus she translated for the purposes of the driver, who spoke only French, the tour guide and her fellow passengers, the communications between them. She took the opportunity of taking photographs and purchasing maps, guides, books, photographs, video and audio tapes. While her fellow tourists were shopping and dining out she and her husband were either carrying out or planning their investigations.
  6. The applicant returned in time to resume her duties at the high school for the commencement of the 1984 school year. In 1985 she became pregnant and, since her child was born, she has taught only on a part time basis. She said that the tour had greatly benefited her fluency and vocabulary in the languages and as a teacher in all of the subjects she taught. Although she had not been appointed a Head Teacher the tour had increased her prospects of promotion both directly and by virtue of the help it will be when she studies for a Master's degree.
  7. Before coming to the basic issue it is convenient to deal first with a matter affecting quantum. The receipts produced by the applicant at the hearing showed that the greater part of the cost of the tours had been paid by the applicant in the year preceding the year of income. For the purpose of obtaining the most favourable terms it had been necessary for the applicant to pay for the tours well in advance. It was submitted for the applicant that that expenditure was not incurred within the meaning of s. 51(1) until the applicant got what she had paid for in the year ending 30 June 1984. In my opinion it is impossible to construe the word "incurred" in that way. A similar submission was rejected by the Taxation Board of Review in Case P72 82 ATC 342. The result is that, on my calculations, no more than about $2600 was paid by the applicant in the year in which the deduction was claimed.
  8. The question whether expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of a taxpayer and is therefore, unless of a capital, private or domestic nature, deductible under s. 51(1) is one of fact and degree. (Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60, per Windeyer J. at 70; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth

[1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 C.L.R. 203 per Wilson J. at 213.) In Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 C.L.R. 458 the High Court reviewed some of the leading decisions on the application of section 51(1) and held that the question was to be answered by the determination of the essential character of the outgoing in question. Two decisions of the Court dealing with the deductibility of expenses incurred by taxpayers obtaining greater professional knowledge or higher qualifications in the field of their employment are those in Finn's case (supra) and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 C.L.R. 494. The reasons for the judgment in those cases were analysed by the Supreme Court of New South Wales (Helsham J.) in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. White (1975) A.T.C. 4018. Having made that analysis Helsham J. said -

"As the result of the decision in the two cases it seems to me possible to say that expenses incurred in pursuing studies associated with employment will qualify as allowable deductions under sec. 51 when it can be said that those studies are part and parcel of the employment, which means that the expenditure is incurred in the process of carrying out the employee's duties, or, even if they are not such, they can be seen to have a direct effect on income. Where that cannot be seen, I think it will be difficult to establish the necessary connection between the study activity and the employment so as to give study expenses the character of outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the income derived from the employment.

In my view there is no sufficient association in the present case. It is not enough, as was said by Menzies J. in Hatchett's case that the course of study relevant to his employment pursued by an employee should be likely to enable him the better to carry out his work and hence to obtain promotion, even if encouraged by his employer. It is not saying anything different if the situation be that without undertaking a course of study encouraged by his employer a person may not be able to improve his position in his employment."

15. The applicant is a dedicated and conscientious teacher and very interested in the subjects she teaches. The Tribunal is satisfied that her tour was of great value to her as a teacher. However, it seems to me that the salient matters to be considered are:-

(a) That one of the purposes of the applicant was to improve her prospects of promotion;

(b) That the tour did improve her knowledge;
  • (c) That it improved her prospects of promotion to Head Teacher, and, in the long run, to Master, with the consequence of an increase in salary;
  • (d) That the tour was not undertaken with the knowledge or at the request of her employer, the Department of Education;
  • (e) That her activities on the tour could not be described as part and parcel of her duties;
  • (f) That the knowledge and experience gained on the tour has not to date resulted in any increase in her salary scale.

16. In my opinion, the facts before the Tribunal do not establish such a relationship between what the applicant derived from her expenditure and her assessable income as to justify the conclusion that the expenditure on the tour was incurred in gaining or producing her assessable income. If I had come to a different conclusion, I would have regarded that part of the outgoing as related to the applicant's husband as being expenditure of a private nature.

17. For these reasons, the objection decision the subject of the application will be affirmed.
