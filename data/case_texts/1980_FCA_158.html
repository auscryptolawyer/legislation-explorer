Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Avco Financial Services Limited Re Avco Financial Services Limited v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1980] FCA 158; (1980) 50 FLR 117 Income Tax (16 December 1980)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: AVCO FINANCIAL SERVICES LIMITED

Re: AVCO FINANCIAL SERVICES LIMITED

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

Nos. G68-72 of 1979 [1980] FCA 158; (1980) 50 FLR 117

Income tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Brennan(1), Deane(2) and Fisher(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income tax - Assessable income - Allowable deductions - Foreign currency borrowings - Exchange gains and losses on repayment - Whether gains capital or income - Gross income - Whether losses deductible outgoings - Outgoings of a capital nature.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss.25(1), 51(1).

Income Tax - Assessable income - Allowable deductions - Money lending business - Money borrowed to lend out from overseas sources - Gains and losses on repayment because of exchange fluctuations - Whether assessable and deductible - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), ss. 25 (1), 51 (1). The taxpayer was in the business of lending small amounts of money to a large number of customers. It borrowed extensively in Australia and in the United States of America to provide this money. In various years of income, losses or gains were made by the taxpayer in the repayment or refinancing of its overseas loans because of exchange fluctuations. The taxpayer in the years of income 1972 to 1977 made gains of over $2,000,000 and losses of over $3,000,000. The Commissioner assessed the taxpayer on the basis that the gains were not assessable and the losses were not deductible. The taxpayer objected, the Commissioner disallowed its objections and the taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court of New South Wales. The taxpayer argued that the losses and gains were all on revenue account; the Commissioner argued that they were of a capital nature. Kearney J. held that the transactions were all on revenue account. The Commissioner appealed.

Held, by majority, Fisher J. dissenting, appeal allowed, the moneys borrowed were not revenue in the hands of the taxpayer. Gains made on their repayment were not assessable. Commercial and General Acceptance Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977), 137 CLR 373, discussed. Therefore losses made on their repayment were not deductible.

HEARING

Sydney, 1980, June 11-12; December 16. 16:12:1980

DECISION

Avco Financial Services Ltd. ("Avco") is a financier. It lends moneys in small amounts to large numbers of customers from a large number of outlets. It provides consumer credit in the form of personal loans, hire purchase retail instalment and similar transactions.

Avco was incorporated in 1964 in the Australian Capital Territory, and it is a wholly owned subsidiary of an American corporation. Until 1967, its parent lent funds to Avco in addition to its investment in Avco's share capital. Since 1967, Avco has raised funds by borrowing in the United States of America and in Australia. At the close of Avco's tax years with which these proceedings are concerned (30 November in each of the years 1972 to 1977 inclusive), its borrowings stood thus:

Save for some minor expenditure on furniture and equipment and a relatively small investment in a Bermudan insurance company, Avco has invested its funds (whether subscribed capital or borrowed funds) exclusively in consumer credit receivables, i.e., personal loans, hire purchase transactions and consumer mortgages. Avco's borrowing programme was so arranged as to meet the requirement for funds for lending to its customers and the requirement for funds to meet accruing obligations for repayment of earlier loans both in Australia and in the United States of America. Some loans were extended beyond their original term or renewed for a further term before the debt owing to the lender was finally discharged. At the time when a particular creditor of a U.S. dollar debt was finally paid out, Avco calculated the gain or loss which it realised on the borrowing of the principal sum due to fluctuations in the exchange rate of the Australian dollar. In the income years 1972 to 1976 inclusive, it made exchange gains of $175,084, $1,579,020, $298,501, $400,705 and $96,708 respectively; in the income years 1975, 1976 and 1977 it suffered exchange losses of $157,677, $223,120 and $2,799,903 respectively.

The Commissioner assessed Avco to tax on the footing that the gains were assessable income and the losses were not allowable deductions. Avco objected contending that the gains were not assessable income and the losses were allowable deductions. Upon the disallowance of the objections to its assessments in respect of each of the years 1972 to 1977 inclusive, Avco requested that the objections be treated as appeals and they were forwarded to the Supreme Court of New South Wales. Kearney J. heard the six appeals together. Before him, Avco submitted that both the gains and losses were on revenue account, and the Commissioner submitted that they were of a capital nature.

In addition to these facts, which are briefly recapitulated from the findings made by Kearney J., his Honour found also that "the gains and losses realised upon repayment of loans arose out of the borrowing transactions of the taxpayer". His Honour then approached the characterization of the gains and losses by ascertaining the nature of those borrowing transactions, for it was his Honour's view that the nature of those transactions determined the issues between the parties.

His Honour found that the borrowings "were an ordinary incident of the taxpayer's business", that they "merged in and became part of the ordinary trading activities of the taxpayer" and that they "formed an essential step in the regular performance in the day to day activities involved in carrying on the taxpayer's business". The gains were held part of Avco's taxable income under s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, and the losses were held deductible under s.51(1) of that Act. The consequence was that the appeals relating to the 1972, 1973 and 1974 years were dismissed; the appeals relating to the 1975 and 1976 years were dismissed as to the inclusion of the gains in Avco's assessable income and allowed as to the deductibility of the losses; and the appeal relating to the 1977 year was allowed. The Commissioner was ordered to pay Avco's costs. Avco appeals against the dismissals, and the Commissioner appeals against the allowance of the respective appeals.

This brief synopsis of his Honour's findings and approach does not exhaust his Honour's careful analysis and lucid exposition of the evidence, but it suffices to expose the critical question for consideration: is it right to conclude that, if the borrowing transactions were part of Avco's ordinary business operations, the gains and losses resulting from those transactions are respectively assessable income under s.25(1) and allowable deductions under s.51. His Honour's approach was "to ascertain the essential nature of the taxpayer's business and the relationship of the subject gains and losses to it", and that approach involved "looking to the transactions out of which the gains or losses arose".

The purpose of a borrowing transaction was referred to in Commercial and General Acceptance Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 137 C.L.R.373 (hereafter "C.A.G.A."), where exchange gains on repayment of U.S. loans by a finance company were held to be on capital account. The purpose for which funds were borrowed in that case was different from the purpose for which, on his Honour's finding, Avco borrowed the relevant funds in the present case.

In C.A.G.A., Mason J. said (at p.384):

"In these circumstances the principal purpose of the borrowing was to strengthen 'the business entity, structure, or organization set up or established for the earning of profit'; it was not part of the process by which the organization operated to obtain regular returns, this being the distinction drawn by Dixon J. in Sun Newspapers Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1938) [1938] HCA 72; 61 C.L.R. 337, at p.359) in elaborating the difference between expenditure and outgoings on revenue account and on capital account. In truth the transaction was designed to strengthen the framework within which the taxpayer intended to carry on business - see Commissioner of Taxes v. Nchanga Consolidated Copper Mines Ltd. ((1964) A.C. 948, at p.959); B.P.Australia Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1965) 112 C.L.R.386, at p.392; (1966) A.C. 224, at p.232)."

It might be thought that, as Barwick C.J., Gibbs and Jacobs JJ. expressed agreement with the reasons of Mason J. (though the agreement of Gibbs J. was subject to a consideration to which reference will presently be made), the Court took the purpose of the borrowing to be the touchstone by which to determine whether the gains were on revenue account. And if the purpose of the borrowing is the touchstone the findings made by Kearney J. distinguish this case from C.A.G.A. and lead logically to the orders which Kearney J. made. But in my judgment, the decision in C.A.G.A. rests on a different analysis.

A transaction of borrowing U.S. dollars is not a transaction of dealing in foreign money as a commodity. When U.S. dollars are borrowed, they are not bought for a price payable in another currency; when borrowed U.S. dollars are repaid, they are not sold at a price payable in another currency. A foreign borrowing of money is to be distinguished from trading in foreign money (see Mann, "The Legal Aspect of Money", 3rd Edn., p.180). In the present case, Avco borrowed U.S. dollars pursuant to a U.S. moneylending contract, and it repaid the principal sum in U.S. dollars in the same nominal amount as it had borrowed. Its contractual obligation and entitlement was to repay the principal sum in that way (Societe des Hotels Le Touquet Paris-Plage v. Cummings (1922) 1 K.B. 451). The gains or losses here in question relate to those repayments of principal; they do not relate to the payments of interest and other charges. There was no U.S. dollar gain or loss in the receipt and repayment of the principal sums borrowed in the U.S.A. Sometimes the moneys lent to Avco in the U.S.A. were transmitted to Australia and exchanged for Australian dollars, sometimes the moneys lent were retained in the U.S.A. to repay an amount due under an earlier borrowing. Sometimes Australian dollars were exchanged for U.S. dollars to repay an amount due under a borrowing, when U.S. dollar funds were not available for the purpose.

The relevant gains and losses were calculated when each creditor was paid out, and represent the difference between the number of Australian dollars equivalent to each creditor's loans calculated at the rate of exchange ruling when each loan was made and the number of Australian dollars equivalent to the loan repayments calculated at the rate of exchange ruling when the creditor was paid out. The question which arises under s.25(1) is whether, if the number of Australian dollars equivalent to the principal sum in U.S. dollars calculated at the time of repayment were less than the number of Australian dollars equivalent to that sum calculated at the time of its receipt, the reduction is "gross income" of the taxpayer; and the question which arises under s.51 is whether any similar increase is an outgoing incurred in gaining assessable income not being an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature. Neither question is determined by commercial principles or accountancy practice; both turn upon the legal criteria set by ss.25(1) and 51 respectively (cf. Caltex Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1960) 106 C.L.R.205 at p.218), though accountancy practice may tend to throw light on whether income is earned or gained (International Nickel Australia Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 137 C.L.R.347 at p.367).

There is a logical difficulty in predicating of a reduction in an outgoing that it is income: Tennant v. Smith (1892) A.C.150 at p.164. But where the outgoing is incurred in a continuing business as a revenue liability in one accounting period and payment of a reduced amount has discharged the liability in a subsequent accounting period, the reduction has been treated as income of the business in the latter period, as Dixon J. explained in Armco (Australia) Pty.Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1948) 76 C.L.R.584 at p.618:

"The comparison made between the beginning and end of an accounting period means that stock in trade and purchases are taken into account not by reference to what is actually paid for them, but according to the value assigned to the one and the liability incurred in acquiring the other. That is done, of course, entirely independently of the period within which the actual disbursement of money is made to discharge the liability. If, as is commonly the case, the amount of the liability is fixed and incapable of subsequent variation, the disbursement itself whenever made could never matter for the purpose of computing profit or loss in that or any subsequent period. But if for any reason the amount is capable of changing, as is the case when the indebtedness is in a foreign currency and the rate of exchange may alter, a further question arises. If the change takes place in a subsequent period and actual payment is then made, is the increase or decrease, as the case may be, to be attributed to the prior period and the net profit or loss reassessed? Obviously not. It is to be taken in as an item belonging to the subsequent period; for the reason that, with continuing trading, when increases beyond the estimates by which assets and liabilities are carried out of one period into the next occur, they must be treated as incidents of the system and they must be regarded as belonging to the period in which they accrue or are realized."

The severance of two accounting periods, the continuity of the business and the reduction in the subsequent period of the amount charged as an outgoing incurred on revenue account in the earlier period are essential to give to the reduction the character of income. The alchemy by which a reduction in an outgoing is converted into income depends upon the system of accounting in periods which adjusts in a subsequent period the amount of liabilities brought to account in the earlier period in the light of the actual payment made to discharge them: see per Fullagar J. in Caltex, supra at pp.226,227; Gibbs J. in International Nickel, supra at pp.354,355. Mason J., in International Nickel, supra, was thus able to speak (at p.366) of "a general concept of income which includes within its embrace a reduction in the amount of an outgoing on revenue account".

In C.A.G.A., the lending transactions gave no room for applying the principle explained by Dixon J. in Armco, supra. Mason J. rejected the application of that principle (at p.383):

"The exchange gain was in reality a saving or reduction in the amount of Australian currency equivalent which the taxpayer required to repay its indebtedness. In essence it was a windfall advantage stemming from a reduction in a liability to repay a borrowing of capital. I can see no persuasive reason for saying that the gain was a receipt of income."

If the sum borrowed had not been capital, then the repayment of the sum might not have been an outgoing of a capital nature. Mason J., in the passage from his judgment first cited, examined the purpose of the borrowing and held that it was a borrowing of capital. The gain on repayment was therefore not part of the taxpayer's assessable income.

Gibbs J. tentatively expressed the view that a gain on repayment of borrowed moneys could never be assessable income. He said (at p.377):

"It can only be regarded as part of the assessable income if it was referable to expenditure incurred in or for the purpose of discharging or providing for liabilities on revenue or income account: Texas Co. (Australasia) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1940) 63 C.L.R.382). I incline to think that an exchange gain or loss on the repayment of moneys lent will always be a capital gain or loss, and can never be taken into account in the assessment of income. That seems to have been the view of Latham C.J. in the Texas Co. Case ((1940) 63 C.L.R., at p.428)."

Latham C.J. had said ((1940) 63 C.L.R.382 at p.428):

"Accordingly, remittances sent by the company to America in repayment of moneys lent or in payment for plant which became part of the capital assets of the company fall outside the principles stated and no exchange in respect of such remittances should be allowed as a deduction."

It is indeed difficult to see how the repayment of moneys borrowed can be an outgoing on revenue account. Moneys which are borrowed under an ordinary moneylending contract are not received as income, and an outgoing in repayment is therefore not an outgoing on revenue account. In the present case, the moneys borrowed from the various lenders were found to have been employed in Avco's business, but that does not clothe the moneys with the character of revenue. The moneys were used (but not exhausted) to earn interest, and no doubt the surplus of the interest received over the interest paid by Avco contributed to Avco's assessable income. But it does not follow that the derivation of assessable income by the lending of money denies the character of capital to the money lent; rather does it suggest that, in a business of lending money, the money lent is the capital ventured in the business.

But in Avco's submissions and in the findings made by Kearney J., Avco's business was not confined to the lending of money. An integral part of its business is said to be the raising of the money to lend, and that in a business of that scope, it is not the nature of the payments received from or payments made to lenders which is material. Rather, one looks to the net profit or loss thrown up by the lending transactions. Then, so the argument runs, assessable income includes the profit from a borrowing transaction or from a series of borrowing transactions; and net losses are deductible under s.51(1).

Mason J., though he saw a difficulty in the way of saying that net profit falls within "gross income", nevertheless thought it possible that a net profit could become assessable income "in some circumstances, as, for example, where it is derived from a gross receipt which is not income" (see pp.382, 383). But that proposition can have no application to a net profit or loss in a transaction which is merely a part of continuing income earning activity. Barwick C.J. pointed this out in C.A.G.A. (at p.376):

"So-called capital profits do not by the very description constitute income. If what is thought to be a profit is the result of an income-producing activity, it will not be the net profit but the gross income of the activity which will fall within the assessable income: thereafter deduction conformably to the Act may be made from the gross income as a whole. For convenience, the net result of the trading account is often carried into an income tax return as the amount of income from which specific deductions are made. But the debit side of those accounts should include only items which fall within s.51."

Gibbs J. (at p.377) adhering to the principle which he had expressed in International Nickel, supra, held that a gain could only be regarded as part of the assessable income if it was referable to expenditure incurred in or for the purpose of discharging or providing for liabilities on revenue account, and he thought that the proper approach was to apply the established tests for the purpose of deciding whether a repayment was an expenditure referable to capital account or to revenue account. The judgment of Murphy J. is to the same effect (at p.386):

"If an amount payable by a taxpayer (the principal sum) is not allowable expenditure, then any increase or decrease in the principal sum is not to be taken into account in determining the taxable income of the taxpayer."

Accepting that the raising and management of the borrowings from lenders in the U.S.A. was an indispensable part of the activities of Avco's business, the gross income of that business did not include the moneys lent to Avco in the U.S.A. from time to time. Avco's cash flow was no doubt dependent upon those borrowings, but Avco's income did not include the moneys borrowed by it. The repayments of those moneys were not expenditure on revenue account, and the Australian dollar cost of repaying the borrowings was not a cost incurred on revenue account. It follows that the gains on repayment of the borrowings are not assessable income.

As the argument in favour of including the gains in assessable income depended upon the repayment of the borrowings being an outgoing on revenue account, the failure of that argument carries with it the rejection of the claim to deduct exchange losses under s.51(1). The additional Australian dollars which would have been required to meet repayments of U.S. dollar loans would have been required for the making of repayments on capital account. Unless the repayments which are said to give rise to the exchange losses are themselves deductible, the losses do not fall within s.51(1). Neither the gains nor the losses are on revenue account, and Avco's argument fails. Had the losses been on revenue account, it would have been necessary to consider whether they could be said to have been "incurred" in those cases where the Australian dollar loss had been merely calculated, and there had not been an actual outlay of Australian dollars to repay a U.S. creditor (see Caltex, supra).

In my judgment, the Commissioner's submissions should be upheld, and his appeals in respect of the disallowance of the losses should succeed. Avco's appeals in respect of the inclusion of the gains in its assessable income should also be allowed. The Commissioner should have his costs here and in the Supreme Court.

Subject to what is said below, I agree with the judgment of Brennan J.

I do not consider that it is necessary for the purposes of the present appeal to express any concluded view on the question whether exchange gains or losses made or incurred on the repayment of borrowed money can ever properly be regarded as gains or losses on revenue account which constitute income for the purposes of s.25(1) or allowable deductions pursuant to s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936. If circumstances can arise in which the receipt and repayment of the borrowed money are themselves properly to be seen as on revenue account, the answer to that question will be in the affirmative. Even if the receipt and repayment of borrowed money are always to be regarded as being on capital account, it is at least arguable that regularly recurring exchange gains which constitute a component of the profit for which a business is carried on should properly be seen as being on revenue account and as constituting income for the purposes of s.25(1). Conversly, it is at least arguable that regularly incurred exchange losses which are a true incident of the income-earning activities which constitute a business should likewise properly be seen as being on revenue account and as deductible pursuant to s.51(1).

In the present case, the taxpayer's United States borrowings were on capital account. Those borrowings were in very large amounts and were logically, as distinct from temporally, anterior to, and distinct from, the multitude of small money lending and financing transactions to which the taxpayer devoted its subscribed and borrowed capital. It is true that what represented the surplus proceeds of the taxpayer's United States borrowings (i.e. the excess over what was required to repay previous borrowings) was brought to Australia to be utilized in the taxpayer's business in this country. Those surplus proceeds were, however, part of the capital derived from the United States to fund the taxpayer's Australian business and, in my view, should properly be seen as representing part of the capital structure of that business rather than the proceeds of borrowings made as an incident of carrying it on. That being so, there is nothing in the circumstances of the case to avoid the consequence that gains or losses made or incurred on the repayment of the taxpayer's United States borrowings, as a consequence of variations in rates of exchange, were in the same category as the repayments themselves. They were on capital account and were neither income for the purposes of s.25(1) nor deductible pursuant to the provisions of s.51(1).

I am conscious of the fact that in expressing the view that the taxpayer's United States borrowings should not properly be seen as an incident of carrying on the taxpayer's Australian business, I am differing from a conclusion reached by Kearney J. in the course of his lucid and carefully reasoned judgment in the Supreme Court of New South Wales. There is, however, no dispute between the parties as to the relevant primary facts and, as I understand the effect of what was said by the majority of the High Court in Warren v. Coombs ((1979) [1979] HCA 9; 23 A.L.R. 405), it is incumbent upon me, while giving respect and weight to the views of the learned trial judge, to give effect to the conclusion which I have myself reached in that regard.

I agree with the orders proposed by Brennan J.

In my opinion the profits or losses on exchange account were, in the circumstances of the present case, revenue items and I agree entirely with the conclusions and the reasoning of Kearney J. in the Supreme Court of New South Wales.

Before us, the appellant Commissioner challenged Kearney J's decision on three scores. The primary submission was that we should adopt the approach of Gibbs J. in Commercial and General Acceptance Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 137 C.L.R. 373 at p.377, namely that "an exchange gain or loss on the repayment of monies lent will always be a capital gain or loss, and can never be taken into account in the assessment of income". The borrowing of money, so the submission went, must be inherently an affair of capital: only the "cost of borrowing" could be an affair of revenue. It was implicit in this submission that an exchange loss is not part of the cost of borrowing.

In the alternative, the Commissioner's case was that in considering the operations of a finance company, no distinction could in substance be drawn between the employment in the course of its lending activities of its share capital and the employment of its external borrowings. Losses or gains in either as a result of fluctuations in rates of exchange, were equally of a capital nature.

Finally, it was submitted that, in the particular circumstances of this matter, the use which the taxpayer made of its overseas borrowings was similar to the use which Commercial and General Acceptance Limited made of like funds in the abovementioned case, namely, that they were applied to improve the structure of the company at a time of increasing demand upon its facilities, and not for the purpose of on-lending to its customers.

In respect of the first challenge, namely the reliance on the views of Gibbs J. in the Commercial and General Acceptance Limited case supra, it is important to note that Gibbs J. stated his general proposition, that an exchange gain or loss on the repayment of monies borrowed will always be a capital gain or loss, as only a tentative and not a final conclusion. His Honour accepted that that proposition might be seen as an "extreme view", and expressly stated his agreement with the reasoning of Mason J. as applied to the facts of that matter. This latter judge, with whom Barwick C.J. and Jacobs J. concurred, saw the particular borrowing there under consideration as having the character of capital by reference to its principal purpose which was to strengthen the profit earning structure of the company. In this he was applying the test of Dixon J., as he then was, in Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 C.L.R. 337 at 359-383 to distinguish between outgoings on capital account and on revenue account. If in fact all borrowings were, in accord with the tentative view of Gibbs J., on capital account, Mason J's reasoning could on one view be seen as redundant. I agree with the conclusion of the trial judge that the majority view in that case leaves open for consideration in each matter whether the relevant gain or loss was on capital or revenue account. Murphy J. would appear to be of the same view when he stated at p.387: "I accept that the appellant's overseas borrowing was a capital transaction, unlike its local borrowings and lendings". This conclusion of the trial judge entails the rejection of the primary submission of the Commissioner.

The Commissioner's second contention, in the form in which it was stated, namely that in the case of a finance company there is no distinction to be made between the lending of its share capital and its external borrowings, may well be correct. However there is in my opinion considerable difference between the funds which form part of the permanent structure of the company and those which it is continuously engaged in acquiring in the market place. Counsel for the Commissioner contended that the external borrowings could not be categorised as circulating capital, and this may well be so. Circulating capital is generally that portion of the fixed or permanent capital of a company which is employed in its day to day operations rather than invested in the permanent structure of the company. External borrowings are substantially different in character and accepting that, both in the dictionary meaning and as defined under the Act, they are not trading stock, they do have the characteristics of "stock in trade" in that the money thereby acquired is the commodity which the finance company deals in or turns over in the course of its business activities. Normally all the funds which a finance company acquires or "purchases" in the market place, and certainly the funds in question here, are used, in the words of Mason J. in the Commercial and General Acceptance case, supra at p.384 in "the process by which the organization operated to obtain regular returns". Such process comprises three separate functions; the acquisition of funds, normally by borrowing in the market place, the loan of such funds to customers, and the ultimate repayment to lenders of the funds borrowed. Each of these three functions involve the finance company in expense, be it, in the case of borrowings, in the nature of payment of interest, other expenses such as cost of advertisements and compliance with legal obligations, and the costs necessarily involved in repayment of borrowings. As one of the conditions of the borrowings here under consideration necessitated the purchase of foreign currency, the cost of such purchases reflected any variation, adverse or otherwise, in the rate of exchange since the date of the particular borrowing, with a consequential increase or decrease, as the case may be, in the cost of such borrowings. This cost was one of the expenses of the "process by which the organization operated to obtain regular returns".

Before the trial judge the submission was presented rather in terms that a cost incurred in borrowing and repayment was not on revenue account unless it related to stock in trade or services. This contention was in my opinion correctly rejected by the trial judge. I see no justification for so limiting the expenditure though I repeat that when one considers the essential nature of the business of a finance company, money has many of the characteristics usually attributed to stock in trade.

The third submission, was as has been mentioned, that the borrowings here were in the same category as those of the taxpayer in the Commercial and General Acceptance case, supra. In my view that submission was also correctly rejected by the trial judge. I see a very considerable difference between the manner in which the two companies used their respective borrowings. In the Commercial and General Acceptance case, supra the High Court saw the borrowings as having been applied to strengthen "the business entity, structure or organization set up or established for the earning of profit". It noted that only to a limited extent were such borrowings able to be used in the business process and the purpose of borrowing was certainly not to augment funds so used. Here the overseas borrowings, made on innumerable occasions, were used in the performance of the taxpayer's day to day business operations. If they were not lent out for the purpose of gaining profit, they were used for the purpose of making repayment of maturing borrowings.

In my opinion the trial judge properly distinguished the circumstances of the borrowings in the Commercial and General Acceptance case, and correctly characterised the essential nature of the taxpayer's business and the part which the overseas borrowings played in such business.

I would dismiss the appeals with costs to be paid by the appellant Commissioner and I would dismiss the appeals of the respondent.
