Re Moneymen Pty Ltd v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1990] FCA 486; 91 Atc 4019/21 Atr 1142/97 ALR 265 (6 December 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: MONEYMEN PTY LTD

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

Nos. G99 to 102 of 1990

FED No. 690

Income Tax

91 ATC 4019/21 ATR 1142/97 ALR 265

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Wilcox(1), Gummow(2) and Hill(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - assignment by appellant of appellant's rights under Indenture - Deed of Defeasance between appellant and assignee whereby assignee covenanted to pay to appellant monthly amounts - whether amounts assessable under s.25 Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 or whether of capital nature - distinction between case where capital asset sold and consideration received is income and where the payment is of capital nature being a fixed amount - meaning of "annuity" for purposes of Income Tax Assessment Act - whether there was agreement to pay fixed gross amount by instalments or agreement to make a number of annual payments - whether amount received as substitution for income is income.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936: ss.25, 26AA, 27H

HEARING

SYDNEY (Heard in Brisbane)

6:12:1990

Counsel and Solicitors for applicant: Mr H.A. Weld instructed by

John P. Kelly and Co.

Counsel and Solicitors for respondent: Mr R.W. Gotterson QC and

Mr W. Vitali instructed by the

Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The appeal be dismissed.

The appellant pay the respondent's costs of the appeal.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

For the reasons given by Hill J, these appeals should be dismissed with costs.

These are four appeals from decisions by a Judge of this Court (Spender J.). The appeals were heard together. The proceedings arose out of the rejection by the respondent of objections against assessments in respect of income derived by the appellant during the financial years ended 30 June 1979, 1980, 1981 and 1982. The appellant requested the respondent, pursuant to s. 187 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), to refer to the Court his decision on the objections, and Spender J. heard the four matters together. In each of them, the issue was whether sums received by the appellant from Maleny Milk Producers Pty Limited ("Maleny") pursuant to contractual arrangements between the appellant and Maleny, were the assessable income of the appellant. The learned primary Judge held that the payments were income according to ordinary concepts. The judgment is reported, (1990) ATC 4,615. His Honour rejected the further contention of the respondent that the sums were included in the assessable income of the appellant as amounts received as or by way of royalty within the meaning of para 26 (f) of the Act. Notices of contention raising the royalty issue were given by the respondent, but the point was abandoned on the hearing of the appeal.

2. The appellant contests the holding that the payments received by the appellant were income according to ordinary concepts; it submits that the learned primary Judge should have held that the receipts were correctly characterised as having been paid on the sale and disposition of a capital asset by the appellant and as being of a capital nature in the hands of the appellant.

3. Before 1972, the appellant had carried on business as a dairy farmer, milk processor and pasteuriser, and as a wholesale and retail seller of bottled milk. It conducted its business, under the names "Polar Milk" and "Pine Mountain Dairies", from a property at Dayboro in the State of Queensland. The business was on a small scale; about 200 gallons were processed each day. In March 1972, the appellant agreed to sell its milk-processing, pasteurising and wholesale vending business to Caboolture Co-operative Association Limited ("Caboolture") and Q.U.F. Trading Pty Limited ("Q.U.F.") for $34,850. The appellant did not sell its business as retail milk vendor, and it desired to provide for the further sale of its milk from the dairy at Dayboro, expected to amount to 600 gallons per day. Accordingly, the appellant stipulated in the sale agreement with Caboolture for the supply to Caboolture of its milk for processing and sale by Caboolture.

4. This arrangement was set out in a written contract dated 30 June 1972 between the appellant and Caboolture ("the 1972 contract"). The 1972 contract, (a) granted to the appellant a "market milk entitlement" for 20 years (cl. 2, 3), (b) obliged Caboolture to purchase from the appellant as "market milk", a maximum of 600 gallons per day, and any excess as "manufacture milk" (cl. 4A, 4B), and (c) provided that upon expiry in 1992 of the term of the 1972 contract, the appellant would revert to "the status of a Caboolture quota milk supplier" with a particular quota established as provided by cl. 10, being on a basis no less favourable than that applicable to any other supplier.

5. There were at this time two prices current for milk supplied by dairy producers to factories, the higher being the "market milk price" for milk up to the limit of the individual quota for each producer, the quota being fixed by the factory, and the lower being the "manufacture milk price" for all other milk. All moneys payable by Caboolture were to be paid not later than the 14th day of the month after the end of the month of delivery to it of the milk in respect of which the payment was being made. Clause 20 provided that the 1972 contract might be "assigned" by the appellant "as a whole contract for the balance of its term" provided notice thereof was given to Caboolture.

6. The effect of the 1972 contract was that the appellant had a purchaser committed 20 years for all the milk it produced, up to 600 gallons per day, at the best price obtainable for it, and the appellant would not be obliged to qualify for a quota, as did other producers, by achieving certain levels of production over the quota, which would be sold at a lower price.

7. Early in 1974, the appellant sustained severe losses in a major flood and later in that year it sold at auction its land, machinery and livestock. The appellant was unable to "sell" the benefit of the 1972 contract and was unable itself to supply further milk pursuant to the 1972 contract. At the auction, Mr Revie, a director of the appellant, was introduced by the auctioneer to Mr Webster, Chairman of Maleny Co-operative Dairy Association. Negotiations were conducted between Mr Revie and Mr Webster and Maleny was incorporated as the vehicle for the contemplated transaction with the appellant. In the negotiations, there was no request by the appellant for the provision to it of consideration in the form of a lump sum price.

8. The appellant and Maleny are parties to three deeds, each dated 13 February 1975, a Deed of Assignment, a Deed of Defeasance and a Deed of Re-assignment. Each deed was a part of the one commercial transaction and each may be construed in the light of the others: Chappuis v Filo (1990) 19 NSWLR 490 at 508. In any event, the Deed of Defeasance refers to the other two instruments.

9. The Deed of Assignment defines the appellant as the "Assignor" and Maleny as the "Assignee". It recites that under the 1972 contract between the appellant and Caboolture, Caboolture as grantor granted to the appellant a "Market Milk Entitlement" and that the appellant has agreed to assign this to Maleny. The assignment did not extend to the rights of the appellant under cl. 10 which were exercisable upon expiry of the contract in 1992, although the appellant regarded them as valuable rights. Thus, the 1972 contract was not assigned "as a whole contract" within the sense of cl. 20 thereof.

10. The three central operative provisions of the Deed of Assignment are as follows:

"1. That (the appellant) doth hereby grant transfer set

over and assign unto (Maleny) the whole right title and

interest of (the appellant) under the said Indenture made

the thirtieth day of June 1972 and to the Contract evidenced

thereby and contained therein together with the benefit

subject to the obligation of (Maleny's) observing performing

and fulfilling (the appellant's) obligations under the

contract to hold the same unto (Maleny) absolutely from the

date hereof.

2. That (Maleny) doth hereby agree and covenant to be

bound by the terms of the said Indenture and the Contract

evidenced thereby and contained therein and to be bound to

the Grantor as (the appellant) was heretofore bound to the

Grantor by the terms of the said Indenture.

3. That (Maleny) doth hereby covenant to indemnify and

keep indemnified (the appellant) against all claims demands

whether liquidated or unliquidated and other liabilities and

obligations howsoever arising under the said Indenture in

respect to anything done or omitted or permitted by (Maleny)

its officers servants or agents on or after the date hereof

in relation to the said Indenture or the performance thereof

or any breach thereof."

No consideration is stated for the assignment. Caboolture is defined as "the Grantor", but it is not joined as a party to the Deed of Assignment. However, the evidence shows that Caboolture knew of the making of these arrangements and accepted milk supplied by Maleny as if there had been an effective novation of the 1972 contract. The Court should proceed on the footing that there was such a novation, and there is no need to determine whether the subject matter of the Deed of Assignment was capable of assignment without novation; cf. Bruce v Tyley [1916] HCA 34; (1916) 21 CLR 277 at 284-285, 288-292.

11. In the Deed of Defeasance, the 1972 contract is defined as "the contract", Maleny is defined as "the Supplier" and the appellant is defined as "the Company". In the recitals, the Deed of Assignment is described in terms of the appellant having transferred and assigned the 1972 contract to Maleny "subject to the burden of the contract being assumed by (Maleny)". The Deed of Defeasance also recites that Maleny has agreed to observe and fulfil the contract for the balance of its term and "has agreed to make to (the appellant) the payments hereinafter provided during the balance of the term of the (1972) contract".

12. The payments to be made by Maleny to the appellant are specified in sub-cl. 3 (2) of the Deed of Defeasance as follows:

"(Maleny) shall pay to (the appellant) where

(the appellant) directs monthly within twentyone

(21) days after the end of each calendar month the

amount calculated according to the formula in

sub-clause (1) of this clause 3 and for that purpose

the number of days for the purpose of 'D' shall be

the number of days in the calendar month in

question."

The formula in sub-cl. 3 (1) is:

MMP-C-L x G x D

7

where

MMP is the market milk price per gallon,

C is the amount of cartage per gallon payable under

cl 9 of the contract,

L is the amount of levy per gallon deducted in

accordance with cl 14 of the contract,

G is the number of gallons specified in the Schedule

to the contract (which for present purposes may be taken as

600 gallons), and

D is the number of days in the relevant month of

calculation.

Clause 3 (1) also states:

"The application and operation of the formula

and the calculation of the amount under or by the

formula shall be independent of the supply or

delivery of milk by (Maleny) to (Caboolture) and

the amount shall be calculated under this clause

by reference to the abovementioned formula and not

in any way by reference to (the) actual milk

produced or supplied or delivered by (Maleny)."

Clause 10 states that the appellant shall not be entitled to any payments from Maleny "for manufacture milk produced or supplied by (Maleny)".

13. However, cl. 8 of the Deed of Defeasance provides:

"8. (a) If (Caboolture) in breach of the contract and in

the absence of any consent or waiver on the part of (Maleny)

fails to pay the total price which is payable in accordance

with the contract for market milk supplied during any month

under the contract then payment of the monthly payment under

clause 3 (2) of this agreement by (Maleny) to (the

appellant) for the market milk supplied during the month the

price for which has not been paid by (Caboolture) shall be

suspended until payment of any part of th (sic) total price

is made by (Caboolture) to (Maleny) and the moneys payment

whereof by (Maleny) shall have been suspended shall then be

paid by (Maleny) to (the appellant).

(b) If at any time there shall be no entity or legal

person in existence within the meaning of the team

'Caboolture' then this agreement shall be cancelled at that

time."

14. In accordance with sub-cl. 3 (4) of the Deed of Defeasance, the amounts calculated in accordance with sub-cl. 3 (2) shall be paid after the first year of the Deed of Assignment by monthly payments, the first of which is to be made in April 1976 in respect of the month of March 1976, and thereafter in each succeeding month, the last payment being made in the month of October 1992, by which date all the moneys payable under the Deed of Defeasance by Maleny shall have been paid in full to the appellant.

15. Sub-clause 6 (1) provides that if the contract is cancelled or rendered void for illegality without the consent of Maleny and otherwise than in consequence of any act or default of Maleny, the Deed of Defeasance "shall be cancelled", provided that if the appellant so requires, it may without any notice give effect to the Deed of Re-assignment so as to revest the contract in the appellant for the use and benefit of the appellant absolutely.

16. Clause 12 states:

"(Maleny) shall not at law or in equity assign

share or part with the possession of or assign

share or part with the benefit or any part thereof

of the contract without the consent in writing of

(the appellant) and as a condition of such consent

without in any way limiting the absolute

discretion of (the appellant) to grant or refuse

such consent (Maleny) shall procure the delivery

to (the appellant) prior to assignment with (the

appellant's) consent a deed of covenant by the

assignee in favour of (the appellant) obliging and

binding the assignee to these presents as (Maleny)

is bound hereby."

Further, cl. 5 of the Deed of Defeasance obliges Maleny not to breach, waive, vary, cancel, prejudice or release or otherwise affect the contract or any of the terms of the contract or any of the rights or obligations of Maleny or of Caboolture thereunder, without the previously obtained consent in writing of the appellant.

17. Clause 4 gives Maleny a right to compound its obligations to the appellant, so that upon payment of the sum calculated in accordance with that clause, the appellant is obliged to release Maleny from its obligations to make future monthly payments. The right is exercisable by Maleny at any time upon giving six months' calendar notice of its intention to compound, and provision is made for the discounted cash sum to be calculated on the basis of the higher of the market milk prices as at the date of the notice and the date of expiration of the notice, over the balance for the 20 year term from the date of the expiration of the notice, with a discount rate of 10%.

18. Sub-clause 6 (2) provides, inter alia, that if the contract is terminated by or with the consent of Maleny or through the act or default of Maleny under the contract, then Maleny shall be liable to pay immediately upon demand to the appellant "a sum equal to the sum that would be payable if (Maleny) had given under clause 4 of this agreement notice of intention to compound which notice had expired on the date on which the contract is cancelled or otherwise howsoever becomes . . . terminated . . .".

19. Clause 17 contains an acknowledgment by Maleny and the appellant that the "assignment" of the 1972 contract is absolute and that Maleny is "holder" of that contract; it further provides for the execution by Maleny and delivery to the appellant of a Deed of Re-assignment to which effect may be given by the appellant for the purpose of revesting the 1972 contract in it as provided, for example, by sub-cl. 6 (1) of the Deed of Defeasance.

20. The third document dated 13 February 1975 is the Deed of Re-assignment, the parties to which are Maleny as assignor, and the appellant as assignee. The Deed of Re-assignment is expressed as immediately operative to transfer the interest of Maleny in the 1972 contract back to the appellant, but its effect would appear to be controlled by clause 17 of the Deed of Defeasance to which I have referred, with the result that effect would be given to the Deed of Re-assignment only in limited circumstances, and by way of security for the performance of the obligations of Maleny.

21. The effect of the three instruments dated 13 February 1975 was that Maleny acquired the principal rights of the appellant under the 1972 contract, namely what one may call the 20 year rights under clauses 2, 3, 4A and 4B, subject to the revesting thereof in the appellant in certain circumstances. The consideration moving to the appellant is found only in the Deed of Defeasance. There is no specification of a lump sum which is or may be paid by instalments; cf. the determination at 34.85m. of the purchase price under the contract in issue in Secretary of State in Council of India v Scoble (1903) AC 299 at 300.

22. Provisions for the computation and payment of the monthly amounts by Maleny to the appellant are contained in sub-cl. 3 (1). The amounts payable monthly are based on a volume of 600 gallons per day of market milk, and vary with the market milk price, cartage and levy deductions, and the number of days in the month. The sums receivable by the appellant are equivalent to one-seventh of what it would have received for market milk had it supplied 600 gallons thereof per day to Caboolture. The amounts receivable by the appellant from Maleny under the Deed of Defeasance were computed independently of the amount of milk actually delivered by Maleny to Caboolture. The receipts by the appellant had the characteristics of periodicity, regularity and recurrence referred to in various authorities, most recently The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v The Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199 at 215.

23. The evidence before the learned primary Judge showed that a monthly cheque was sent by Maleny to the credit of a bank account of the appellant. One notification of such a payment was in the following terms:

"31 Days Quota @ 2728 Litres =

84568 Litres @ 25.51c per litre $21 573.30

Less Cartage Levies Etc. $ 1 739.27

$19,834.03

Royalty @ 1/7 of $19 834.03 is $ 2 833.43"

600 gallons almost exactly equates to 2728 litres.

24. Spender J. found that about the end of the financial year ending 30 June 1978, there was an amalgamation between Caboolture and Maleny so that effectively Caboolture owned Maleny. After the amalgamation, Maleny acted on the footing that it was not exercising its right to supply milk because Caboolture was in effect supplying and receiving its own milk. Before this amalgamation, payment by Caboolture to Maleny had been on the amount supplied to Caboolture, but Maleny had paid the appellant in accordance with the formula in the Deed of Defeasance which, as I have noted, did not reflect the quantity of milk actually supplied by Maleny to Caboolture.

25. No doubt, if the appellant had continued (or, in the events that had happened with the 1974 flood, it had resumed) the supply of milk to Caboolture, it would have derived income therefrom over the balance of the term of the contract; it had, inter alia, the right to oblige Caboolture to purchase a maximum of 600 gallons per day as market milk. In place of the contractual rights which the 1972 contract gave the appellant against Caboolture, including those to periodic payments, the appellant acquired a right to periodic payments from Maleny, over the balance of the 20 year term of the 1972 contract. As I have indicated, the long series of periodic payments was to be computed by a formula which would produce the result that the appellant received one-seventh of sums it would have received as income had it continued to supply 600 gallons of market milk per day to Caboolture under the 1972 contract. The quantum of the payments computed in this way would not necessarily be equal; there would be variations from time to time in the market milk price per gallon and in other integers in the formula in sub-cl. 3 (1) of the Deed of Defeasance.

26. In my view, these considerations point clearly to the receipts by the appellant from Maleny as bearing an income character. Even if the dealing by the appellant with its rights under the 1972 contract be regarded as an affair of capital, the appellant would have acquired an income by an outlay of capital: Egerton-Warburton v The Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 40; (1934) 51 CLR 568 at 572-573; The Commissioner of Taxes (Victoria) v Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 55 CLR 144 at 155-157; Cliffs International Inc. v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1979] HCA 8; (1979) 142 CLR 140 at 148-151; Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Ltd, supra; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Inkster (1989) 20 ATR 1516 at 1535.

27. Upon the appeal, counsel for the appellant sought to diminish the force of these considerations by reference to certain provisions of the Deed of Defeasance. In particular, he stressed that an end might be put, before the expiry in 1992 of the term of the 1972 contract, to the stream of recurrent and regular periodic payments to the taxpayer from Maleny. The Deed of Defeasance would be "cancelled" if Caboolture went out of existence: sub-cl. 8 (b). Maleny's conduct might bring about the termination of the 1972 contract, in which case Maleny would be obliged to pay the appellant a lump sum calculated as provided in sub-cl. 6 (2) of the Deed of Defeasance. Further, Maleny had the right under cl. 4 thereof to oblige the appellant to accept a composition of Maleny's obligations to make future monthly payments.

28. These provisions indicate that the receipt by the appellant of the stream of monthly payments (of variable amounts) over the balance of the term of the 1972 contract would always be subject to various vicissitudes not within the control of the appellant. But that will be the case to some degree with most if not all executory contractual rights. The contractual arrangements between the appellant and Maleny had as their primary characteristic the making of the monthly payments to the appellant and the appellant received the sums at issue on the assessments the subject of these proceedings pursuant to the performance by Maleny of its contractual obligations in the ordinary course. This is not a case where payments are not periodic in character because the agreement pursuant to which the payments are received provides for a fixed sum with an alternative form of instalment payments of that fixed sum: see Professor Parsons, "Income Taxation in Australia", 1985, para 2.203. I would not qualify the view expressed above that even if the appellant disposed of a capital asset, it did so for a stream of income.

29. Nevertheless I should, in response to the submissions for the appellant, express my views on the nature of the disposition by the appellant to Maleny. The relevant authorities are collected in Allied Mills Industries Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1988] FCA 357; (1989) 20 FCR 288 at 299-303, 309, and I shall not repeat them here, save to refer to what was said by Lord Evershed M.R. in Anglo-French Exploration Co., Ltd v Clayson (1956) 36 TC 545 at 557. Much depends upon the circumstances of each case.

30. The appellant emphasised that as matters stood in 1975 at the time of the entry of the appellant into its arrangements with Maleny, the appellant no longer had the wherewithal to continue the business as a retail milk vendor which it had excepted from the sale to Caboolture and Q.U.F. in 1972. The appellant had then parted with its milk processing, pasteurising and wholesale vending business. Further, after the flood in 1974, the appellant had sold off its land, machinery and livestock. It retained the 1972 contract with Caboolture which for 20 years gave it a committed purchaser for the milk it produced. But the appellant now lacked by itself the means to perform the contract by supplying milk to Caboolture. The 1972 contract provided (cl. 18) that it would be terminated if the appellant failed for a period of two years to supply to Caboolture the quantities of market milk stipulated in the 1972 contract. Hence, it was imperative to the appellant that it turn to account its rights under the 1972 contract.

31. This is not a case where, after making its arrangements in 1975 with Maleny, the appellant still had the means of carrying on operations as a retail milk vendor; cf. Heavy Minerals Pty Limited v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1966] HCA 60; (1966) 115 CLR 512 at 516-517. I take into account the considerations that excepted out of the assignment were the rights of the appellant (under cl. 10 of the 1972 contract) to revert to the status of a Caboolture quota milk supplier in 1992, and that the Deed of Re-assignment provided in certain circumstances for the revesting of the 1972 contract in the taxpayer, so that the "assignment" was not made entirely on a "once and for all" basis. Nevertheless, in my view, the appellant was correct in its submission that in February 1975, the appellant parted with the substantial part of the then remainder of its business undertaking.

32. However, this is a case where the taxpayer parted with a structural asset for a consideration which yields an income over a lengthy period.

33. Accordingly, I would dismiss the appeal, with costs.

The appellant, Moneymen Pty Limited, appeals from the judgment of a judge of this Court (Spender J) disallowing the appellant's appeals in respect of assessments made by the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation, of income tax for the four years of income ended 30 June 1979 - 1982 inclusive.

2. The sole issue in the appeals is whether sums received by the appellant in each of the years of income from Maleny Milk Producers Pty Limited ("Maleny") were income derived by the appellant and thus made assessable income of the appellant under s.25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended ("the Act") (as the Commissioner submitted) or were of a capital nature and thus not assessable (as the appellant submitted).

3. The facts are not in dispute. Prior to 1972 the appellant carried on the businesses of a dairy farmer, a milk processor and pasteuriser and as a wholesaler and retailer of milk. On 20 March 1972 the appellant sold to Caboolture Co-Operative Association Limited ("Caboolture") and QUF Tradings Pty Limited ("QUF") certain assets representing its milk processing and pasteurising business and its wholesale milk business ("the 1972 agreement"). It thereafter continued in business as a dairy farmer and as a retailer of milk. This transaction was part of a rationalisation of milk production in an area of Queensland which prior to the 1972 agreement was serviced by the appellant, Caboolture and QUF.

4. As part of the 1972 agreement, and as part consideration for it, there came into existence an indenture dated 30 June 1972, the parties to which were Caboolture and the appellant. That indenture recited the agreement for sale and purchase of 20 March 1972, the desire of the appellant to provide for the sale and disposal of its milk which was expected to amount to 600 gallons per day, and the desire of Caboolture to secure the supply of that milk for processing. By the indenture, Caboolture granted to the appellant, what is referred to as a "market milk entitlement" for a period of approximately 20 years expiring on 30 September 1992, for a number of gallons per day, stipulated in the schedule to the agreement, commencing with an initial gallonage of 350 gallons, rising to 600 gallons in the third year and remaining at that level thereafter. In respect of that "market milk entitlement", Caboolture agreed to purchase and the appellant to sell as "market milk", "that number of gallons of milk each day that Moneymen has available not exceeding the number of gallons per day specified in the Schedule." The price payable for the "market milk" so referred to was to be the highest price paid or payable by Caboolture to its suppliers other than the appellant on each day.

5. Milk in excess of the gallonage referred to in the schedule was to be purchased by Caboolture as "manufacture milk" at the highest price paid or payable by Caboolture to its suppliers other than the appellant for "manufacture milk".

6. There were in existence at the time of execution of the indenture, two prices for milk supplied by producers to factories, the higher price, for "market milk", was payable for milk up to the limit of each producer's individual quota (set, it would seem, by the factory) and a lower price for "manufacture milk", being milk produced in excess of the quota list. Thus, a significant advantage to the appellant from the arrangement was that it could sell all the milk it produced on its farm up to the maximum quantity stipulated at the best price for 20 years without having to qualify for a quota, as other producers were required to do by achieving certain levels of production of milk over an annual quota qualifying period. The usual break up of production for a producer, was 55% of production as "market milk" and 45% as "manufacture milk".

7. Clause 10 of the indenture provided:

"At and from the expiration of the term of this

agreement Moneymen shall revert to the status of a

Caboolture quota milk supplier with the quota

established during the quota determining period in the

last year of this agreement provided however if at the

end of the period of operation there is not in

existence a quota determining system applicable to

Caboolture suppliers, Moneymen shall share the market

milk supplied by Caboolture suppliers on a percentage

of production basis no less favourable than that

enjoyed by any other Caboolture supplier."

8. Although the indenture contemplated that the milk to be sold to, and purchased by, Caboolture, would be milk emanating from farms owned and operated by the appellant, clause 20 of the indenture provided:

"This contract may be assigned as a whole contract for

the balance of its term by Moneymen to any person or

group of persons or company or group of companies other

than any entity or subsidiary of such entity conducting

or intending to conduct a factory producing dairy

products AND Caboolture and the assignee shall upon

receipt of notice of such assignment and of the

execution of a deed by the assignee and if more than

one by each of them agreeing to the terms of this

contract be bound each to the other as Caboolture and

Moneymen are bound hereby."

9. The indenture entitled Caboolture to deduct from monies payable to the appellant for milk, amounts for cartage of milk by Caboolture and for levies. Production was made for the "market milk entitlement" in certain events, and it was provided that the agreement was to be terminated if the appellant should fail to supply any milk pursuant to the "market milk entitlement" for a period of two years.

10. The appellant suffered thereafter losses and decided to quit the dairying business and realise its assets. In July 1974 it offered for sale by auction its land, machinery, livestock and the benefit of the indenture of 30 June 1972. As a result of the auction, the appellant sold its land, all its livestock and certain other assets, but not the benefit of the indenture.

11. Subsequently, an agreement was reached between the appellant and Maleny for the assignment to Maleny of the appellant's rights under the indenture other than the "quota status" referred to in clause 10. This arrangement was reduced to writing in the form of three deeds dated 13 February 1975. The first deed was a Deed of Assignment between the appellant and Maleny, whereby the appellant, therein referred to as "the assignor" (assigned):-

"...the whole right title and interest of the Assignor

under the said Indenture made the 30th day of June 1972

and to the Contract evidenced thereby and contained

therein together with the benefit subject to the

obligation of the Assignee's observing performing and

fulfilling the Assignor's obligations under the contract."

No consideration was stated in the deed for the assignment.

12. The second deed was described as a "Deed of Defeasance". Under it, Maleny covenanted to perform the obligations under the indenture to supply milk to Caboolture. It further covenanted to pay to the appellant monthly an amount calculated in accordance with a formula, and to pay on execution, by way of advance, the sum of $9,000. The formula was expressed as:

"MMP - C - L x G x D"

7

Where "MMP" was the market milk price per gallon, "C" and "L" the charges for Caboolture for cartage and levies per gallon, "G" was for relevant purposes 600 gallons, and "D" was the number of days in the month that milk was able to be supplied. The amount payable was not dependent upon the amount of milk actually supplied by Maleny to Caboolture. The effect of the formula, therefore, was that the appellant was to receive 1/7 of the "market milk" price for 600 gallons payable by Caboolture, less levies and cartage charges.

13. The Deed of Defeasance gave by clause 4 to Maleny the right to bring these payments to an end in certain circumstances by payment of a lump sum. This right was expressed as follows:

"4. If the supplier shall have duly and punctually

observed and performed at all times including during

the currency of the notice in this clause mentioned its

obligations under this agreement and under the contract

the supplier shall at any time upon giving six calendar

month's notice of intention to compound as a condition

precedent to the exercise of this right have the right

to pay in full satisfaction and discharge of its

obligations to the company hereunder except in respect

of any breach antecedent to the payment and so as to

release and exonerate the supplier from any obligation

hereunder to make any payment on a monthly basis to the

company as a compound of all future payments hereunder

the discounted cash sum calculated on the basis of the

higher of the market milk prices as at the date of the

notice and the date of expiration of the notice (if the

market milk price at these two dates differs) over the

balance of the term specified in the schedule to the

contract from the date of expiration of the notice

being the present value of all payments that would from

the date of expiration of the notice be made if the

notice were not given and if the contract and this

agreement remained on foot throughout the balance of

the said term specified in the schedule to the contract

the present value to be ascertained at the discount

rate of 10 percent ascertained in accordance with the

tables for that purpose set forth in Murray's

Principles and Practice of Valuation and upon payment

thereof the company shall so far as relates to the

obligation to make future monthly payments release and

cancel these presents."

14. If the Deed of Indenture by the consent of, or by virtue of default of, Maleny, was brought to an end or prejudiced, then Maleny was to be liable to pay to the appellant an amount equivalent to the lump sum payment referred to in clause 4 of the Deed of Defeasance as if the notice of intention to compound referred to in that clause had expired on the date the 1972 agreement was cancelled, prejudiced or became inoperative (clause 6 (2)).

15. As "security" for the performance by Maleny of its obligations under the Deed of Defeasance, that deed provided for the execution by Maleny of a reassignment of the rights under the Deed of Indenture. Thus, the third deed executed was a further Deed of Assignment in identical terms to the first Deed of Assignment, but being an assignment from Maleny to the appellant.

16. Thereafter, and for some years at least, Maleny produced and sold milk to Caboolture and paid to the appellant the amounts owing under the Deed of Defeasance. At about the end of the 1975 income tax year, Caboolture and Maleny amalgamated with the result that Caboolture owned Maleny. A consequence of this amalgamation was that no milk was thereafter actually sold by Maleny to Caboolture but Maleny continued to make payments to the appellant under the Deed of Defeasance. No attempt was made by Maleny to compound the monthly payments, nor did the appellant seek to enforce payment of the lump sum amount, on the ground that Maleny had discontinued its supply of milk for two years, an event that would have brought to an end the obligations of Caboolture under the indenture unless waived by the parties.

17. In consequence, the appellant received in each year of income, the subject of the appeals, monthly amounts from Maleny pursuant to the Deed of Defeasance. It is these amounts which the Commissioner, in the assessments under appeal, treated as assessable income under s.25 of the Act.

18. Before Spender J, the Commissioner sought unsuccessfully to argue that the payments by Maleny to the appellant were "royalties" and as such made assessable under s.26(f) of the Act. Although the Commissioner filed a Notice of Contention, submitting that his Honour had erred in this respect, the Notice of Contention was not pressed before us. A further argument that the Commissioner was entitled to apply s.26(2) of the Act to treat the amounts in question as assessable income, was withdrawn before Spender J and need not here be considered.

19. For the appellant it was argued:

1. That the rights under the indenture were a capital

asset of the appellant.

2. That as part of the disposal of the totality of its

milk producing business, the appellant had sold this capital

asset to Maleny and had thereafter ceased its milk producing

business.

3. That the monthly payments represented, therefore, the

sale price for this capital asset and were received by the

appellant on capital account.

4. That the fact that the sale proceeds were measured by

a formula which might be thought to take into account loss

or deprivation of future income or earnings was irrelevant.

20. For the purpose of the present appeal it may be accepted, without the necessity of deciding the point, that the contractual rights of the appellant pursuant to the Deed of Indenture, were a "structural asset" rather than a "revenue asset" to adopt the classification of Professor Parsons in his work Income Taxation in Australia, Law Book Company 1985, 2-478 ff. That is to say that the Deed of Indenture formed part of the "profit yielding subject" of the appellant in contra-distinction to the contractual rights involved in cases such as Allied Mills Industries Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1988] FCA 357; (1989) 20 FCR 288, and Heavy Minerals Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1966] HCA 60; (1966) 115 CLR 512, cf Kelsall Parsons and Co. v Commissioners of Inland Revenue [1938] ScotCS CSIH_1; (1938) 21 TC 608, and Barr Crombie and Co. Ltd v Commissioners of Inland Revenue (1945) 26 TC 406, Californian Oil Products Ltd (In Liquidation) v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 35; (1934) 52 CLR 28.

21. It may also be accepted that, at least in a colloquial sense, the transaction reflected in the three deeds of 13 February 1975 could be referred to as a "sale". Strictly, of course, notwithstanding the provisions of clause 20 of the Deed of Indenture permitting assignment, there can be no assignment of an obligation. Nor could the rights under a personal service contract, such as the Deed of Indenture here in question, be assigned. The correct legal analysis seemed to be that the appellant and Maleny agreed that the contractual rights between the appellant and Caboolture were to be novated and this novation was effected with the consent of Caboolture express or implied. But nothing turns upon the characterisation of the transaction, whether as a sale or as a novation.

22. For present purposes, it can also be accepted that after the deeds of 13 February 1975 had been executed and implemented with the assent of Caboolture to the novation, the appellant had no asset then remaining of its milk business, notwithstanding the rights it had to a quota at the expiration of 20 years which the parties believe were not assigned to Maleny. Unless these rights could be "assigned" to a purchaser for consideration, they were of no practical value to the appellant who had neither farm nor livestock to produce milk.

23. Nevertheless, it does not follow that the payments received by the appellant were received by it on capital account.

24. A taxpayer may sell a capital asset for a stream of income, in which case the consideration he or she receives will be income in ordinary concepts. Such a case was EgertonWarburton v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 40; (1934) 51 CLR 568. In that case, the taxpayer sold to his sons certain lands, admittedly capital assets, being the farming land on which the taxpayer had carried on business, in consideration of his sons paying to him an annuity of 1,200 during his life and of an annuity to his wife if he predeceased her. As the unanimous judgment of the High Court (Rich, Dixon and McTiernan JJ.) points out at p 573:

"A transaction by which an owner of capital assets

disposes of them for a consideration which includes

annual payments may serve the double purpose of

converting a capital asset into money and of converting

the money, which otherwise would be capital, into

income."

25. In Egerton-Warbuton, the High Court distinguished the case where the purchase price for the asset was payable by instalments over a long period of time, where the instalments (although not any interest thereon) were instalments of capital, from the case where the consideration was a covenant to pay a series of certain (or uncertain) amounts where the payments received were income. Quoting Walton J in Chadwick v Pearl Life Insurance Co. (1905) 2 KB 507 at 514, their Honours affirmed that the distinction was a fine one depending:

"...on whether the agreement between the parties

involves an obligation to pay a fixed gross sum."

A similar conclusion was reached in Just v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1949) 8 ATD 419.

26. The appellant sought to distinguish Egerton-Warburton in a number of different ways. First, it was suggested that the comments of the High Court were dependent upon the fact that the payments there in question were related inter alia to the life of the father, appeared of uncertain duration and were "a true life annuity", thus being of an income nature. In the present case, it was pointed out, that the payments were to continue only for the period of the indenture: approximately 20 years. That, however, is not a matter of great moment. An "annuity" is not only to be found where payments are referable to life. The word denotes, as its Latin roots indicate merely an "annual sum", Deputy Federal Commissioner of Land Tax Sydney v Hindmarsh [1912] HCA 27; (1912) 14 CLR 334, Foley v Fletcher (1853) 3 H and N 769; [1858] EngR 1107; 157 ER 678. Thus, Jowitt's Dictionary of English Law, (2nd Ed.) defines the expression "annuity" as meaning:

"A yearly payment of a certain sum of money granted to

another in fee, or for life, or for a term of years,

either payable under a personal obligation of the

grantor or charged upon his pure personalty, although

it may be made a charge upon his freehold or leasehold

land..."

27. As the present use of the term, in the Act, as illustrated by s.27H(1) makes clear, periodical payments, for a fixed term, may be an annuity.

28. More importantly, however, for present purposes, is not whether the periodical payments here in question should be termed "an annuity", but whether there has been an agreement to pay a fixed gross amount by instalments or rather merely an agreement to make a number of annual payments (clearly having regard to the formula in the Deed of Defeasance, of uncertain amount).

29. The appellant submitted that the present was a case where there was in truth a fixed purchase price. It relied upon clauses 4 and 6(2) to the Deed of Defeasance for this submission and pointed to the decision of the House of Lords in Secretary of State in Council of India v Scoble (1903) AC 299 in support of its submission.

30. In Scoble, the Great Indian Peninsula Railway Company had sold a railway for a lump sum pursuant to an agreement which gave to the purchaser, the Secretary of State for the Council of India, the option, instead of paying the gross amount, of paying an annuity. The House of Lords held that the annual sum paid was partly capital and affirmed the decision of the Court of Appeal to this effect. A similar result is reached under the Australian Act by virtue of the present provisions of s.27H(1)(a) and cf the earlier provisions, now repealed, of s.26AA of the Act.

31. Even if Scoble's case may be regarded as of wider significance than the statutory context would suggest (that being whether the payments were an "annuity" within the meaning of s.102 of the Income Tax Act 1842 and Schedule C or Schedule D of the Income Tax Act 1853) it does not avail the appellant here. The significant difference is that in Scoble there was an ascertained purchase price for the capital asset which was fixed. That is not the case here. It is impossible to point to a purchase price that was ascertained at the time of the 13th February 1975 deeds, or indeed at any time thereafter. There was, of course, a right to commute the monthly payments existing in the assignee Maleny (not in the appellant). However, even if this right were exercised, the amount payable depended upon the "market milk" price not only as at the date notice was given under clause 4, but upon the price payable at the date of expiration of the notice. The evidence was that the "market milk" price changed from time to time; it was not fixed. No attempt was made by Maleny to exercise the right of commutation under clause 4, when the amount payable might then have been a capital payment. At no time could it be said that there was ever an ascertained purchase price payable for the rights "sold" by the appellant to Maleny.

32. The case is not, in my view, relevantly distinguishable from Egerton-Warburton (supra) and it follows that the periodical receipts of the appellant pursuant to the Deed of Defeasance are income in ordinary concepts and thus assessable income within s.25 of the Act. No question arises in the present case as to whether some part of the periodical payments were to be treated as "undeducted purchase price" as defined in s.26AA(4) of the Act as in force during the relevant income tax years, the taxpayer having put its case on an all or nothing basis. However, it might be observed that s.26AA would not have availed the taxpayer in this case, there not being a "purchase price" the required starting point for the definition of "the undeducted purchase price".

33. In the present case there is much to be said for the view that there is another and related reason why the monthly payments should be seen as income in ordinary concepts. Here the monthly payments under the Deed of Indenture, viewed objectively, were but a substitute for the income which the taxpayer could have derived under the Deed of Indenture had it continued to produce milk and not assigned its rights to Maleny. The payments were calculated as a fraction (1/7) of the gross sale price of "market milk" less the deductions authorised to be made by Caboolture pursuant to the Deed of Indenture. The recitals in the Deed of Indenture suggest that the appellant contemplated that it would produce 600 gallons of "market milk". While no doubt the fraction of 1/7 was arrived at by way of commercial negotiation and left Maleny able to make a commercial profit over and above its costs of production, the formula suggests that the monthly payment was intended to compensate the appellant for some, at least, of the net profits which, had it continued to produce milk and sell to Caboolture, it would have derived. Of course, it must be accepted that prior to the assignment, the appellant no longer had either farm or livestock with which to produce and sell milk under the Deed of Indenture.

34. Generally speaking, an amount received, particularly if received periodically, as a substitution for income will be itself income. Thus in The Commissioner of Taxes (Victoria) v Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 55 CLR 144 at 156, the contract of a governing director was terminated in consideration of a sum being paid by monthly instalments to him as compensation calculated as a percentage of the employer's estimated net profits for the unexpired term of his service contract of ten years (his remuneration being stipulated in the service contract as the same percentage of net profits per year). The periodic receipts were treated as income. In so holding, Dixon and Evatt JJ said, at p 156:

"No prima facie reason exists for regarding as

instalments of capital annual payments which are taken

in place of the contractual rights such a contract

gave."

35. A similar notion is to be found in cases such as, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, 567-8, per Fullagar J, Chadwick v Pearl Life Insurance Co. (supra) at 514, referred to with approval in Egerton-Warburton (supra)[1958] HCA 62; , (1957-58) 98 CLR 460. This will be so, notwithstanding that to treat an amount as income merely because it is computed by reference to loss of future income will be fallacious, at least if the amount is paid in a lump sum; Californian Oil Products (supra) at p 46, Van den Berghs v Clark (1935) AC 431 at 442.

36. The appeal must be dismissed and the appellant must pay the Commissioner's costs of it.
