Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. By letter received by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT), now the Administrative Review Tribunal (Tribunal),[1] on 27 November 2019 (and by a review application form received by the Tribunal on 4 December 2019), the Applicant applied to the Tribunal for review of the Respondent’s (Commissioner’s) objection decision, in which the Respondent (Commissioner) disallowed the Applicant’s objections to amended assessments and penalty assessments for the income years ended 30 June 2009 to 30 June 2014, following an audit of the Applicant’s tax affairs, for those income years, by the Commissioner.
  2. However, in August 2025, the Applicant withdrew his review application as it relates to the Commissioner’s objection decision on the amended assessments for the income years ended 2009, 2011, 2012 and 2013. Consequently, the Tribunal’s review is confined to a review of the Commissioner’s objection decision as it relates to:
    (a) the amended assessments for the income years ended 30 June 2010 and 30 June 2014 (as adjusted by the objection decision); and

    (b) the penalty assessments for the income years ended 30 June 2009 to 30 June 2014.

  3. For the following reasons, the Tribunal affirms the parts of the Commissioner’s objection decision that are under review, as set out immediately above.

PROCEDURAL HISTORY

  1. As stated above, the Applicant originally commenced these proceedings in November 2019. The proceedings were ultimately heard by the Tribunal, over three days, on 15 to 17 December 2025.
  2. Suffice it to say that the procedural history of this a matter is long and plagued by the Applicant’s repeated non-compliance with the Tribunal’s directions and associated delays. The reasons for the Applicant’s non-compliance and associated delays include multiple changes in legal representation and the Applicant’s overseas absences. Another reason for the Applicant’s non-compliance and associated delays is the Applicant’s imprisonment for seven years, for extortion, from 3 October 2015 until 2 October 2022. The Applicant describes this period as follows:

Arrest & Incarceration

58. The Applicant was arrested and charged with extortion by officers of the Western Australia Police Force on 15 May 2024.

59. The Applicant was ultimately found guilty of this charge and sentenced to nine years’ imprisonment.

60. From 3 October 2015 until 2 October 2022, the Applicant was incarcerated. Whilst incarcerated, the Applicant spent time at Hakea Prison, Acacia Prison, Casuarina Prison, Karnet Prison Farm and Wooroloo Prison Farm.[2]

  1. Whilst the Tribunal acknowledges that the Applicant’s imprisonment, in various Western Australian prisons, from 3 October 2015 until 2 October 2022 (i.e. seven years),[3] undoubtedly presented him with practical difficulties, in terms of accessing documents, computer services, telephone, accountants and lawyers, at least in the three year period from the commencement of these proceedings (in November 2019) and his release from prison (on 2 October 2022), the Tribunal considers that the Applicant has, nevertheless, had ample opportunity to prepare his case for hearing, on 15 to 17 December 2025, since his release from prison on 2 October 2022.
  2. A fair and holistic reading of the procedural history of these proceedings, demonstrates that the Tribunal has, over a sustained period, followed fair and proper procedures, in the face of continued non-compliance by the Applicant and has ensured that the Applicant has been given a ‘reasonable opportunity’ to present his case, access any information or documents to which the Tribunal proposes to have regard in reaching a decision in the proceedings and make submissions and adduce evidence, in accordance with s 55(1) of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (ART Act). The procedural history of these proceedings must also be considered in the context of the Tribunal’s statutory ‘Objective’, in s 9 of the ART Act, which reads (in part):

The Tribunal must pursue the objective of providing an independent mechanism of review that:

(a) is fair and just; and

(b) ensures that applications to the Tribunal are solved as quickly, and with as little formality and expense, as proper consideration of the matters before the Tribunal permits;

(emphasis added)

  1. A series of case management directions hearings were conducted by the Tribunal in May, July, October and November 2020. On 15 January 2021, the Tribunal conducted a dismissal hearing for non-compliance with the Tribunal’s directions. On 21 January 2021, the Tribunal refused the Commissioner’s dismissal application.
  2. However, at a case management directions hearing, held on 27 April 2021, the Tribunal dismissed the proceedings because of the Applicant’s non-appearance. That directions hearing had been convened because of the Applicant’s non-compliance with various directions made by the Tribunal.
  3. On 14 June 2021, the Applicant applied to the Tribunal for the proceedings to be reinstated and, on 7 July 2021, the Tribunal made orders refusing the Applicant’s application for reinstatement (AAT Reinstatement Refusal Decision).
  4. The Applicant subsequently appealed the AAT Reinstatement Refusal Decision to the Federal Court of Australia and, on 22 July 2022, her Honour Banks-Smith J remitted the proceedings to the AAT.
  5. Following the remittal of the proceedings to the AAT, the Applicant’s non-compliance continued. The AAT made directions on six occasions, between December 2022 and April 2024 (i.e. on 8 December 2022, 17 March 2023, 11 May 2023, 11 July 2023, 10 August 2023 and 4 April 2024) for, among other things, the filing of an amended Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFIC) by the Applicant and further evidence of the Applicant (at the Applicant’s behest). The Applicant did not file his (first) amended SFIC and evidence by any of the deadlines provided for in the AAT’s directions until 8 May 2024. Further, during this period, many directions hearings were listed, vacated and relisted, mostly because of the Applicant’s non-compliance with the Tribunal’s directions.
  6. On 8 October 2024, the AAT listed the proceedings for a three day hearing on 25 November 2024 to 27 November 2024. On 10 October 2024, the Applicant advised the AAT that he no longer had legal representation and was overseas until 23 October 2024 and requested an adjournment of the hearing to enable him time to obtain legal representation. On 15 October 2024, the Applicant’s former solicitors, Zafra Legal, wrote to the Tribunal confirming that they had terminated their engagement with the Applicant on 4 October 2024. On 5 November 2024, the Applicant formally applied to the Tribunal for an adjournment of the hearing, listed on 25 November 2024 to 27 November 2024, which the Tribunal granted to enable the Applicant to obtain legal representation, as requested.
  7. In or around November 2024, the Applicant notified the Tribunal and the Commissioner that he had appointed Birchstone Legal Group (Birchstone) as his new legal representatives. On 12 December 2024, Birchstone contacted the Commissioner advising that the Applicant intended to introduce new evidence and amend the Applicant’s (first) amended SFIC. On 16 December 2024, the Tribunal adjourned the case management directions hearing, listed on 16 December 2024, to 15 January 2025, by consent of the parties.
  8. The Tribunal made directions on two further occasions, on 6 February 2025 and 2 May 2025, for the filing of the Applicant’s (second) amended SFIC and further evidence. The Applicant did not file his (second) amended SFIC and evidence by the deadlines provided for in the Tribunal’s directions, nor did he make any applications to extend the time for compliance prior to the expiry of the relevant date for compliance. Specifically, the 2 May 2025 directions provided that the Applicant was to file and serve his (second) amended SFIC and further evidence on or before 11 June 2025. As stated, the Applicant failed to comply with this direction.
  9. On 12 June 2025, Birchstone emailed the Tribunal requesting an extension of time in which to comply with the 11 June 2025 filing deadline, to 27 June 2025. On 16 June 2025, the Commissioner emailed the Tribunal advising it that the Commissioner opposed the Applicant’s extension request. On 17 June 2025, the Tribunal made directions extending the deadline by which the Applicant was required to provide his (second) amended SFIC and further evidence to 27 June 2015, as requested by the Applicant (and providing the Commissioner with an equivalent period to file his amended SFIC and further evidence). At the time of making the 17 June 2025 directions, the Tribunal communicated to the parties that given the long history of non-compliance with directions by the Applicant it would not entertain any further requests, by the Applicant, for extensions of time for compliance with its directions: s 9 of the ART Act.
  10. The Applicant failed to provide a (second) amended SFIC and further evidence on or before 27 June 2025, as directed on 17 June 2025. On 28 June 2025, Birchstone emailed the Tribunal advising that the Applicant failed to comply with the 17 June 2025 directions due to personal illness and overseas travel for holiday.
  11. As a result, on 1 July 2025, the Tribunal listed the proceedings for a case management directions hearing on 29 July 2025. On 24 July 2025, the Commissioner applied to the Tribunal for the proceedings to be dismissed, under s 100 of the ART Act, at the case management directions hearing on 29 July 2025, for failure by the Applicant to, within a reasonable time, proceed and/or comply with the Tribunal’s directions (Commissioner’s July 2025 Dismissal Application).
  12. The Commissioner provided an Affidavit from Mr Adam Lukacs, AGS lawyer, dated 24 July 2025 (Lukacs Affidavit), in support of the Commissioner’s July 2025 Dismissal Application. The Applicant filed an affidavit, dated 4 August 2025, opposing the Commissioner’s July 2025 Dismissal Application.
  13. In the Lukacs Affidavit, the Commissioner outlined the ‘Prejudice to the Respondent’ by allowing the proceedings to remain on-foot (in part) as follows:
    1. On 15 November 2022, the Respondent lodged caveats against both [of the Applicant’s Australian properties] for the purposes of protecting the Respondent’s rights with respect to enforcing and seeking recovery of the Applicant’s taxation debt...
    2. No arrangement has been entered into between the Applicant and the Respondent for the payment of the Applicant’s taxation debt.
    3. Since these proceedings have been on foot, the Respondent has deferred commencing recovery action against the Applicant due to the Applicant disputing his assessments in these proceedings which would likely cause any recovery action to be stayed pending determination of these proceedings.
    4. The delays in the proceeding to date have consequently caused any recovery of the Applicant’s taxation debt to be significantly delayed, thereby prejudicing the Respondent.
  14. The Lukacs Affidavit was supported by the ‘Respondent’s Submissions in Support of Application for Dismissal’, dated 6 August 2025 (Commissioner’s Written Submissions in Support of July 2025 Dismissal). As at the date of the Commissioner’s Written Submissions in Support of July 2025 Dismissal, the Applicant had not provided a (second) amended SFIC and further evidence.
  15. On 28 July 2025, the Tribunal vacated the case management directions hearing on 29 July 2025, and listed the matter for an interlocutory hearing on 7 August 2025, to determine the Commissioner’s July 2025 Dismissal Application, and directed the Applicant to file any responsive material to the Lukacs Affidavit and the Commissioner’s Written Submissions in Support of July 2025 Dismissal, on or before 4 August 2025.
  16. On 6 August 2025, the Applicant notified the Tribunal and Commissioner that he wished to withdraw his review applications insofar as they relate to his primary tax liabilities for the 2009, 2011, 2012 and 2013 income years.
  17. At the interlocutory hearing, on 7 August 2025, having heard submissions from both parties, the Tribunal refused the Commissioner’s July 2025 Dismissal Application and made directions (by consent) programming the matter to hearing in December 2025. These directions were subsequently varied, on several occasions, at the request of the parties.
  18. The Applicant finally filed his (second) amended SFIC on 15 August 2025.
  19. On 21 August 2025, Birchstone formally filed a Notice of Withdrawal narrowing the proceedings by withdrawing the Applicant’s review applications for the Commissioner’s objection decision as it relates to the amended assessments for the 2009, 2011, 2012 and 2013 income years.
  20. On 26 September 2025, the Tribunal notified the parties that the matter had been listed for a three-day hearing on 15 to 17 December 2025.
  21. On 5 November 2025, Birchstone advised the Tribunal and the Commissioner that the Applicant had terminated their legal representation and that they would no longer be representing him in the proceedings before the Tribunal.
  22. The matter was ultimately heard, over three days, on 15 to 17 December 2025. The Applicant was self-represented at the hearing.[4]
  23. The above summary of the ‘Procedural History’ of this matter demonstrates that there was almost total inaction on the part of the Applicant to obtain the documents that were the subject of numerous directions made by the Tribunal over a long period. This is entirely unacceptable.

Mr Corentin – Summonses

  1. Mr Luc Cyril Corentin, of Bedford Accounting Services (Mr Corentin), became the Applicant’s accountant and tax agent in around 2011,[5] well before these proceedings commenced in November 2019. Mr Corentin continued to be the Applicant’s accountant and tax agent until sometime in 2020.[6]
  2. In his amended SFIC, dated 18 August 2025, the Applicant states the following regarding Mr Corentin:

Management of Tax Affairs

  1. The Applicant has always relied on an accountant and taxation agent to prepare books of account for his businesses and to help him meet his taxation obligations.
  2. The Applicant is not trained in accounting or Australian taxation law. As such, he has never felt confident to prepare books of account, financial statements or tax returns without the assistance of an accountant and taxation agent. This is because he does not understand the intricacies of accounting, taxation law or the processes that must be followed in the preparation of tax returns.
  3. The Applicant has always placed full trust in his accountants and relied on their advice as to his own taxation liabilities and the taxation liabilities of his related entities.
  4. Julian Kovacs (Julian) was the Applicant’s accountant and taxation agent from in or around 2000 until he passed away in or around December 2010. Julian was a partner of an accounting firm known as Stantons Partners and then Stantons International Services. The Applicant had a close working relationship with Julian and had gotten to know him through their mutual involvement in the sport of boxing before Julian became an accountant.
  5. After Julian passed away, the Applicant needed to find another accountant and taxation agent. He ultimately engaged Luc Cyril Corentin (Cyril) in or around 2011. Cyril ran an accounting and taxation agent business at the time through Bedford Accounting Services Pty Ltd trading as Bedford Accounting Services (Bedford Accounting Services).
  6. The Applicant knew Cyril and felt that he could trust him as Cyril had previously worked with Julian at Stantons International Services.
  7. The Applicant continued to utilise the services of Cyril and Bedford Accounting Services until in or around 2020.

...

2010 Income Tax Returns
  1. The Applicant’s income tax return for the 2010 Income Year was prepared by Cyril of Bedford Accounting Services, who was the Applicant’s accountant and taxation agent at that time.
  2. The Applicant’s income tax return for the 2010 Income Year was lodged with the Commissioner on 27 February 2013, and reported that he had taxable income of $15,136 during that period. [footnote omitted] A notice of assessment was issued on 18 March 2013.
  3. An income tax return was not prepared or lodged for the [Applicant] Family Trust in respect of the 2010 Income Year. However, Cyril/Bedford Accounting Services prepared draft financial statements for the [Applicant] Family Trust for that period.[footnote omitted]

...

2014 Income Tax Returns

  1. Cyril/Bedford Accounting Services prepared both the Applicant’s and the [Applicant] Family Trust’s income tax returns for the 2014 Income Year. The [Applicant] Family Trust’s income tax return for the 2014 Income Year was lodged on 27 March 2015 and reported that the [Applicant] Family Trust had net income of $192,598 in that period.[footnote omitted]
  2. The entirety of the net income of the [Applicant] Family Trust was recorded as having been distributed to the Applicant as a beneficiary of the [Applicant] Family Trust. The Applicant signed a resolution to this effect on 26 March 2015.[footnote omitted]
  3. Cyril/Bedford Accounting Services prepared financial statements for the [Applicant] Family Trust in respect of the 2014 Income Year,[footnote omitted] which were finalised on 26 March 2015 and which the [Applicant] Family Trust’s income tax return for the 2014 Income Year appears to have been substantially based upon.

...

  1. The Applicant contends that the shortfall penalty assessments were not correctly made, as:

254.1 he filed his taxation returns for the relevant years in accordance with the advice of his accountant and taxation agents at the time; and

254.2 as he is not an expert in accounting or the intricacies of Australian taxation law, it did not amount to intentional disregard of the law for him to follow his accountants’ advice in that regard.

  1. On 29 August 2018, Mr Corentin swore a ‘Statutory Declaration’, the stated sole purpose of which was ‘in support of the [Applicant] Family Trust’s objection to the amended assessment issued to it by the ATO for the 2009/2010 financial year’ (Corentin 2018 Statutory Declaration).[7] At that time, the Applicant was represented by Lawton Gillon Lawyers.
  2. Despite the Applicant’s reliance on Mr Corentin’s expertise, as an accountant and tax agent, in preparing his income tax returns for the 2009 to 2014 income years, at no time, after commencing these proceedings (in November 2019), did he, or any of his former solicitors, seek to obtain a witness statement or expert report from Mr Corentin in support of the Applicant’s review applications. The only document from Mr Corentin, in the materials before the Tribunal in these proceedings, is the Corentin 2018 Statutory Declaration which, as stated immediately above, was provided by Mr Corentin to the ATO solely in support of an objection by the Applicant’s family trust to an amended assessment.
  3. That is, the Corentin 2018 Statutory Declaration predates the commencement of these proceedings and was not provided by Mr Corentin to the Tribunal directly in support of the Applicant’s review applications but, rather, was provided by Mr Corentin to the ATO in support of an objection by the Applicant’s family trust to an amended assessment issued to the Applicant’s family trust. Given the strong reliance the Applicant places on the alleged errors made by Mr Corentin in the preparation and lodgement of his income tax returns for the 2009 to 2014 income years, in support of his contention that the amended assessments are excessive, it beggars belief that he and/or his former representatives failed, in the many years they have had available to them, to obtain a witness statement or expert report from Mr Corentin directly in support of these proceedings.
  4. On 17 October 2025, Birchstone filed a ‘Request to Issue Summons’ to Mr Corentin. The stated ‘Reasons for the request’ were as follows:

The Applicant wishes to summons Mr Luc Cyril Corentin (Mr Corentin) to provide documents and give evidence at the final hearing of this matter regarding Mr Corentin's role as the accountant and tax agent for the Applicant and his associated entities in respect of the 2010 to 2014 financial/income years. The Applicant believes that Mr Corentin's evidence, as well as the documents requested, will be relevant to the issues of:

(a) whether the shortfall penalty assessments were correctly made; and/or

(b) whether the shortfall penalty assessments should be remitted in whole or in part.

The Applicant believes that Mr Corentin's evidence will:

(a) corroborate the Applicant's contention that the Applicant provided all relevant information and documents for the preparation of the Applicant's and [Applicant] Family Trust's income tax returns for each of the 2010 to 2014 income years to Mr Corentin;

(b) provide further detail regarding the process that was followed in the preparation of those returns; and

(c) establish that Mr Corentin and/or the companies through which he carried on business were deregistered as registered tax agents by the Tax Practitioners Board.

  1. The above summons was issued by the Tribunal to Mr Corentin on 20 October 2025.[8]
  2. The Tribunal subsequently convened a directions hearing, on 5 November 2025, to discuss, among other things, the summons of Mr Corentin in these proceedings, which the Commissioner opposed. Having heard arguments from both parties, noting that at that time the Applicant was represented by counsel instructed by Birchstone, the Tribunal decided to revoke the summons of Mr Corentin.
  3. In summary, the reasons the Tribunal revoked the summons of Mr Corentin were two-fold. First, the Applicant has, and his various former solicitors have, had ample opportunity (i.e. since at least the time the Applicant was released from prison on 2 October 2022, if not before, noting these proceedings were commenced by the Applicant in November 2019) to obtain a witness statement or expert report from Mr Corentin in support these proceedings, but failed to do so, without an adequate explanation. Second, to summon a witness to give evidence at the hearing, that has not provided a witness statement or expert report in advance of the hearing, so close to the commencement of the hearing, would undoubtedly prejudice the Commissioner. Without the evidence of Mr Corentin being reduced to writing, in the form of the a witness statement or expert report, in advance of the hearing would mean that the Commissioner would have little or no notice of what evidence Mr Corentin would likely give, leaving the Commissioner in a position that he is unable to properly prepare his cross-examination of Mr Corentin and would be prejudiced in preparing his case. This, when balanced against the fact that the Applicant has had since November 2019 (when he commenced these proceedings) or, since at least October 2022, when he was released from prison, to obtain a witness statement or expert report from Mr Corentin in support of his review applications, weighed heavily in favour of revocation of the summons of Mr Corentin by the Tribunal.
  4. On 5 December 2025, just ten days before the commencement of the hearing, the Applicant (then unrepresented) emailed the Tribunal requesting that Mr Corentin be summonsed to give evidence at the hearing. No formal summons application was made by the Applicant. Then, on 15 December 2025, on the first day of the substantive hearing, the Applicant again sought the issue of a summons, as requested by email on 5 December 2025, to Mr Corentin to give evidence. For substantially the same reasons as set out above (at [39]), the Tribunal did not issue the summons to Mr Corentin.
  5. It is well-established that the power of the Tribunal to issue and/or revoke a summons is discretionary. The general principles relating to the issue of a subpoena by a court are applicable to the issue of a summons by the Tribunal.[9] The directions power of the Tribunal should be sufficient to ensure that all relevant material is before the Tribunal.[10]
  6. As contended by the Commissioner:
    1. There is no substantive explanation proffered as to why evidence was not sought from Mr Corentin (or any other witness) prior to May 2024 (which was after the Applicant was released from prison). This ignores some 5 years these proceedings were on foot prior to that date. This is inexplicable given what the Applicant contends in paragraph 12 of the Applicant’s Response is the significance of Mr Corentin’s evidence in that “That Statutory Declaration [of Mr Corentin] was a foundational document in support of the objections lodged by the Applicant’s then-solicitors, Lawton Gillon Lawyers, on 30 August 2018”. Why was such foundational evidence not filed in compliance with any of the nine separate occasions that the Applicant failed to comply with the Tribunal’s directions since the proceedings were remitted back to this Tribunal?
    2. ...the Applicant’s delay in these proceedings has to be considered holistically from the commencement of the proceedings.[11]

BURDEN OF PROOF

  1. In proceeding for review by the Tribunal of a reviewable objection decision, the Applicant has the burden of proving, under s 14ZZK(b) of Part IVC of the TAA:
    (a) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment – that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been; or

    (b) in any other case – that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently: s 14ZZK(b)(i) and (ii) of Part IVC of the TAA.

  2. The taxpayer must prove these matters on the balance of probabilities.[58] That is, the standard of proof is the balance or probabilities.
  3. The operation of the onus of proof on a taxpayer in Part IVC proceedings has been the subject of several significant Full Federal Court proceedings.[59]
  4. A taxpayer assessed by way of a default assessment, under s 167 of the ITAA 1936, must positively prove the correct amount of their actual taxable income upon which tax should be levied. It is insufficient to prove that the Commissioner erred in the way he calculated the assessed amount in the s 167 default assessment. As Derrington J observed in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Ross (2021) 174 ALD 77; [2021] FCA 766 at [46]:

...the effect of s 14ZZK(b)(i) is that the taxpayers bear the burden of proving, on the balance of probabilities, both that the assessment is “excessive” and, also, what the assessment should have been to make the assessment right, or “more nearly right”: Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 (Trautwein) at 88 per Latham CJ; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco (1990) 168 CLR 614 (Dalco) at 623-625 per Brennan J and at 632-634 per Toohey J; Gashi v Commissioner for Taxation [2013] FCAFC 30; (2013) 209 FCR 301 (Gashi) [61] – [67]...It should always be kept steadily in mind that the rationale for the onus imposed by s 14ZZK(b)(i) is that the facts relating to a taxpayer’s taxable income are peculiarly within their knowledge, and they must be taken to know what their income is and how it was derived: Trautwein at 87. It follow[s] that there is no undue harshness in requiring a taxpayer, who failed to lodge a return or whose return is not compliant with the taxation legislation, to bear the onus of establishing their true taxable income for the relevant income year.

(emphasis added)

  1. That is, it is not necessary, for the purpose of s 14ZZK(b)(i) of Part IVC of the TAA, for the taxpayer to determine their taxable income to the exact dollar amount.[60] However, taxpayers must establish an amount which is ‘more nearly right’.[61]
  2. There is no requirement that the Commissioner provide evidence to back up his assessments. As noted by the High Court in Gauci v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 CLR 81 at 89 per Mason J, there is no:

...onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were incorrectly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be sustained or supported by evidence...unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it [the amended assessments] will prevail.

  1. If the taxpayer is unable to establish that the assessment in question is excessive, then the assessment will stand irrespective of whether there are any facts or circumstances which would prima facie support the Commissioner’s assessment.[62] The taxpayer will not necessarily prove that an assessment is excessive by showing the Commissioner made an error in forming a judgment as to the amount of the assessment.[63]
  2. As stated by the High Court of Australia, in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614 at 624 per Brennan J:

The Act [i.e. the TAA] does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with s 190(b) for it is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.[64]

  1. In other words, the Applicant cannot discharge his onus, under s 14ZZK(b) of Part IVC of the TAA, by seeking to attack the basis upon which the Commissioner has calculated the amount in a s 167 default assessment or by seeking to show that specific items of income included in his assessments by the Commissioner should not have been included, or that specific deductions claimed by him but not allowed by the Commissioner should have been allowed. The Applicant can only discharge his onus by establishing what his actual taxable income was in the relevant income year.

Role of the Tribunal

  1. The role of the Tribunal is generally to make the correct and preferable decision based on the evidence before it.[65]
  2. The effect of the shift in onus on the taxpayer, under s 14ZZK(b) of the TAA, was discussed by the Full Federal Court in Rawson Finances Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (2013) 133 ALD 39; [2013] FCAFC 26 (Rawson). In Rawson, Jagot J, with whom Nicholas J agreed (at [110]) observed that a distinction is drawn between the obligation of the taxpayer to establish that the assessment is excessive and the usual role of the Tribunal to determine what is the correct and preferable decision based on the evidence before it. It is only the former issue that is before the Tribunal in tax proceedings.
  3. Rawson was endorsed by a differently constituted Full Federal Court in Rigoli v Commissioner of Taxation (2014) 141 ALD 529; [2014] FCAFC 29 (Rigoli (2014)) at [26] setting aside the decision of the AAT. Rigoli (2014) returned to the AAT in Rigoli and Commissioner of Taxation (2015) 147 ALD 182; [2015] AATA 169 (Rigoli (2015)). In Rigoli (2015), the AAT undertook an extensive review of the numerous cases relating to s 14ZZK of the TAA which was accepted by the Full Federal Court in Rigoli v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (2016) 68 AAR 478; [2016] FCAFC 38 at [25] ff, in dismissing an appeal from the AAT’s decision.[66]
  4. The Tribunal must consider questions of weight and relevance and can have regard to evidence that it considers both rational and probative. The Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence, but may inform on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate: s 52 of the ART Act. However, as the High Court made clear in Rodriguez v Telstra Corporation Limited [2002] FCA 30 at [25] per Kiefel J (as her Honour then was):

...This does not mean that the rules of evidence are to be ignored. The more flexible procedure provided for does not justify decisions made without a basis in evidence having probative force...

Penalty assessments

Relevant law

  1. A taxpayer who has a shortfall amount for a year of income may be liable to pay an administrative penalty under Division 284 of Schedule 1 to the TAA.
  2. Broadly, s 284-75(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA imposes an administrative penalty where:
    (a) a taxpayer (or agent) makes (or fails to make) a statement to the Commissioner (or to an entity that is exercising powers or performing functions under a ‘taxation law’, as defined in s 995-1(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997); and

    (b) the statement is false or misleading in a material particular, whether because of things in it or omitted from it.

  3. Item 1 of s 284-80(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a taxpayer will have a shortfall amount where their tax-related liability for an accounting period, worked out on the basis of the statement, is less than it would have been if the statement were not false or misleading.

Base penalty amount

  1. Where a shortfall amount results from a false or misleading statement, s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that the ‘base penalty amount’ is:
    (a) 75% of the tax shortfall amount if the shortfall amount resulted from an ‘intentional disregard’ of a taxation law by the taxpayer or the taxpayer’s agent;

    (b) 50% of the tax shortfall amount if the shortfall amount resulted from ‘recklessness’ by the taxpayer or the taxpayer’s agent as to the operation of a taxation law; and

    (c) 25% of the tax shortfall amount if the shortfall amount resulted from a failure by the taxpayer or the taxpayer’s agent to take ‘reasonable care’ to comply with a taxation law.

  2. The terms ‘intentional disregard’, ‘recklessness’ and ‘reasonable care’ are not defined in the TAA or another taxation law.
  3. However, in Miscellaneous Taxation Ruling MTR 2008/1, titled ‘Penalty relating to statements: meaning of reasonable care, recklessness and intentional disregard’ and issued on 12 November 2008 (MT 2008/1), the Commissioner provides his views on the meaning of the terms ‘intentional disregard’, ‘recklessness’ and ‘reasonable care’ for the purpose of s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA.
  4. As stated above (at [84] to [85]), in the Objection Decision the Commissioner imposed a ‘base penalty amount’ of 75% on the tax shortfall amounts of the Applicant for the relevant income years on the basis that shortfall amount resulted from ‘intentional disregard’ of a taxation law by the Applicant or his agent.
  5. In MT 2008/1 (at [109] to [116]), the Commissioner describes what he considers is meant by ‘intentional disregard’, for the purpose of s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA, in part, as follows:
    1. Under item 1 of the table in subsection 284 90(1), a BPA of 75% of a shortfall amount applies if the shortfall results from intentional disregard of a taxation law. Similarly, under Item 3A of the table in subsection 284 90(1), a BPA of 60 penalty units applies if a statement is false or misleading because of intentional disregard of a taxation law that does not result in a shortfall amount. In the graduated scheme of penalties, the penalty for intentional disregard is the most severe sanction in response to a serious failure to comply with tax obligations.
    2. The adjective 'intentional' means that something more than reckless disregard of or indifference to a taxation law is required.
    3. Unlike the objective test which applies to determine whether there has been a want of reasonable care or recklessness, the test for intentional disregard is purely subjective in nature. The actual intention of the entity is a critical element.
    4. Intentional disregard means that there must be actual knowledge that the statement made is false. To establish intentional disregard, the entity must understand the effect of the relevant legislation and how it operates in respect of the entity's affairs and make a deliberate choice to ignore the law.[footnote omitted]
    5. Dishonesty is a requisite feature of behaviour that shows an intentional disregard for the operation of the law. This is another significant difference between this type of behaviour and behaviour that shows a want of reasonable care or recklessness where dishonesty is not an element.
    6. Evidence of intention must be found through direct evidence or by inference from all the surrounding circumstances, including the conduct of the entity.
    7. A mere failure to follow the Commissioner's view contained in a private ruling is not evidence of intentional disregard. However, if an entity ignores an unfavourable private ruling on a matter where the law is clearly established, that may constitute intentional disregard.
    8. Weyers v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2006] FCA 818; 2006 ATC 4523; [2006] FCA 818; (2006) 63 ATR 268 illustrates the proposition that intentional disregard of the law can be inferred from the facts and surrounding circumstances. In that case a tax agent prepared tax returns for his clients that failed to return trust distributions as income on the basis that the amounts were not trust distributions but payments by way of loan. Dowsett J concluded at paragraph 168 that on the evidence the tax agent must have known that the amounts were trust income derived and not payments by way of loan. The evidence included the fact that the tax agent had told his clients the money drawn from the trust was their money which implied that it was available for them to use in their absolute discretion. Further, the court was able to conclude that the tax agent knew the amounts paid by the trustee of the trust were not loans because he knew they did not have to be repaid and that no interest was payable. Although there was no direct evidence of the taxpayer's knowledge, the surrounding facts supported the inference that the tax agent must have intentionally disregarded the requirement to disclose the income.

(emphasis added)

Increase in base penalty amount

  1. Section 284-220(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides for an increase (uplift) in the base penalty amount’ by 20% in certain circumstances.
  2. The increase (uplift) under s 284-220 of Schedule 1 to the TAA applies automatically and is not a matter for the exercise of the discretion of the Commissioner: Federal Commissioner v Ross [2021] FCA 766.

Safe harbour exemption

  1. No penalty under s 284-75(1) or (4) of Schedule 1 to the TAA applies against a taxpayer who engages a registered tax agent or BAS agent if the taxpayer has given the agent all relevant tax information and the statement did not result from the agent’s intentional disregard of, or recklessness as to the operation of, as taxation law: s 284-75(6) of Schedule 1 to the TAA.

Commissioner’s discretion to remit

  1. The Commissioner ‘may’ remit all or part of an administrative penalty: section 298-20 of Schedule 1 to the TAA. The discretion is unconstrained but must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the subject matter, scope and purpose of the objects of the TAA and according to law.[98] The purpose of the penalty regime is to defer infringement of the law in an environment of self-assessment.[99]
  2. The existence of ‘special circumstances’ is not a prerequisite for remission, nor is the mere fact that no harm has been done, of itself, a matter that can be taken into account.[100] Rather, the question is whether, having regard to the taxpayer’s particular circumstances, it is appropriate to remit the penalty in whole or in part.[101]
  3. The Commissioner’s practice in relation to penalties imposed for false or misleading statements is set out in Practice Statement Law Administration PSLA 2012/5, titled ‘Administration of the false or misleading statement penalty – where there is a shortfall amount’ and issued 23 August 2012 (PSLA 2012/5). PSLA 2012/5 is an ‘internal instruction to ATO staff’ in relation to the administration of the penalty, under section 284-75(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA, for making a false or misleading statement that results in a shortfall amount: PSLA 2012/5 at [1].
