Re Lauris Lynette Nicholson and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 385 (30 October 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: LAURIS LYNETTE NICHOLSON

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V87/232

AAT No. 3873

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

Dr D.M. Sutherland (Member)

C.G. Woodard (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - issue of benefit classification certificate - paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia - meaning of "real risk" - risk of sudden death small - possibility of further deterioration necessitating treatment with drugs or surgery - real risk of incapacity resulting from treatment

Words and Phrases: "real risk"

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 3(1), 16(4), 154

Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 ss. 2(1), 6(1)

Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN N93

Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293

Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN N129

HEARING

CANBERRA

30:10:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

The applicant is an eligible employee, as that term is defined in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). She became an eligible employee in 1985 when she was appointed as a permanent employee, that is to say as an officer for the purposes of the Public Service Act 1922. In connection with that appointment she was medically examined to ascertain her fitness for appointment and also to see whether a benefit classification certificate ought to be issued under the provisions of section 16(4) of the Act by the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") or one of his delegates. It was decided to issue one; it was issued specifying "paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia" ("PST") and "history of whiplash injury". On 7 April 1976 the Commissioner informed the applicant that the benefit classification certificate had been issued and sent her a copy. On 18 April 1986 she requested him to reconsider his decision to issue it. In pursuance of section 154(4) of the Act a delegate of the Commissioner reconsidered the decision. He varied it by deleting from it "history of whiplash". The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal pursuant to section 154(6) of the Act for review of the decision as varied.

2. At the hearing of her application she was represented by Mr E. J. Lorkin, of Counsel; the respondent was represented by Mr R. Whithear, the Assistant Commissioner for Superannuation. The documents lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 included copies of the report of the original medical examination of the applicant and of three reports supplied to the Department of Health and the Commissioner by Dr V. M. J. Jelinek, a cardiologist. Also tendered in evidence were copies of the explanatory memorandum which accompanied the Bill for the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 and of extracts from Hansard containing reports of speeches made in the Senate by the Minister for Community Services, Senator Grimes, and in the House of Representatives by the Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs and Minister assisting the Treasurer, the Hon. C. Hurford MHR, when moving the second reading of that Bill.

3. At all material times section 16(4) of the Act has contained provisions applicable to the subject matter of the decision. However, a number of important amendments to it were made by section 6(1) of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986. Although that Act was enacted in June 1986, section 2(1) of it provided that the amendments which it made to section 16 were to be deemed to have come into operation on 1 July 1976. There is, in our view, therefore, no doubt that in deciding the matter before us now we have to apply section 16(4) as amended by the 1986 amending Act. Section 16(4) as so amended reads:-

"16. (4) The Commissioner shall consider the report

or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the

opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical

or mental condition or conditions referred to in the

report or reports, will not continue to be an

eligible employee until the person attains his

maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a

benefit classification certificate to that effect,

being a certificate in which the relevant condition

or conditions is or are specified."

4. The meaning of the expression "real risk" is not stated in the Act; nor was the intention of the draftsman stated in the Explanatory Memorandum for the Bill, beyond a bare reference to the fact that it was the test which the Commissioner had applied, following the Tribunal's decision in Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN N93, in giving effect to the provisions of section 16(4) as they were before amendment. Those provisions were in terms of the Commissioner being of the opinion that a person was "not likely" to continue to be an eligible employee until maximum retiring age. The amendment was made apparently because the decision in Re Linden had not been followed by the Tribunal in Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293.

5. In Re Linden the Tribunal did not state precisely what it meant by "real risk"; it was, of course, not concerned then to construe that phrase, as it was not used in the statute. However, some indication of what it meant can be found by reference to the facts of the case. Mr Linden had been found to be suffering from "labile hypertension". Although, by bringing his weight problem under control, he had already reduced significantly the risk of developing a permanent hypertensive condition, the Tribunal considered that it was still too early to form any confident prognosis that a permanent hypertensive condition would not develop. As a result it formed the opinion that there was a real risk that he would not continue to be an eligible employee to his maximum retiring age.

6. In Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN N129 the Tribunal declined to follow Re Bewley but expressed itself not in terms of a real risk, as it had done in Re Linden, but in terms of "an event which may well happen". That being so, we consider that little help is to be obtained from that decision in the construction of the words "real risk" which now appear in section 16(4).

7. In Re Linden the Tribunal observed that section 16:-

". . . (is) obviously concerned with providing some

protection for the superannuation scheme established

by the Act against claims due to the premature

invalidity or death of a person who at the time when

he becomes an eligible employee is suffering from a

potentially disabling or fatal physical or mental

condition or conditions."

The Tribunal in Re Dakin accepted that that was the purpose of section 16. We similarly have no doubt of that.

8. Both Mr Lorkin and Mr Whithear drew a distinction between a bare possibility and a real risk. We consider that that is a proper distinction to make. We were invited to examine the meanings of the individual words "real" and "risk" given in various dictionaries, and have done so; however, we are satisfied that the mere conjunction of any of the meanings of "real" with any of the meanings of "risk" does not capture the essence of the phrase "real risk", which is a phrase having connotations of its own distinct from the dictionary meanings of the two words comprising it. In the phrase "risk" refers to a chance that something detrimental will occur. There is a "real risk" where a factual situation exists which has inherent in it a particular potential to result in that detrimental occurrence. Usually the phrase is used in relation to the process of making an assessment of whether precautions are warranted to prevent either the occurrence of the detrimental thing or the consequences of it occurring. We are satisfied that in the context of the Act section 16 is intended to provide that a benefit classification certificate should be issued where an eligible employee is suffering from an identified condition which has a particular potential to result in his or her dying, or becoming so incapacitated as to be unable to continue in employment, before reaching maximum retiring age. In our view, if an eligible employee has a permanent medical condition which may directly or through its complications cause premature death or incapacity, rarely, if ever, can an opinion properly be formed that there is not a real risk that by reason of it or for a reason connected with it the employee will die or retire before reaching the maximum retiring age.

9. In the present case the facts are not in dispute. The applicant suffers from the condition PST and has done so for at least the past 11 years. PST is not a disease of the heart in the sense of being a morbid condition of it; it is an abnormality which affects the heart, causing it to beat on occasions at a rate considerably faster than it should. Dr Jelinek said that it was almost certainly an inborn condition and that in a mild form it was not uncommon. In the applicant, however, it is in a severe form. Dr Jelinek said that he had his own method of grading the condition at four levels. At the lowest level the person is aware of an occasional faster heart beat but it does not cause sufficient distress for him to consult a doctor about it. At the second level the condition is still fairly mild but causes the person sufficient concern for him to seek the advice of a doctor. However, at that level no treatment is required. At the third level the condition is more serious. The frequency and length of the episodes of tachycardia are such that the person has to be treated with drugs, either during the course of an episode in order to bring it to an end, or continually in order to reduce the frequency and severity of the episodes. At this level during the episodes the person is temporarily incapacitated to a greater or lesser extent. At the fourth level the severity of the condition is such that surgical intervention or the implantation of an electrical pacemaker for the heart may be required.

10. Dr Jelinek expressed the opinion that the applicant's condition at present is at the third level. For two years she has been taking Digoxin to control it. The dosage was originally 250 micrograms per day but had subsequently to be increased to 500 micrograms. Before that regular maintenance treatment was instituted, Isoptin had been administered to her intravenously on three occasions during the course of episodes of tachycardia, in order to bring the episodes to an end. We accept that at present, and in 1985, the applicant's condition was at the third level. In one of the reports included in the section 37 documents Dr Jelinek stated that he "would describe her condition as being moderately severe at present with the possibility of deteriorating later in life" and that "the most likely cause for this deterioration is advancing years". He gave oral evidence to similar effect. We have no doubt that there is considerably more than a bare possibility that the applicant's condition will reach the fourth level of severity before she has attained her maximum retiring age.

11. In one of his reports in the section 37 documents Dr Jelinek said that there must be a possibility that the applicant's working life could be reduced by her PST "or its complications". Giving oral evidence he said that the PST itself was unlikely to prevent the applicant continuing to work to normal retirement age and that, although theoretically it might cause sudden death by heart failure if an episode of tachycardia continued for several days, in practice no episode would be left to continue for so long. It would be brought to an end by the administration of drugs before it had persisted for a sufficient time to endanger life. We accept that and find that the chance of the applicant dying directly as the result of PST is so small as not to amount to a real risk.

12. However, Dr Jelinek was less reassuring about the possible results of "complications" of PST. Asked what he had meant when he had used that expression in his report, he said that he had been referring to complications resulting from surgical or other intervention needed where the condition deteriorated to the fourth level. Cardiologists, he said, tried to avoid such intervention; before resorting to it they often prescribed drugs more potent than those prescribed where the condition was at the third level. Such drugs could have side-effects, including damage to the nerves, liver, lungs, skin and blood. After a pacemaker was fitted, the output of the patient's heart was sometimes not as good as it had been before. Where surgery was performed, the scar in the chest could become infected. He regarded all of those possibilities as unlikely to occur, but that nevertheless there was a chance that any one of them might do so. If it did, it could result in incapacity, necessitating premature retirement. However, because of the applicant's personality, they were, in his view, all unlikely to occur and, even when they were all taken together, they were still unlikely to do so.

13. In assessing the magnitude of the chance of any of the possible complications referred to by Dr Jelinek occurring if the applicant's PST deteriorates, we regard it as significant that cardiologists prescribe potent drugs capable of causing serious side-effects before resorting to surgery or the implantation of a pacemaker. Having had regard to all the evidence, we have come to the conclusion that there is a real risk of the applicant's retiring before attaining her maximum retiring age by reason of PST or for a reason connected with it. In coming to that conclusion we have paid particular regard to the fact that the condition is permanent and that it cannot improve but may well deteriorate. Because it may well deteriorate and more drastic treatment of it will then be required, we find that there is a real risk that she will become so incapacitated that she will not be able to continue to be an eligible employee until she attains her maximum retiring age.

14. Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed.
