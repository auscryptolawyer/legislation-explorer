REASONS FOR DECISION



14 January 1991 Mr B J McMahon (Deputy President)



1. The applicant is an Australian subsidiary of a foreign company. It imports goods manufactured by its parent and sells them by retail in 3 stores which are largely patronised by overseas tourists. The shops are now run as duty free outlets, but at the time, transacted normal retail business.



2. At all relevant times, the applicant was not a registered person pursuant to the provisions of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930, nor was it eligible to seek such registration. Accordingly, sales tax was paid on the goods on their entry for home consumption in accordance with the provisions of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 5) 1930. The goods were then distributed to the various retail stores. The applicant alleged that approximately 95 per cent of them were then sold by retail to non residents who subsequently exported them from Australia.



3. An application for refund of sales tax was made by the applicant. The grounds for the application were stated as follows -



"The goods have been sold to overseas nationals who have exported the goods and exemption has been claimed pursuant to Item 110 in the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act."



4. The item referred to is -



"Goods exported or to be exported or goods sold by any person for export by the purchaser from him."



The item thus is divided into 3 parts. Goods come within the terms of the item if they are exported, or if they are to be exported, or if they are sold by any person for export by the purchaser.



5. The application for refund was disallowed for the following stated reasons -



"1. there is no provision in the sales tax law which authorises a refund to [the applicant];



2. there has been no overpayment;



3. there is insufficient evidence that the goods sold were exported; and



4. at the time of sale, the goods do not qualify for exemption under Item 110 of the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935".



6. The applicant then lodged a formal objection on 4 stated grounds -



"A. During the relevant period, the taxpayer sold goods in respect of which sales tax had been paid in the amount of [sum set out];



B. Those goods subsequently became exempt from sales tax pursuant to Item 110 in the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act;



C. As a consequence sales tax has been overpaid in respect of those goods within the meaning of section 11(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 5); and

D. Pursuant to section 11(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 5) the taxpayer is entitled to a refund of sales tax overpaid."



7. The formal objection then went on to list a number of allegations of fact and submissions of law. In my view, none of the supplementary material extends or diminishes the extent of the formal grounds of objection. It consists only of material intended to persuade the assessing officer that the grounds are supported by the circumstances alleged.



8. The objection was disallowed on the following stated grounds -



"1. Section 11(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act No 5) is not considered to authorise a refund in the present circumstances;



2. There has not been any overpayment of sales tax. Sales tax was payable at the time of importation of the goods and was correctly paid at this time. It is not accepted that where a subsequent event has the effect of exempting an item that has had sales tax paid on it that this payment will constitute an overpayment;



3. Goods must remain "goods" as defined in sub-section 3(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1). Any goods which have gone into use or consumption in Australia after the retailing process has been completed no longer satisfy the requirements of that first definition; and consequently do not satisfy Item 110 of the First Schedule Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act."



9. This application is brought to review that objection decision. Similar decisions were made in both the applications before this Tribunal. The only differences relate to relevant periods over which sales tax was paid by the applicant. The result of one application will therefore determine the result of both applications.



10. Apart from preliminary conferences, there were 2 directions hearings, the second being on 31 October 1990. Pursuant to formal directions given on that day, the applicant filed and served a "revised statement of facts and contentions". The principal contentions were as follows -



"1. The Applicant contends that the relevant goods were, at the time of importation, exempt from sales tax pursuant to Item 110 of the First Schedule to the Sales tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act as goods "to be exported" by purchasers from the Applicant, or as goods sold by the Applicant for export by the purchasers from it, on the basis that either:-



(a) statistical evidence that more than 95% of goods sold by the Applicant were subsequently exported was a relevant matter able to be taken into account in determining, at the time of importation, that a proportion of the goods imported were goods which were "to be exported", or were goods which would be sold by the Applicant for export by the purchasers from it, within the meaning of item 110; or



(b) alternatively, the fact of export of goods sold by the Applicant was a relevant matter able, subsequent to importation, to be taken into account in determining whether those goods were, at the time of importation, goods "to be exported", or were goods which would be sold by the Applicant for export by the purchasers from it, within the meaning of item 110.



2. The Applicant contends that, as the relevant goods were exempt pursuant to item 110 at the time of importation, sales tax paid pursuant to the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 5) upon importation of those goods was "overpaid" within the meaning of section 11(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 5).



3. Alternatively the Applicant contends that even if the relevant goods were not, at the time of importation, goods "to be exported", or goods which would be sold by the Applicant for export by the purchasers from it, within the meaning of item 110:-



(a) upon subsequently being exported the relevant goods became exempt pursuant to item 110 as goods "exported" within the meaning of that item;



(b) upon the goods becoming exempt the sales tax paid on importation became sales tax "overpaid" within the meaning of s 11(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 5)."



11. Apart from putting factual matters in issue, the respondent contended "that the issues as presently formulated by the applicant are [not] issues which on a proper reading of the notice of objection are able to be raised." The point of this argument was that the formal objection alleged that the goods subsequently became exempt sometime after importation, whereas one of the contentions put forward by the applicant was that the goods were exempt upon importation. The supposed inadequacy of the objection was not raised at any of the prior directions hearings. The applicant was not made aware of the proposed submission until the respondent's response was filed on 19 November, some 4 weeks before the hearing of this application.

12. Section 42E(a) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) provides that on a review before this Tribunal, the objector is, unless the Tribunal otherwise orders, limited to the grounds stated in the objection. The statutory provision is identical with that to be found in sub-section 190(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act which has been considered in a number of cases. On 30 November 1990, the applicant's solicitors wrote to the respondent's solicitors noting the proposed argument as to a proper reading of the notice of objection. The letter continued-



"We do not agree with the assertion that the Notices of Objection do not permit the Applicant to raise the issue of exemption at the time of importation, as distinct from exemption arising at a later point in time.



Nevertheless, should you propose to take that point at the hearing we wish to advise that we will, if necessary, make application to the Tribunal that the Applicant be permitted to rely upon the following additional ground of objection:-



"That the relevant goods were, at the time of importation, exempt from sales tax pursuant to Item 110 of the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act as goods "to be exported" by purchasers from the Applicant".



13. On the hearing, counsel for the applicant confirmed his submission that an amendment was not necessary in order to be able to argue the first limb of Item 110. Nevertheless, for greater precaution, he made application for leave to amend the objection in accordance with the prior notice he had given.



14. The principles upon which this Tribunal should exercise its discretion in allowing any such amendment were set out in Case W65 89 ATC 590 particularly at paragraphs 47 and 48 -



"I consider that on the whole, the purpose behind the Income Tax Assessment Act appears to be that an assessment once made is final until challenged either by objection or an amended assessment is sought, provided they are both made within the time constraints that apply to either of those two avenues. I consider that the scheme of the ITAA seems to be to keep the two separate. The situation which prevailed prior to the amendment to sec. 190(a) was one of inflexibly holding a taxpayer to their original grounds of objection. The amendment viewed in that light and in the context of the Income Tax Assessment Act itself

suggests that the distinction between sec. 170 and 190(a) has not varied and that the discretion to allow new grounds should not be allowed to amount to an amendment of the relief sought itself.



It follows that by attempting to increase the amounts on which the assessment was made one is not objecting to the assessment but one is actually seeking to have the assessment varied or, more properly expressed, amended. To that extent a taxpayer would be seeking to enlarge the grounds for relief and therefore should not be allowed to rely upon sec. 190(a)."



15. The case was a decision of Hartigan J, a former President of this Tribunal and, as such, must carry authority. It has been followed on a number of occasions in this Tribunal. I refer, by way of example, to a decision of Senior Member Roach in Case X2 reported at 90 ATC 105 and a more recent decision of Senior Member Grbich in Case X88 reported at 90 ATC 640. The principles applied in these cases make it clear that the discretion ought not to be exercised in the applicant's favour where the only purpose of the amendment is to introduce a new ground. To do so would amount to a back door grant of an extension of time and would defeat the object of the Act in having all relevant material before the primary decision maker. There can, however, be no objection to a benign exercise of discretion if all that is sought is an opportunity to elaborate the original ground.



16. In my view, nothing that was said in the joint judgment of Lockhart, Beaumont and Einfeld JJ in Allsene Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 5333 at 5348 contradicts this proposition. As in that case, I consider that the point sought to be raised by the present applicant is covered by the grounds of objection on a liberal reading of them. Throughout the whole of the preceding negotiations, the applicant has consistently claimed exemption under Item 110. No distinction has been made between its component parts. The formal objection, in my view, does not cut back this contention. If it is necessary to deal separately with each of the three limbs of Item 110, then the applicant should be permitted to do so in the interest of justice. There is no suggestion of any prejudice to the respondent. However, like their Honours, I feel that for more abundant caution, I should grant leave to amend the objection in accordance with the proposal quoted above. In my view, this will not amount to the introduction of a new ground which, in turn, will amount to an amendment of the relief sought. I will therefore proceed in these reasons on the basis that it is open to the applicant to argue the whole, or any part, of Item 110 as being applicable to support its application for a refund.

18. It is alleged by the applicant that the overwhelming majority of the goods were sold by retail sales to Japanese tourists who returned with them, unopened, to Japan. The applicant, of course, bears the onus of proof in establishing his claim to the exemption provided by Item 110. How is this allegation to be proved? Clearly the best evidence would be from each of the thousands of Japanese tourists who have since returned home. It would be, however, forensically impractical to attempt to establish the facts through evidence of this nature.



19. Section 33(1)(c) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 provides that this Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence but may inform itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate. There is no reason why the Tribunal cannot accept evidence of a hearsay nature (Re Hancock and Department of Resources and Energy 10 ALN N65) provided that it is of reasonably probative value.



20. The applicant tendered evidence by way of affidavit detailing training and practice of its sales staff and their experience with customers, accounting evidence, establishing the method of calculating the amount claimed by way of refund, and evidence concerning the company's practices in obtaining certificates from purchasers and in analysing sales information. All of this evidence was objected to on the grounds of relevance. In my view, it is both admissible and relevant in the present circumstances. A similar problem arose in characterising millions of spoons, serviettes and refresher towels by reason of the nature of their use after they had been acquired in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Kentucky Fried Chicken Pty Ltd 88 ATC 4363. The court considered that a statistical approach was a valid mechanism for identifying goods which subsequently become exempt. At page 4374 Hope JA said -



"..... How is effect to be given to this indication of legislative intention where exemption or liability depends upon purpose as to user or dealing and/or actual future use or dealing. In my opinion the answer to this question is the same as the answer which would arise if the purpose for which all the goods were acquired and the use of and manner of dealing in respect of all the goods would bring them within the terms of item 91(1). It is relevant and material that Kentucky intends to use or deal with a significant part of each of the kinds of goods in a way which will bring them within the terms of the exemption, and that, effect being given to this purpose or intention, particular goods are in fact used or dealt with in a way which satisfies the conditions for exemption. As I have indicated, the provisions of reg. 14(2) clearly contemplate a flexibility in the application of the legislation in cases such as the present where an intention as to user or dealing which would attract exemption relates to an undifferentiated portion of a larger amount of goods. Such an approach is implicit in sec. 3(5) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act. In my view evidence as to the intention and purpose of Kentucky in relation to some undifferentiated part of the goods which it purchases and the subsequent actual use of an identified part of those goods is both relevant and admissible to establish its claim for exemption. The Commissioner could no doubt act upon the basis of the statistics as to user, but I do not think that Kentucky or the other defendants could require him to do so."



21. Evidence was given by way of affidavit and orally by one of the applicant's store managers. She had been employed as a trainee manager for 3 months before her appointment. She had managed 2 of the applicant's stores since then. Her own observations were that a very high proportion of the applicant's customers were Japanese tourists travelling on Japanese passports. Part of her job was to keep statistics and to report to head office the proportions in which sales were made to customers of various nationalities. From these reports, a schedule was prepared. Sample reports show an occasional Australian. The overwhelming majority were Japanese. As part of her training the witness (who, although Australian, speaks Japanese) showed a price list to proposed purchasers divided into 3 columns. The first showed the price of the particular goods in Yen, being the cost of the items in Japan. The second showed the domestic price in Australian dollars. The third showed a local price free of sales tax. The third price was available only to persons who were travelling overseas, where they could show proof that they were leaving Australia. This meant that any purchaser wishing to buy at the third column price was obliged to show his passport and airline ticket. He was also obliged to complete a form referred to as X3.



22. This form is headed "Certificate of Export" and states that it is to be completed by a purchaser departing by air, acquiring goods from a vendor holding tax free stock. It is addressed to the Commissioner of Taxation and to the Commonwealth of Australia. The form of the certificate is contained in paragraph 498 of a publication entitled Australian Sales Tax published in 1984 by Australian Government Publishing Service, presumably on behalf of the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Green Book"). In that publication, the certificate is said to be accepted as evidence of compliance with exemption requirements when dealing with a registered person holding tax free stocks of goods. Relevant paragraphs from the Green Book were tendered as Exhibit C. It is clear that, at least in the case of sales by registered persons, the respondent accepts certificates in this form as evidence of the matters set out in them.



23. The certificate sets out the name of the buyer, refers to the invoice, certifies that the goods are to be exported from Australia and claims exemption under Item 110. The name, home address, passport number, airline ticket number, aircraft flight number and date of departure are all recorded and the document is signed by the purchaser.



24. The witness confirmed the sales procedures taught to her and in turn taught by her to her staff. These procedures required that the purchaser should himself complete the answers, subject to any assistance that might be required in relation to travel details. The customer however was to fill in his own name and address and sign the form. If he did not have his airline ticket number, he was invited to use his tour number details. However the manager gave evidence (which I accept) that instructions were that tour number details were to be verified by comparing it with the customer's passport. If the customer had an open airline ticket which did not have details of a flight out of Australia, employees were not to make a tax free sale to that customer. The employee was bound to be satisfied that a customer would be taking the goods out of the country before a tax free sale could be made.



25. At the end of each day, the manager prepared a document called the daily sales record, which contained the following information in a schedule format -



Invoice number, Stock code, Descriptionquantity, Unit cost, Retail price (domestic), Retail price (tax free), Sex and Age group of customers, Nationality and product category.



26. From these basic statistics, analyses were prepared and were elaborated upon by other witnesses. Other analyses were prepared from information contained in the X3 Forms.



27. The manager, speaking from her own experience, said that many of the customers were honeymooners. She knew this from talking to them and from congratulating them. In all cases the items sold were wrapped in distinctive paper as part of the applicant's policy. The manager referred to the process as gift wrapping. Her understanding was that these goods were to be taken home by customers as gifts for their friends and family. She knew this from talking widely to the various customers. The majority of goods sold were small and could not be used (for example) for carrying other items on to the aircraft.



28. The manager was cross-examined but there was no substantial departure from her evidence in chief.



29. Evidence was given, by way of affidavits, by a partner in a large chartered accountancy firm which kept stock and sales tax records for the applicant in its private business services division. The affidavits set out the manner in which the quantum of claims for refunds was calculated. It is based on the "first in first out" method of stock control. Individual items of stock are not marked or ticketed. The evidence was that this method of stock control and valuation is an accepted accounting practice. Details of all product shipments and sale tax paid per product category for those shipments were maintained by the witness' firm in respect of the entire period of the refund claims concerned. Using a computer program, a moving average sales tax figure for each product category was calculated and was used to calculate the amount of sales tax refund claimed by the applicant. The deponent was not required to attend for cross- examination and no attempt was made to dispute this evidence. Indeed the respondent's response to the applicant's revised statement of facts and contentions concedes the amounts claimed, if eligibility can be established.



30. Evidence was given by way of affidavits and orally by the applicant's finance director. He particularised the amount of sales tax that had been paid. This was not in dispute. He gave evidence that the applicant's marketing experience of the preference by Japanese tourists for its goods reflected similar preferences experienced by sister companies elsewhere in the world. He confirmed the instructions to sales staff deposed to by the manager and referred to the primary lists prepared by the 2 previous witnesses. An analysis of the returns showed that for the whole of the relevant period, 93 per cent of the applicant's sales had been made to Japanese tourists, 2 per cent to United States and "other" tourists and 5 per cent to Australian customers. Primary documents were produced to support the basis of these calculations and these, no doubt, were subject to check analysis by officers of the respondent. More detailed analyses of sales were available, broken down into different categories on a store by store basis. These reports also included details of the age groups into which purchases fell, product categories sold and the sex of the customers. An analysis of these reports and of the X3 certificates was prepared.



31. It is impractical to reproduce all the accounting and statistical evidence that was put before me. A random sample selection (annexure G in the third set of affidavits) will suffice to illustrate the inferences capable of being drawn from the primary information. In the month of July 1987 the value of goods sold in the applicant's Sydney store was set out from 366 transactions. None of these was a sale to an Australian resident. Noting the intended date of departure, figures have been compiled from various store returns showing the average number of days difference between the date of purchase and the date of intended departure. In that month in Sydney, it was 2.27. Similar figures were provided for the applicant's Melbourne store. The average number of days difference between date of purchase and date of intended departure in that case was 2.75. The longest interval was at the applicant's Surfer's Paradise store, namely 3.427 days. These short intervals reinforce the hearsay evidence that the goods were purchased at the end of the buyers' holidays with the intention of taking them back to the buyers' homes.



32. The finance director was required for cross-examination. He confirmed that the applicant now no longer sells goods exclusive of sales tax in the manner he described. It now operates duty free stores. Although they have similar procedures, he believes that the method of selling duty free is more advantageous to the applicant than the old method. Otherwise, as he put it, they would not have made the change. He did not agree, therefore, and I accept his opinion, that there was a commercial advantage in the old system compared with the new. Under the duty free arrangement, the retailer notifies a trade association, known as the Duty Free Traders Association of the name and departure time of each of its customers. Employees of the association "plucked the tickets" of buyers at the airport. If the purchaser fails to produce his goods and have his ticket plucked, the applicant would be charged with the appropriate amount of duty.



33. There was no other evidence given by the finance director in cross-examination which in any way derogated from the strength of his evidence in chief. The Commissioner called no evidence in reply. The totality of the documentary evidence shows meticulous attention to detail, unchallenged analyses of primary records and a systematic statistical approach to proof. Inferences based on it are not mere guesses or anecdotal generalities.



34. In the light of this evidence, and in the absence of any evidence whatsoever to the contrary, and upon the authority of Kentucky Fried Chicken as to the relevance and admissibility of such evidence and the method of presenting such evidence, I find as a fact, that 95 per cent of the value of the goods upon which sales tax had already been paid during the relevant period, have been exported or alternatively (with the benefit of hindsight) were to be exported or alternatively were sold to another person for export by that person. Compliance with the "to be exported" criterion at the time of entry for home consumption can also be demonstrated in a way that does not depend on hindsight and retrospect effect. From past experience, we know that more than 500 people will be killed in road accidents this year, although we can not as yet identify the victims or particularise the circumstances. In the same way, past experience indicates a conclusion that at the time of importation, 95 per cent of the value of the applicant's goods were destined to be exported, even though at that stage one could not identify the particular items of stock or the buyers. This finding of facts however will not conclude the matter.



35. As to the first limb of Item 110, it might be said (if the respondent's submissions were correct) that the exemption could never be available at the time of entry for home consumption as far as the applicant is concerned, because one could not say at the time of importation that the goods fell within that limb. In response to that argument, however, it was pointed out by the respondent that Regulation 58 provides the machinery for the benefit of an exemption to be passed on when the exempting circumstances subsequently arise. It is true that it could not have been used in the present case, because sales tax was not passed on to the Japanese buyers and the exemption application would have to come from those buyers. It was submitted however that the procedure was available and that this was the proper procedure that ought to have been adopted.



36. The role of the Regulations has been referred to on a number of occasions. The starting point is always the dictum of Dixon J in Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation for the State of South Australia v Ellis and Clark Limited [1934] HCA 54; (1934) 52 CLR 85 at 89 where he said -



"Moreover, the legislation depends in a remarkable degree upon the regulations made under the power which it confers on the Executive. Without the regulations, not only is it unworkable, but the expression of legislative policy is so inadequate as almost to be unintelligible."



37. This is not to say, however, that regulations can amend or alter the meaning of an Act of Parliament. Nowhere in his consideration of the legislative scheme did His Honour propound this. In Brayson Motors Pty Limited (in Liquidation) v The Commissioner of Taxation [1985] HCA 20; (1985) 156 CLR 651 at 652 both Deane J and Mason J, in argument, referred to the nature of the same regulations. As Mason J said : "One looks at regulations, not to construe an overall scheme or to throw light on ambiguity in a statutory provision, but to ascertain what the scheme is". Whatever its importance in the scheme of assessment and collection of sales tax, Regulation 58 remains a regulation made pursuant to power conferred by an Act of Parliament. In "The American Commonwealth", Bryce expressed its characteristics figuratively : "A stream can not rise above its source" (referred to indirectly by Dixon J in Shrimpton v The Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 CLR 613 at 630).



38. The Commissioner contended that the only mechanism to obtain a refund was to be found in Regulation 58. I am unable, however, to find any words in the Regulation or elsewhere which supports this proposition. In its terms, the Regulation does not purport to cover the field to the exclusion of any other available remedy. In any event, it prescribes only a mechanism for recovery and does not confer any substantive right. The Green Book (he submitted) draws a distinction which is appropriate for tax free goods exempt at the time of entry for home consumption, by restricting the procedures there available to registered persons. I also find this argument difficult to accept. How can a regulation limit the operation of any part of an Act? Item 110 is in a schedule to an Act of Parliament and must be construed in accordance with the ordinary rules of statutory construction.



39. One of those rules requires one to adopt a construction which will promote the purpose or object underlying the Act for preference. Furthermore, in considering whether particular goods fall within an exemption item, a court should (where possible) read the legislation in favour of the taxpayer (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Nimrod Theatre Company Limited 85 ATC 4092 and Hygienic Lily Limited v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4327). A passage from the judgment of Bowen CJ and Ellicott J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Top of the Cross Pty Limited and Travel Holdings (Australia) Pty Limited 81 ATC 4563 at 4571 illustrates the purposive approach that is required -



".... The general purpose of taxation legislation is to raise revenue. This enactment in sec 62A is directed to relieving particular taxpayers from the burden of taxation. Its purpose is not to raise revenue but rather to encourage taxpayers to enter into such franchise arrangements with the governments or authorities mentioned and to incur expenditure upon an undertaking which will inure ultimately for the benefit of the particular government or authority. We mention these general considerations because, as will appear from the discussion which follows, there are some ambiguities in the section. In choosing between possible interpretations, a construction which will promote the purpose or object underlying the Act is to be preferred (Acts Interpretation Act 1901, sec 15AA)...."



40. In its terms, the purpose of Item 110 is to exempt from sales tax goods that are to be sent out of the country rather than be consumed domestically. I have been unable to find the historical basis for the creation of this item. However I take it to be self evident that it is concerned to promote exports. It is notorious that goods exported have a beneficial effect in balancing Australia's import account and it is equally notorious that this is a result desired by governments of all persuasions at all times. The beneficial economic effects of exports are obtained by individual dealings with tourists as much as, say, from the sale and transport of commodities. Goods that are, in effect, merely passing through Australia should not, in principle, be subject to sales tax. Item 110 therefore should be interpreted in such a way as to promote its evident purpose and in such a way that, where possible, it favours the taxpayer. In Kentucky Fried Chicken at page 4370 Hope JA said : "However as it seems to me the purposes of this type of legislation, and relevantly for the purposes of the sales tax legislation, are often better served by looking at the substance and reality of the matter".



41. As in that case, there are in the present case "additional circumstances which will exist in the future upon which inclusion within the exemption depends .... As it seems to me all these qualifying conditions will not exist at the time when the container is purchased; they will come into existence or will occur later". (page 4371). There is also in the present case what Hope JA referred to as "the element of futurity which can be involved in the application of the legislation in relation to exemptions" (page 4372).



42. In my view, once these exempting circumstances come into existence, then the item operates ex post facto. Thus if the goods are subsequently exported in accordance with normal statistical procedures and expectations, it is valid to say that at the time they were entered for home consumption, they were in fact "to be exported". Regulation 58 recognises that exempting circumstances can subsequently arise.



43. It is true that Kentucky Fried Chicken was concerned with a different exemption and with different circumstances. However the language of the judgment is so broad as to be relevant in its application to the present circumstances and, being a judgment of the Court of Appeal, constitutes authority which I should follow.



44. Because of the retrospective effect of subsequent circumstances, the argument that the goods are no longer "goods" as defined by s 3 of the No 1 Act must fail. The respondent submitted that s 3 had the effect of excluding goods that had been retailed. The mere act of retailing, it was submitted, meant that the goods ceased to be goods for the purposes of the statute and that when they were exported they were therefore no longer subject to any item of exemption. It was submitted that the mere signing of undertakings would not assist the applicant. In my view, this submission cannot succeed. The later sale to an end user operates retrospectively to exempt the goods at the point of entry, as if the use had been determined at that stage. One looks back with the benefit of hindsight to determine whether the exemption applies. This would occur notionally in point of time before the retail sale to the end user. If one adopts the historical experience line of reasoning, it is not even necessary to look for retrospective effects. It is true that Regulation 58 does not give a general right of recovery to retailers. It vests the right to a refund in end users. However in my view and for reasons to which I have referred, it cannot claim exclusivity, or override the terms of the legislation and exclude the applicant from the exemption to which it is entitled.



45. It was agreed between counsel that the question whether or not relevant sales tax had been passed on to the Japanese buyers (for the purposes of s 11(1A) of the No 5 Act) should not be dealt with at the hearing. It was thought possible to settle this, either by negotiation or through a subsequent hearing in the absence of agreement. I was therefore invited to stand the matter over after giving my reasons. I have considered whether I should follow this course and have decided that it would not be fair to either party, in case it is desired to test my reasons. For this purpose, I should provide a decision so that, if necessary, proceedings may be taken under s 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act. I think the proper order I should make is to set aside the objection decisions under review and to remit the matters to the respondent with the direction that at all relevant times the applicant was entitled to the exemptions claimed, subject to satisfying the respondent that sales tax in each of the relevant transactions was not passed on to the end user. I will grant liberty to apply, restricted to the determination of that issue.
