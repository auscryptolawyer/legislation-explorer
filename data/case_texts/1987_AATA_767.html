ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - s 51(1) - deductibility of legal expenses - s 64A - legal expenses incurred by taxpayer in litigation for recovery of loan by Swiss Bank - loan granted for purpose of financing construction of building - whether a contract existed for construction of the building - whether the loan moneys were used for approved purposes - whether the expenditure on the litigation was necessarily incurred in the carrying on of the taxpayer's business for the purpose of gaining assessable income.

REASONS FOR DECISION

2 September 1987 Deputy President C J Bannon PC

This review concerns the deductibility or otherwise of legal expenses from the assessable income of the taxpayer for the years of income ended 30th June 1979 and 30th June 1980 pursuant to section 51(1) or section 64A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.) ("the Act").

The taxpayer is a private company incorporated in the State of New South Wales. In its income tax returns for the years ending 30th June 1971 to 30th June 1979 inclusive (part of Exhibit 2), the nature of its business was described as "Electrical Contracting and Manufacturing (Administration)". In its income tax returns for the years ending 30th June 1980 to 30th June 1982 (part of Exhibit 2), the description of the nature of its business was changed to "Investment Company". In order to preserve its anonymity as far as possible I shall refer to the taxpayer's managing director as Mr Gulliver.

The shares in the taxpayer all belong to Mr Gulliver and members of his family. Mr Gulliver is a director or managing director of a number of companies incorporated in Australia. He gave evidence that the taxpayer had acted as a banker and moneylender to the other companies of which he is a director and/or shareholder, which will be referred to compendiously as "the Gulliver Group". The taxpayer held moneylenders licences in the State of New South Wales under the Money-lenders and Infants Loans Act 1941 of that State for the years ending 31st March 1970, 31st March 1974 and 31st March 1985 (Exhibit LL).

The Gulliver Group of companies have similar shareholdings but are not related companies. There is a further company which forms part of the Gulliver Group which is incorporated in the Crown Colony of Hong Kong. Neither Mr Gulliver nor members of his family are shareholders of that company which will be referred to herein as "the Operator". The shareholding in the Operator is held by two persons in Hong Kong, who hold it in trust for persons in Liechenstein who hold it in trust ultimately for Mr Gulliver's family.

The Middle East

One or more companies in the Gulliver Group have engaged in the past in building construction work in countries in South East Asia. Through some Americans, Mr Gulliver was given a contact with a sheik in one of the oil rich Middle East countries. He sent a family relative there and one of the Gulliver Group obtained a contract to build a laboratory for the sheik. Other work was also done in the Middle East country by one or more companies in the Gulliver group. The taxpayer however did not engage in contract work. Mr Gulliver then obtained an introduction to a Prince in the Middle East country from the Prince's agent. According to Mr Gulliver the Prince asked him if he would be interested in financing a business and I shopping centre complex the Prince was proposing to build near the sea. The Prince stated he had a builder and a fixed price for the building.

The Prince also had an architect who lived on an island in the Meditteranean. According to Mr Gulliver the architect had prepared plans for the building complex which were little more than sketches (transcript p 103 and p 116). The fixed price builder had provided plans and a specification for the building complex which became Exhibits BB3 and BB2. Mr Gulliver said those plans were not much better than sketches. The builder had already sunk concrete and steel foundations for the building complex before Mr Gulliver came upon the scene. According to Mr Gulliver they were rusted and needed to be reinforced or replaced (transcript p. 114). The taxpayer later engaged an Australian company, which will be referred to herein as "the Engineering firm", to check the foundations and received a proposal from the Engineering firm on 30th May 1974 (Exhibit CC).

The Prince's agent sent the specification for the commercial centre to the taxpayer under cover of a letter dated 12th March 1973 (Exhibit R). The letter expressed the hope that the taxpayer would proceed to negotiate for the loan in Geneva. On 17th July 1973 the taxpayer wrote to the Prince, care of his agent, a letter (Exhibit PP) setting out the revised terms of the proposed loan agreement for the commercial centre project as discussed during Mr Gulliver's meeting with the Prince. It appears from such document that the taxpayer required detailed plans and specifications to be prepared covering all aspects of construction and also a priced bill of quantities. Advances out of the loan were to be made by the taxpayer to the builder on behalf of the Prince against progress claim certificates approved by the Prince's Clerk of Works. The completed commercial centre was to be managed by the taxpayer which would handle all lettings and collect all rents until its loan to the Prince was repaid. The loan was to be repaid over five years. The money was to be borrowed by the taxpayer in Swiss francs in order to make its advances to the Prince. The Prince was to compensate the taxpayer for any exchange loss suffered by the taxpayer in loan repayments by the Prince if the value of the currency in the Prince's country fell in relation to the Swiss franc. The taxpayer also required that a bank in the Prince's country gave an unconditional bank guarantee as security for the loan by the taxpayer. Mr Gulliver commenced negotiations on behalf of the taxpayer with the bankers of Zurich to raise a loan somewhere about this time.

The taxpayer followed up its proposal of 17th July 1973, with a letter dated 18th July 1973 (Exhibit S) which suggested that it be given "total and undivided responsibility for the final design and construction of the building based on the preliminary designs already prepared" and be "in complete financial control during the course of construction and for the duration of the Head Lease" which the taxpayer proposed be granted to it to recoup the amount of the loan (Exhibit S). A reply dated 5th August 1973 from the Prince's agent (Exhibit T) appeared to confirm the offer in Exhibit S, although it speaks of it as an offer valid for 60 days. However it became clear from a further letter dated 22nd November 1973 from the Prince's agent to the taxpayer (part of Exhibit G) that the zenith of consensus between the parties was as set out in that document, which was acknowledged and accepted by Mr Gulliver on the part of the taxpayer on a copy which also forms part of Exhibit G.

Omitting formal parts that letter reads as follows (with names omitted or substituted):-

"This is to confirm our meeting of today which took place in our Office and the discussions outlined in connection with the issuance of the Bank guarantee by the (Middle East Bank) at the request of ( )

Wherefrom, the following points were discussed and agreed upon:-

1. (Taxpayer) is to confirm in writing to the Swiss Bank Corporation in Zurich that the amount of the loan will be used entirely for construction of the building of ( ). Acknowledgment of the said confirmation should be obtained by the Swiss Bank Corporation.

  1. A Performance bond in the amount of 10% (ten percent) of the total value of the loan will be issued in favour of ( ) with a validity covering the period of construction of the building complex. The said performance bond will be provided by (taxpayer) within three weeks from the date of signature of the contract.

    1. Bill of Quantities.

(Taxpayer) representative will submit statements of account for payment which will be released or rejected within fourteen days from date of submission.

Your acknowledgment and acceptance of this letter require your signature on the duplicate copy to enable us act accordingly."

A letter of the same date (Exhibit V) addressed to Swiss Bank Corporation was written on behalf of the Middle East Bank, guaranteeing jointly and severally with the taxpayer, the full amount of a loan of Swiss francs 22,800,000. The taxpayer had already sought a loan of Swiss francs 19,000,000 from the Swiss Bank Corporation and by a letter written from Zurich on 4th September 1973 (part of Exhibit U) the Swiss Bank Corporation had confirmed the availability of the loan to be utilised in two drawdowns, the first of Swiss francs 9,500,000 to be made before 15th October 1973 and the second of the same amount to be made during October 1975. This was the subject of a further letter dated 15th November 1973 (part of Exhibit U) from the Swiss Bank Corporation. The documents detailed were followed by a further important communication dated 24th November 1973 from the taxpayer to Swiss Bank Corporation (Exhibit W) which, omitting matters of identification was as follows:-

"His Royal Highness ( ) has requested that we write to you and confirm that the funds made available to (the taxpayer) under the guarantee issued by the (Middle East Bank) are to be used for the construction of a building for His Royal Highness in (Middle East town).

His Royal Highness would appreciate if you would acknowledge receipt of this letter by signing a duplicate copy of this letter. The duplicate will be returned to (Middle East town) for inclusion in His Royal Highness's files."

The first drawdown on the Swiss loan went ahead as per letter from Swiss Bank Corporation to taxpayer dated 3rd December 1973 (Exhibit Y). However finalisation of building arrangements for the Prince's commercial centre did not proceed. A letter which Mr Gulliver described as setting out the minutes of a meeting with the Prince and his agent was written by the taxpayer to the Prince's agent dated 23rd February 1974 (Exhibit AA) and omitting formalities and identification reads as follows:-

"• • •

Points of Agreement

1. Contractual;- (Taxpayer) to proceed forthwith with the construction of the building subject to the following terms and conditions being fulfilled.

a) Signing of contract agreement which is now being redrafted by the lawyer of the (Middle East Bank). The redrafted agreement is to be based on the contract prepared and submitted to H.R.H. for his approval by (taxpayer). However the new agreement will include a clause holding (taxpayer) responsible for the completion of the building on a turnkey basis within a nominated period and for a sum not exceeding 19 Million Swiss Francs.

b) (Taxpayer) is now only required by HRH to arrange for the issue of a building construction performance guarantee equal to 10% of the value of the building contract. This guarantee to be provided within twenty-one days after the signing of the contract agreement for the construction of the building.

It was noted however that additional foundations would be required for the site area on which the proposed twenty storey building was to be built. It was agreed despite these assurances by

H.R.H. agreed that (taxpayer) could if they so desired request payment for the cost of the building as it was built or alternatively could take a head lease of building as previously discussed and recover payment from the cost of rents received.

h) H.R.H. confirmed that area surrounding building site would be filled in and the sewrage (sic) system extended so that present odours and swamp would disappear prior to the completion of the building, otherwise H.R.H. would assume responsibility for letting of the building.

  1. (Taxpayer) requested to provide airial (sic) photograph of the complete city (Middle East Town) for H.R.H.
  2. Iff (sic) possible H.R.H. requested that all formalities in relation to the contract be finalised promptly and the contract signed before (Mr Gulliver) leaves (Middle East Town) next week.

c) H.R.H. promised to use His influence in expediting the drawings required from his building consultant, and produce the drawings at the earliest possible date.

d) H.R.H. expressed His confidence in (taxpayer's) ability to perform this contract to H.R.H. complete satisfaction. (Mr Gulliver) on behalf of (taxpayer) thanked H.R.H. for his kind words and assured H.R.H. his trust would not be misplaced."

No contract agreement as envisaged was ever signed. On 30th October 1974 the taxpayer wrote to the Prince's attorneys a letter (Exhibit DD) which reads as follows:-

"The proposals put forward by your goodselves are no where in line with what was agreed between His Royal Highness and his agent ( ) and ourselves. Most of the significant clauses in your draft proposals are diametrically opposed to the contract we submitted to His Royal Highness based on the exchange of letters between His Royal Highness' agent, ( ), and ourselves.

We had discussions at quite some length during my last visit to (Middle East town) and on earlier occasions with you acting as His Royal Highness' legal adviser, with His Royal Highness' building adviser ( ) and others when all proposals and counter proposals in relation to the proposed contract were covered in detail, we believed, at the conclusion of the last meeting that all parties had reached agreement on the salient points of the proposed contract and head lease agreement.

We understood that our contract as presented was to be the basis of a formal contract which you were to draft up for our consideration. Some additional clauses being added or clauses modified only where it was necessary to avoid ambiguity and possible misunderstanding. We were therefore non-plussed and astonished when we received your drafts of the contract and head lease agreement for our consideration. Let it be sufficient for us to say we are only prepared to enter into a contract or lease agreement based on the contract prepared on our behalf by our solicitor based on the exchange of letters between His Royal Highness' agent, ( ) and ourselves. A copy of this contract was translated into Arabic by (agent) and submitted to His Royal Highness for his consideration and approval before we proceeded to firm up our loan agreement with the Swiss Banking Corporation.

There was no doubt in our mind that His Royal Highness was fully informed of the contents of our draft contract which after all only formalised a contract which was already in existance as far as we were concerned. We believe it would not be reasonable to come to any other conclusion bearing in mind that His Royal Highness had instructed the (Middle East Bank) to issue the bank guarantee to the Swiss Banking Corporation so the project could proceed. We have now considered so many proposals put forward by your which (sic) are so far apart from the original agreement reached with His Royal Highness that we have grave doubts that we will ever be able to resolve this to our mutual satisfaction. Too many cooks spoil the broth, and so it seems in these negotiations. The continued delays and procrastination have only added to the final cost of the project. Inflation has increased building costs so that the final cost of the building as originally envisaged would now be in the region of SR40,000,000.00. Therefore we have two alternatives, either to raise additional finance or re design the building complex to reduce the capital cost of the building. Some firm and final decision must be made in this regard by His Royal Highness or his advisers before we can proceed further.

The existing foundations of course are still a problem but it has been agreed between ourselves and (agent) that he would accept responsibility on behalf of His Royal Highness for the costs incurred in having these foundations tested by a competent consulting engineer. We have nominated (Senior Engineer of Engineering firm) of Sydney, Australia and have requested that a visa be issued for his visit to (Middle East town). (Engineering firm) are a subsidiary of ( ), a world authority on the particular type of foundation design used on His Royal Highness (Middle East town) site. Further, this company, (Engineering firm), through their associated office in (Capital City) have some background knowledge of this project and can more readily assess whether the foundations as now designed and installed are satisfactory or whether they should be re designed in line with the latest donstruction (sic) techniques to ensure a more stable foundation.

We await your further advices after you have discussed the points raised in this letter with His Royal Highness and received additional instructions.

We are willing and anxious to proceed, but only if we are satisfied as to the financing of the building and the commercial viability of the whole project. After all in the final analysis our Company is to be totally responsible for the success or otherwise of the whole venture."

There was no evidence before the Tribunal as to the law of the Middle East country and in its absence, the Tribunal assumes the law does not differ from the law in force in Australia. (See Cross on Evidence (Third Australian edition) (Butterworths, 1986) p. 1054).

It appears from the terms of the documents Exhibits G and W, that the taxpayer would not have been able to sustain an action based on the existence of contract. The case appears to fall within the third class discussed in Masters v Cameron [1954] HCA 72; (1954) 91 CLR 353, 360. The difficulty of ascertaining what plans and specifications required to be produced, would fulfil the intentions of the parties and what particular pricing was required of a bill of quantities to be provided render the ascertainment of an enforceable obligation a task of complexity. (See Cudgen Rutile (No 2) Pty Ltd v Chalk [1975] A.C. 520, 536; Upper Hunter County District Council v Australian Chilling and Freezing Co. Ltd [1968] HCA 8; (1968) 118 CLR 429, 436, 437).

While the modern approach is not to be narrow or to rob commercial arrangements of contractual significance, a proper construction of terms if obscure, or a standard for ascertaining the meaning of contractual terms if uncertain, must be found. A perusal of the specification and plans (Exhibits BB2 and BB3) produced to the taxpayer by the Prince's builder, leaves me with the view that the scope of the works envisaged therein was not too uncertain to be carried out, nor would the ascertainment and pricing of a bill of quantities within the scope of the builder's original fixed price be too uncertain. The document Exhibit AA if it is correct, does in my opinion fulfil the requirements of the second class discussed in Masters v Cameron (supra). However the terms of conditions 1(c)(d)(e) and (f) therein were never fulfilled and the taxpayer's letter (Exhibit DD) appears to accept that there is no contract on foot (see last paragraph).

Mr Gulliver maintained the building plans disclosed that the building the Prince wanted could not be built and that the foundation piles already driven which the Engineering firm proposed to test as per their quotation of 30th May 1974 (Exhibit C) were unsuitable. Mr Gulliver asserted the plans would not suffice for a building of the size proposed and hence the taxpayer was insisting on what it regarded as proper plans and specifications before any building could be commenced. The taxpayer did not believe the Prince's specification and drawings to be sufficient. Hence it was insisting on the preparation of further detailed drawings, foundation tests and a priced bill of quantities, before it became embroiled in a building project. The Tribunal is conscious of the fact that it does not have available to it the evidence of the Prince, his agent, his builder or architect. It is not prepared to draw inferences adverse to those persons in spite of the criticisms made by Mr Gulliver unless such inferences appear inescapable upon the documents and evidence. It appears that there is no proper basis for holding that the taxpayer believed a certain contract to be in operation between the Prince and the taxpayer for the construction of the building at least after 30th October 1974.

Although the taxpayer had no certain construction contract in hand with the Prince, it went ahead with the first drawdown from the Swiss Bank Corporation on or about 3rd December 1973 (Exhibits Y and Z) after obtaining Reserve Bank approval to the borrowing of the first Swiss francs 9.5 million, on the terms that 75% of the initial drawdown be retained overseas for contribution to the Middle East venture and 25% be lodged with the Reserve Bank of Australia as an interest free loan and further authority being obtained for the second drawdown (Exhibit X). According to Mr Gulliver the initial drawdown was utilised by putting $A500,000 into gold, $A1,000,000 into the Eurodollar market and $A489,488.79 was deposited with the Reserve Bank of Australia (transcript p. 93). He said the taxpayer's aim was to avoid exchange loss in the short term. He said the taxpayer had done some design work for the proposed commercial centre, together with calculations and feasibility studies, but it had no written building contract and was not in possession of the site (transcript pp 95-96).

After meeting the Prince on 19th February 1974 and preparing notes of the meeting as set out in Exhibit AA, as set out above, the taxpayer and the Operator company both executed a form of contract between themselves (Exhibit BB). The printed form adopted is one used by Master Builders in New South Wales. It refers to a fixed price in the currency of the Middle East country and to the plans and specifications obtained from the Prince's builder and architect. It contains such incongruous references as mention of Acts of Parliament and of local authority and of Workers Compensation. The outside cover of the form of contract states that it is a contract between the taxpayer "on behalf of" the Prince as proprietor and the Operator company as builder. Mr Gulliver frankly admitted that the taxpayer had no authority from the Prince to enter into a contract on his behalf (transcript p. 102). He further said the purpose of the document was so that the taxpayer had a formal contract to enable it to secure a performance bond and also to get builders into the Middle East country and to "get the job moving" (transcript p. 101). He also admitted that the document was not intended to create formal legal relations with the Operator company (transcript p. 301). I am satisfied the alleged contract with the Operator company is a sham.

Mr Gulliver stated that his son-in-law was already living in the Middle East country working on other projects. He was general manager of the Operator company. Mr Gulliver's son was sent to the Middle East country in February or March 1974 with the title of project manager of the Operator company. He said some work was done on the site clearing debris from the foundations, already placed there by the previous builder and dewatering the site.

Switzerland

It is here apposite to refer to some of the terms of the loan obtained by the taxpayer from the Swiss Bank Corporation as set out in the bank's letter of 4th September 1973 (part of Exhibit U). The loan required the giving of the joint and several guarantees by the taxpayer and the Middle East Bank of Swiss francs 22,800,000. It contained provision for the debt to become immediately repayable if there was default in repaying principal or interest for certain periods.

On 12th May 1975 the taxpayer obtained Reserve Bank approval to make the second drawdown of Swiss francs 9.5 million on the Swiss loan, conditional upon the retention overseas of the funds for on-lending to the Operator company "to be disbursed as a further contribution to your company's" Middle East venture (Exhibit FF). On 30th June 1975 the Swiss Bank Corporation was instructed by the taxpayer that the drawdown, arranged to take place on 11th July 1975, was to be dispersed by paying Swiss francs 380,000.00 to the taxpayer's account with the bank, by repaying the taxpayer's overdraft account with the bank amounting to approximately Swiss francs 1,500,000.00 and by paying Deutsch Marks 500,000.00 to the account of the Operator company with the Swiss Bank Corporation. The balance of the Swiss francs 9.5 million was directed to be converted into United States dollars and paid to the credit of the Operator company's US dollar account with the Volksbank,. Zurich (Exhibit GG).

On 30th July 1975 after the distribution had been made, the Swiss Bank Corporation wrote a letter (Exhibit HH) to the taxpayer which stated that it had been advised by the Middle East Bank that the proceeds of the Swiss franc 19 million loan to the taxpayer had not been used for the execution of the project of constructing the building commissioned by the Prince and that the Middle East Bank requested the Swiss Bank Corporation to claim repayment of the loan. The letter continued:-

"Under the circumstances, we are compelled to declare our loan of Sfr. 19 million, including interest up to the date of receipt of your payment, immediately due and payable and request you to reimburse us without delay."

It is true as Mr Gulliver pointed out, that there was no express provision in the terms of the loan document (Exhibit U) for acceleration of payment in the circumstances outlined. On the other hand the advance had been obtained because of the guarantee by the Middle East Bank (Exhibit V) and the taxpayer had given the undertaking to the Swiss Bank Corporation to use the funds for the construction of the commercial centre (Exhibit W). The taxpayer proceeded with the second drawdown knowing perfectly well that no building was proceeding or even likely to proceed. None of the moneys in either drawdown was used for the approved purpose.

There appears little doubt that the transfer of funds to the Operator company was in breach of arrangements with the Prince in spite of the taxpayer's Directors' minutes of 9th May 1975 (Exhibit EE). Following the letter of demand the bank took proceedings in Switzerland, which appear to be similar to a Mareva injunction application, to arrest all assets of the taxpayer and the Operator company in Switzerland. Those proceedings were later discontinued but only after the Prince had commenced similar proceedings in Switzerland.

At a date prior to 8th January 1976 (see Exhibit JJ), the Swiss Bank Corporation also commenced proceedings against the taxpayer to recover the full amount of the loan. On 26th February 1976 the Bank wrote to the taxpayer giving details of its demand (Exhibit KK). On 8th January 1981, the taxpayer wrote to the Swiss Bank Corporation stating that it was no longer prepared to continue paying interest or capital repayments on the loan and alleging that the Gulliver Group of companies had incurred considerable loss because of the bank's "unwarranted actions in collusion with" the Prince (Exhibit JJ).

The taxpayer engaged legal representation in Switzerland and first pleaded a cross action for damages suffered as a result of the call-up of the loan. This was abandoned because security was required from a foreign suitor. The taxpayer then pleaded a set-off, but the net result of the proceedings was judgment given on 19th October 1978 by the Commercial Court, Canton of Zurich, in favour of the bank for the full amount of the loan, interest and costs (Exhibit 01). An appeal to the Swiss Federal Court was dismissed on 11th January 1980 (Exhibit 02).

All assets of the taxpayer and the Operator company in Switzerland were appropriated towards payment of the judgment, but a large part remains unpaid. The Swiss Bank Corporation has recently registered the Swiss judgment in the Supreme Court of New South Wales. No action for its enforcement has yet occurred and Mr Gulliver claims the matter can be settled for considerably less than is owing, but this is not yet proved.

The Prince also commenced proceedings in Switzerland against the taxpayer and the Operator company claiming a lawful interest to require the taxpayer to pay the Swiss loan and claiming damages for wrongful use of the borrowed moneys. The Commercial Court of Zurich dismissed the Prince's claim on 11th December 1980, deciding that the action was "superfluous" and ordering the court costs to be borne equally by the plaintiff and the defendant (Exhibit P). However on appeal the decision was reversed on 20th June 1984 by the Commercial Court of the Canton of Zurich. The Court ordered the taxpayer to repay the balance of the loan to the Swiss Bank Corporation and to pay the Prince's costs (Exhibit B).

The Prince and the Middle East Bank commenced proceedings in the Supreme Court of New South Wales, Equity Division, (Exhibit Ql) against the taxpayer, the Operator company, Mr Gulliver and others seeking as subrogated creditors by virtue of the Middle East Bank's guarantee to the Swiss Bank Corporation to require the taxpayer, the Operator company and Mr Gulliver to exonerate the guarantor from liability and to trace moneys received from the Swiss loan said to be in the hands of the defendants.

In the course of the proceedings in Switzerland by the Prince against the taxpayer and the Operator company the Prince raised in his pleadings (Exhibit C) a claim that the taxpayer was obliged to repay the Swiss bank loan in terms of the penultimate clause of Exhibit G because no construction contract had been made between him and the taxpayer. The taxpayer by its defence denied that the undertaking in the said clause was operative because of changed circumstances, but did not plead there was any contract in force. The Commercial Court of the Canton of Zurich found in favour of the Prince on the issue that the penultimate clause of Exhibit G bound the taxpayer, hence it appears to me that the taxpayer is estopped from asserting that there was any contract between it and the Prince in terms of the minutes set out in Exhibit AA.

New South Wales

On 28th April 1978 Holland J. in a lengthy judgment considered an application for injunctive relief and as far as I can ascertain restrained the taxpayer, the Operator company and possibly Mr Gulliver from removing from the jurisdiction any assets into which the proceeds of the Swiss loan could be traced. In those proceedings some of the defendants including the taxpayer sought to raise cross claims hut on 13th July 1979 in a lengthy judgment, Holland J. struck out the cross claims (Exhibit Ql). An appeal to the Court of Appeal of New South Wales from Holland J's interlocutory judgment was dismissed on 10th August 1979 (Exhibit Q2). No further steps in those proceedings have been brought to my attention.

Before Mr Justice Holland it was argued in the interlocutory proceedings that the funds obtained by the taxpayer from the Swiss bank were subject to a constructive trust on the basis of the decision in Barclays Bank Limited v Quistclose Investments Limited [1970] AC 567. His Honour did not find it necessary to decide this. However it appears to me important to observe that the taxpayer did not raise as an issue before the Swiss Courts or in the New South Wales injunction proceedings, that it was entitled to retain the Bank loan to carry out an existing building contract or for any other purposes of its business so far as I can ascertain.

Submissions

The legal expenses the subject of this review were mainly incurred in the Swiss proceedings. It has been agreed between the parties that $27,465.14 of legal expenses were incurred in the financial year ending 30th June 1979 and $135,057.09 of legal expenses were incurred in the financial year ending 30th June 1980. There is some discrepancy between these amounts and the amounts claimed and disallowed in those years. In Exhibits N1 and N2, terms of agreed facts are set out. It is also admitted between the parties that of the sums of money on which expenditure was incurred, $108,624.01 was incurred in the litigation between the Swiss Bank Corporation and the taxpayer whilst $43,351.22 was incurred either in that litigation or in the litigation between the Prince on the one hand and the taxpayer and the Operator company on the other hand. (This agreement appears to refer only to the Swiss litigation). It is also agreed that payments amounting to $10,547 were made on account of Senior Counsel's fees in the litigation occurring in New South Wales. An argument was put to me that those moneys were not paid by or on behalf of the taxpayer but by another company in the Gulliver Group. Some of the payments are noted in Exhibits K and 00. I am satisfied that payment through the other company was a machinery provision, probably because the taxpayer's funds were the subject of an injunction and I accept that the legal expenses were paid on behalf of the taxpayer and later made the subject of a journal entry recognising them as a debt of the taxpayer.

In giving evidence in re-examination Mr Gulliver said the taxpayer raised the Swiss loan for two reasons, firstly to make a profit and secondly to gain favour with the Prince who was interested in a bulk wheat venture in his country, in which venture the taxpayer or one of the Gulliver Group wanted to be involved (transcript p 6). Mr RF Edmonds of Counsel on behalf of the taxpayer said he was not submitting that any enforceable contract had ever come into existence between the Prince and the taxpayer, nevertheless the taxpayer anticipated that this would happen. He said the purposes for which the taxpayer engaged in the Swiss litigation were firstly because it had been advised that it had a rightful claim by way of cross-claim, although this was later abandoned. Secondly, it had suffered an exchange loss because of the devaluation of the Australian dollar as against the Swiss franc and wanted to be compensated for this. Thirdly, it wished to defer liability to repay. These were all good commercial reasons, counsel submitted, because by this time the taxpayer was effectively the financier to the Gulliver Group, acting as money lender to the associated companies. Counsel submitted that the taxpayer was entitled to deductions for the expenses of litigation in accordance with section 64A and section 51(1) of the Act. He said that in determining whether or not the expenditure was necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, the Tribunal should have regard to the business ends to which the expenditure was directed and that those business ends are a matter of decision for those carrying on the business. Counsel said a similar approach should be adopted to determining whether or not the outgoings were of an income or capital nature, that is, the way in which business treated the outgoings was crucial.

Counsel referred me to passages in the judgment of Latham CJ and the dissenting judgment of Dixon J, as he then was, in Hallstroms Pty. Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at 641/2 and 648/9 in support of these propositions. He said the litigation was not about the quantum of liability but concerned the timing of when the obligation had to be satisfied. Reference was made to Fullagar J.'s remarks in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Snowden and Willson Pty Limited [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at 443/4 where His Honour approved a statement of the late Dr Hannan which said:-

"The meaning of 'necessarily' in that context is probably not limited to compulsion in the legal sense ...and may extend to business expenditure arising out of exigencies created by unusual or difficult circumstances."

Fullagar J continued:-

"I would respectfully adopt that passage, omitting the word 'probably' and substituting the word 'does' for the word 'may' The interpretation of the word 'necessarily' which is involved in this view is familiar in many contexts, and in a variety of instruments...It means for practical purposes that, within the limits of reasonable human conduct, the man who is carrying on the business must be the judge of what is 'necessary'. It accords with the general principle on which the Assessment Act is framed, and it leaves the revenue adequately safeguarded by the express exclusion of expenditure of a capital nature".

In the same case Dixon CJ said (at p 437):-

"What is meant by the qualification is that the expenditure must be dictated by the business ends to which it is directed, those ends forming part of or being truly incidental to the business."

In this case I would add the observation that the business end must be a business end of the business conducted by the taxpayer and not the business ends of Mr Gulliver or of the other companies such as the Operator company in the Gulliver Group.

In the Federal Court in the case of Magna Alloys and Research Pty. Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1980) 80 ATC 4542 at 4559[1980] FCA 150; , (1980) 33 ALR 213 at 235, after referring to Fullagar J's judgment in the lastmentioned case, Deane and Fisher JJ said:-

"The controlling factor is that, viewed objectively, the outgoing must, in the circumstances, be reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of the business being carried on for the purpose of earning assessable income."

The learned justices then referred to the judgment of Dixon J, as he then was, in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v The Midland Railway Co of Western Australia Limited [1952] HCA 5; (1952) 85 CLR 306, 313, where he said:-

"...what governs the issue is the business purposes for which the outgoing was incurred from the point of view of the taxpayer company. The controlling factors are those which arise from the character of the business or undertaking and the relation which the expenditure or the liability to make it bore to the carrying on of the business or the gaining of assessable income."

Deane and Fisher JJ. then said:-

"It is implicit in what has been said that the distinction between immediate direct purpose or object and remote indirect purpose or object does not play an essential part in determining whether an outgoing is properly deductible pursuant to the second limb of s 51(1) of the Act. To the extent that the subjective element is relevant, what is important in the case of a voluntary outgoing is the identification of the advantage or advantages which the outgoing was intended to achieve on behalf of the taxpayer regardless of whether that advantage or those advantages were seen as the direct result of the outgoing or as indirectly flowing therefrom or of whether the pursuit of them should be seen as 'purpose' or as 'object' or as 'motive'. Business outgoings may be properly and necessarily incurred in pursuit of indirect and remote, as well as direct and immediate, advantages. The fact that the business advantage sought is indirect or remote will not of itself preclude the pursuit of that advantage from characterizing the outgoing as an outgoing necessarily incurred in carrying on the relevant business."

It appears to me that the prime advantage the taxpayer intended to achieve by expending money on the litigation was to delay the Swiss Bank Corporation and the Prince in recovering from it the moneys it had salted away in investments in gold and the Eurodollar market and in its associate the Operator company, the latter money movements being done under the guise of the sham contract between the Prince and the Operator company. A second advantage sought to be achieved may have been to delay recovery in the hope that the exchange rate between the Australian dollar and the Swiss franc might improve. However no evidence was given to support this, and in any event it was not a direct or incidental advantage which in my opinion was proper to the conduct of the taxpayer's business.

The taxpayer had given a solemn undertaking to the Prince to repay the Swiss loan in the event that no contract was signed between it and the Prince on or before 15th December 1973 (Exhibit G). No agreement had in fact been signed by that date. The terms of the loan were explicit. The money was not to be used for any purpose of the taxpayer's business except for the construction of the Prince's commercial centre (Exhibit W). The Prince never proposed to guarantee the taxpayer against loss due to a fall in value of the Australian dollar as against the Swiss franc but only in relation to currency fluctuation between the currency of his country and the Swiss currency (Exhibit PP, clause 4). The loan was for a specific purpose and any loss made by the taxpayer should be classified as a loss of a capital nature and not a revenue item for the purposes of section 51(1) of the Act. (See Avco Financial Services Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1982) 150 GLR 510, 532).

The loan was not for the purpose of adding to the taxpayer's investment capital to lend to the Operator company or to speculate in gold or Eurodollars. It was to be drawn in Swiss francs and utilised solely in building operations in the Middle East country. The money spent in legal expenses to resist early repayment (whether prematurely demanded or not) was not spent in protecting the taxpayer's legitimate investment revenue, but in resisting a specific purpose capital repayment. The expenditure was "undeniably on capital account" to use the words of Dixon C.J. in John Fairfax & Sons Pty. Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30, 36. (See also PBL Marketing Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1985) 85 A.T.C. 4416, 4421).

Whether or not the Ouistclose (supra) doctrine can be applied to the loan moneys, there was no general or incidental purpose of the taxpayer's business which justified the expenditure of the moneys incurred in the Swiss litigation. Up until 1979 its business was described as "Electrical Contracting and Manufacturing (Administration)". In its income tax returns for the years of income ended 30th June 1980 to 30th June 1982 the nature of its business was described as "Investment Company", but the evidence establishes that the business so carried on was as a financier to the Gulliver Group of companies and not as financier to an Arab Prince.

In my opinion the taxpayer has failed to establish that the moneys expended in legal expenses whether in Switzerland or Australia were deductible pursuant to section 51(1) or section 64A of the Act and the objections to the assessments are disallowed.

Although I have reached a conclusion adverse to the taxpayer, it would not be proper to leave this matter without recording the assistance given by both counsel in reducing a threatened mass of documents to reasonable bounds. With regard to the issues proposed to me in Exhibit L, I see no reason to doubt that the expenditure was incurred as alleged and at the times stated in Exhibit N1.
