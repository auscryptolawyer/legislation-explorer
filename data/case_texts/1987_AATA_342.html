ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - deductions - loss incurred after truck repossessed -expenses incurred selling private residence to pay debt

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.51 (1), 67A, 68 Case S9, 85 ATC 138

FC of T v EA Marr and Sons (Sale) Ltd., 84 ATC 4580

Case U47, 87 ATC 326

Inglis v FC of T, 80 ATC 4001

AAT No 3793

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

25 September 1987 Deputy President RK Todd

  1. For part of the year ended 30 June 1980 the applicant and her husband carried on a business in partnership. The husband had been a self-employed truck driver for some 22 years prior to February 1980. During that period he had owned or leased many different vehicles. He was kept very busy driving the truck for long hours on the road, and the applicant helped with the book-keeping, kept in touch with their accountant, and took telephone calls.
  2. In October 1978 the partnership leased a Volvo F12 Prime Mover from a finance company. A trailer was also involved, but I shall refer to both prime mover and trailer as "the truck". The agreement itself is not available, but it seems clear that the lease was in familiar form, obliging the lessees to make 48 monthly lease payments of $1,582.50 with a residual value of $20,000 (see the "Advice of Acceptance of Lease which is Ex. B). Nor was it even disputed that the finance company was entitled to repossess the truck should monthly payments fail to be made, and to recover an amount in satisfaction of the lessee's liability to the finance company, a liability which was presumably calculated in terms of the difference between the residual value stated in the lease and the total of rental payments for the unexpired portion of the lease, with an offset of the amount obtained from the sale of the truck. This was the case of the agreement in Case S9, 85 ATC 138, which is referred to below. The absence of evidence as to the terms of the lease agreement is not a bar to the Tribunal dealing with the matter, as it was conceded that the lease was a "straight" lease agreement and was content for as the applicant's counsel accepted that the matter should be decided on the footing that the lease had been duly terminated and that the calculations of the liability of the partnership to the finance company had been correctly made.

3. Another feature of the agreement was that the liability of the partnership to the finance company was secured by the grant of a mortgage of the home of the applicant and her husband to the finance company.

4. The story of the events leading up to the repossession was as follows. The husband was finding it hard to make the business pay, and to keep up the payments on the truck. He sought out a person whom he thought might take an assignment of the lease and make the payments thereunder. This person, having been introduced to the finance company by the applicant, was good enough to go behind the applicant to the finance company and make an offer to them to purchase the truck. On a Friday night the husband drove the truck from Sydney to his home in Canberra en route to Melbourne with a load. The applicant told him that there was a letter advising that the truck was to be repossessed. On the Saturday, 23 February 1980, representatives of the finance company arrived to repossess the truck, and after some debate in which they told the husband that, he said, he had "no law, no rights", took the truck; load and all, to a farm just over the ACT border into New South Wales. On the Monday the company for whom the husband was subcontracting recovered the load, he having told them what had happened.

5. In order to pay out the finance company the applicant and her husband put the house on the market and sold it. By the time the finance company and the Commissioner for Housing had been paid out, agent's commission and legal and other fees paid, there were only a couple of hundred dollars left. The husband said in evidence: "We were a bit devastated at having lost everything - our whole life".

  1. The applicant claimed to be entitled to deduct her share of what was described as the loss on repossession of the truck, and of the costs of the sale of the house. The applicant's share of the former was $12,336, and the claim was made under s51(1). Her share of the latter was ultimately claimed and accepted to be $194.
  2. After the repossession the husband had taken some time to settle down. He occupied himself in the matters relating to the sale of the house and he eventually obtained some driving work. This however was not until May, June and July 1980. Mr Enniss, who appeared for the applicant and who said everything on her behalf that could be said, sought to have these activities construed as the continued carrying on of the business which the partnership had carried on prior to the repossession. I am quite satisfied that such a finding is not open to me and that I should find, as I do, that the partnership business ceased to be carried on after the repossession. The transhipment of the goods effected by the company which had subcontracted the carriage of those goods to the husband was simply a recovery or "stop loss" transaction. It did not constitute part of the carrying on of the business of the partnership, but rather the avoidance of some of the consequences of its cessation.
  3. In these circumstances I am reluctantly compelled to find that the case is completely governed by Case S9, referred to above, which I find to be indistinguishable. I find myself in agreement, with respect, with the reasons of the majority, Mr HP Stevens (Chairman) and Mr PM Roach (Member, now a Senior Member of this Tribunal). The case of FC of T v E A. Marr & Sons (Sales) Ltd 84 ATC 4580 is distinguishable, and indeed Mr Enniss expressly disclaimed reliance on it. There the leased plant was hired by subsidiaries of the taxpayer in the course of the taxpayer's carrying on of its own business activities, which were transactions of a trading character. In this case, as in Case S9, the plant (there the typesetter, here the truck) was put to use by the business as its essential profit-making structure. It follows that while the rental payable under the lease had the character of an operational expense, once the lease was put to an end and the business of the partnership had ceased, as it in my opinion did when the truck was re-possessed, the overall liability to the finance company was a loss incurred not in carrying on the business but in winding it up, and was in the nature of a loss of capital.

Sale of Residence

  1. Mr Enniss places some reliance on Case U47, 87 ATC 326, but I agree with Mr. Miller, who appeared for the Commissioner, that in that case the goods were purchased, and that the business in question had not ceased, the question before the Tribunal then revolving around the on-going operations of that business. Mr Enniss argued that the expenditure was incurred at the time of the repossession, the liability crystallising at that point. This argument has, however, effectively been dealt with above in the discussion of Case S9. Unfortunately the partners were caught either way. Had they chosen to cease the business even although under a continuing obligation to pay rent, their entitlement to a deduction for the rent-would have been lost: cf Inglis v FC of T 80 ATC 4001. Having ceased the business, the profit yielding subject having been returned and the partners having become liable to the lessor as a result of premature determination of the obligation to hire and pay rent, the moneys representing that liability are not deductible.
  2. As far as the claim to deduct expenses of the sale of the house are concerned, that claim cannot be sustained under s 51.
  3. The claim is not allowable under S67A because the mortgage was not given as security for the repayment of money borrowed by the partnership or the payment by the partners of the whole or a part of the purchase price of property purchased by the partnership. The mortgage was given as security for the payment of all moneys payable under the lease agreement, for the performance and observance of the covenants and conditions contained in the lease, and for the fulfilment of all other present and future indebtedness of the partners under the lease agreement.

12. Deductibility of the expenses was also claimed under s.68, but quite apart from the possible difficulty of construing a Crown Lease in the Australian Capital Territory as a lease for the purpose of s.68, I am obliged to find that the house in question was not held for the purpose of producing assessable income.

13. One cannot help feeling very sorry for these hard-working honest people, but I have no option but to confirm the objection decision under review.
