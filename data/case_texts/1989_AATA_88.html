Re Taxation Appeals [1989] AATA 88; 5023 Taxation 20 ATR 3454 (13 April 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. ST88/8

AAT No. 5023

Taxation

20 ATR 3454

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - Sales tax - Exemptions and Classifications - Invalid Chairs - Medical or Surgical Appliances - Design and Manufacture for Disabled Persons - whether certain chairs fell within any of the exemptions claimed

Words and Phrases : invalid chairs; invalid wheeled lounges and

lounge chairs; viz; medical or surgical

appliances; designed; disablement; goods of a

kind not ordinarily used by persons who are

not suffering from . . .

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s 159P(4)(f)

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 ss 25(2A), (2B), 45(2)

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 s 5(1); schedule 1,

items 42(10); 42B(1); 123(1)

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Ildes 88 ATC 4214

Hygienic Lily Limited v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4327

ICI Australia Operations Pty Ltd v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 5110

Re Scholle Industries Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (1983) 5 ALN N118

HEARING

SYDNEY

13:4:1989

ORDER

Pursuant to subsection 43(1)(c) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal sets aside the objection decision.

The assessment is reduced to nil.

DECISION

The applicant conducts a business which I will refer to as the Chair Company. It manufactures individual chairs to individual orders. Sales tax is payable pursuant to the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 in respect of chairs, unless they are exempt from tax by virtue of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 ("the Exemption Act"). The applicant claimed exemption from sales tax on its chairs by virtue of s 5 of the Exemption Act and by the operation of one or more of 3 items in the First Schedule to the Exemption Act, namely Items 42(10), 42B(1) and 123(1).

2. The terms of the section and of the 3 Items in question are as follows -

"s 5(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in any Sales Tax

Assessment Act, sales tax shall not, subject to

this section, be payable upon the sale value of any

goods covered by any item or sub-item in the first

column of the First Schedule, under any Act specified

in the second column of that Schedule opposite

that item or sub-item."

. . . . . . . . . .

Item 42 Surgical appliances (and parts therefor), viz -

. . . . . . . . . .

42(10) Invalid chairs, invalid carrying chairs, invalid

wheeled lounges and lounge chairs, invalid

tricycles including motor propelled invalid wheel

chairs and invalid tricycles; spinal carriages and

other invalid hand carriages; wheeled beds of the

kind used by invalids.

. . . . . . . . . . . .

42B(1) Medical or surgical appliances of a kind used

exclusively or principally by persons suffering

from sickness, disease or physical impairment for

the purpose of alleviating or treating that

sickness, disease or impairment or the effects of

that sickness, disease or impairment.

. . . . . . . . . . . .

123(1) Goods designed and manufactured expressly for use

by persons suffering from sickness, disease or

disablement (whether sickness, disease or

disablement, generally, or a particular kind, or

one or more particular kinds, of sickness, disease

or disablement), being goods of a kind not

ordinarily used by persons who are not suffering

from sickness, disease or disablement."

3. Following investigations and discussions, the claim for exemption was rejected and an assessment was issued under the provisions of s 25(2A) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930. The assessment covered all manually operated chairs manufactured by the applicant and either sold, treated as stock for sale by retail, or applied to the applicant's own use during the period 1 August 1983 to 31 October 1986 ("the assessment period"). During the same period, a number of electrically operated chairs were produced by the applicant. These were not included in the assessment.

4. The amount of tax assessed was $9,850-77. In addition the respondent assessed additional tax under sub-section 45(2) of Part VIII of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 amounting to $2,373 and additional tax under sub-section 25(2B) of the same Act amounting to $730, making a total due and payable under the assessment of $12,953-77. On the hearing of these proceedings, it was agreed between the parties that irrespective of the outcome of this application, and of any appeal from the decision given in this application, the additional tax would be waived by the respondent. The amount in issue is therefore $9,850-77. A formal objection to the assessment was disallowed and this application is brought to review that objection decision.

5. Evidence was given by the managing director of the applicant by way of an affidavit with annexures which were amplified orally. He was cross-examined on his evidence by counsel for the respondent.

6. The Chair Company is a member of a group which mass produces furniture for general sale to and use by the public. The Chair Company deals only with specialised chairs required by particular individuals who, because of some physical disability or inadequacy, can not achieve comfort in normal chairs. Since 1983, the applicant has developed a technology based on experience, and advice from organisations that deal with such people, which has enabled the applicant to achieve that end. The technology was described in the affidavit in the following terms-

". . . . . . . .

13. To measure up the customer, we seat the

customer in a standard chair that we have.

This standard chair has been made to allow us

to make the proper measurements for the

individual customers. We then use a measuring

form which is easily filled in while we are

determining the measurements required for that

particular individual. The form also has a

space for "special remarks", in which we record

the particular requirements in relation to

extra padding and types and grades of foam.

Other particulars that are included under this

are in relation to the amount of incline and

any other specification that would be required

to make the chair user-specific.

14. We first determine the width of the chair for

the person so that their arms will have the

support in line with the shoulders so that

there is no unwanted pressure or pulling on the

upper spine and to prevent muscle fatigue in

the shoulders and neck region.

15. We then determine the height that the seat area

should be off the floor, so that there will not

be any hindrance to the blood circulation under

the thigh and behind the knees. This seat area

height is also determined with particular

attention being paid to alleviate any build up

of pain in this area. The measurement is then

incorporated with the armrest height which will

allow the user to get into or out of the chair

with a minimum of effort. This combination

minimises the strain on the hips, back, legs,

knees and arms.

16. We then take measurements for the back height

which is important for the user to maintain

correct support for the head and neck and to

alleviate stress and fatigue in the neck and

upper shoulder muscles. This also gives

correct support to the upper spine.

17. The next aspect of the chair that we look at is

the foot pad on a recliner chair. This

measurement is important to the user to get

total support in the calf, ankle and heel area.

With the correct support, the foot pad ensures

that the weight of the foot does not pull and

stress the ankle and calf muscles and does not

cause any blood circulation blockages.

18. The next area that we look at is the lumbar and

shoulder support. These measurements are

determined while the user is seated in our

standard measuring chair. We feel with our

hands, the spine being supported by the chair

and where there is a requirement for extra

support. These details are taken down and thus

decides the amount, density and graded foam

that is required to "fill in the gaps". This

allows the back to be totally supported, and

therefore the correct posture, which relieves

stress on the vertebrae and muscle in the

spine.

19. The chairs usually have upholstery that is

picked by the customer. This is done on the

basis of the colour, pattern etc which the

customer wants. Although every effort is made

to give these chairs the characteristics for

which they are designed, that is, total support

and comfort, along with easing pain and

discomfort associated with being physically

disabled, the company takes pride in also

trying to give the user a psychological

advantage in having such a lounge chair.

Considerable work goes into the finishing of

the chairs to make them appear and look to be

the kind that are used by someone who does not

suffer from any physical problems. I have

always endeavoured for psychological reasons,

to make the chair one that looks ordinary and

one that would suit the customer's liking.

20. Having taken these measurements, the

measurement form goes to the factory and the

chair frame is made up to the specifications

that have been taken down. Every part of each

chair is made up from its raw materials. On

some occasions, (the applicant) has purchased

sub-assemblies which have been put together to

make up the chair frame. (The applicant) makes

up the frame right from the start to the

correct size. The upholstery fabric is cut and

shaped to the correct size. The seat cushions

are made from a standard block of foam which is

cut to the size and design requirements. The

back and lumbar foam is again specially

designed according to the specification. The

company purchases various grades and qualities

of foam to enable it to put together these

chairs. Each and every chair is specifically

designed and manufactured to its own size and

measurements. There was also a time when we

used to purchase an oversized standard frame

and cut this down to the required size."

. . . . . . . . . .

7. Evidence was given that the applicant sells 6 to 7 chairs per week. Most of the measurements and details of customers' requirements are obtained in the customers' own homes because of their usual inability to attend at the applicant's factory. In the course of ascertaining the customer's requirements the applicant is told of the customer's particular type of disability and usually of the customer's medical condition. Customers are referred to the applicant in large measure by the Independent Living Centre of South Australia, an organisation that advertises itself as an information centre for the disabled and which employs such professionals as occupational therapists. Other customers are referred to the applicant by physiotherapists, chiropractors and doctors.

8. The applicant also seeks customers in the market of disabled people by directly approaching them through advertising. In the Yellow Pages telephone directory there is a general advertisement referring to the name of the applicant and its product which, the advertisement says, is "designed for problem backs or backache, arthritic conditions, blood circulation problems, hip joint pain or breathing difficulties". It adds that all chairs are tailor-fitted for each "senior and handicapped person". In addition to advertising in the general market, the company produced a brochure which was mailed directly to physiotherapists. As a result of this advertising and these contacts customers have also come to the applicant through recommendations from associations dealing with particular illnesses, such as muscular dystrophy and arthritis. Customers have also come through recommendations from occupational therapists and medical centres.

9. The applicant has no direct competitors in its market. There are, however, companies that mass produce chairs referred to in the trade as geriatric chairs. These are a good deal cheaper than the applicant's chair. On the other hand, the applicant's chair is only slightly more expensive than the ordinary top quality reclining chair.

10. Evidence was given that more than 90% of the customers of the applicant were invalid, disabled or were suffering from a sickness or a disease. It was agreed however that the remaining customers required bespoke chairs for other reasons, such as excessive height or other physical abnormalities which made the achievement of a comfortable posture difficult in a normal chair.

11. In the course of discussions with the respondent and following appropriate submissions, the respondent issued Taxation Ruling No ST2241 on 7 May 1986. The terms of the ruling can not, of course, affect the outcome of the present application. I mention it here for completeness. It is possible that the respondent may wish to revise the text of that ruling when the present proceedings have been completed.

12. It is convenient to deal first with the claim for exemption under Items 42 and 42B. Item 42 commences with the words "surgical appliances (and parts therefor), viz :-" It then goes on to list 17 specific objects or collections of objects in 17 separate paragraphs. I was informed that Item 42B was inserted in 1979 after it was found that the itemised approach adopted in the drafting of Item 42 had resulted in certain unintended omissions. Whatever the reason for the addition to the legislation, I am of course, bound by the terms of those 2 Items as they were during the assessment period and as they continue to be today.

13. The applicant claimed exemption under Item 42(10) because it was said the chairs in question were invalid chairs in the sense that they were chairs intended to accommodate, or give comfort to, invalids. Alternatively it was argued that the phrase "invalid wheeled lounges and lounge chairs" could be read to mean "invalid wheeled lounges and invalid lounge chairs". In either event, the applicant claimed that the product in question would be covered by the description. An invalid is defined in the Macquarie Dictionary as "an infirm or sickly person, one deficient in health or one who is weak or sick". The applicant's evidence was that the great majority of customers who required its chairs fell within this category and the submission was that the chairs might therefore be fairly described as invalid chairs or invalid lounge chairs. I can not accept this argument.

14. In common parlance, "invalid chair" has a recognised meaning. The Macquarie Dictionary defines it as "a chair, usually collapsible, and always mobile, used for the transport of invalids unable to walk". This meaning would explain why the term is found in paragraph (10) in the company of other items of equipment, all of which are mobile and are used for conveying sick people. This commonality of qualities in the various items nominated in paragraph (10) would also indicate that the interpretation contended for by the applicant of the phrase "invalid wheeled lounges and lounge chairs" is not correct. Ordinary principles of interpretation of a list of items with identical or parallel qualities would compel a reading of the phrase to mean that both the lounges and the lounge chairs must not only be for invalids but must also be wheeled.

15. In any event, however, the claim for exemption under Item 42 must fail by virtue of the opening words of the Item. These words mean that of all the implements that might be regarded as surgical appliances only those set out in the 17 paragraphs are to be entitled to the exemption. This follows from the sense of the English language words used together with the Latin abbreviation "viz". "Viz" is, according to the Macquarie Dictionary, an abbreviation for the Latin videre licet, meaning literally "it is permitted to see". This is further abbreviated to "videlicet" which the dictionary defines to be an adverb meaning "namely; that is to say used to introduce examples, details, lists, etc". Thus relevant chairs are included in the exemption only if they are surgical appliances. There is no definition of this term in the Exemption Act.

16. The meaning of the phrase has however been considered in a different context by Spender J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Ildes 88 ATC 4214. His Honour was there considering what constituted a medical appliance for the purposes of s 159P(4)(f) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. I respectfully adopt his reasoning. There seems no reason in logic why the same object should not bear the same meaning in two different taxing Acts, in the absence of definitions or other differentiating circumstances. He concluded that the distinguishing feature of either a medical or a surgical appliance was that it constituted an aid to function or capacity. At pages 4216 and 4217 he said-

"In my view, the Tribunal misdirected itself as to

what constituted a "medical appliance" for the

purposes of sec 159P(4)(f). Accepting for present

purposes that an appliance can comprehend a

fixture, it seems to me that the essential

ingredient of a medical appliance for the purposes

of sec 159P(4)(f) is that it constitutes an aid to

function or capacity. The mere fact that an

article has a therapeutic purpose does not

constitute the article a medical or surgical

appliance within the meaning of the section.

. . . . . . . . . .

In short, in my view, whether an appliance is a

medical or surgical appliance within the meaning of

the subsection is not determined, as the Tribunal

concluded, by asking whether its prescription had a

therapeutic purpose, but by asking whether the

appliance is an aid to function or capacity."

17. It can not be said that the chairs in question constitute an aid to function or capacity. They provide comfort and possibly relieve pain. However they do not help a customer to do something as, for example, a wheel chair provides a mobility function.

18. For the same reason, the claim for exemption under Item 42B(1) must fail as the chairs are not medical or surgical appliances, whether or not it could be fairly said that they "alleviated . . . the effects of . . . an impairment", such as by providing comfort. The applicant's claim for exemption must stand or fall, in my opinion, on whether it is "covered" by Item 123. In order to come within its terms, the applicant must meet 3 tests; (a) that the goods are designed and manufactured expressly for use by particular persons, (b) that those persons are suffering from sickness, disease or disablement, and (c) that the goods are of a kind not ordinarily used by persons not suffering from sickness, disease or disablement. This last test requires an identification of the relevant kind of goods.

19. Whilst conceding that the goods were manufactured by the applicant, the respondent argued that they were not designed expressly for use by relevant persons because the process of designing did not take place. What occurred, it was submitted, was an adaptation of a standard model to make it comfortable for a particular individual. It was argued that this was a variation process and was not part of a design process. It was argued that the changes that were made to accommodate a particular customer did not alter the basic design of the chair and that consequently the first test could not be passed.

20. In my view this argument runs contrary to the ordinary accepted meaning of "designed". The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary defines designed as : "Marked out; planned, purposed; drawn, outlined; fashioned according to design". An appropriate definition of design is : "Contrivance in accordance with a pre-conceived plan; adaptation of means to ends; pre-arranged purpose." It seems to me that here the pre-arranged purpose is the adaptation of upholstery and other elements of a chair to the specific requirements of a particular person. The achievement of the end result involves a conceptual plan in the mind of the applicant and the preliminary conception of an idea that is to be carried into effect by action. These are the seminal qualities of design. As a tailor or dress-maker adapts a suit or dress (both standard garments) to the requirements of a customer and thus designs the end product as well as executing the design, so also the process in the applicant's case is to design each individual chair for a particular purpose even though the design proceeds originally from a basic model.

21. The respondent then submitted that the applicant had failed to satisfy the second test, namely that the persons for whom the goods were designed and manufactured were suffering from sickness, disease or disablement. The managing director of the applicant company readily agreed that he had no medical training and was unable to diagnose medical conditions, although he could recognise gross symptoms which resulted in easily observable deformities. In my view, disablement, particularly when associated with words such as sickness and disease, must refer to an incapacity of action or use of some part of the body. The evidence was that all of the customers who bought the subject goods required a modification of one sort or another because of some abnormality of physique or function, that is to say because of some disablement. This was conveyed to the designer either by the customer himself, or by the professional who referred the customer, or by visual inspection of the customer by the chair designer. In legislation imposing a tax which is self assessing, commonsense principles should be applied in determining whether pre-conditions of exemption exist. In my view, the fact that the designer was appraised of the sickness, disease or disablement of his customer in the above manner, and not in a manner that would be regarded as satisfactory in a court of law, is sufficient.

22. A small minority of the customers were said neither to be sick nor diseased. Nevertheless they required individually modified chairs for their comfort because of variations from the norm in their physique or possibly in their functions. The use of the dispersive phrase "or one or more particular kinds" in Item 123 indicates to me that in those cases, it is possible to look at disablement as a category separate from sickness or disease. If a person has a want of ability, or an inability, or an incapacity rendering him unable or incapable of achieving comfort in normal chairs then in my view he is suffering from a congenital disablement within the meaning of Item 123 in its application to lounge chairs.

23. It then becomes necessary to decide whether the chairs are of a kind not ordinarily used by particular persons. The phrase in the Item is inelegant, containing as it does a double negative. Nonetheless, one must take the words as one finds them. It is not, in my view, permissible to read the words as if they were "goods of a kind which are commonly used by (particular persons)", as counsel for the applicant submitted.

24. It is necessary first to identify the relevant kind of goods. Counsel for the respondent submitted that the relevant genus was "lounge chairs" and not "chairs for disabled persons". The choice of a relevant genus is a factual choice. Counsel pointed to certain factual evidence in support of his submission. He said that the product of the applicant was designed as a lounge chair, that it looked like one, that it functioned as one, that it was advertised as one, even though only to a particular group of customers, that its advertising bore a price comparison with recliner chairs, and that it was on occasions sold as a lounge chair to non-disabled people. It was, he submitted, just another form of lounge chair designed for the comfort of certain people.

25. In considering the phrase "goods of a kind" for certain other purposes under the Exemptions Act, Gummow J in Hygienic Lily v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4327 at 4330 said-

"Further, the setting in which the phrase "goods of

a kind" appears suggests it is directed not to the

use for which the particular goods in question were

designed or manufactured, nor to the purpose to

which it is intended those particular goods shall

be put, but rather to the nature, quality and

adaptation of goods in the class or genus in

question. Thus, goods are "of a kind ordinarily

used for household purposes" if they are to be

recognised as members of a class or genus which

commonly or regularly (albeit not necessarily

exclusively or principally) is used for household

purposes: cf. Customs and Excise Commissioners v

Mechanical Services (Trailer Engineers) Ltd (1979)

1 WLR 305 at pp 312-313, 315, 316-317.

. . . . . . . . . .

In my view, the cups involved in this case, are of

a kind ordinarily used for household purposes

because they are members of a class or genus (paper

cups) which commonly or regularly (albeit not

necessarily exclusively or principally) is used for

household purposes (viz the carrying of beverages

and the consumption thereof). In my view, a

purpose may be a household purpose even if not

exclusively or principally pursued in situ a dwelling."

26. His Honour there found the relevant genus through an objective examination of the inherent qualities of the object, without reference to the purpose or intended use of that object. I respectfully adopt that approach in the present circumstances. The relevant kind of goods is to be determined by inference from the appearance and nature of the goods themselves rather than from the use to which the goods are to be put. Use becomes relevant when considering the remainder of the sentence in Item 123. It is first necessary to determine what is the kind of goods in the present context to which the item refers. Thus the identity of the users, disabled or not, the nature of the advertising, the use to which various people put the chairs (some even slept in them, I was told) or even the use intended by the designer, are all subordinate in determining what kind of goods these chairs are. The genus is to be established by an examination of the goods on the sale room floor by an informed person.

27. This objective approach in defining a genus is commonly adopted for the purpose of determining the "essential character" of particular goods in the application of the Customs Tariff. For example, it was an approach adopted by a Tribunal presided over by its then President, Justice Davies, in Re Scholle Industries Pty Ltd (1983) 5 ALN N118. In ICI Australia Operations Pty Ltd v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 5110, Gray J at 5112 identified "the kinds" of road vehicles with which he was there concerned by reference to their primary but not necessarily exclusive, purpose and customary use. However in arriving at their purpose he depended upon objective evidence as to the nature of the vehicles themselves.

28. It is true that all the items the subject of the present assessment are lounge chairs. Each one however differs from the other. This difference is apparent on inspection by an informed person. Each chair has been modified in the upholstery, design and measurements to accommodate the particular contours, indeed the particular body, of a particular customer. It would, in my view, be as inappropriate to regard them all as falling within the wide genus "lounge chairs" as it would be to regard them as falling within the even wider genus of "furniture". An examination of each chair will show that it is different from all others. Indeed the existence of these differences constitutes its principal characteristic. It is why it is sold and bought. It has no reason to exist except for the fact that it is made to accommodate another person's body. In my view the appropriate genus is "lounge chairs adapted to the requirements of particular customers". No other genus would fairly describe this kind of goods. It follows from this that no other persons would ordinarily use them, whether or not those persons are suffering from sickness, disease or disablement.

29. I have concluded therefore that the subject goods fall within the terms of Item 123 and are, for reasons already given, therefore exempt from sales tax. Accordingly the objection decision is set aside and the assessment reduced to nil.
