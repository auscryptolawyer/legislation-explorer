Last Updated: 11 March 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Assessable Income - Livestock - calculation of losing value of stock - whether value other than market selling value or cost value may be adopted.

Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) – ss 28-34 and 36

Applicant

Respondent

No. TT86/371

No. B.5156/1986

AAT Decision No 3206

Mr P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

13 November 1986

Hobart

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. B5156/1986

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And

Respondent REASONS FOR DECISION

The question arising upon this reference is as to the correct manner of valuing Livestock or a primary producer at the end of a year of income. In his return of income for the year of income ended 30 June 1981 the Applicant brought to account "gross profit cattle trading" $39,639 and in relation to sheep trading a loss of $623 - a net gain of $39,016. The cattle profit was calculated in a Livestock account presented as follows:-

Loss on sheep trading was similarly calculated. As nothing turns on the question as to whether the livestock comprised cattle or sheep Ishall hereafter refer only to cattle.

2. Upon assessment the Commissioner increased the taxable income or the Applicant from $17,637 to $47,748 explaining the adjustment in an adjustment sheet which accompanied the notice of assessment which said:

"Add

Livestock in Victoria and Tasmania treated as separate entities and therefore two livestock schedules for both sheep and cattle have been prepared, giving rise to this increase in income $30,111."

The difference between the approaches or the Applicant and the Commissioner arises because to calculate the value of closing livestock the Applicant has aggregated the "carried forward value" of opening livestock and the costs of purchases during the year and divided that aggregate by the sum of the numbers of opening stock and the number of purchases. The Commissioner contends that in so doing the Applicant has not complied with the "Trading Stock" provisions (Section 28 and following) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") and, in particular, Section 32 of that Act which provides:-

"The value of livestock to be taken into account, at: the end of the year of income shall be, at the option of the taxpayer, its cost price or market selling value, and where a taxpayer does not exercise his option within the time and in the manner prescribed, the value so to be taken into account shall be the cost price:

Provided that, where a taxpayer satisfies the Commissioner that there are circumstances which justify the adoption by him of some value other than cost price or market selling value for the whole or part of his livestock, he may, with the leave of the Commissioner, adopt that other value."

3. The Applicant acknowledges that he has not calculated the value of Livestock by reference to either "cost price or market selling value" but claims that the Commissioner should be satisfied that there are circumstances which justify the adoption by the Applicant "of some value other than cost price or market selling value" and that he should be allowed to adopt that other value.

4. The background to the problem lies in the circumstance that at the commencement of the year of income, the Applicant was carrying on business in Victoria running a cattle and sheep property. It became uneconomic for him to continue on that property and ho chose to start up on another. The selected property was in Tasmania, That being so the Applicant had a choice. He could either transfer his Victorian stock to Tasmania or sell his Victorian stock and buy new stock in Tasmania. He chose the latter course, considering the former course to be unsound economically because of freight costs and the costs which would be occasioned by a substantial period in quarantine which would have been required by Tasmanian authorities. Upon sale of his Victorian cattle he realised their market value $76,362 ($264 per head) - a figure in sharp contrast to the opening value at which they had been brought to account, namely $6,690 ($23.00 per head). That difference was the consequence of having brought natural increase to account over many years at the nominal figures allowed for by Section 34 of the Act.

5. In the case of persons carrying on business involving trading stock the Act in its general operation requires that in addition to the proceeds of sale being treated as assessable income pursuant to Section 25 of the Act, changes in the value of trading stock on hand from year to year should be brought to account. Where the value of all trading stock on hand at the end of a. year of income exceeds the value of all trading stock on hand at the beginning of that year, the assessable income of the taxpayer shall include the amount of the excess (Section 28(2)). Where the value of opening stock exceeds the value of closing stock the amount of the excess shall be an allowable deduction (Section 28(3)). Implementation of those principles requires that an assessment of the "value" of trading stock be made at the commencement and at the end of the year of income in question. The value of opening stock ("whether livestock or other trading stock) to be taken into account at the beginning of the year is fixed by that Act. Section 19 provides that opening value shall be equal shall be equal to closing value at the end of the year immediately preceding the year of income. Assessment of "value" at the end of the year of income varies according to whether the stock in question is livestock (Section 32) or other trading stock (Section 31). The difference is occasioned by the circumstance that Livestock numbers can rise by reason of natural increase. Section 32 (already quoted) deals with the problem of livestock closing values but it does so in the context of other provisions. Section 34 is particularly significant. It provides a statutory formula for fixing "the cost pries per head of natural increase" to be taken into account in calculating "cost price" pursuant to Section 32. Further, Section 33 provided that a taxpayer may not change "a basis of valuation of his livestock" without the leave of the Commissioner.

6. The result is that, however it may be calculated, a taxpayer such as the present Applicant is obliged to bring to account the "value" of ail of his livestock at the close or each year of income.

7. As to closing values Section 32 offers the Applicant a choice. He may select "market selling value". If he does questions of the origin of the stock and prior "values" will become irrelevant. Whether any part of closing stock was purchased in that year of income or any earlier year; and whether any natural increase on hand was natural increase of that year or any earlier year will have no significance in determining closing market value, even though costs of purchases and "costs" of natural increase in prior years will be reflected in opening value. If he fails to so elect, he will be deemed to have opted for "cost price" in which case prior "values" will influence the final result. In the circumstances of this case there was no election for market price. That being so, but for the proviso should it be applicable, Section 32 requires that the "cost price" of the stock on hand should be brought to account in fixing closing values. That is what the Commissioner has done.

8. However, the Applicant says that notwithstanding that, the circumstances which forced the Applicant to sell his Victorian stock and to buy fresh stock in Tasmania are such that he should be allowed to adopt another measure: or valuation. He has proposed an averaging of the actual cost of his recent purchases and of the fictional costs created by the operation of Section 34 and 29. In doing so he suggests that cattle having an average cost - established recently in the market - of $297 per beast be valued at $126 per beast. I find it hard to understand that the figure so proposed constitutes an expression of "value" at all. At the same time I recognise that Section 32 contemplates that "value" may be expressed otherwise than as "cost price" or "market selling price". It has been suggested that one valuation concept which might be contemplated by the proviso is "probate value". Another might be discounted market value adjusted by reason or" special characteristics of the holding such as size or number. Another more likely possibility might be the adoption of a "value" exceeding "cost price" but less than "market selling price".

9. The Applicant by his representative also points to the realities of economic hardship occasioned by the move interstate, that move being made economically more difficult by the presence of no less a natural barrier than Bass Strait. Hardship considerations have been recognised by the Parliament for the Act provides for relief where a "forced sale", whether or not coupled with a replacement of livestock, is occasioned by such considerations as the compulsory acquisition of land; or by natural disasters such as fire, flood, drought and cattle tick (Section 36). But the mode of relief has been by deeming the relevant assessable income to have been derived over a period of years (of Section 36 (3) and (3a)), not by proposing a fictitious value. Nothing in the argument persuades me that Section 32 is to be construed as authorising the adoption of a fictitious value.

10. I would uphold the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection. Hopefully, the Applicant will find that the long-term advantages of his move will outweigh the short-term disadvantage of losing the benefit of deferring as income some of the financial advantages which accrued in the past.
