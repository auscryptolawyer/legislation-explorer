Last Updated: 6 May 2026

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

  1. There be no order as to costs of the interlocutory process filed by Stingray Management Pty Ltd and Zagga Investments Pty Ltd on 18 August 2025 and amended on 23 December 2025.

Note: Entry of orders is dealt with in Rule 39.32 of the Federal Court Rules 2011.

REASONS FOR JUDGMENT

MARKOVIC J:

  1. This proceeding was brought by Rahul Goyal and Catherine Margaret Conneely in their capacity as joint and several liquidators of FSM Developments Pty Ltd (in liquidation) ACN 653 713 878 and four related companies seeking orders under ss 596A, 596B, 596D, 596F and 597 of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) and r 30.34 of the Federal Court Rules 2011 (Cth) for the issue of examination summons and orders for production of documents to specified persons and/or companies. The orders sought included that orders for production be issued to Ray White Capital Pty Limited ACN 611 275 711 (RWC) and Keyview Investment Management Limited ACN 665 351 726 and that summons for examination be issued to representatives of those companies.
  2. By amended interlocutory process filed on 23 December 2025 (Amended IP) Stingray Management Pty Ltd ACN 681 727 015 and Zagga Investments Pty Ltd ACN 615 154 786 sought orders, among others, that they be entitled to uplift and copy documents produced by RWC and Keyview in response to the orders for production made compelling those companies to produce documents in this proceeding. Ultimately, the Amended IP was resolved by consent between the parties save in relation to the question of costs.
  3. RWC and Keyview seek an order that Stingray and Zagga pay their costs of production of the documents in response to the Amended IP in relation to the work done in reviewing the documents for confidentiality, negotiating a confidentiality regime and producing the documents and that those costs be paid on a lump sum basis. Stingray and Zagga oppose the orders sought.

BACKGROUND

  1. On 14 May 2024 Perpetual Corporate Trust Limited ACN 000 341 533 and La Trobe Financial Services Pty Limited ACN 006 479 527 entered into a loan agreement with, among others, FSM as borrower (La Trobe Facility). That facility was relevantly secured by a first ranking mortgage over a property located at 317-335 Liverpool Road, Ashfield, New South Wales (Ashfield Property).
  2. Zagga financed another company related to FSM’s director, Frank Guo, to develop a project at 2-4 Eton Road and 205 Pacific Highway, Lindfield, New South Wales. Zagga’s facility was secured by a second ranking mortgage over the Ashfield Property.
  3. On 7 August 2024, pursuant to a deed of assignment, AMAL Trustees Pty Limited as trustee for Adelaide Street Holdings Trust, which is managed by RWC, acquired the debt from La Trobe. The La Trobe Facility was subsequently amended by way of a deed of amendment and restatement.
  4. On 1 November 2024 Zagga redeemed the mortgage by paying the secured debt owing under the La Trobe Facility and transferred the mortgage to its subsidiary, Stingray.
  5. On 7 November 2024 Stingray and Zagga appointed the liquidators as joint and several administrators of FSM and related companies. On 12 December 2024 FSM and its related companies were placed into liquidation.
  6. Stingray entered into a funding deed with the liquidators which provided a funding facility to be used by the liquidators in discharging their duties and functions in the liquidation of FSM and its related companies.
  7. Zagga is a competitor of RWC and Keyview, each of which is described as an alternative investment firm operating in the real estate sector.
  8. RWC and Keyview responded to the orders for production made by the Court and directed to them in this proceeding.
  9. In the meantime, on 25 April 2025 Stingray commenced a proceeding as an eligible applicant under Pt 5.9 of the Corporations Act in the Supreme Court of New South Wales (Supreme Court Proceeding) seeking orders for production and summons for examination in relation to FSM and its related companies. Orders for production and summons for examination were issued to RWC and Keyview and their respective representatives in the Supreme Court Proceeding.
  10. By the Amended IP (which was first filed on 18 August 2025) Stingray and Zagga relevantly sought orders that they be permitted to uplift and copy documents produced to this Court by RWC and Keyview in response to the orders for production issued to each of those parties in this proceeding and that they be released from the implied undertaking in relation to those documents.
  11. On 20 August 2025 this Court made orders by consent granting Stingray and Zagga liberty to uplift copies of documents from those produced by RWC and Keyview in this proceeding over which they had not made confidentiality claims and standing over the balance of their interlocutory process to 28 August 2025 for hearing on questions of confidentiality.
  12. On 25 August 2025 orders were made in the Supreme Court Proceeding in effect staying the orders for production issued to RWC and Keyview and the examination of their respective representatives in that proceeding.
  13. On 26 August 2025 orders were made by consent standing Stingray and Zagga’s interlocutory process over to the Corporations List and vacating the orders made on 20 August 2025 for the hearing or questions of confidentiality.
  14. Thereafter the parties engaged in negotiations in order to facilitate the further production of documents which were subject to claims for confidentiality which I do not intend to describe. In short, those negotiations were detailed and continued over a period of some six months culminating in further production and orders made by consent on 19 February 2026 dismissing the Amended IP and allowing the parties to file and serve submissions on the question of costs.

COSTS OF THE AMENDED IP

  1. There is no dispute that the Court has power to award costs to a party who produces documents in answer to an order made under s 597 of the Corporations Act for production of documents: see s 23 and s 43 Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth), s 597 Corporations Act.
  2. In Hodgkinson, in the matter of Kupang Resources Ltd (Subject to Deed of Company Arrangement) [2017] FCA 1342; (2017) 125 ACSR 301, in the context of an application for security for costs of compliance with an examination summons and orders for production issued under s 596A and s 597 of the Corporations Act I considered the issue of when a party can obtain its costs of production, referring to the principles at [42]-[47] including relevantly:
    1. Surpion Pty Ltd v M R Works Pty Ltd (recs and mgrs apptd) (2010) 80 ACSR 635; [2010] FCA 1262 (Surpion) concerned a review of a decision of a registrar who had made an order for payment of 60% of a liquidator’s costs incurred in an inquiry into whether documents produced by the examinee, a solicitor in the firm that had acted for the companies before they were wound up, at an examination conducted under s 596B of the Corporations Act were privileged.
    2. In considering the issue before him Finklestein J summarised the effect of the cases where examinees had made application for their costs at [16] as follows:
The remaining cases that deal with cost orders concern applications for costs by examinees. The effect of the cases may be summarised as follows:

(A) It is unjust to require a person to attend and give evidence or produce documents without making adequate provision for his/her costs of coming to and from the place of examination together with the costs of his/her sustenance while required to remain there: see Bank of New South Wales v Withers [1928] ArgusLawRp 98; (1981) 35 ALR 21 at 37, a subpoena case.

(B) The power to summon a person to be examined confers the right to impose conditions or make ancillary orders: Re Kempal Pty Ltd (in liq) (1989) 17 NSWLR 550 at 551; Re Spedley Securities Ltd (in liq); Ex parte Australian National Industries Ltd (1991) 4 ACSR 322 at 327.

(C) This right to impose conditions and the power to make ancillary orders enables the issuing court to ensure that an examinee be paid his/her reasonable costs of attending the examination. In some cases the examinee can also be compensated for searching for the documents (if any) which he/she is required to produce: Re Equiticorp Finance Ltd; Ex parte Brock (1992) 6 ACSR 725 at 734 (Re Equiticorp Finance) and the cases cited therein.

(D) The power cannot be exercised to cover an examinee’s consultation with lawyers to prepare for the examination: Re Equiticorp Finance Ltd; Ex parte Brock (No 2) (1992) 27 NSWLR 391 at 397; 7 ACSR 13 at 18.

(E) Generally the power is not available for the examinee to obtain the costs of legal representation: Re Moreton Joinery Pty Ltd [1975] Qd R 121 at 122; Re Equiticorp Finance at 734. But, in limited circumstances, (for example when the examination may properly be characterised as “litigation” between the liquidator and the examinee, payment of the costs of representation of the examinee may be justified: Re Lutscher; Ex parte Waddell (1877) 6 Ch D 328 at 331 (Re Lutscher); Re Appleton French & Scrafton Ltd [1905] 1 Ch 749 at 755-6.

(F) In some cases it has been suggested that a distinction should be drawn between examinees who were officers of the company (insiders) and third parties (outsiders) and that a costs order should generally only be made in favour of outsiders: Re Equiticorp Finance at 734. In Re Imobridge Pty Ltd (in liq) [1999] 1 Qd R 38 at 52, Lee J doubted whether insiders should be treated any differently from outsiders. I think there is no principled reason for the distinction.

(G) Sometimes it is best to await the conclusion of the examination when all the relevant circumstances will be known before exercising the power: Re Total Entity Pty Ltd (2003) 47 ACSR 577; [2003] NSWSC 924 at [27]; Re Fox Home Loans Pty Ltd (in liq) [2005] NSWSC 1050 at [4].
  1. RWC and Keyview each give reasons as to why a costs order should be made in their favour.
  2. First, they both submit that they are not insiders directly concerned in the management of FSM. To the extent such a distinction exists for the purposes of determining the entitlement to costs in a case such as this, I accept that is so. While the examinations by the liquidators (and as intended by Stingray) may have been for the purpose of investigating the circumstances relating to the deed of amendment and restatement entered into by the director, Mr Guo, on behalf of FSM, including the extent to which RWC, Keyview and persons related to those companies were involved in its execution, RWC and Keyview are not insiders in the sense contemplated in the authorities. They are not officers of FSM or its related companies.
  3. Secondly, RWC and Keyview submit that the orders ought in the Amended IP required them each to undertake considerable work in reviewing documents for commercial sensitivity and confidentiality and, at least initially, in a limited time frame. They say that it would be oppressive and unjust for them to be materially out of pocket, particularly as a result of being compelled to protect their confidential and commercially sensitive material. However, as Stingray submits, it in fact pursued the Amended IP so as to avoid a multiplicity of proceedings. In other words, Stingray sought to minimise the prejudice that would be caused to RWC and Keyview by seeking the same production in the Supreme Court Proceeding as the liquidators sought in this proceeding. Stingray’s intent was thus to minimise cost and prejudice to RWC and Keyview.
  4. True it is that RWC and Keyview have incurred costs in negotiating a confidentiality regime and reviewing the documents which were to be produced in line with that regime. However, most of the costs claimed are not for undertaking searches of the material to be produced which, in an appropriate case, may be compensated and are to be compensated by the liquidators. Rather the costs claimed principally relate to the review of documents already identified and produced to the liquidators for confidentiality as well as engaging in correspondence with the parties, the Court and as between each of RWC and Keyview and their respective solicitors.
  5. Thirdly, RWC submits that, unlike the liquidators, Stingray and Zagga did not seek documents because they were carrying out examinations in fulfillment of a public duty but because they were pursuing their private interests and for use in parallel examinations in the Supreme Court of New South Wales in circumstances where the liquidators were already carrying out examinations. RWC also says that ultimately Stingray’s examinations went nowhere because they were stayed. This submission does not take the matter any further. As I have already observed, and as Stingray explained, the purpose of the Amended IP was to minimise the very prejudice about which RWC and Keyview complained. That there may be duplication in the investigations being undertaken by the liquidators and by Stingray is not a matter for this application.
  6. Having regard to those matters I am not satisfied that an order should be made that Stingray pay RWC’s and Keyview’s costs of the Amended IP. The additional work required to respond to the Amended IP concerned a review of material which had already been identified for production for confidentiality claims, given that Stingray and each of RWC and Keyview are competitors. Ultimately the claims for confidentiality were resolved by consent and limited to a portion only of the documents.
  7. Given that conclusion I do not need to address the question of whether a lump sum order is appropriate in the circumstances of this case.
  8. It follows that there will be no order as to costs of the Amended IP.
I certify that the preceding twenty-seven (27) numbered paragraphs are a true copy of the Reasons for Judgment of the Honourable Justice Markovic.
