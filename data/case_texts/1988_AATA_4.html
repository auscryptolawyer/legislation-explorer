ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - deductions - whether business carried on – cattle leasing project - single lease payment - whether capital

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss51, 260

REASONS FOR DECISION

6 January 1988 Deputy President RK Todd

  1. The applicant in this matter is a medical practitioner who, in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1981, had a claim for a deduction of $42,026 disallowed both initially and on objection. The deduction claimed was the applicant's share of a partnership loss incurred in a venture involving the leasing of cattle.
  2. The applicant, who presented his own case with clarity, explained that the proposal first came to his notice when he spoke to his accountant about options that would broaden his financial base, provide a medium term investment and also involve deferment of taxation liability.
  3. The brochure which the applicant was given was tendered in evidence, and it is useful to set out the program outline as described in the brochure:

"1. A partnership will be formed under the Queensland Mercantile Act (1867-1896). This partnership will consist of 1500 units @ $1,000 each. Total investment $1,500,000.

2. The partnership will then negotiate with the lease company and obtain the following lease:

240 breeders to be transferred to the partnership name, immediately the lease is signed. These breeders will be leased for a 5 year period, with an option to renew the lease agreement. The lease payments will be as follows:

1 payment of $92,121 followed by

59 payments of $92,105

Total Lease Payments for 60 months = $5,526,316

Less Discount (available if finalized

in 12 months) = 276,316

$5,250,000 The lease company will guarantee 500 live calves over the first three breeding seasons, provided that all 1,500 units have been taken up in the partnership. In the event of the partnership being closed with less than 1,500 units, the guarantee will be geared in the above ratio to the number of units in the partnership (as will all other figures mentioned in this proposal).

IN THE EVENT THAT THE OPTION UNDER THE DISCOUNT CLAUSE OF THE LEASE IS EXERCISED THEN:

  1. The partnership will obtain a loan of $3,875,00 from a financier over a five year period. It is envisaged that this loan will be interest free for the first four years with 5% flat payable for the balance of the term. To secure this loan the financier would take a mortgage over all partnership assets with the exception of the first 500 progeny born, from the commencement of the partnership, or cash resulting from the sale of the said progeny.
  2. The partnership will set aside an amount of $125,000 in a Running Costs Account and then pay out the total lease amount of $5,250,00 on or before 30.6.81, thus obtaining a discount of $276,316. This will result in an approximate reduction of $3,500 to the assessable income of each special partner for every $1,000 unit held."
  3. In fact only 105 units @ $1,000 per unit, of which 12 were purchased by the applicant, were taken up by the public and consequently the whole project was reduced in scale. The option under the discount clause of the lease was exercised.
  4. In his evidence, the applicant explained that before investing in the proposal, the applicant spoke to the promoters, to a friend who was involved in the cattle industry, and to a second accountant who was from a large firm. Both accountants said that the project looked worthwhile and also that they had previously dealt with the promoters and had had no particular problems.

6. In summarising his evidence and the basis for his claim that a business was being carried on, the applicant stated:

"I guess that the basis of the argument that I am trying to present is that to the best of my knowledge that I entered into a scheme which was -you know, it certainly was geared up to be a tax deferment scheme that we would get some benefit initially, but that tax would be paid back subsequently when the company made a profit. It was certainly - I want to try and make the point that it was, from my point of view, not a tax avoidance scheme. I also want to make the point that as far as I believe that the project was in place and running at the time it was said to be in place and that from the information I gained from others, although the management was at times suspect, that the actual cattle leasing did take place and that a herd was established and was sold and that there was subsequent profits which have been in fact recognised by the Taxation Department and been included in my assessment in subsequent years. So I am just really trying to establish the legitimacy of the whole operation."

7. The Commissioner's reasons for rejecting the claim, which were all in the alternative, can be summarised as being:

(1) That the lease payment was capital and therefore excluded from 51(1);

(2) That the payment, having been made "once and for all" prior to the commencement of trading, was consequently not a working expense in the course of trading;

(3) That the purpose of the payment, subjectively and/or objectively, was to achieve a taxation advantage, and for no other purpose, and was therefore outside the provisions of s51(1);

(4) In the context of the second limb of s51(1), the payment was one not "necessarily incurred" because the payment was one that no business man would regard as worthwhile, in the circumstances, to be made from a business point of view;
  • (5) That s.260 operated to deny any deduction;
  • (6) That the transaction was, at least in part, a sham; and
  • (7) That the parties themselves were not conducting a business. They were in receipt of the proceeds of a business being carried on by someone else.
  1. In support of these contentions the respondent prepared a flow chart, reproduced here as Appendix 1, showing the transactions that occurred on 29 June 1981. Evidence of each of these transactions in the form of copies of bank statements, bank deposit slips, cheques and trust account ledgers was included in the formal documents.
  2. The flow chart exposes the reality of the financial transactions and of the "round robin" contained therein. It can be seen that the amount of $276,420 was introduced to the partnership from M Pty Ltd, passed to G Investments Pty Ltd and then returned to M Pty Ltd.
  3. From the evidence given by the applicant it was clear that, until receiving the documents for this hearing, he was unaware of this "round robin" and believed that the "loan" of $276,420 was to be used to develop the property. It also became apparent from his evidence, and from letters included in the documents, that the applicant had become dissatisfied with the management of the project and had begun corresponding with the management in September 1983 and subsequently with the other special partners in April 1984. The special partners were, however, legally powerless due to the structure of the partnership, and in the year of income ended 30 June 1985 the whole of the herd was sold.
  4. The difficulties confronting the applicant in establishing the deductibility of the amounts claimed are enormous. For example, the documentation of the agreement contained internal inconsistencies such as different cattle being identified in the head lease from those in the sub-lease, and the rights and obligations of the parties to both lease and sub-lease being incapable of reconciliation. This is borne out by the fact that while clause 1 of the sub-lease refers to the sub-lease being granted for the "exclusive use and enjoyment of the cattle", clauses 8 and 9 require the sub-lessor to fulfil a large number of obligations which would be impossible unless possession of the cattle were retained.
  5. The applicant was also at a disadvantage because, as stated above, he was unaware of the true structure of the arrangement until informed of it by the respondent. While he was undoubtedly conscious of the tax advantage to be gained by participation in the venture, I was left with the impression that he had formed, or had persuaded himself of, the view that the venture was a genuine commercial endeavour in the area of primary production. I am nevertheless afraid that I am a long way from being so persuaded, and I consider that the taxpayer's claims must be rejected. For that purpose it is unnecessary to refer to the more refined of the Commissioner's submissions, namely those referred to as submissions (3), (4), (5), (6) and (7) in para 7 above. In my opinion the case is readily determined by submissions (1) and (2), the correctness of each of which I accept.

Submission 1

13. This was that the lease payment was capital and therefore excluded from s.51 as an allowable deduction. The basis of the submission was that the payment that was made represented the purchase of an asset, namely a lease or a sub-lease of cattle from which income was to be produced in the form of progeny as a saleable product. The payment was made once and for all and was not a recurrent payment. There was here, it was said, an "up-front" payment made prior to the carrying on of trading and prior to the production of an economic object for sale. Such a payment did not possess the character of a payment of a kind traditionally regarded as of a revenue nature. I am completely satisfied that this is the correct view of the facts in this case and I so find. The law on the matter is to be found in the authorities referred to me by Mr. Uren, Q.C who appeared for the Commissioner: Sun Newspapers Ltd. v. FC of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 361-3; Texas Australasia Ltd v FC of T [1940] HCA 9; (1939-40) 63 CLR 382 at 468; John Fairfax and Sons Pty Ltd v FC of T [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at 36; Hallstrom's Pty Ltd v FC of T [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at 646; and FC of T v Creer (1986) 65 ALR 485 at 490, 493. The summation of the passages referred to for present purposes is that a payment of the kind here made lacks the quality of recurrency that would otherwise give a payment of rent a non-capital character. The purpose of this payment was in my view to obtain a capital asset.

Submission 2

  1. This was that the payment having been made "once and for all" prior to the commencement of trading it was not a working expense in the course of trading. It was an expense remote in time from the earning of any income in respect of which the expense was incurred and was therefore not an outlay embraced by the terms of s51(1). Mr Uren here referred to the John Fairfax Case (above) at 48-9.
  2. I again agree with this submission. Accepting the applicant's prognosis of what was going to occur, there would be no trading operations for a long time. What was going to happen over a period of seven years was the acquisition of a herd of cattle from which income might later be earned by their sale. The payment here possessed the character of a payment for the acquisition of an asset in respect of which trading would later occur.
  3. As previously indicated, it is unnecessary, in view of the findings and conclusions in respect of Mr. Uren's first and second submissions, to consider the questions of sham, of mere taxation advantage, and of the applicability of the old s.260. It would be evident enough that I am of the view that in broad terms the whole venture was one which in objective terms was no more than a taxation scheme lacking any business reality. Those homely words however lack legal precision, and while they may well translate into the legal conclusions which Mr. Uren traced out for me (necessarily so, in case his initial submissions should not succeed), and which he traced out fully and with great clarity, the very considerable exercise involved in such translation would be for present purposes a work of supererogation the doing of which would be "more ... than of bounden duty is required".
  4. In the circumstances, the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
