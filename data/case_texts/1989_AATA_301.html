ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION - ASSESSABLE INCOME - Arts Administrator - overseas travel to undertake Apprentice Director traineeship and converse with other Arts Administrators and technicians, and attend theatrical performances to improve expertise for future employment - unemployed at time of expenditure - whether expenditure incurred deductible.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, sub - s.51 (1)

Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v F.C. of T. (1935) CLR 295

Ronpibon Tin N.L. v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60

REASONS FOR DECISION

7 February 1989 Deputy President R.A. Layton

Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)

Dr D.B. Williams (Member)

  1. This is an application for review of an assessment made by the Commissioner wherein he disallowed overseas travel expenses amounting to $10,083 claimed by the applicant as a deduction pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act") in the income year ending 30 June 1984.
  2. The Commissioner does not dispute the quantum of the claim nor that such expenditure was incurred during that financial year. The Commissioner's basis for disallowing the claim is that the expenditure was not incurred in the gaining or producing of assessable income as required by the section.
  3. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by her father, and the Commissioner was represented by one of his officers. The information before the Tribunal consisted of supplementary documents lodged with the Tribunal and further documentary material was tendered by the applicant during the course of the hearing. The applicant, being unable to appear at the hearing, gave evidence by telephone from Sydney.
  4. Certain facts were agreed between the parties and these are set out in a document, Exhibit Al, but this document was amended as the respondent did not agree to all matters set out therein and required evidence from the applicant to prove some aspects.
  5. We have no difficulty accepting the evidence of the applicant whom we regarded as a truthful witness. Our findings of fact on the evidence are as follows.
  6. The applicant is aged 30 years and is presently employed as a Stage Company Manager. She graduated from University in 1979 with a Bachelor of Arts degree including First Class Honours in Drama and History. As part of her Honours degree in Drama, the applicant undertook the study of "stage management" which included both theory and practice of arts administration. Following completion of her degree, the applicant gained employment as a Production Manager for an amateur theatre company and also undertook part-time work with the Adelaide Festival of Arts.
  7. On 29 December 1980, the applicant commenced employment with the Australian Opera Company. She was initially employed in the stage management department and progressed to the position of Associate Workshop Manager in the scenery-making section. This position required her to be responsible for budgeting of props and costuming.
  8. The Australian Opera Company is the most prestigious and highly subsidised opera company in Australia. The administrative heirarchy within the Australian Opera Company at that time consisted of the General Manager, Artistic Director, Assistant Artistic Director, Technical Director, Assistant Technical Director, Company Manager, Stage Managers and technicians. The applicant as Associate Workshop Manager worked directly with one of the Stage Managers.
  9. The applicant gave evidence that she consulted the Artistic Director of the Australian Opera Company for advice as to her future career path in the theatre and particularly in arts administration. The Artistic Director advised the applicant that opportunities for further development within the Australian Opera Company were limited and that there was no appropriate vacancy which would suit her career path. He also stated he was not aware of any other career possibilities which may be available locally. The Artistic Director suggested that the applicant seek an association with one of the "sister" opera companies overseas and also suggested a second language might be useful in increasing her expertise in the area of arts administration (Exhibit A2).
  10. Acting on this advice, the applicant contacted the Artistic Director of the Canadian Opera Company and managed to obtain a three month traineeship with that company. She also successfully arranged to undertake instruction in the Italian language at the Eurocentre in Florence. The Australian Opera Company and other companies also provided the applicant with many contacts mainly in the operatic and music fields in the United States, United Kingdom and Europe, and she wrote to these contacts to make arrangements to meet with them as part of her planned overseas itinerary.
  11. The applicant ceased her employment with the Australian Opera Company on 29 October 1983. She was at that time earning $400 per week. After a short period of holidaying with her family, she used the remaining time until 4 April 1984 to make the above arrangements. Her itinerary is contained in the annexure to her income tax return. Her travel commenced on 4 April 1984 and ceased on her return to Australia on 30 December 1984.
  12. The applicant initially travelled to America visiting Los Angeles, Washington and New York where she attended various schools, theatres and centres. The applicant met with a number of contacts to discuss make-up and special effects including techniques utilising lasers and lighting, and observed technical "set ups". In addition, she viewed a variety of operatic and other theatrical productions.
  13. On 1 May 1984, the applicant began her pre-arranged traineeship as an Apprentice Director with the Canadian Opera Company. This included participation in planning for the Opera Season as well as Apprentice Directorship work on two productions and stage management for another.
  14. On 1 July 1984, the applicant went to the United Kingdom where she met with various marketing and technical administrators as well as attending a number of theatrical productions.
  15. For a period of two weeks in July 1984, the applicant took time for a holiday in Greece, Egypt and Turkey, none of the expenses or fares of which constituted any part of her claim.
  16. The applicant then travelled to Florence where she attended the Eurocentre to learn Italian as from 29 September 1984.
  17. She hired a car and also travelled by Eurail to France, Italy and Brussels as detailed in her income tax return annexure. Many of these trips involved attendance at various operatic and musical productions at publicised Festivals.
  18. The applicant returned to London in December 1984 and again visited various theatre companies and attended opera, ballet, and musical productions as well as workshops. Finally she travelled to Hong Kong where she attended the Hong Kong Arts Festival before returning home on 30 December 1984.
  19. The applicant gave evidence that whilst in the United Kingdom and United States, apart from her two week holiday period, five out of every seven days would be spent attending some theatrical production, sometimes as a member of the audience, at other times in the orchestra pit or in the lighting box or backstage. She would preferably try and arrange a meeting with the managers or administrators prior to the perfomance and then view their performance later in the evening. In Europe, slightly less time was spent in such activities, namely four out of seven days.
    1. The applicant has claimed her air fares, local travel expenses, entertainment and the cost of theatre and festival tickets, as well as accommodation, all of which totaled $10,083.71.
  20. Before her departure for overseas, the applicant had not negotiated any re-employment on return to Australia, either with the Australian Opera Company or with any other employer. Whilst she was overseas, the applicant's father sent to her an advertisement for a position of Senior Stage Manager with the South Australian State Theatre Company. She applied for this position upon receiving notice of the vacancy and was successful in gaining same in early October 1984, that is, before her return.
  21. Following her return to Australia the applicant commenced employment as Principal Stage Manager with the South Australian State Theatre Company on 7 January 1985 and remained in that employment until 16 November 1985. At the time of this engagement, the State Theatre Company was contemplating staging a musical production which required knowledge of the use of large-scale scenery and flying equipment. The applicant had experience in these areas and was engaged by the State Theatre Company at a salary of $430 per week.
  22. On 21 November 1985, the applicant obtained employment with T. Pty Ltd as Deputy Stage Manager, her salary being $500 per week. T. Pty Ltd was a commercial theatre production company and staged a large musical which toured Adelaide, Melbourne and Sydney. After the production had finished touring, and the applicant's term of employment with T. Pty Ltd was completed, she obtained employment in November 1986 with D. M. Pty Ltd as a Production Stage Manager for a production starring two international celebrities. The company wanted someone with touring knowledge and experience in handling difficult performers. The applicant satisfied both criteria, in particular because of her experience in the operatic field, and she was engaged on a salary of $600 per week.
  23. When the production's season ended in May 1987, the applicant returned to South Australia to attend to her personal business. During this time she undertook a little employment which included being Show Director for a commercial company and Workshop Stage Manager for a State Theatre Company, both at a salary of approximately $500 per week. The applicant did this piece-work to obtain further experience.
  24. In February 1988, the applicant was employed as a Company and Tour Manager for the Adelaide Festival of Arts at a salary of $700 per week to manage a visiting dance theatre from New York. This employment terminated when the tour was completed.
  25. The applicant's present employment is with a commercial theatre production company as Company Manager for a drama production. This is her most prestigious appointment to date and she is currently receiving a salary of $600 per week. The structure of commercial production companies is not as elaborate as Government subsidised companies, the former consisting of a Producer, Production Manager and either one or two Company Managers who may take the role of artistic and/or technical managers.
  26. The applicant gave evidence that employment in arts administration is generally on a production basis. One does not remain employed beyond the particular production for which one is initially engaged. The exception is employment with a subsidised company such as the Australian Opera Company or the State Theatre Companies. Therefore, it is part of the applicant's employment pattern that she be engaged on a short-term basis for a specified period depending on the timing of productions. In a general sense, moving toward her desired career path of a Company Management position must necessarily be indirect, as there are no specific qualifications for such a position and all administrative experience is valuable. Consequently, the applicant has utilised many opportunities often at a lesser salary to increase her experience and knowledge.
    1. The applicant has not been engaged in any operatic work since her return from overseas. Instead, she has worked on theatre, dance and musical productions in a managerial capacity.
    2. We turn now to the legal considerations. In this review we are only concerned with the first limb of sub-s.51 (1) of the Assessment Act, as there is no evidence to suggest that the applicant was involved in a business; she has at all times earned income as an employee.
  27. The case of Amalgamated Zinc (DeBavay's) Ltd v F.C. of T. (1935) CLR 295, is authority for the proposition that the expression "in gaining or producing" in sub-s.51 (1), has the force of "in the course of gaining or producing" and as Dixon J remarked "looks rather to the scope of the operations or activities and the relevance thereto of the expenditure rather than to the purpose in itself", at page 309.
  28. There is no requirement in sub-s.51 (1) that the expenditure must occur in the year in which assessable income is gained or produced (Ronpibon Tin N.L. v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47). Expenditure can therefore relate to either income derived or yet to be derived (see also Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60). In this case, the applicant suggests that the expenditure relates to income derived subsequent to the incurring of the expenditure.
  29. It has also been said that expenditure does not meet the requirements of the section if it is incurred by an employee in the getting of employment and not in the doing of it (F.C. of T. v Maddelena (1971) ATC 4161 and Case V7 88 ATC 142).
  30. There has emerged through the case law a variety of judicial formulae which have been applied as tests for the application of sub-s.51 (1). In the case of Ronpibon Tin N.L. (supra), the Full High Court at page 48 stated that "for expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end".
    1. Later the High Court in Lunney v F.C. of T. (1958) 100 CLR 478, their Honours Williams, Kitto and Taylor JJ gave a qualification to that test which they were concerned had been regarded as "exclusive and exhaustive" by commenting that the test of "incidental and relevant" was merely an attribute for appropriate deductibility and one must look not necessarily for the purpose for which an item of expenditure has been incurred, but rather to "the essential character of the expenditure itself" which must be either a business or an income producing expense. This suggested test has been described as "the essential character" test and has also been noted as being unrealistic if it were applied rigidly (see discussion in CCH Australian Federal Taxation Reporter Vol.3, 22-105).
  31. A still later development is what has been called the "perceived connection" test which is said to have specifically emerged in F.C. of T. v Hatchett 71 ATC 4181 at 4187. This case concerned self education expenses of a teacher who incurred them whilst obtaining a teacher's higher certificate. Menzies J at 418 said "... there must be a perceived connection between the outgoing and the assessable income" and that merely the self evident fact that a teacher who pursued university studies was likely to be a better teacher and more likely to obtain promotion in the Education Department, was not sufficient.
  32. This test was also promoted by Waddell J in F.C. of T. v Kropp 76 ATC 4406 at 4411, but a different result was obtained for a chartered accountant who resigned his employment with an Australian firm of accountants in order to take up employment with an affiliated organisation in Canada for two years. He then returned after two years and was re-employed by his original Australian company with a promotion and an increase in salary. Waddell J cited the "perceived connection" test with approval and said at page 4411:

"... I infer from the evidence that his promotion and increase in income in April 1974 are to be attributed, to a substantial degree, to his overseas experience to which the outgoing was directed. And, I think, it is also to be inferred that, in the circumstances of this case, the pursuit of overseas experience was inherently likely to result in the taxpayer receiving on his proposed return to Australia promotion and an increase in his salary earlier than he might otherwise have expected."

  1. Justice David Hunt in the case of Martin v F.C. of T. 83 ATC 4722, expressed reservations as to the "perceived connection" test at pages 4724 and 4725 when he said:

"... The 'perceived connection' has not, in my view, replaced the requirement that the expenditure must have been incurred in or in the course of gaining the taxpayer's assessable income. What Menzies J. said in Hatchett's case was that there must be a 'perceived connection' between the outgoing and the assessable income; his Honour did not say that such a connection was sufficient by itself. The terms of the statute remain clear, and the expenditure must be 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income'. Those terms remain the criteria of deductibility notwithstanding the various tests which have been posed in the various cases."

Thereafter David Hunt J cited with approval the test espoused by G.N. Williams J in F.C. of T. v Wilkinson 83 ATC 4295 at 4303, namely "the nexus is established if there is a reasonable assurance that promotion will follow". A similar warning about the application of the "perceived connection" test was given by Ormiston J in F.C. of T. v Klan 85 ATC 4060 at 4065 as well as by the Full Federal Court in Martin's case (supra) on appeal 84 ATC 4513.

  1. An excellent detailed analysis of all the above purported tests is given by Ormiston J in Klan's case (supra) with a re-emphasis by him of the need for the expenditure to be incurred "in" or "in the course of" gaining or producing income and being incidental and relevant to that end, which merely emphasises the wording of the section itself.
  2. Applying this case law and in particular the words of the section to the facts of this case, we note that the applicant was not in employment at the time her expenditure was incurred and therefore her circumstances differ significantly from successful applicants who whilst remaining employees gained deductions for either self education or overseas travelling expenses directed to and/or obtaining new or better paid appointments with the same employer.
  3. We accept that the applicant was clearly directing her attention in undertaking the overseas travel, to better equip herself to perform arts administration activities in the future with a particular emphasis on operatic and musical productions, hence her desire to learn Italian being a classical and important operatic language. But the question this case raises is, can it be said that the applicant's expenditure was incurred "in" or "in the course of" gaining or producing income being that which she has already derived or will derive in the future?
  4. We have no doubt that the applicant is and will continue to be a more attractive candidate for any arts administration position as a consequence of her overseas experience. We have also no difficulty in accepting that she has gained "confidence" as a result of her overseas experience which will be to her advantage in any arts administration career. We have, however, no evidence which satisfies us that any of the applicant's employment since her return from overseas (which has occurred at a higher salary) was secured either in part, or at all, as a consequence of such overseas experience. Nor do we have any evidence even by inference that her overseas experience will be reasonably likely to result in an increased salary in the future. The applicant's own evidence on this dilemma was commendably candid and expressed with clarity.
  5. When asked if there was any relationship between her overseas experience and obtaining employment with T. Pty Ltd, the applicant responded:

"I know that some of the information I managed to obtain whilst overseas I certainly used. Certainly a lot of the contacts I got overseas allowed me to be of use to the Director, but in terms of actually saying, We are going to [the applicant] because she has been to New York and knows about it - I cannot say that."

(transcript, p.28)

Similarly with respect to her present employment, the applicant stated:

"I am now entering a field where I am completely in charge of a production on my own. The Producer will leave me in charge of the production and as such, he trusts my experience and my talents. But I cannot say that because I spent three weeks with the Canadian Opera Company, [the Producer] trusts me to be Company Manager; it does not work like that."

(transcript, pp.28-29)

  1. In our view the applicant must fail, not merely because she was not employed at the time (which, on a strict and narrow interpretation of the decision of Ormiston J in Klan's case (supra) at page 4067, would lead to the demise of her case), but because even on a generous interpretation and evaluation of the relevant case law, we are unable to see that there is the appropriate nexus between her expenditure in 1984 and the gaining or producing of assessable income thereafter and in the future. Even allowing for the random nature of her employment and that all experience must inevitably be good experience, there must be a closer connection between the two activities namely the expenditure and the gaining or producing of assessable income. In addition the applicant would have difficulty in overcoming an argument that her expenditure was incurred in the getting as distinct from the performing of her work, partly because of the necessarily short term nature of her employment. Further many of her specific overseas experience was directed to operatic and musical productions, particularly the learning of Italian, when those experiences have not been the subject of her current nor even her planned future career.
  2. These cases are always difficult and whilst we were impressed with the applicant's professionalism and her clear dedication to arts administration, these considerations alone are not capable of overcoming the statutory requirements of the Act as they have been interpreted over the years by eminent courts and tribunals.
  3. For these reasons the applicant must fail in her objection and the Commissioner's assessment is upheld.
