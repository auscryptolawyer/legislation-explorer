Re Michael James Bergin and Commissioner of Superannuation [1989] AATA 103 (24 April 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: MICHAEL JAMES BERGIN

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V86/367

AAT No. 5044

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mrs J.R. Dwyer(1) (Senior Member), Mrs L. Rodopoulos(1) (Member) and Professor R. Webster(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - airport firefighter - BCC issued pursuant to sub-section 16(4) of the Act specifying condition - "Knee reconstruction operation" - subsequently varied to "history of knee injury" - knee reconstruction operation performed 3 years prior to medical examination for permanent appointment - whether there is a "real risk" that the applicant by reason of his knee condition or for a reason connected with it will not continue to be an eligible employee until he reaches age 65 years - medical evidence that artificial cruciate ligament renders the knee more susceptible to further rupture and that injury and operation increase the risk of development of osteoarthritis in the knee - only small risk of these eventualities leading to retirement before age 65 - evidence as to demands of employment - intention of applicant to obtain promotion to officer - evidence as to fitness requirements of firefighters and officers - applicant's intention to retire before age 65 - possibility of transfer to less physically demanding employment.

Superannuation Act 1976 s.3(1), 16(4) and 154(4) and (6)

Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 sub-s.2(1) and 6(1) Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.37

Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293

Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217

Re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) No 4724, delivered 4 November 1988

Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN 129

HEARING

MELBOURNE

24:4:1989

ORDER

The decision under review will be varied by substituting for the condition "history of knee injury" the condition "left knee injury and reconstruction operation".

DECISION

A. Background

This is an application under s.154(6) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") to review a decision to issue a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") in respect of Mr Bergin. That decision was made on 16 April 1985 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") and was reconsidered by a delegate of the Commissioner pursuant to s.154(4) of the Act who on 28 May 1986 decided to vary the decision by substituting for the condition mentioned in the BCC, "knee reconstruction operation", the condition, "history of knee injury".

2. At the hearing Mr H Borenstein of Counsel appeared for the applicant and Mr B Eadie, Director Superannuation Reconsideration B, appeared for the respondent. The Tribunal received in evidence the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T documents") and the further exhibits tendered during the hearing. The applicant gave evidence and evidence on his behalf was also given by his treating orthopaedic surgeon, Mr Grant and by another general and orthopaedic surgeon, Mr Combe. Evidence on behalf of the respondent was given by Mr Rudd who recently retired from the Civil Aviation Authority and by Mr Davie an orthopaedic surgeon.

3. The facts are straightforward and not in dispute. Mr Bergin joined the Department of Aviation which has recently become the Civil Aviation Authority, as an airport firefighter, in February 1984, when he was aged 26. At that time he had completed his plumbing apprenticeship and worked for some years as a plumber. His appointment as a firefighter was made permanent on 17 September 1984 and he therefore became an eligible employee, as that term is defined in s.3(1) of the Act, from that date.

4. Mr Bergin was medically examined for the purposes of his permanent employment on 5 January 1984. He disclosed to the Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") who examined him, Dr Loewy, that he had undergone a knee reconstruction operation in August 1981. It appears that he produced at the medical examination a copy of a report dated 30 June 1983 from Mr Grant who had performed the operation. That report reads as follows:

"Mr Michael Bergin of 35 Edward Street,

Essendon, had operation of Knee Reconstruction

performed in August 1981.

He made a satisfactory recovery. He has been

under observation at six monthly intervals.

He was last seen on the 8.4.1983, and I

consider that he is fit to apply for a

position with the Melbourne Metropolitan Fire Brigade."

5. Dr Loewy in his report after the examination indicated that Mr Bergin was fit for permanent appointment but he also wrote that he considered Mr Bergin did not meet the general medical standard prescribed in the handbook for CMO's due to the condition of:

"Knee reconstruction operation performed August 1981."

It was on the basis of Dr Loewys's report and the recommendation of a Senior Medical Officer, that the decision was made to issue a BCC specifying "knee reconstruction operation". On 20 April 1985, Mr Bergin requested reconsideration of the decision to issue a BCC, on the ground that his knee was fully rehabilitated.

6. In evidence Mr Bergin explained that he injured his knee when he twisted his foot coming from underneath a house carrying a tool box, while working as a plumber. During the 6 months between the original twisting injury and the operation he returned to work as a plumber. He felt pain on kneeling but had no problem in walking or climbing ladders. Mr Bergin said that he had approximately 8 months off work following the reconstruction operation. He believes that he made a full recovery in that period of time, because when he went back to his previous employment as a plumber, he did not suffer any continuing symptoms from the knee injury or any continuing weakness in the left leg. He was able to perform his duties as a plumber without restriction. Mr Bergin also said that he played basketball, tennis, golf, volleyball, badminton, rode a bike and ran after the operation. He said he did not find himself restricted in any way in any of those sports.

7. Mr Bergin said that he does not find work as a firefighter any more arduous than being a plumber and he has had no problems whatsoever in carrying out his duties as a firefighter. He still plays volleyball, badminton, golf, tennis, runs and rides a bike. He said he has no pains in his knee resulting from the injury when he does any of these activities.

8. Mr Bergin agreed that as a firefighter he has to work in all kinds of weather. He said that it can be very cold in Melbourne and he may have to work in mist, rain or heavy fog. He said that occasionally a runway may be slippery because of ice, snow or other reasons such as fuel spills. He said that working as a firefighter involves running and jumping from a height and carrying breathing apparatus and hoses. On occasions a firefighter may have to carry a person on his shoulders as well. He explained that a firefighter is always expected to work as quickly and as safely as possible, in any given conditions.

9. It is clear from Mr Bergin's evidence that he enjoys his duties as a firefighter. He said it had been his ambition to become a firefighter from the time he left school. It was because he was too young to do so, that he did his plumber's apprenticeship first. He said he hopes to become a fire officer and he is now eligible to undertake an officer training course because he has completed 5 years of service as a firefighter. He has also obtained a certificate of fire technology from Swinburne College of TAFE and he is now doing an associate diploma of fire technology which he will finish in December 1989. He was asked whether he intended to work until he is 65. He replied:

"I would say, quite categorically, no, because

fire-fighting is not a game where - you do not

see very many firemen at all at the age of 65.

And most other fire services have an early

retirement scheme, whereas ours does not

because of the Public Service Act at this

stage, but it will be something that we will

probably be fighting for in the future so I

could not envisage myself as a fire-fighter at 65."

10. Mr Bergin's evidence as to the duties performed by firefighters was confirmed by that of Mr Rudd. He recently retired from the position of Assistant General Manager for Victoria and Tasmania of Fire Services, Aviation Rescue and Firefighting of the Civil Aviation Authority. He was asked whether there are any essential differences between the physical activities that might be required of a firefighter, and a fire officer and he replied:

"No, the officer must be able to do exactly the

same.....except he has the added

responsibility of over all co-ordinating

rescue and fire-fighting so an officer - I

would expect one of my officers to be able to

do exactly the same as a fire-fighter but he

is trained to do more as far as sizing up and

being in charge of a rescue and an incident."

..............

And actually going into the fire or the

accident situation?---Yes, I would expect an

officer to lead a team in there. We have - if

I may just qualify what I am saying, at

Melbourne airport we have three officers. One

officer would lead a rescue team inside a

fuselage in the event of an emergency and it

would not matter which officer it is, they are

all trained to do that exactly the same."

11. Mr Rudd said that every firefighter has a period of half an hour set aside each day for activities to keep himself to the required standard of fitness and that while working in the gym all firefighters and officers are watched to ensure that they appear to be at the appropriate standard of fitness. He said this supervision to ensure fitness is also carried out at all training exercises. Mr Rudd said that he is not aware of Mr Bergin ever having any problem or difficulty in coping with the requirements of his job as a firefighter. He said that Mr Bergin is an extremely reliable and efficient firefighter.

12. Mr Rudd was asked what would be the prospects of a firefighter being redeployed on less demanding duties, either temporarily or permanently, if he suffered some injury that made him unfit to carry out the normal duties of a firefighter. He said there is no such thing as "light duties" in the Firefighting Service, but in the past special projects have been found as a temporary measure while people are undergoing rehabilitation. He said that he hopes that in the future the Authority will have a special service which would entail fire extinguisher maintenance and non-operational work, and if that comes about, then that sort of work would be available for permanent redeployment of people requiring light duties.

13. Mr Rudd said that currently the retirement age for someone working as a firefighter or fire officer is 65, but not many "go beyond 60" (trans. 31). Mr Bergin had mentioned that he had an interest in working as a fire safety officer. Mr Rudd said that currently there is only one such position in Victoria. The incumbent, like every other fire officer, has to maintain the same standard of fitness as a firefighter.

B. The Statutory Provisions

14. The BCC system was well explained by Mr Eadie in the written outline of submissions which he produced at the hearing. He wrote:

"2. THE BCC SYSTEM

2.1 The BCC System is at the heart of the

Superannuation Scheme established under the

1976 Act. The essential principle is that

everyone accepted as fit for permanent

appointment (and in many instances temporary

appointment) will also be accepted as a full

member of the Superannuation Scheme.

2.2 However, if an employee (an expression

which I will use as shorthand for "eligible

employee") is found to be suffering from some

condition that gives rise to a "real risk" of

premature retirement, a BCC is issued in which

that condition is specified. That BCC may

(and it is important to stress may) have the

effect of restricting the employee's benefits

on retirement, but only in very limited

circumstances. These are if an employee with

less than 20 years contributory service

. is redeployed with a salary decrease that

can properly be regarded as attributable

to physical or mental incapacity (S.78);

or

. is retired on the ground of invalidity

(S.66); or

. dies (S.80),

and the incapacity or death was caused or

substantially contributed to by the BCC

condition or by a connected condition.

2.3 Otherwise the BCC will have no effect.

Indeed, in the case of an employee who

completes 20 years contributory service, the

Act expressly provides (see. S.16(5)) that the

BCC will no longer be in force."

15. At the time the original decision to issue a BCC in respect of Mr Bergin was made, s.16(4) read as follows:

" (4) The Commissioner shall consider the

report or reports, and such other matters (if

any) as the Commissioner considers relevant,

and, if he is of the opinion that the person

is not likely, by reason of or for a reason

connected with a physical or mental condition

or conditions referred to in the report or

reports, to continue to be an eligible

employee until the person attains his maximum

retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a

benefit classification certificate to that

effect, being a certificate in which the

relevant condition or conditions is or are

specified."

16. The decision in Re Bewley (Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293 at 294) was delivered on 18 October 1985. The Tribunal there said of s.16(4):

"The meaning of the words in question seems to

us to be clear. It is that in order to form

the relevant opinion there must be a positive

satisfaction that the person "is not likely to

continue" etc. There must be a greater

likelihood that the person will not so

continue than that he or she will. Unless

this be the case, how can it be opined that

the person is not likely to continue? It is

in our view crucial that the phrase "not

likely" be kept intact, and be regarded as a

composite expression. The word "not" should

not be detached and emphasis placed on the

meaning of the word "likely". In consequence

we consider that it is not possible to form

the opinion in question until it can be said

that it is more probable than not that the

person will continue until attainment of

maximum retiring age. This being what seems

to us to be the plain meaning of the statutory

expression, no further inquiry is really

necessary."

17. Amendments to s.16(4) were made by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (the "Amendment Act") which was assented to on 24 June 1986. Section 6(1) of the Amendment Act omitted from s.16(4) the words "the person is not likely" and substituted the words "there is a real risk that the person" and also omitted the words "to continue" and substituted the words "will not continue". The provision therefore now reads:

" (4) The Commissioner shall consider the

report or reports, and such other matters (if

any) as the Commissioner considers relevant,

and, if he is of the opinion that there is a

real risk that the person, by reason of or for

a reason connected with a physical or mental

condition or conditions referred to in the

report or reports, will not continue to be an

eligible employee until the person attains his

maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall

issue a benefit classification certificate to

that effect, being a certificate in which the

relevant condition or conditions is or are

specified."

Section 2(1) of the Amendment Act provides that s.6(1) shall be deemed to have come into operation on 1 July 1976. It is therefore clear, as Mr Borenstein conceded, that this Tribunal must apply s.16(4) as amended by the Amendment Act. The issue of statutory interpretation in the case concerns the meaning of the term "real risk".

C. The Medical Evidence

18. The evidence of all the expert medical witnesses is that there are two ways in which Mr Bergin's knee injury and reconstruction operation may create a real risk of premature retirement. The first is by the artificial cruciate ligament rendering the knee more susceptible to further rupture of the cruciate ligament than it would be if it had retained the natural ligament. The second is by the injury and operation increasing the risk of osteoarthritis developing in the knee. If either of these events occurred it would not necessarily lead to premature retirement but it may do so. We will consider each possibility separately.

(i) Further rupture of cruciate ligament

19. The Tribunal had before it a number of reports from Mr Grant, the treating surgeon. In a report of 6 March 1986 Mr Grant advised that it was "somewhat impossible" to quantify the additional risk of further rupture of the cruciate ligament, because the particular type of knee reconstruction has only been in existence since 1964. He said that on experimental testing the knee would be ninety per cent normal by which he meant that the cruciate ligament would have ninety per cent of the strength of a natural cruciate ligament.

20. In his report of 10 March 1988, Mr Grant said that on examination on 1 March 1988, he found a stable knee and a small amount of wasting of the quadriceps muscle and no other abnormality. He expressed the opinion that the prognosis was good and that as Mr Bergin had survived for seven years without a meniscal injury or further rupture of his cruciate ligament he considered that he had minimal disability. The report of 14 July 1988 was in similar terms but concluded in a reference to the test in s.16(4) of the Act:

"His knee situation should not prevent him

continuing his occupation until the age of

sixty five."

21. The report of 11 October 1988 repeated the prognosis that Mr Bergin should be able to work until the age of retirement. It then continued:

"In that the anterior cruciate graft can at

maximum develop 90% strength of an anterior

cruciate ligament, he would have a 10% risk of

having to retire at a premature age. As with

the original type of injury, the graft could

rupture, landing from a jump, or with a "blow"

to the knee, with 90% of the force of a normal

anterior cruciate ligament."

In giving evidence, Mr Grant said that the sentence about premature retirement was badly put and should have referred to a 10% risk of rupturing his anterior cruciate ligament.

22. When Mr Grant was asked to express a view as to the risk of complications arising from the knee injury which might prevent Mr Bergin continuing in employment he replied:

"Basically, he would have to have another

serious injury to alter his prognosis. At the

moment, he would have to be unlucky to have

the five per cent chance."

Mr Grant explained that usually if the operation of replacing the cruciate ligament is going to fail, it fails within two years, and that a small group of patients rupture at about five years, but if a patient survives that period the chances of further injury are fairly negligible.

23. Mr Grant explained the load carried through a knee as follows:

"The load through a knee is twice body weight

walking on the flat; it is four and a half

times body weight going up stairs or hills;

six times body weight going down stairs or

hills; and eight times body weight sprinting,

squatting, landing from a jump, or kneeling

for a time."

24. Mr Grant said Mr Bergin would be at risk of a further rupture of the anterior cruciate if he was fatigued or if he was cold, because you cannot shiver and have your muscles co-ordinating correctly at the same time. When Mr Grant was told about the sporting activities and regular exercise Mr Bergin undertakes, he said that the fact that he has taken eight times his body weight landing from jumps over a period of time, means that his knee is pretty secure. Mr Grant agreed that the graft could rupture on landing from a jump or if Mr Bergin twisted his knee either at work, in sport or even doing things around the house or garden. He said that if there was another rupture it would require further surgery.

25. Mr Combe examined Mr Bergin on 16 March 1988. He found on examination one centimetre of wasting of the left quadriceps, very modest crepitations on movement, no cruciate or collateral ligament laxity and otherwise no abnormality other than the surgical scars. He concluded:

"I would believe that Mr Bergin is at only

slight risk from the premature development of

post-traumatic degenerative osteoarthritis in

the left knee. I do not believe that his left

knee is weak or that it is pre-disposed to

further injury. I believe that he has no

residual disability in the left knee."

26. In evidence Mr Combe said that he did not regard the one centimetre of wasting as significant. He said it is not sufficient to indicate reduced quadricep strength such as to render that knee weaker than it otherwise would have been.

27. Mr Davie saw Mr Bergin on 14 November 1988. He found:

"There was a slight degree of instability on

forward pressure of the tibia in relation to

the femur but the medial and lateral ligaments

to the left knee was (sic) normal. There was

a 5 degree loss of flexion in the left knee

and 1 cm of wastage in the left thigh

measured 15 cm above the superior pole of the

patella."

He concluded there was a minimal but definite sign of impairment of the left knee due to slight loss of flexion "a one plus draw sign" and slight diminution in the bulk of the left thigh.

28. Mr Davie wrote:

"The knee is stable to all extents and purposes

at the present time for ordinary stresses and

strains and also for the demands placed on the

knee in Fire Fighting except only in the most

unforeseen circumstances when a sudden twist

or fall would make this particular knee more

vulnerable to further injury than a normal

knee."

Mr Davie said that he thought the knee would be stable for ordinary stresses but the demands placed on the knee in firefighting duties increase the risk of a sudden twist or fall and could make the knee more vulnerable to further injury.

29. Mr Davie in evidence confirmed that he thought there was a small but identifiable risk of premature retirement for Mr Bergin as a result of his knee. He estimated it as in the range of 5 to 10%. In cross-examination he agreed that if Mr Bergin did not continue with the arduous duties of firefighting including climbing ladders, pulling people out of aeroplanes and jumping off trucks, but instead moved to a more sedentary occupation, the risk of injury would be smaller than that expressed in his report. Mr Davie said that the risk of early retirement might be less than 5% if Mr Bergin does sedentary work after he turns 50. Like Mr Grant, he stated that the estimate of 90% cruciate strength of the grafted ligament was a top estimate and many grafts are less strong than that. He also said that they tend to stretch with time thereby causing some instability. He said that a ligament that has been grafted is more likely to rupture on sudden landing.

30. Thus Mr Grant and Mr Davie both said that there is a greater risk of Mr Bergin's cruciate ligament rupturing than there would be if he still had the natural ligament. The fact that Mr Bergin's ligament has already ruptured once when he was not engaged in a particularly dangerous activity, shows that the rupture of a ligament even in an uninjured knee does not require any exceptional jolt or twist. The risk of Mr Bergin further rupturing his knee either at work or in other activities before he reaches 65 is small, but not insignificant. We consider it to be in the range of 5 to 10%.

(ii) The risk of osteoarthritis developing

31. Mr Grant's report of 14 August 1985 said:

"Because his menisci are intact he is less

likely to develop osteoarthritis than a

patient who has had a meniscectomy."

In evidence Mr Grant explained that if a person has not lost a cartilage the prognosis is good, but if they lose a cartilage then there is a 90% to 95% chance of osteoarthritis occurring in that compartment of the knee at a later date. He said Mr Bergin will have some degenerative change in his knee, but it is not going to advance at any great rate, because he has not lost a meniscus (tr. p74).

32. Mr Grant said that because Mr Bergin's menisci are intact the only way his knee joint would undergo degeneration leading to osteoarthritis is if the joint capacity is reduced. He said that is usually noticed because the joint is not able to stand up to high load activity. He said that in a successful knee reconstruction such as Mr Bergin had, he would put the risk of developing osteoarthritis at perhaps 5%. Mr Grant's evidence became somethat confusing as to whether this 5% risk was the risk if he did not have a further significant injury to the knee or whether it took the risk of such a further injury into account. When this confusion was explained to Mr Grant he said that he considered there was a 5% risk of Mr Bergin developing osteoarthritis not associated with another rupture. There would presumably be a slightly higher risk if one also takes into account the additional risk of osteoarthritis developing following further injury to the knee.

33. All the doctors detected some crepitus. Mr Grant said "there is always some kneecap crepitus after cruciate injuries". He explained that the crepitus is due to the fact that in a cruciate injury the knee half extends and the kneecap gets jammed against the femur and suffers some damage. He said it is part of the general injury, but in his experience, it does not advance. He said he did not regard that site as a possible site for osteoarthritis to develop.

34. In a report dated 12 August 1988 Mr Combe put the risk of premature retirement due to the left knee condition as less than 5%. He said that he did not believe that there was a significant risk of Mr Bergin developing post-traumatic degenerative osteoarthritis in the left knee, but if it occurred the symptoms would be adequately controlled by anti-inflamatory medications.

35. Mr Combe said that it is impossible to have surgery within your knee joint to carry out internal reconstruction from a previous injury, without placing the joint both in terms of the original injury, and because of the trauma of operative treatment, at some slight risk from the premature development of degenerative changes. He said it varies enormously from knee to knee. In some people degenerative changes progress quite rapidly, in other people there is no progression for years. He said that the finding of crepitus indicates that there must be some slight irregularities on the articular cartilages within the knee joint. He said there was no finding of crepitations in the other knee.

36. Mr Combe said that by the age of 50 Mr Bergin may well have a moderately arthritic knee, but he does not believe that there will be a premature development of arthritis much before that. He said that if Mr Bergin did develop degenerative arthritis he would find he might get knee pain and swelling caused by less physical stress to his knee than would at present be required to create those symptoms. He said that perhaps by the age of 55, knee problems may develop which will require some modification of duties. Mr Combe said that he chose 5% as the percentage likelihood of Mr Bergin having to retire prematurely because of his knee, because he thought it was a highly unlikely event.

37. Mr Davie in his report of 14 November 1988 wrote:

"In all fairness I think that deterioration of

his knee is not likely to be a major problem

until his 50s and even then it is not likely

to interfere greatly with his ordinary

activities but I personally don't think he

would be undertaking strenuous Fire Fighting

duties much after the age of 50. The fact

that the medial and lateral menisci of the

knee have been preserved makes the risk of

osteo arthritis lessened but I still believe

his knee is likely to slowly deteriorate due

to the fact as mentioned above."

He said that the knee was unlikely to require further treatment apart from anti-inflammatory drugs.

38. Mr Davie said that although there was a good result after the operation, the knee was not perfect and as the knee is one of the commonest joints to get osteoarthritis, there is a small but definite risk, more than in people without injured knees, of osteoarthritis developing in Mr Bergin's left knee. He said this is a result of the slight weakness of the knee shown by the muscle wasting and the slight instability which means that there is some wearing of the surface of the joint and this makes the risk of osteoarthritis a little higher than normal. He agreed that the osteoarthritis would be less likely to prevent Mr Bergin working if he was not required to perform arduous tasks as a firefighter.

39. Mr Davie explained the mechanism which brings about the greater likelihood of osteoarthritis developing in this knee than in a normal knee. He said:

"Well, it is the wastage in the thigh. It is

the slight instability which I felt was

present. And that means that the joint is not

completely congruous. The muscles supporting

the joint are a little bit flabby and the

forward movement is slightly forward, so there

is different areas of weight bearing going

through the knee in different cycles of

movement such that you do not get a uniform

load through the knee and if you get not

uniform stresses you get excessive stresses to

a certain part of the joint and that will tend

to degenerate and that is what we mean by

osteoarthritis. So it is basically - I would

accept he has got a very good result. I have

said that. But I do think there is some

problem there and there is a risk over the

normal population."

(iii) The risk of either further injury or osteoarthritis leading to premature retirement

40. In conclusion Mr Grant made it clear that he thought that probably Mr Bergin would not have to retire prematurely on account of his knee condition; but he acknowledged that there is "a very slight risk" (Tr p 81) of premature retirement due to further injury to the knee or perhaps also to osteoarthritis. He said he would put the risk of early retirement at five per cent overall (Tr p82). He said that if Mr Bergin had surgery almost immediately after a further rupture, he would have a 9 out of 10 chance of almost getting back to what he was, but he acknowledged that there are failures at surgery and in those cases a person might not be able to return to gainful employment.

41. The evidence of all three doctors was that if Mr Bergin developed osteoarthritis it would probably not be before he was 50 and would probably only require treatment with anti-inflammatory drugs. The evidence was that it would probably not require Mr Bergin to retire if he was no longer engaged in the full duties of an active firefighter.

42. On the other hand although Mr Bergin said he would like to retire before the age of 65, there is currently no early retirement scheme, nor are there any positions in the Firefighting Service for officers or firefighters in which high fitness standards do not apply. Mr Rudd said most firefighters do not "go beyond 60" but he did not explain whether they retire early for medical reasons or whether they retire early without an early retirement scheme even though it reduces their superannuation.

43. On the medical evidence we find that there is only a small risk of Mr Bergin suffering a further injury to his knee and then having an unsuccessful operation or developing osteoarthritis so severe as to cause his premature retirement. We do not find the estimate in percentages of assistance, but we note that the range in estimates given by the three medical witnesses seems to be either just below 5% or 5 to 10%. We therefore turn to consider whether a 5% or very small or very slight risk of premature retirement is sufficient to require the issue of a BCC under s.16(4) of the Act.

D. The Legal Issue

44. If the test for issue of a BCC was still the same as it was when the decision under review was made or reconsidered we would have set aside the decision to issue the BCC. On the medical evidence in this matter we could not find that Mr Bergin "is not likely", by reason of his knee condition, "to continue to be an eligible employee until he reaches retiring age". That however is no longer the test. We are now bound to affirm the decision under review if we find "that there is a real risk" that Mr Bergin by reason of his knee condition "will not continue to be an eligible employee until he reaches" age 65.

45. The first Tribunal decision to consider s.16(4) in its amended form was Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217. In rejecting the approach of looking for dictionary meanings of the words "real" and "risk" the Tribunal there said:

"we are satisfied that the mere conjunction of

any of the meanings of "real" with any of the

meanings of "risk" does not capture the

essence of the phrase "real risk", which is a

phrase having connotations of its own distinct

from the dictionary meanings of the two words

comprising it. In the phrase "risk" refers to

a chance that something detrimental will

occur. There is a "real risk" where a factual

situation exists which has inherent in it a

particular potential to result in that

detrimental occurrence. Usually the phrase is

used in relation to the process of making an

assessment of whether precautions are

warranted to prevent either the occurrence of

the detrimental thing or the consequences of

it occurring. We are satisfied that in the

context of the Act s 16 is intended to provide

that a benefit classification certificate

should be issued where an eligible employee is

suffering from an identified condition which

has a particular potential to result in his or

her dying, or becoming so incapacitated as to

be unable to continue in employment, before

reaching maximum retiring age. In our view,

if an eligible employee has a permanent

medical condition which may directly or

through its complications cause premature

death or incapacity, rarely, if ever, can an

opinion properly be formed that there is not a

real risk that by reason of it or for a reason

connected with it the employee will die or

retire before reaching the maximum retiring

age." (emphasis added)

The Tribunal in that matter affirmed the decision to issue a BCC.

46. In Re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation No 4724, delivered 4 November 1988 the result was more favourable to the applicant in that the decision under review was set aside and a decision was made that the BCC issued in respect of the applicant be revoked. Mr Borenstein pressed the Tribunal to adopt the test used by the Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe and Mr Eadie submitted that the appropriate test was that applied in Re Nicholson. Mr Eadie did however say that although, because of the result Re Gelsthorpe appeared to be slightly more helpful to an applicant, one would need to question whether that is really the case.

47. The Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe said it had some difficulty in applying only the test set out in Re Nicholson and criticised the use of the words "particular potential" in substitution for the words "real risk". The Tribunal said:

"The word "risk" refers to a chance or hazard

(see Macquarie Dictionary) and the word "real"

in that context would mean something other

than nominal, imaginary, fictitious or

fanciful. Therefore a "real risk" would be a

chance which is more than nominal, imaginary,

fictitious or fanciful but less than a

probability; how much less than a probability

it is not possible to specify."

48. The Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe also considered that the statutory provision requires consideration not only of the medical condition but also the characteristics of the person suffering the condition and all other factors of the condition relevant to the person's employment. The Tribunal also questioned whether it is appropriate to ignore the nature of the employees' employment.

49. We do not have any difficulty with the words "particular potential" but we agree that it is preferable not to substitute another test for that laid down in s.16(4). We consider that the explanation of the words "real risk" given by the Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe is helpful. We consider it appropriate to take into account all relevant facts including the characteristics of the person and of the person's employment. We take into account that Mr Bergin is an exceptionally fit man, but on the other hand, if he was not he would not be able to be a firefighter. That employment because of its physical demands does carry some additional risk of stress to the knee which could cause further injury. We also note that the physical activity which Mr Bergin undertakes in order to maintain his fitness is a factor which reduces the risk of him developing osteoarthritis, but it does not entirely remove that risk.

50. We also agree with the Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe that percentages of risk are not of great assistance to the Tribunal in deciding whether or not there is a real risk. We do not consider it a correct approach to use an equation where a risk of more than a certain percentage equates to a real risk and a lesser risk, though raised on the medical evidence as a possible consequence of the condition, is not seen as a real risk.

51. The test used in Re Gelsthorpe describes a risk which is real as one which is more "than nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful but less than a possibility". During the hearing of this matter the Tribunal referred to the Shorter Oxford Dictionary for the meaning of the word "nominal". The only meaning which appears relevant is meaning "4" which is as follows:

"Existing in name only, as dist. from real or

actual; merely named, stated, or expressed,

without ref. to reality or fact."

A risk which exists in name only as distinct from a real or actual risk, is not a risk at all and neither is a risk which is named, stated, or expressed without reference to reality or fact. Neither is an imaginary risk a real risk. Similarly a fictitious risk and a fanciful risk are not in fact risks at all.

52. If the only risk present was a nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful risk there would be no real risk. In those circumstances neither the test in Re Gelsthorpe nor that in Re Nicholson would be satisfied. The factual situation would not exist which had inherent in it the potential to result in incapacity or death due to the specified medical condition. Nor would there be evidence that the medical condition may directly or through its complications cause incapacity. We agree with Mr Eadie that the test formulated in Re Gelsthorpe is not more favourable to an applicant than that in Re Nicholson. It was the Tribunal's application of the test to the facts it found which led to the more favourable result.

53. The Tribunal in Re Gelsthorpe set out in paragraph 75 four principles which summarised its views as to the meaning of real risk, with all of which we agree. They read as follows:

"(1) The term "real risk" is not a term of art

and should be interpreted in accordance

with its dictionary meaning;

(2) "Real risk" refers to a chance of an

occurrence which is real and is more than

nominal, imaginary, fictitious or

fanciful but less than a probability;

(3) "Real risk" requires consideration and

assessment of the characteristics of the

person, the nature, gravamen and

characteristics of the condition in the

context of employment to maximum

retirement age;

(4) Although percentages may sometimes assist

in assessing the seriousness of otherwise

of certain conditions in relation to

employment, "real risk" is not a

quantitative assessment such that it can

be reduced to percentage terms in order

to determine its existence."

54. However when the Tribunal applied those principles to its findings of fact, it concluded that there was no real risk of Mr Gelsthorpe not continuing to be an eligible employee until age 65 by reason of the conditions "injury to Lumbo-sacral Intervertebral Disc" or "Lumbo-sacral disc degeneration". We have some difficulty in understanding how the Tribunal reached this conclusion in view of the statutory provision which is expressed in mandatory terms and the medical evidence which it summarised as follows:

"All three doctors were in agreement that there

was a less than 10% risk of the applicant not

being able to continue with his employment as

an orderly up until 65 years due to

lumbo-sacral degeneration, one doctor

assessing the risk as only 5%."

55. We are of the opinion that provided the facts satisfy us that there is in reality a risk of Mr Bergin's retirement before age 65, for a reason connected with his knee condition, we are bound to affirm the decision under review. We have already found that there is a risk, although only small, that either due to the knee sustaining further injury or due to development of osteoarthritis, Mr Bergin will experience further trouble with the knee and that although it is an even smaller risk, the risk still exists that as a result of that trouble he may have to retire prematurely. It is probable that osteoarthritis would be able to be managed with anti-inflammatory drugs so that he does not need to retire. It is probable that if the ligament was again injured a second operation would be successful and he would not have to retire but there is also a real risk, although it is unlikely, that either of those events could lead to premature retirement.

56. We have already said that we agree that it is appropriate to take into account the characteristics of Mr Bergin and the nature of his employment. Mr Bergin is presently aged 30. The maximum retiring age in the Civil Aviation Authority is currently 65. We do not consider that the evidence establishes that by age 50 Mr Bergin will not be required to be fit for running, jumping and the other arduous work expected of firefighters. Even if he is an officer as seems likely, Mr Rudd's evidence was that all officers, even those in administrative work, are still expected to keep their fitness to the required level. There are no light duties positions for firefighters within the Civil Aviation Authority. It is true that as Mr Bergin is a permanent employee, if he could no longer meet the firefighter fitness standards he may be able to transfer to an administrative position within the Civil Aviation Authority or in the public service. However such a transfer would only take place if Mr Bergin wanted to transfer. In view of his evidence about his long standing ambition to be a firefighter and his desire to retire before 65 it would not be surprising if he chose early retirement on medical grounds rather than a transfer of employment, if his osteoarthritis developed to a stage where he no longer met the high standard of fitness required of a firefighter. We note that s.16(4) does not only apply if an eligible employee is unable to continue to maximum retiring age because of the knee, but applies whenever there is a real risk that he will not continue to be an eligible employee because of his knee condition.

57. We consider that the strict interpretation of s.16(4) which we have adopted is consistent with its purpose as described by the Tribunal in Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 10 ALN 129 where the Tribunal said at page 131:

"We think that some assistance is to be gained

from a consideration of the legislative

purpose in enacting s.16 of the Act. We

accept that the purpose was to provide some

protection for the superannuation scheme

established by the Act against claims due to

the premature invalidity or death of an

eligible employee who at the time when he

became an eligible employee was suffering from

a potentially disabling or fatal physical or

mental condition which, in the event, results

in his early retirement or death: see Re

Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation

supra at p 660. As the Tribunal there pointed

out, the protection afforded to the scheme is

not absolute since a benefit classification

certificate ceases to have any effect at all

after the eligible employee has completed 20

years' contributory service (sub-s.16(5)) and

has a diminishing effect in the event of death

or retirement on the grounds of invalidity

where the period of contributory service is

not less than 8 years (ss.66,81)."

58. We consider that the effect of the legislation as amended is that if in reality there is any risk, on the medical evidence that the employee may have to retire before the age of 65 because of a specified condition or a condition connected with the specified condition, then the BCC must be issued. We do not find this to be unjust because the legislation in effect contains a safety mechanism to benefit both the applicant and the respondent. If the applicant's contention that there is no real risk of him retiring because of his knee condition before he reaches the age of 65 is correct, then the BCC, which will no longer be in force after he has completed another 15 years of contributory service, will not cause him any difficulty. Even if the small risk which the medical witnesses have identified becomes a reality and Mr Bergin retires because of his knee condition at the age of 50 or 55, the BCC will no longer be in force and he will not suffer any disadvantage because of it. It is only in the unlikely event that the small risk of retirement before age 65 becomes a reality of retirement before Mr Bergin has completed another 15 years of contributory service, that the BCC will cause him any disadvantage. In those circumstances the respondent will be protected. It appears that s.16 of the Act is intended to provide for just that eventuality.

59. The decision under review will be varied by substituting for the condition "history of knee injury" the condition "left knee injury and re-construction operation".
