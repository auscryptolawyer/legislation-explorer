Statement of Reasons

ISSUES AND CONCLUSIONS

  1. This case is about assessments issued by the Commissioner to Mr Fan for the tax years ending 30 June 2018 to 30 June 2020 (inclusive) (“the Relevant Years”).
  2. Mr Fan is a chef. In the Relevant Years he worked in a Chinese restaurant in New South Wales. That restaurant is owned by a company that will be called “E Ltd” in this decision.
  3. The Commissioner says Mr Fan derived more income than he returned in his income tax returns in the Relevant Years and funded personal living expenses in the Relevant Years using unreported cash income.[1] The Commissioner issued assessments pursuant to section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (“ITAA 1936”), imposed a 75% shortfall penalty on Mr Fan for the Relevant Years, and the base penalty amount was uplifted by 20% in the 2019 and 2020 years.[2]
  4. Mr Fan says that the Commissioner has got it horribly wrong. The funds deposited into his bank account that the Commissioner says are his income were not his income but the result of an array of matters, including loan repayments, the fruits of the sale of Chinese investments and property, or gifts from family members (amongst other matters). He also says that in the Relevant Years he derived no income from E Ltd other than his salary as a chef. He also says no penalties should have been imposed.
  5. I have concluded that:
    (a) Mr Fan has not met his burden of proof to demonstrate to the Tribunal that the income tax assessments issued by the Commissioner were excessive and what his income was in the Relevant Years. The Commissioner’s income tax assessments are affirmed.

    (b) Mr Fan has not met his burden of proof to demonstrate that the penalties imposed upon Mr Fan were excessive. The Commissioner’s shortfall penalty assessments are affirmed.

    (c) However, each decision not to remit shortfall penalties and the base penalty uplift in each of the Relevant Years is set aside. In the overall circumstances, considering the object of the penalties regime, I am satisfied that the imposition of shortfall penalties at 75% and a base penalty uplift is unfair and unjust. That matter is remitted to the Commissioner with a direction to issue assessments of shortfall penalties for each of the Relevant Years at 50% and to fully remit the base penalty uplift.

THE INCOME TAX ASSESSMENTS AT ISSUE, THE AUDIT, AND THE OBJECTION

Mr Fan’s income tax returns

  1. Mr Fan filed his income tax returns for each of the Relevant Years on 25 September 2018, 3 September 2019 and 7 September 2020.[3] He returned the following income:
    (a) 2018 $24,010, later amended to be $13,407;[4]

    (b) 2019 $36,598;[5] and

    (c) 2020 $44,383.[6]

  2. The Tribunal notes that Mr Fan’s “cash analysis” spreadsheet[7] suggests that there was greater income derived by Mr Fan than was returned in Mr Fan’s tax returns. Of course, some of the figures in that spreadsheet may not comprise income for tax purposes and this requires further consideration. This point is returned to below.
  3. On or around 24 September 2019 Mr Fan filed a personal living expenses questionnaire for the 2018 income year with the Commissioner.[8] From the face of this document the entity under audit was E Ltd, and this questionnaire was completed by Mr Fan during the Commissioner’s audit of E Ltd. The matters in that questionnaire are declared by Mr Fan to be true and correct.[9] The questionnaire is returned to below.

The audit, first amended assessments, and first objection

  1. Following the completion of an audit of E Ltd, the Commissioner commenced an audit of Mr Fan’s returns in or around 6 July 2022.[10] On 12 August 2022 the Commissioner issued a position paper to Mr Fan explaining its findings and seeking a response.[11] After liaising to obtain an extension of time to respond to that position paper, and extending the time for the Commissioner to issue assessments,[12] Mr Fan responded to the position paper on 11 October 2022.[13] Further consent to extend the amendment period was provided by Mr Fan to the Commissioner after this.[14]
  2. On 31 March 2023 the Commissioner issued amended assessments in accordance with section 167 of the ITAA 1936 (the “first amended assessments”),[15] alongside a letter setting out the results of the Commissioner’s audit.[16] The nature of an assessment pursuant to section 167 of the ITAA 1936 is cogently explained by the Tribunal in Tran and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 1036 at [101]- [103]. The short point is that such an assessment is by nature an estimate and when issuing such assessments the Commissioner adopts methods which consider matters such as unexplained cash deposits as well expected expenditure compared to the income returned. The Commissioner bears no burden to show the correctness of a section 167 assessment. Rather it is for the taxpayer to show not just that the Commissioner’s assessment is incorrect, but what their actual income is.
  3. The first amended assessments assessed Mr Fan for tax on the following amounts of increased income for each of the Relevant Years:
    (a) 2018 $138,267, an increase of $124,860;[17]

    (b) 2019 $128,149, an increase of $91,551;[18] and

    (c) 2020 $103,211, an increase of $58,828.[19]

  4. Mr Fan objected to the first amended assessments on 12 May 2023 (“the first objection”).[20] Mr Fan said that what he returned in his tax returns was his income and the Commissioner was wrong.
  5. The first objection was on the following grounds:
    (a) Mr Fan did not receive any dividends from E Ltd and was unaware he was a director of E Ltd.[21] The Commissioner’s explanation of its conclusions for the first amended assessments had in part contended that some of the income that was not returned had comprised dividend income from E Ltd.

    (b) In the Commissioner’s income/expense analysis, the Commissioner assumed his daughter Ms Fan is a dependent of his when she is over 30 years old, is married, lives separately, and is not his dependent.

    (c) Deposits in Mr Fan’s bank accounts in the Relevant Years were not income to Mr Fan. Those deposits comprised the repayment of a loan by Ms Betty Chan, gifts or financial support from his daughter Ms Fan or his wife or other family members,[22] or the proceeds from the sale of property in China (totalling around $150,000) which he brought into Australia incrementally.

    (d) Mr Fan also said he was able to meet his expenses from matters he has returned in his tax returns, such as rental income, or from prior savings, gifts or loans.

    (e) Alleged cash payments were not derived by Mr Fan. Mr Fan said there was no unreported cash income in the Relevant Years[23] and the Commissioner’s assertions otherwise were mere speculation.[24] The Commissioner made assumptions in its analysis about Mr Fan’s lifestyle costs which were not correct. For instance, he does not drink nor smoke, nor does he have hobbies, nor significant medical expenses. Mr Fan said he accessed wholesale prices for food, obtained breakfast, lunch and dinner at the restaurant, and never ate out.[25] Mr Fan said he had not stolen any money from the restaurant or E Ltd, although it is not clear to the Tribunal that this was ever asserted by the Commissioner in the first place. Perhaps Mr Fan was assuming that the Commissioner’s assertions that cash payments must have been derived is an assertion he was inappropriately pocketing cash at the restaurant.

    1. Section 14ZZK(a) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (“TAA”) provides that on an application for review before the Tribunal, the taxpayer is limited to their grounds of objection unless the Tribunal orders otherwise.

The second amended assessments and second objection

  1. A further letter was issued by the Commissioner on 19 June 2023.[26] Further amended assessments were to be issued, described as “updated assessment notices” which imposed a penalty on Mr Fan related to aiding and abetting the possession and the use of electronic sales suppression software (“ESST”) at the restaurant, and a tax shortfall penalty was imposed at 75% on the basis that Mr Fan acted with intentional disregard (“the second amended assessments”).[27]
  2. The penalty assessments did not originally appear on the T documents and nor did a penalty position paper issued on 6 April 2023.[28] The Tribunal is bemused by the T documents failing to include the penalty assessments and related material, where they were at issue in the second objection[29] and objection decision,[30] and in the Application for Review.[31] It is true that the Applicant’s SFIC does not mention this issue but, given the Application for Review, it is not at all obvious why the Commissioner concluded these issues were not disputed and decided not to include relevant material in the T documents.[32] The Tribunal communicated with the parties after the hearing to obtain this material from the Commissioner.
  3. Mr Fan objected to the second amended assessments on 12 August 2023 by letter[33] and 7 September 2023 (using the objection form)[34] (“the second objection”). The second objection essentially combined the first objection with objections to penalties, including to the tax shortfall penalty. The grounds of assessment did not change substantively,[35] other than to say that the penalties should not have been applied or should be remitted in full.[36]

The objection decision

  1. The Commissioner disallowed Mr Fan’s first objection and second objection on 10 December 2024.[37]
  2. Mr Fan filed an Application for Review in this Tribunal on 5 February 2025.[38] As noted above, that application included grounds relating to shortfall penalties, the base penalty uplift, and penalty remission.

The nature of Mr Fan’s burden of proof before the Tribunal

  1. Pursuant to section 14ZZK of the TAA, Mr Fan bears the burden of proof before the Tribunal both to demonstrate that the assessments for the Relevant Years are excessive and what the assessments should be.[39] That burden is on the balance of probabilities.[40] For the penalty assessments Mr Fan must demonstrate penalties should not have been imposed, were excessive, or should be remitted.
  2. In the context of the income tax assessments issued under section 167 of the ITAA 1936 (which is the case here)[41] to meet the burden of proof it is not enough for Mr Fan to demonstrate an element of error in the Commissioner’s assessments.[42]
  3. The burden of proof requires Mr Fan not to merely focus upon the Commissioner’s assessments. He must also demonstrate what his income was in the Relevant Years. Mr Fan says his income is what he returned in his tax returns. If he does not satisfy the Tribunal of that on the balance of probabilities, the assessments will stand even if Mr Fan is able to point to an error in any part of the Commissioner’s assessments.[43] This was confirmed in Commissioner of Taxation v Ross [2021] FCA 766 at [48(7)]:
    [I]t is insufficient for a taxpayer to prove that an item in their asset betterment statement was wrong or should not have been included: Gashi [63] – [67]; Rigoli [12].[44] If they do not also satisfactorily explain the source or sources for the other unexplained wealth, that is that they were derived from non-income sources, the onus under s 14ZZK(b)(i) will remain unsatisfied: Gashi [66]. A deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the assessment results in the challenge failing: Dalco at 624 – 626.[45] Necessarily, this prevents a successful challenge to an assessment being made by a process of “picking and choosing” part or parts of the increased wealth relied upon by the Commissioner and attacking them as being improperly included as part of the taxpayer’s taxable income: Gashi [66]; Rigoli [25]. A process which involves attacking elements of the Commissioner’s calculation and facts in respect of which the taxpayer chooses to lead evidence is not sufficient.
  4. That point was confirmed more recently in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Bazzo; Bazzo v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2024] FCA 452. At [131] the Federal Court dismissed the Commissioner’s argument on appeal from the Tribunal that the burden of proof in section 14ZZK operates on an “all or nothing” basis. That is, using an example, it is not correct for the Commissioner to say that a taxpayer that demonstrates that all but $10,000 of $1m income assessed by the Commissioner was not income, fails to meet the burden of proof because the taxpayer must show that the entire amount of $1m was not income to succeed. However, the Federal Court also stated at [133]:
    Of course, a taxpayer who fails to establish the extent of the taxpayer’s income according to ordinary concepts will be unable to demonstrate that the assessment is excessive, but that is because the full extent of the taxpayer’s income remains unknown. .... [A] taxpayer has a separate burden of demonstrating what the assessment should have been. It is not enough to show that the assessment was excessive.
  5. At [138] in referring to Gashi, the Federal Court said:

As the passage in Gashi at [67]-[68] ... makes plain, it was not the mere failure to demonstrate that an amount included in the asset betterment statement should not have been included that was considered to be the reason why Mr Gashi wholly failed in his challenge to the assessment. What their Honours were addressing was an instance where a taxpayer demonstrated that an item included in an asset betterment analysis was wrongly included but the taxpayer ‘did not show the source or sources of funds from which he acquired the increase in assets in each of the relevant years’. Further, Mr Gashi made no attempt to explain the source of his funds. In consequence, there was no basis upon which a conclusion could be reached as to the extent of the taxpayer’s income.[46]

  1. The burden of proof requires Mr Fan to establish the facts upon which he seeks to rely: Danmark Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1944) 7 ATD 333 at 337. While witness evidence is to be considered carefully in its own right,[47] corroborative evidence to witness evidence may make it more probable that the witness evidence is credible: Imperial Bottleshops Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 276 [31]. Where there is a lack of particularity and comprehensiveness in the evidence presented by a taxpayer, that taxpayer may fail to meet the burden of proof.[48] Lastly, where amounts derived by the taxpayer are unexplained (including, for example, deposits in bank accounts), that taxpayer is not likely to meet their burden of proof.[49]

INCOME TAX ASSESSMENTS - EVIDENCE AND CONCLUSIONS

Witnesses before the Tribunal

  1. Mr Fan emigrated to Australia in 2007. During the Relevant Years, Mr Fan worked at the restaurant owned by E Ltd. He left that job in March 2020.[50] Two witnesses appeared before the Tribunal on Mr Fan’s behalf – his daughter Ms Chujun Fan and Ms Betty Chan. Their evidence will be returned to shortly.
  2. The Tribunal understands that Mr Fan’s English is rudimentary, and he is a fluent Cantonese speaker. Despite the availability of translators at the Tribunal, Mr Fan did not appear before the Tribunal, and was represented by Ms Hon. For the avoidance of doubt, I reject the inferences raised by the Commissioner in the Respondent’s Revised Submissions dated 8 December 2025 (“Commissioner’s Revised Outline”) at [21] and [28] that this of itself may mean he does not meet his burden of proof.
  3. Case law establishes that it is not obligatory for a taxpayer to call all possible witnesses in order to meet their burden of proof.[51] However, where there is no evidence, the evidence is inconsistent, or there is a lack of particularity or comprehensiveness in the evidence, the absence of witness evidence when they could have been called that may be telling.[52] It all depends upon the nature of the case and an assessment of the available evidence.

The evidence before the Tribunal

Allegations about E Ltd’s use of ESST

  1. The Commissioner commenced an audit of Mr Fan’s returns in or around 6 July 2022.[53] This followed an audit of E Ltd. The Commissioner noted that Mr Fan was a director and shareholder of E Ltd.[54]
  2. Mr Fan’s submissions to the Commissioner included stating via a tax agent that he invested $30,000 in the restaurant but never received any dividends nor any benefit for that investment.[55] Mr Fan later said that did not occur at all and his former tax agent made that submission quite wrongly.[56] The Tribunal assumes there was no such investment and this statement was made to the Commissioner in error by Mr Fan’s former tax agent.
  3. The Commissioner was concerned that E Ltd was operating the restaurant using ESST.[57] The Commissioner’s website sets out information about such software which is prohibited because it is a tool designed to evade tax. The Commissioner’s website information includes detail about the potential application of significant ESST penalties including on directors.[58] ESST penalties were initially imposed upon Mr Fan but reversed at objection, so ESST penalties are not considered further.

Allegations about Mr Fan deriving undeclared income

  1. Following its audit of Mr Fan, the Commissioner concluded that Mr Fan had not returned the entirety of his income in his tax returns. The Commissioner said this was demonstrable from an array of matters, including deposits in his bank account, his wages being under award rates, expenses he incurred without obvious income to meet those expenses, and a lack of expenditure on ordinary items giving rise to concern that Mr Fan was deriving undeclared cash income.
  2. On that latter point, as an example, the Commissioner said it was surprised to see no costs in Mr Fan’s bank accounts for everyday expenses such as for groceries. Mr Fan explained that he lives frugally, and during the Relevant Years all food was provided to him at E Ltd’s restaurant. Mr Fan also said he met expenses such as mortgage expenses from his income or from savings.

Deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian bank accounts

Inadequate T documents

  1. The Commissioner identified cash deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian bank accounts which it concluded may comprise further income not returned in Mr Fan’s tax returns. The Commissioner filed submissions at the Tribunal about what the amount of these cash deposits were.[59]
  2. In the context of considering cash deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian bank accounts, there are issues with the T Documents filed by the Commissioner which was of frustration to the Tribunal.
    (a) The T documents do not contain all the bank statements which the Commissioner refers to in the Commissioner’s filings.[60] It should not be difficult for the Commissioner to file all relevant material as part of the T documents and it is not satisfactory to give the Tribunal only some of the material, and then to make submissions relying upon missing material.

    (b) Where bank statements were provided to the Tribunal, they were not filed as part of the T documents, but as annexures to the Commissioner’s submissions. In this decision I refer to those annexures by their reference and page number as part of “T documents.” This is because they should have been part of the T documents and not filed as annexures to submissions. It is unclear as to why they were not included in the T documents in the first place. Of course, this is not problematic as the Tribunal and Mr Fan have been provided this material, although clearly (as noted above) not all of it.

  3. The T documents[61] include bank statements from Mr Fan’s NAB and Commonwealth in bank accounts which demonstrate the matters which follow.

Deposits from Ms Fan

  1. Deposits from Mr Fan’s daughter comprising twenty deposits of $2,000 into his Commonwealth Bank account between 2 July 2017 and 23 April 2019.[62] Ms Fan provided a statutory declaration dated 19 August 2025 (“Ms Fan’s Witness Statement”) to the Tribunal.
  2. Ms Fan gave evidence before the Tribunal on re-examination that the regular $2,000 deposits she made into Mr Fan’s account are not relevant as these deposits were part of a family arrangement to avoid account fees.[63] She said this involved $2,000 deposited by her in Mr Fan’s account, Mr Fan then transferred the same amount to Mr Fan’s wife’s account, and then the same amount was transferred by Ms Fan’s wife back to Ms Fan – a form of money-go-round. There might be some limited evidence to corroborate this, as (for example) A3-24 shows $2,000 deposited by Ms Fan in Mr Fan’s bank account on 23 April 2019, and two days later a withdrawal of $2,000 was deposited on 25 April 2019[64] into another Commonwealth Bank account, although it cannot be discerned whose account that withdrawal was paid to.
  3. In addition, none of Ms Fan’s evidence about the $2,000 deposits appeared in Ms Fan’s Witness Statement, and it was new evidence elicited on re-examination by Ms Hon to which the Commissioner objected. The Commissioner was given the opportunity by the Tribunal to ask further questions about this evidence but the way this evidence was given was unfair and I have taken that into account.
  4. In any event, Ms Fan’s evidence is insufficient to provide a complete picture. Ms Fan has only now raised this evidence, and I need corroboration of it. I am not satisfied on the balance of probabilities what the $2,000 deposits were. I cannot corroborate Ms Fan’s evidence from the evidence before me. It is possible what she said is correct, but I have insufficient evidence to conclude that her explanation is credible on the balance of probabilities. It is not sufficient to persuade me otherwise. I cannot see where the withdrawals from Mr Fan’s account go to, and I cannot determine that they were deposited in his wife’s bank account. I do not have corroborative evidence from Mr Fan’s wife’s bank accounts showing the $2,000 coming in from Mr Fan and then going out to Mr Fan’s daughter’s account in short order. There is nothing that corroborates Ms Fan’s evidence. As a result, I cannot clearly identify what was happening with the $2,000 each month after it was deposited in Mr Fan’s account.
  5. I cannot determine what the $2,000 deposits are for. Of course that does not mean they are necessarily taxable income. But I have no corroborated explanation, so Mr Fan has not satisfied me that the better view is that the $2,000 deposits are not income.
  6. Ms Fan’s Witness Statement provided evidence that on or around June 2017 she provided $5,000 to Mr Fan to cover his daily living expenses. That date does not match the deposits that can be seen from Ms Fan on Mr Fan’s Commonwealth Bank account statements nor his NAB statements in any way. In cross-examination from Ms Vasik, Ms Fan’s evidence was very vague about the date on which she provided the $5,000 to her father. She said she did not recall and claimed she could not access banking material. This was not satisfactory evidence. I give it little weight. I have no banking material from Ms Fan’s accounts to demonstrate any such withdrawal was made by her to make a payment, and I do not have an explanation as to why she would not just have transferred the funds from her bank account to Mr Fan’s as she did with the $2,000 deposits, as opposed to having made a deposit of what must have been $5,000 cash. I find that concerning and it is an adjustment in Ms Fan’s behaviour that is not explained. I return to Ms Fan’s evidence when considering deposits in Mr Fan’s bank account that do not have a clear source.

Deposits from Mr Fan’s wife

  1. Two deposits into Mr Fan’s Commonwealth Bank account from Mr Fan’s wife of $2,000 each on 1 September 2017 and 4 March 2018.[65] There is no explanation for these amounts. Of course that does not mean they are necessarily taxable income, but without any explanation, that cannot be concluded on the balance of probabilities. It was for Mr Fan to demonstrate what these amounts were.

Deposits from Ms Kitty Chan and Ms Betty Chan

  1. Two deposits in Mr Fan’s NAB account are said to be from Ms Betty Chan. Each are of $10,000 on 10 July 2017 and 17 July 2017.[66]
  2. Additional evidence was provided to the Tribunal about these deposits:
    (a) The reference in the NAB statement refers to “Kitty Payment - Betty Chan”. The evidence before the Tribunal was that Kitty Chan and Betty Chan are sisters, and Kitty Chan is Mr Fan’s friend. Betty Chan gave evidence that she needed a new car and ended up borrowing $20,000 from Mr Fan temporarily while she waited for two term deposits to mature. When those term deposits matured, she then repaid Mr Fan, via her sister Kitty Chan. The loan duration was for two months. It is not clear to me why Ms Chan simply did not wait for her term deposits to mature before purchasing a car, but there may have been an urgent need for the vehicle.

    (b) Betty Chan gave evidence before the Tribunal that she gave two $10,000 amounts to her elder sister Kitty who then made the deposits into Mr Fan’s account. It was not explained why Betty Chan could not have simply made a wire transfer of the total amount to Mr Fan and why this payment via her sister in $10,000 increments was required.

    (c) Mr Fan’s bank statements that are available to the Tribunal do not include statements for the two months prior to July 2017, where the Tribunal might be able to see $20,000 being paid by Mr Fan to Ms Betty Chan and which might be evidence of the amount of the loan. It is important that the amount of the loan is corroborated to determine that the payment received did not include any element of income.

    (d) Ms Vasik cross-examined Ms Betty Chan. That cross-examination raised several issues. The first issue Ms Vasik raised was the fact that the loan arrangement between Ms Chan and Mr Fan was not documented and that security was not provided, points which Ms Chan accepted. I do not accept that this point has the significance the Commissioner appears to think it does. The case law is to the effect that the Commissioner must take what it finds and the lack of documentation is not determinative,[67] a lack of documentation is not of itself a failing that means that the burden of proof is not satisfied,[68] and witness evidence can be found credible and relied upon.[69] In addition, as was recently noted by the Full Federal Court in Charles Apartments Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No.2) [2025] FCAFC 180 at [17], great injustices can be done if the Commissioner does not approach questions of evidence taking into account the real world context for small businesses and individuals. Not documenting short term loans to trusted friends or family may not be considered unusual in some cultures or contexts. This is a two-month loan.

    (e) Ms Vasik also asked why Ms Betty Chan did not borrow funds from a bank, or from a close friend of hers (given it was said Mr Fan was a friend of her sister’s and not her). I agree with the Commissioner that in the Australian context what occurred was unusual. I have taken that into account. Again though, in some cultures or contexts, borrowing from family or friends may be more common than from institutions, and these aspects might not be altogether unusual. This was said to be a loan of a short- term nature. The questioning from Ms Vasik did not result in a compelling challenge to Ms Chan’s evidence.

  3. Ms Chan’s evidence had some credibility. She was a good witness and was untroubled by Ms Vasik’s questioning on cross-examination. As stated above, I do not accept that an absence of documentation or security is fatal to the existence of a loan arrangement between friends, where credible witness evidence is given. However, I also need to balance the fact that I do not have complete information before me. I am not satisfied by Ms Chan’s evidence alone given the vagueness of the arrangements. Similarly to the holdings in Palmer v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2000] FCA 1508 at [22], I find corroborative evidence is needed, particularly as contemporaneous documentary evidence from Mr Fan’s bank should have been presented.
  4. Other than Ms Chan’s oral evidence, there is no evidence to demonstrate the $20,000 leaving Mr Fan’s accounts in the first place to corroborate the amount of the loan. That is important as I cannot discern whether any element of the $20,000 deposited in Mr Fan’s account by Ms Chan was not income without corroborative evidence of the amount of the loan. Bank statements may have given that evidence, but they were not presented.
  5. Other than Mr Fan being said to prefer dealing in cash (which is an unattractive aspect of his case), it is not explained why wire transfers were not done between parties with Australian bank accounts. It is also not explained why there was a routing of funds from Ms Chan to her sister and then from Kitty Chan to Mr Fan. While I have considered cultural differences including the importance of respected relationships in the Chinese Australian community, this rerouting of funds via Kitty Chan when all the parties know each other does not have a clear explanation. I do not know the source of the $20,000 Mr Fan says he loaned to Ms Chan; could those funds be income that Mr Fan has not returned? It is not explained. I am not persuaded and cannot accept that Ms Chan’s evidence alone meets Mr Fan’s burden of proof in relation to the $20,000.
  6. One deposit was also made in Mr Fan’s NAB account from Kitty Chan of $2,000, but this was outside of the Relevant Years[70] so it is left aside.

Cash deposits without an obvious source

  1. These are comprised of the following which are evidenced on the T documents:[71]
    (a) In Mr Fan’s NAB account on 20 September 2017 deposits of $5,000 and $50, on 28 September 2017 a deposit of $5,000, on 30 May 2018 a deposit of $5,000, on 31 October 2018 a deposit of $3,000, and on 31 March 2019 a deposit of $5,100.[72]

    (b) In Mr Fan’s Commonwealth Bank account five cash deposits of $1,000 on 3 May 2018, 27 July 2018, 11 December 2018, 12 February 2019, 5 June 2019.[73]

    (c) In Mr Fan’s Commonwealth Bank account other cash deposits of $2,000 on 7 September 2017 and $1,500 on 23 January 2018.[74]

  2. Several explanations were either given by Mr Fan to the Tribunal via Ms Hon for the cash deposits in his accounts that have no obvious source, or the T documents suggest that Mr Fan has raised an explanation previously with the Commissioner even if not directly put to the Tribunal at the hearing. Those explanations and the Tribunal’s conclusions about them are as follows:
    (a) As is noted above, Ms Fan’s Witness Statement is to the effect that on or around June 2017 she provided $5,000 to Mr Fan to cover his daily living expenses. The Tribunal does not accept this is an explanation as the date referred to by Ms Fan does not even broadly match unexplained deposits referred to in the NAB account statements and nor any of the Commonwealth Bank account statements. Ms Fan’s evidence before the Tribunal was that she did not know the specific date and could not get her bank statements. It is not explained why Ms Fan would make a cash deposit as opposed to a wire transfer, when she made wire transfers of other amounts. This was not satisfactory evidence. I give it little weight. Even if I had accepted that evidence, it explains only one deposit of $5,000 in any event and is not sufficient for Mr Fan to meet his burden of proof.

    (b) There also is said to be extra rental or what is described as a “contribution” derived from the Campsie property, as suggested by a Statutory Declaration from Xiuqiong Peng[75] (said to be a relative of Mr Fan’s).[76] This is said to be an amount of $300 per week between June 2017 and March 2020,[77] and this might have some linkage to some cash deposits in Mr Fan’s bank account. Mr Fan provided a ‘cash analysis spreadsheet which suggests $15,600 was provided by Xiuqiong Peng in each of the Relevant Years.[78] This amount does not seem to appear as income in Mr Fan’s tax returns. The arrangements with Xiuqiong Peng were not raised by Ms Hon at the hearing although this was raised in Mr Fan’s filings at the Tribunal.[79] I cannot be certain that these payments were not rental or any other form of income. There is nothing that suggests the unidentified bank deposits in Mr Fan’s accounts have Xiuqiong Peng as a source. Nor did Xiuqiong Peng appear as a witness at the hearing for the Commissioner to cross-examine. None of the unexplained deposits have a clear linkage to a $300 per week payment. I disregard that evidence as a result. Without more evidence, it cannot provide a valid explanation for the deposits which do not have a clear source.

    (c) There are bank statements from China-based banking institutions on the T Documents which relate to Mr Fan’s wife or to Mr Fan.[80] To the extent the bank statements relate to Mr Fan’s wife, they demonstrate there are funds held by her in China. Mr Fan has produced these records as evidence (Ms Hon submitted) of the source of funds to pay mortgage obligations and other expenses in Australia. Nobody appeared before the Tribunal to tell it that a particular amount was withdrawn on a date that closely matches a mortgage payment date, and that the source of that amount was the sale of property in China. The Tribunal cannot assess the credibility of assertions in submissions. There needs to be evidence presented. There was no evidence presented other than bank statements which do not provide a complete picture. There is nothing reconciling any payments made in Australia with amounts withdrawn by Mr Fan’s wife, nor is there any evidence that Mr Fan’s wife was the source of unexplained cash deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian accounts, nor do I know where the deposits in Mr Fan’s wife’s account are sourced. I have no evidence about any of that. While Mr Fan says that these amounts are sourced from the sale of Chinese property, I have no evidence that demonstrates that – no witness evidence, no documentary evidence, merely assertions in submissions.[81] Mr Fan’s submissions are not evidence.[82]

    (d) Bank receipts from China-based banking institutions were produced by Mr Fan as an attempt to provide some linkage. Mr Fan says that he and/or his wife brought back funds from China in amounts of under $10,000 each time,[83] and that seven bank receipts[84] provide some explanation for the above cash deposits into his bank accounts and/or for how he managed to meet expenses. I do not find these bank receipts provide an explanation for the unexplained cash deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian bank accounts because:

    (i) The dates reflected in the bank receipts do not correlate with any unexplained deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian accounts.

    (ii) The amounts referred to in the bank receipts are not reflected in unexplained deposits in Mr Fan’s accounts. At one point on the T documents it is said in submissions for Mr Fan that Mr Fan deposited what was left after meeting living expenses.[85] There is no evidence provided in support. There is no reconciliation demonstrating amounts withdrawn from China, how those funds were used to meet what expenses and when, then matching the remainder to unexplained deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian accounts. The Tribunal did not hear from Mr Fan nor his wife as witnesses. Given that mortgage payments appear to be met by transfers from Mr Fan’s bank accounts[86] it should be demonstrable from the bank statements that these amounts were deposited into Mr Fan’s accounts if their purpose was to meet mortgage payments as is suggested by Mr Fan. But there is no such evidence.

    (iii) The bank receipts are described as a “remittance” and appear to suggest cash was collected, as there is nothing suggesting wire transfers into Mr Fan’s Australian bank accounts. There is nothing on the material before the Tribunal that links the bank receipts to any expenses nor to any deposit in Mr Fan’s account with any specificity. As is noted above, the Tribunal has been provided no form of reconciliation.

    (iv) Why amounts withdrawn by Mr Fan’s wife from her savings would be deposited in Mr Fan’s Australian bank account rather than her own is also unexplained. No evidence was presented from Mr Fan’s wife.

    If Mr Fan was seeking to explain that the deposits referred to at paragraph ‎50 were sourced from Chinese accounts he needed to explain the above matters and provide evidence. The Tribunal cannot be satisfied that the seven bank receipts provide a complete explanation for the cash deposits in Mr Fan’s Australian bank accounts.

    (e) In his personal living expenses questionnaire filed with the Commissioner on or around 24 September 2019[87] Mr Fan said he had $2,500 of gambling winnings in Macao,[88] $18,000 provided by his son-in-law, and $9,000 of funds provided to him by his mother.[89] I am not satisfied that these explanations have any linkage to the cash deposits in Mr Fan’s account that have no obvious source. There are no dates provided for when Mr Fan received the payments of $2,500, $18,000 or $9,000 other than that it must be assumed these amounts arose in the 2018 income year as the personal living expenses questionnaire related to that year. This in turn means that when considering the unexplained cash deposits, only the first three deposits at paragraph ‎50‎(a) above, the first deposit at paragraph ‎50‎(b) above, and the deposits at paragraph ‎50‎(c) above can have any linkage to the amounts Mr Fan said were sourced from gambling winnings, his son-in-law and his mother. The total of the unknown source deposits in Mr Fan’s accounts evidenced before the Tribunal is $19,550, and $2,500, $18,000 or $9,000 in aggregate is $29,500. None of the unexplained deposits in the 2018 year in Mr Fan’s account mirror the amounts Mr Fan says arose from gambling winnings, his son-in-law and his mother. There is no further evidence to help clarify the position. Other than the personal living expenses questionnaire, I have no witness or docum[90]tary evidence confirming the $2,500, $18,000 and $9,000 amounts arose,90 and they were not raised by Mr Fan at the hearing. I cannot be satisfied on the balance of probabilities that these amounts explain any of the unexplained cash deposits. No evidence is given by anyone as to the nature of these payments.

    (f) There are other suggestions on the T documents about funds being otherwise provided by Mr Fan’s mother or his father-in-law[91] or being sourced “overseas” but these funds are not quantified or explained.[92] Consequently, I cannot be satisfied on the balance of probabilities that these amounts explain any of the unexplained cash deposits and I cannot be satisfied they are not additional income.

    (g) I note that there are more references to “cash gifts” in the T documents that were said to either not be deposited in Mr Fan’s bank accounts, or the remainder deposited, and that are not further expanded upon by Mr Fan.[93] I do not know if these ‘gifts’ explain the unexplained deposits in whole or any part. I do not know when they were made. I do not know the amounts. I do not know who they came from. These may be other amounts that Mr Fan received which he has not explained and which might be income for tax purposes. Mr Fan asserts gifts and loans are not taxable without explaining further.[94] The Tribunal is unpersuaded by incomplete evidence and incomplete submissions.

Mr Fan’s wages – were there additional cash payments?

  1. Mr Fan derived wages from E Ltd from his work at the restaurant during the Relevant Years. The Commissioner identified that Mr Fan’s income from his job as a chef was under the Restaurant Industry Award rate[95] and suggested that of itself means cash may also have been provided. In Closing Submissions at the hearing, Ms Hon said if this is so, Mr Fan was underpaid, Mr Fan had no idea, he was an elderly migrant that was exploited, the amount of his wages was not something in Mr Fan’s control, and it was the fault of E Ltd. The Tribunal is bemused by Mr Fan’s reference to himself as “elderly.” He is in his 60s, hardly in advanced old age.
  2. Mr Fan submits that during the Relevant Years there was a bonus arrangement entered into between Mr Fan and E Ltd.[96] The bonus arrangement is said to have been focused on retaining his services when he was thinking of leaving the restaurant. There is nothing on the T documents between E Ltd and Mr Fan evidencing that arrangement. Mr Fan says no payments were received under that arrangement.[97] The Commissioner says it cannot be certain whether Mr Fan received such bonus payments. While there is evidence provided from E Ltd’s accountants that Mr Fan did not derive dividends from E Ltd,[98] even if that was to be accepted (despite there being no evidence from a witness to test that), that does not answer the questions before the Tribunal. No witness from E Ltd’s accountants appeared at the Tribunal to be examined and nor was there evidence from E Ltd’s accountants about the bonus arrangement.
  3. More acutely, there is no evidence from E Ltd. There is nothing from E Ltd on the T documents corroborating Mr Fan’s submissions that there was a bonus arrangement, nothing corroborating Mr Fan’s submission that no bonus was paid in accordance with any such bonus arrangement (if a bonus arrangement existed), and nothing from E Ltd confirming that Mr Fan’s wages as deposited in his NAB account comprised the entirety of his income from E Ltd. While I can see the deposits of wages in Mr Fan’s bank account,[99] I have no evidence challenging the Commissioner’s point about being paid below the award rate,[100] and no evidence from E Ltd that Mr Fan was not paid additional amounts from E Ltd to the amounts that appear in Mr Fan’s bank statements. Additional cash payments might explain some of the unexplained cash deposits in Mr Fan’s bank accounts. Perhaps they were sourced from E Ltd or perhaps they were not. There is no complete evidence to say where the cash payments were sourced from. In addition, there might have been other non-cash taxable benefits provided to Mr Fan by E Ltd that have not been returned in his tax returns. Mr Fan says other benefits were provided by E Ltd and this is discussed below.[101]
  4. Mr Fan said that even if cash payments were made to him by E Ltd, they may not be income for tax purposes, and the Commissioner must prove any such payments are taxable.[102] Mr Fan also maintains that it is not fair for the Commissioner to assert the Applicant has received income without concrete evidence.[103] Those submissions are rejected as they are wrong at law. The problem with those submissions is that it is for Mr Fan to demonstrate there is no such taxable income. The Commissioner bears no burden of proof of any kind, be that to show cash payments arose, or be that to demonstrate their nature for tax law purposes.[104] The Commissioner’s assessments are conclusive proof at law that they are properly made and are correct.[105] Pursuant to section 14ZZK of the TAA, it is for Mr Fan to demonstrate on the balance of probabilities what his actual taxable income is and that the Commissioner’s assessments are excessive.

Was there undeclared rental income in the Relevant Years?

  1. Mr Fan owns two properties in NSW – one in Kellyville Ridge and one in Campsie. The Kellyville Ridge house is a rental property, and the Campsie property is Mr Fan’s home. The bank statements on the T Documents demonstrate that rental income was derived in the Relevant Years[106] from the Kellyville Ridge property and Mr Fan said he returned that income in his tax returns.
  2. One matter not noted by the Commissioner is that it appears that, at least at some point, Mr Fan’s daughter Ms Fan resided at the Kellyville Ridge property.[107] It is not clear when the arrangement for Ms Fan to occupy that property started, whether she occupied that property in the Relevant Years, and when or if that arrangement ended. The purpose of raising this matter is that it might suggest that the regular $2,000 payments made by Ms Fan into Mr Fan’s bank account in the Relevant Years could comprise additional rental payments that have not been returned by Mr Fan. I consider that to be unlikely for the reasons outlined below, although I have insufficient evidence to conclude that with certainty.[108]
  3. Ms Fan’s address in Ms Fan’s Witness Statement might also have been stated in error. It is noted for instance that the Application for Review states that Mr Fan’s residential address is the Kellyville Ridge property,[109] when the other material on the T Documents is to the effect that it is the Campsie address that is Mr Fan’s residential address.
  4. Assuming Ms Fan’s Witness Statement states her address properly, I consider whether the $2,000 per month might be additional rental income. An internet search reveals that the current (2026) average weekly rent in Kellyville Ridge is $820 per week, and in the Relevant Years the average rental was between $530 – $660 per week.[110] Of course, a market appraisal of the actual property would be needed for the Relevant Years.
  5. The rental derived by Mr Fan in the Relevant Years needs to be compared to that broad market rental:
    (a) From the Commissioner’s 12 August 2022 position paper[111] the Kellyville Ridge property was rented out for approximately $514 per week (or approximately $2,228 per month and $26,738 for the income year) in 2018, $501.88 per week (or approximately $2,174 per month and $26,098 for the year) in 2019, and $581.53 per week (or approximately $2,520 per month and $30,240 for the year) in 2020.

    (b) Those figures are consistent with Mr Fan’s “cash analysis” spreadsheet annexed to his letter dated 10 October 2022.[112] Broadly speaking, and without knowing more about the nature of the property in Kellyville Ridge or having a market appraisal, the rental that the Commissioner says was derived by Mr Fan seems broadly to be in the market range in the Relevant Years. That tells against the $2,000 contributions from Ms Fan being rental. But of course, it is unclear.

    (c) When considering Mr Fan’s tax returns, they claim a net rental property loss,[113] and the returns on the T documents show only gross rent one year – 2020[114] which is a figure consistent with the figures in the Commissioner’s position paper.

    (d) When looking at the NAB bank statements, the rental deposited by Successful Properties Group (which appears to be a property manager) is not what is said above to be the gross rental but significantly less - $20,327.53 in 2018, $21,810.76 in 2019, $25,720.60 in 2020.

    (e) Perhaps the difference between the 12 August 2022 position paper and the NAB bank account statements is explained by property management fees or other expenses paid prior to Successful Properties Group depositing funds to Mr Fan’s account. Mr Fan provided a “cash analysis” document that sets out what are said to be rental expenses totalling $4,600 in 2018, $5,800 in 2019, and $4,500 in 2020.[115] Even taking those figures into account, that does not explain the differential between what are said to be gross rental figures in the 12 August 2022 position paper and what was paid into Mr Fan’s NAB account by Successful Properties Group. Additional expenses such as mortgage interest being claimed in Mr Fan’s tax returns would not reduce what Successful Properties Group would pay to Mr Fan as they would be claimed after the deposit was made by Successful Properties Group. Perhaps the figures for rental expenses in Mr Fan’s “cash analysis” spreadsheet are inaccurate. Any Successful Properties Group statements on the T Documents do not relate to the Relevant Years so there is no obvious explanation.[116] The material does not reconcile.

  6. On the assumption that Ms Fan rented the Kellyville Ridge property in the Relevant Years, I have concluded that the $2,000 deposits from Ms Fan are not likely to be rental for the Kellyville Ridge property. This is because what has been returned by Mr Fan as rental income seems to be within market range. But none of the material reconciles properly.[117] This of course does not mean that the $2,000 deposits by Ms Fan have been demonstrated by Mr Fan as not being income to him, merely that it is unlikely those amounts are rental. The question remains unanswered as to what these amounts are. The evidence is insufficient.
  7. As is noted above at paragraph ‎51(b), $15,600 was said to be contributed by Xiuqiong Peng (said to be a relative of Mr Fan) in each of the Relevant Years in relation to the Campsie property. These amounts do not appear to have been returned by Mr Fan in his tax returns as “rental” or any other form of income. Mr Fan’s submissions appear to be that these payments were “gifts,” but he has presented no evidence other than an untested statement from Xiuqiong Peng to that effect. Xiuqiong Peng did not appear as a witness at the Tribunal. The Tribunal cannot understand why these payments were not rental payments or other taxable income. Mr Fan placed reliance upon the amounts he said Xiuqiong Peng provided to him to meet his expenses per his “cash analysis” spreadsheet at T18-177. This is (potentially) undeclared income which has not been properly explained or accounted for. Of course, it is not obvious that this income was derived as it is not visible on Mr Fan’s bank statements available to the Tribunal, and it seems likely Xiuqiong Peng made cash payments.

Lack of expenditure on ordinary items and the use of cash by Mr Fan

  1. As has been noted above, the Commissioner also focused on there being a lack of expenditure on everyday matters such as grocery items demonstrable in Mr Fan’s bank statements. Of itself, this is not particularly determinative. It might be that Mr Fan’s wife pays for such expenses for instance. But no such explanation was given by Mr Fan.
  2. Mr Fan’s explanation was that he was provided three meals a day by E Ltd at the restaurant during the Relevant Years and was also possibly able to access wholesale grocery prices.[118] There is no evidence given by E Ltd of this arrangement. An array of issues may arise from this submission by Mr Fan if it was accepted:
    (a) Leaving aside questions as to whether fringe benefits tax may or may not arise for E Ltd from such an arrangement, there are questions about the nature of this arrangement whether an allowance may have been provided to Mr Fan by E Ltd.

    (b) There are issues as to whether non-cash benefits arose to Mr Fan from his employment that might be income to him. For example, this arrangement might explain the differential to the award rate when considering Mr Fan’s wages. That might suggest that the value of any such arrangement might be income to Mr Fan. I am not determining this point and there are arguments against it. I am merely noting that further issues might arise from Mr Fan’s submissions that have not been considered or explained. Insufficient evidence has been provided by Mr Fan.

  3. Then there are issues relating to the use of cash. Ms Hon submitted that Mr Fan is elderly and used to dealing with cash.[119] Mr Fan says that cash was brought into Australia by Mr Fan and/or Mr Fan’s wife, cash was received by Mr Fan (for instance (potentially) from Xiuqiong Peng as well as from others), and he paid expenses in cash.[120] All of this makes it very difficult for Mr Fan to meet his burden of proof before the Tribunal. Nothing can be reconciled. This is exactly the scenario referred to in Chiem v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2007] FCA 1300 at [8] (and other cases) where the taxpayer was unsuccessful. I also have found nothing supporting the idea that the Chinese-Australian community deals in cash or there is some sort of cultural difference that in any way justifies Mr Fan’s behaviour. The material I have found suggests that the Chinese community may seek to take cash from China to avoid Chinese government policy relating to the removal of capital from China, and this material also indicates that such behaviour is a significant money laundering risk.[121] The various media reports suggest that rather than dealing in cash, the Chinese community is adept at managing financial matters digitally, and the concern in China is it becoming a cashless society adversely impacting tourism.[122]
  4. If Mr Fan continues with this approach using cash in the Australian context, that might give rise to further action from the Commissioner. This approach is simply not workable in the Australian context. The Tribunal suggests Mr Fan needs to change his approach and use his bank accounts in an ordinary manner. If he had done that, he may have met his burden of proof in this case.

INCOME TAX ASSESSMENTS - SUMMARY OF TRIBUNAL’S CONCLUSIONS

  1. I am not satisfied that Mr Fan has met his burden of proof to demonstrate that the Commissioner’s income tax assessments were wrong and what they should be.[123]
  2. Mr Fan has not demonstrated on the balance of probabilities that the Commissioner’s assessments were excessive as required by section 14ZZK of the TAA. There are obvious reasons for Mr Fan to challenge the Commissioner’s inclusion of certain amounts in its assessments. However, further corroborative evidence was needed from Mr Fan to satisfy his burden of proof before the Tribunal. There are large holes in Mr Fan’s evidence, and this is telling in this case. This similar to the situation in Chiem v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2007] FCA 1300, Palmer v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2000] FCA 1508, Huyng v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2005] AATA 195, and Buzadzic v Commissioner of Taxation [2024] FCAFC 50 [17]-[18] as examples.
  3. For instance:
    (a) While Ms Betty Chan’s evidence was broadly credible as to the existence of a loan arrangement between herself and Mr Fan which might explain a $20,000 aggregate deposit in Mr Fan’s bank account in the 2018 year, further evidence was needed from Mr Fan. Further evidence was needed to (amongst other matters) substantiate the amount of that loan so the Tribunal could be satisfied that the deposits sourced from Ms Chan did not in whole or in part comprise income. The source of the funds said to have been loaned to Ms Chan was also not explained by Mr Fan.[124] The Tribunal may well have accepted this explanation from Mr Fan if complete evidence had been given to it. Complete evidence was not given to the Tribunal. It was Mr Fan’s burden to provide sufficient evidence.

    (b) It may be that the $2,000 deposited each month by Mr Fan’s daughter was part of a monthly money-go-round between herself and her parents to eliminate unnecessary bank fees. This explanation was raised without notice to the Commissioner and insufficient evidence has been provided to substantiate it. The flow of funds from Ms Fan to Mr Fan, from Mr Fan to Mr Fan’s wife, and from Mr Fan’s wife back to Ms Fan is not evidenced as the bank statements are incomplete. to enable the Tribunal to I cannot determine where the funds in Mr Fan’s account went to. The Tribunal may have accepted this explanation if complete evidence had been given to it. It was Mr Fan’s burden to demonstrate this. I cannot be satisfied about the $2,000 deposits either.

    (c) Ms Fan’s evidence of a $5,000 payment she made to Mr Fan is also not properly explained; particularly its date, and why this was said to be a payment made by cash deposit when Ms Fan’s $2,000 deposits evidence her capability of doing a bank transfer. This evidence does not align with the dates of unexplained deposits in Mr Fan’s bank accounts. The Tribunal has not accepted this evidence has any weight. It is too imprecise and entirely insufficient.

    (d) Mr Fan has not explained all the cash deposits made into his bank accounts. His submission that they are potentially sourced from withdrawn funds from China is not properly explained as the dates and the amounts do not reconcile with withdrawals from Chinese bank accounts. Nor is it clear why amounts withdrawn by Mr Fan’s wife would be deposited in Mr Fan’s Australian bank account rather than her own. None of this is explained and nothing reconciles.

    (e) While Mr Fan has produced a letter from an accountant that asserts that he did not derive director’s fees from E Ltd, the writer of that letter was not called to give evidence, Mr Fan has not provided evidence from E Ltd substantiating Mr Fan’s submissions that he was not paid any additional cash payments by E Ltd for his services, nor explaining the bonus or meal arrangements Mr Fan asserts were in place between himself and E Ltd. The Tribunal does not have any material from E Ltd corroborating Mr Fan’s assertions.[125] Mr Fan has not challenged the Commissioner’s assertion that he was paid below award rates in wages, which might give rise to an inference that he was paid additional amounts or provided additional benefits by E Ltd which could comprise income for tax purposes. Mr Fan has said he did obtain other benefits from E Ltd, such as meals and wholesale grocery prices but he has not demonstrated why these benefits (if they were provided) should not be considered income for taxation purposes.

    (f) Mr Fan refers to other payments he received in cash (for example, from Xiuqiong Peng, Macao, his son-in-law, his mother and (potentially) other unexplained sources)[126] but he has provided no substantive evidence of their nature, when they were derived, why they are not clearly visible as deposits in his bank accounts, what all those amounts actually were, and why those amounts are not taxable.[127] There also might be additional rental income. Mr Fan’s evidence does not reconcile. The evidence is simply not satisfactory.

    (g) It is not sufficient for Mr Fan to simply pick at bits and pieces of the Commissioner’s estimates and assessments. He does not meet his burden of proof by doing so.

  4. I am also not satisfied Mr Fan has demonstrated what his income in the Relevant Years was as required by section 14ZZK of the TAA. He has not met his burden of proof to demonstrate to the Tribunal on the balance of probabilities that his income is what he returned in his tax returns as he submitted.[128] There is evidence of cash deposits in his bank accounts which are not properly explained or reconciled. There is evidence of income derived from sources (such as Xiuqiong Peng and potentially elsewhere)[129] which has not been returned in Mr Fan’s tax returns without explanation, and there is insufficient evidence of the source of all funds Mr Fan has received in his bank accounts. Further, Mr Fan provided submissions that there were benefits provided to him by E Ltd that might give rise to additional income.
  5. In those circumstances, I can only conclude that Mr Fan has not met his burden of proof, and I must affirm the Commissioner’s decision as a result.

THE PENALTIES ASSESSMENTS AT ISSUE - FACTUAL BACKGROUND, EVIDENCE AND CONCLUSIONS

Legal principles and the facts in this case

  1. Section 284-75 of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that liability for an administrative penalty arises if a taxpayer makes a statement to the Commissioner that is “false or misleading in a material particular.” A ‘statement’ can include a statement in a tax return.[130] I have concluded that Mr Fan did make a false or misleading statement in his tax returns because I am not satisfied that he has demonstrated that his income was as stated in his tax returns.
  2. If a shortfall amount arises under section 284-80 of Schedule 1 of the TAA, and that statement results from not taking reasonable care to comply with the tax legislation, recklessness as to the operation of a taxation law or intentional disregard of a taxation law, section 284-90 of Schedule 1 to the TAA defines the base penalty amount as a percentage of that shortfall. In the case of intentional disregard, that is 75%. For the avoidance of doubt, for the reasons stated above, I am not satisfied that Mr Fan has shown that the Commissioner’s assessments were excessive and what his income was in the Relevant Years. That in turn means that Mr Fan cannot demonstrate there is no shortfall for the purposes of the penalty regime.
  3. The shortfall penalty assessments imposed upon to Mr Fan in the Relevant Years in accordance with sections 298-75 and 284-90 of Schedule 1 to the TAA were on the basis that tax shortfalls resulted from intentional disregard by him or his tax agent.[131]
  4. The statutory provisions provide certain exclusions from the penalties regime, some of which cannot apply where an intentional disregard penalty is imposed.[132] One exclusionary provision that could apply is subsection 284-75(5) of Schedule 1 to the TAA. Subsection 284-75(5) provides that if a taxpayer or its agent took reasonable care in connection with making the statement, there is no liability for an administrative penalty. My conclusion is that Mr Fan cannot demonstrate that this exclusion applies to him. That is because he cannot demonstrate that reasonable care was taken by himself or his tax agent. He has provided no evidence or submissions to this effect. He may not have disclosed cash deposits to his tax agent. Mr Fan says his tax agent made errors.[133] I have found above that he has not met his burden of proof to satisfy me that his returns accurately record his income in the Relevant Years and there is unexplained income. That cannot be said to demonstrate reasonable care on Mr Fan’s part.
  5. There are also potential reductions to the base penalty amount. For example, section 284-225 of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides for such a reduction where there is a voluntary disclosure by a taxpayer of the shortfall. These provisions are not relevant to this case as a voluntary disclosure requires that a disclosure be made in the approved form as defined in section 388-50 of Schedule 1 of the TAA. There is nothing on the T documents demonstrating any such disclosure arose. Mr Fan proceeded on the basis that his tax returns demonstrated what his income was and he did not disclose errors. A voluntary disclosure has a tax technical meaning. Merely answering questions that the Commissioner asks is not a voluntary disclosure. Mr Fan’s suggestion in the Application for Review[134] that these provisions are relevant to his case is rejected by the Tribunal.
  6. There is a potential increase to the base penalty amount pursuant to section 284-220 of Schedule 1 to the TAA. This can apply in several contexts, including where the taxpayer has had a penalty imposed before (paragraphs 284-220(1)(c) to (e) of Schedule 1 to the TAA). This is relevant in this case as the Commissioner uplifted the base penalty amount in the 2019 and 2020 years by 20% in accordance with section 284-220 of Schedule 1 of the TAA.[135]
  7. When it comes to the penalty issues before me, Mr Fan bears the onus of proof that the imposition of a penalty was excessive.
  8. There is a wide discretion to remit shortfall penalties set out in subsection 298-20(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA which simply provides “The Commissioner may remit all of part of a penalty”. The Commissioner’s objection decision refused to remit the penalties in whole or in part in this case.[136] Mr Fan bears the onus of demonstrating to me that the penalties should be remitted in whole or in part.

The parties’ arguments

  1. The parties’ arguments are as follows:
    (a) Mr Fan says he did not knowingly understate any income nor make any false statements. Any errors were due to misunderstandings and misrepresentation by his prior tax agent, and not from deliberate or dishonest actions. He also says the base penalty uplift should not apply to him, as he did not know about the shortfalls, could not correct them and was not given an opportunity to address any issues. Mr Fan also points out how co-operative he was with the Commissioner, and that the penalties were disproportionate and unfair and should be remitted in whole or in part.[137]

    (b) The Commissioner says Mr Fan understated his income, did so “systematically” for three years, the shortfall was proportionally significant, it is reasonable to infer Mr Fan knew the omitted income should have been returned, repeated cash deposits and the use of cash demonstrate an intention to disguise or hide Mr Fan’s real taxable income, Mr Fan had actual knowledge his returns were wrong, and all of that means that Mr Fan met the dishonesty element of intentional disregard.[138] The Commissioner also says the base penalty uplift in paragraph 284-220(1)(c) of Schedule 1 to the TAA is applicable to 2019 and 2020 because Mr Fan had a penalty imposed in 2018.[139] Citing PS LA 2012/5, the Commissioner says that the base penalty uplift should not be remitted because Mr Fan’s behaviour amounted to intentional disregard and there was no voluntary disclosure that saved the Commissioner time.[140] The Commissioner says the penalty should not be remitted because there “are no further exceptional circumstances which would warrant a remission of [the] shortfall penalty applied.”[141]

Tribunal’s conclusions - Intentional disregard

  1. Intentional disregard is said by the case law to involve a number of factors:
    (a) The taxpayer must know that what is being done is contrary to tax law.[142]

    (b) There is an element of dishonesty.[143]

    (c) There must be deliberate conduct or a deliberate choice by the taxpayer to flout or ignore the tax law.[144]

    (d) The test for intentional disregard is subjective and requires considering the taxpayer’s actual knowledge.[145]

    (e) Intentional disregard differs from the recklessness penalty in section 284-90 which is an objective test. Recklessness arises when a claim is made not caring whether the claim is false. [146]

  2. The Tribunal has not had the benefit of an opportunity to assess Mr Fan’s credibility. He did not appear before the Tribunal as a witness or at all. What he has done however, is make suggestions as to why particular receipts that the Commissioner has treated as income are not his income. The Tribunal is not satisfied on the balance of probabilities that Mr Fan has established his case to that effect for the reasons outlined above. Mr Fan has simply presented incomplete and insufficient evidence. The fact that the Tribunal has no evidence from Mr Fan or anyone else as to what was thought about when he filed his tax returns does not help his case on penalties.
  3. On the other hand, the Tribunal is bemused by the Commissioner’s analysis on penalties. The Commissioner’s original position paper reached conclusions about Mr Fan linked to conclusions about E Ltd and the use of ESS and it later abandoned that in relation to Mr Fan.[147] In substance there is only one factor against Mr Fan when considering whether there is intentional disregard. That is his cash dealings.[148] This is a commonplace method to try to hide income from relevant authorities such as the Commissioner. The Tribunal can certainly understand and shares the Commissioner’s concern. Mr Fan puts his cash dealings down to cultural differences and his age.[149] The Tribunal does not accept those explanations.
  4. However, as was noted by Justice Logan in Russell v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2009] FCA 1224 at [186]:

I mean no disrespect to the Commissioner and his officers in observing that it is sometimes easy....to classify what is truly and objectively ignorance or genuine misunderstanding on the part of others as intentional disregard.

  1. There is no evidence in this case that resembles the evidence in (for example) Endycott and Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2026] ARTA 214 [430] that the taxpayer clearly knew that he was disregarding his obligations. The Commissioner says actual knowledge and deliberate dishonesty can be inferred in Mr Fan’s case merely because of his use of cash. I cannot see why such an inference demonstrates actual knowledge and dishonesty, as opposed to such an inference demonstrating indifference, a lack of care, and/or ignorance. That is particularly when I consider Mr Fan’s circumstances as presented to me. The problem I face is that Mr Fan has presented no evidence as to what he or his tax agent considered when his returns were filed. I have no witness evidence from him or his tax agent. I do not have any argument about whether a lesser standard of penalty should be imposed.
  2. In the light of that, I cannot conclude that Mr Fan has met his burden of proof on the balance of probabilities. I cannot reach a conclusion that shortfall penalties were improperly applied in the absence of any evidence or argument. In the light of that, I must affirm the Commissioner’s decision.

Tribunal’s conclusions - Remission

  1. Not for the first time of late,[150] the Tribunal finds itself bemused by a penalty remission decision from the Commissioner. The Commissioner’s objection decision barely cites any case law,[151] and nowhere does the Commissioner outline Mr Fan’s personal circumstances in the context of the remission power despite the case law clearly identifying to the Commissioner that it must consider those factors.[152] The Commissioner instead baldly refers to its guidance in PS LA 2012/5, particularly in respect of the base penalty uplift on aspects which the Tribunal has highlighted should be reconsidered taking into account the wording of the statutory provision.[153] The Commissioner relies upon the fact that there was no voluntary disclosure as a justification for failure to remit which has also been previously identified as a point to reconsider.[154] Compounding that, the objection decision suggests “exceptional circumstances” are needed to warrant remission.[155] That test is not in the statutory provisions and has been rejected by the Courts.[156]
  2. It is left to the Tribunal to outline Mr Fan’s relevant circumstances when considering remission. Mr Fan is in his 60s. He has been in Australia since 2007, so arrived here in his 40s. The bulk of his life has been spent in an entirely different jurisdiction to Australia. He is a chef. Mr Fan worked in a Chinese restaurant with, presumably, other Cantonese or Mandarin speakers. The Tribunal is told he is not proficient in English. He is not educated in Australian tax law. He appropriately sought tax advice when filing his tax returns. It is understood that Mr Fan may no longer be working and there is evidence on the T documents that he has some health issues. Mr Fan has been subject to serious scrutiny by the Commissioner, and due to his use of cash and his inability to reconcile payments, he has not been able to meet his burden of proof to demonstrate what his income in the Relevant Years was. That has left the Commissioner’s assessments standing.
  3. I am satisfied, on balance, that the penalties imposed upon Mr Fan should be remitted in part. I agree with and adopt the comments in Yuen and Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2026] ARTA 279 at [66]. I am not deciding that a lesser shortfall penalty should have been imposed, I am determining the question of remission, and I have concluded that overall, Mr Fan’s penalty should be partly remitted, but only to 50%.
  4. I have concluded that the imposition of intentional disregard penalties plus a base penalty uplift is unfair and unjust in the overall circumstances. This is for the following reasons:
    (a) The Commissioner’s objection decision applies tests that are not correct at law. Exceptional circumstances do not need to be demonstrated for the remission power to be exercised, and the imposition of an intentional disregard penalty does not of itself mean that penalties (including the base penalty uplift) cannot be remitted. That is demonstrable from the legislative provisions at issue. Section 298-20 of Schedule 1 to the TAA imposes no such constraints, and the Commissioner should not be creating its own additional tests to the legislative tests.

    (b) The Commissioner mechanically applied its policy in PS LA 2012/5 and did not consider Mr Fan’s circumstances properly in its objection decision when considering remission. The Commissioner drew inferences against Mr Fan, when less serious inferences could have been drawn from the same facts.[157] The Commissioner has not proceeded with ESS penalties it originally imposed upon Mr Fan which might have reflected dishonesty on Mr Fan’s part. The Commissioner does not appear to take that changed position into account when considering the remission of the shortfall penalties.

    (c) From the documentary evidence before the Tribunal, Mr Fan is not a sophisticated taxpayer. He is a migrant to Australia and worked in a relatively low skilled job. Errors have been made by Mr Fan, but the fact there are errors does not of itself mean that the remission power should not be exercised in the overall circumstances.

    (d) The object of the penalties regime needs to be considered. Penalties at 90% (75% plus a base penalty uplift of 20%) should be imposed on the worst category of taxpayers. Mr Fan is not in the category of taxpayers where there is clear evidence of actual knowledge and dishonesty, and evidence of resistance to the Commissioner’s inquiries.[158] The Commissioner has not proceeded with ESS penalties it originally imposed upon Mr Fan where that could be clear dishonesty. Mr Fan changed tax agents but did not fail to respond to the Commissioner’s questions and made detailed submissions. The facts that remain against him (his use of cash) could be viewed as demonstrating indifference, ignorance or a lack of care, as opposed to dishonesty.

    (e) Mr Fan should not be using cash in the manner he has done to date. But that alone is not a justification for a 90% overall penalty on top of income tax. Mr Fan may have been able to explain at least some aspects with a modicum of further evidence (for example if he had demonstrated the amount of the Betty Chan loan with further bank statements showing $20,000 leaving his account and the source of those funds). Of course, I cannot conclude that he would have been able to present such evidence, and this is why Mr Fan has not met his burden of proof when challenging the income tax assessments and why a penalty should be imposed upon him. But the point being made is that Mr Fan’s case is not nearly as clear cut as the other cases I have cited in this decision where taxpayers have been found to have acted with intentional disregard and had the base penalty uplift imposed as well.

  5. I have concluded that remission should be granted in the following manner:
    (a) The base penalty uplift should be remitted in full. I agree with Mr Fan’s submissions that it is unfair to impose this penalty for repeated errors in circumstances where it was only when the penalties were imposed that he knew of their repetition. I reiterate the comments made in SBXB and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 999 at [220] about example 12 of PS LA 2012/5. The mere fact that an intentional disregard penalty was imposed is not a rationale for there being no remission of the base penalty uplift. That was the Commissioner’s only rationale for not remitting the base penalty uplift.[159] The statutory provision contains no such constraint.

    (b) The shortfall penalty should be remitted to 50%. Mr Fan was grossly indifferent[160] in concluding cash and other benefits received did not have any tax consequences. I am concerned about the following aspects of Mr Fan’s behaviour:

    (i) Mr Fan has ignored the nature of cash payments he received. He appears to believe they have no taxation consequences. That is not necessarily the case. This is clear from the payments from Xiuqiong Peng for example. The Tribunal cannot see why those payments were not treated as rental income or another form of taxable income. Mr Fan has not considered the tax treatment of non-cash benefits he may have received from E Ltd. These also may have been taxable. It is also unclear.

    (ii) Mr Fan has been unable to cogently explain the cash deposits in his bank accounts. While the evidence he has presented to the Tribunal has raised questions, that evidence did not come close to meeting Mr Fan’s burden of proof. He cannot reconcile anything.

    (iii) There is also evidence of other cash payments which may have been received by Mr Fan which are not explained. There may be a hidden trickle of funds which is not visible. If Mr Fan persists in this behaviour, he will undoubtedly face further consequences. This is not an appropriate way to participate in the Australian community, and a penalty should be imposed.

  6. Because of those concerns, and after considering the object of the penalties regime, I consider it would be fair and reasonable to remit the penalty to 50% but no further.

OVERALL CONCLUSIONS

  1. The decision under review in each of the Relevant Years not to remit shortfall penalties and the base penalty uplift is set aside. That matter is remitted to the Commissioner with a direction to issue assessments of shortfall penalties for each of the Relevant Years at 50% and for the base penalty uplift to be fully remitted.
  2. All other aspects of the decisions under review are affirmed.
