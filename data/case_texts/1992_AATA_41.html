Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 41; 92 ATC 133 ; (1992) 23 ATR 1043 (4 February 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT88/575

AAT No. 7723

Income Tax

92 ATC 133

(1992) 23 ATR 1043

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Exempt income - Insurance business of friendly society - Advertising targeted at (i) assessable income, (ii) exempt income and (iii) mixed assessable and exempt income - Whether cost of all advertising apportionable - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1987, sec 23(g)(i), 116G, 116E.

W. Nevill and Co Ltd v F.C. of T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290

Palvestments Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1965] HCA 47; (1965) 112 CLR 661

F.C. of T. v Total Holdings 79 ATC 4279

John v F.C. of T. [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 166 CLR 417

Case Y5 91 ATC 120

HEARING

MELBOURNE

4:2:1992

ORDER

The objection decisions for the years under review be set aside and the assessments remitted to the Commissioner for amendment in accordance with the decision of the Tribunal.

DECISION

In this case, I have reached a conclusion on the relevant law which differs substantially from that adopted by either party. The end result is thus one which bears little resemblance to the case sought to be made by the applicant or the Commissioner of Taxation. The background, the facts and the law need therefore to be set out in some detail.

2. In the tax years ended 31 May 1984 to 1987 inclusive, the applicant, a Friendly Society (henceforth referred to as "FS") and a "registered organisation" for purposes of Division 8A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") claimed the following amounts as "advertising and public relations expenses" as a deduction pursuant to sub-sec 51(1) or sec 116H of the Act.

1984 1985 1986 1987

$ 145,634 $183,159 $140,567 $192,956

3. The special tax position enjoyed by registered organisations was recently examined by the Full Federal Court in Independent Order of Odd Fellows of Victoria v F.C. of T. 91 ATC 4001 and by the Full High Court on appeal (91 ATC 5032). In an useful summary of that position, both courts noted that the income derived by a registered organization is exempt income pursuant to sec 23(g)(i) of the Act. In their decisions both courts stated that this general exemption became qualified in respect of the income year ended 30 June 1985. However, on backtracking through the relevant legislation, I have, with the utmost respect, reached a different conclusion, viz. that this statutory qualification came into effect, not in the 1985 tax year as stated by both courts, but from the year of income beginning 1 July 1983. These different conclusions are of considerable significance to this taxpayer, whose applications include the 1984 year and, for good measure, ends its tax years on 31 May. Thus Div 8A of Pt III, comprising secs 116E-116J was inserted into the Act by the Income Tax Assessment Amendment Act (No 3) of 1984, coming into operation "in respect of income of the year of income that commenced on 1 July 1983 and of all subsequent years of income" cf sec 60(4) of Act No 47 of 1984. The effect of sec 116F and 116G (as amended, having regard to the definitions contained in sec 116E) was that notwithstanding sec 23(g)(i):

116G(1) The assessable income of the year of income of a

registered organization being a friendly society shall include so

much of the total income (other than premiums) of the society of

the year of income as is derived from eligible insurance business

of the society.

The term "eligible insurance business" is defined by sec 116E to mean:

business of, or in relation to, the issuing of, or the undertaking of

liability under eligible insurance policies.

which are in turn defined to mean:

(a) a life assurance policy;

(b) an accident policy; or

(c) a disability policy,

but does not include so much of a policy referred to in paragraph (a), (b) or (c) as is a sickness policy, a funeral policy or an eligible policy.

Sec 116H provided during the relevant period that:

Notwithstanding any other provision of this Act, the deductions

allowable to a registered organization in relation to the year of

income are:

(a) any deductions that, apart from this section, would be allowable

to the organization in relation to the year of income, being

deductions that relate exclusively to the assessable income of the

organization; and

(b) so much of any other deductions that, apart from this section,

would be allowable to the organization in relation to the year of

income (other than deductions that relate exclusively to exempt

income of the organization) as bears to the amount of those other

deductions the same proportion as the assessable income of the

organization of the year of income bears to the total income of the

organization of the year of income.

4. Thus certain classes of income of a registered organisation are deemed to be assessable pursuant to sec 116G, and deductions relating exclusively to that assessable income are taken into account in determining the taxable income of that registered organisation. Deductions (other than those that relate exclusively to exempt income) which, apart from sec 116H would be allowable, relating generally to the business of the registered society, are deductible on a proportional basis, having regard to the assessable income and the total income of that organisation.

5. In each of the years now in dispute, FS included the cost of all the advertising of the various Management Funds as part of its apportionable expenses, which thus became part of its apportionable expenditure pursuant to the formula contained in para (b) of sec 116H; in each year the Commissioner excised it. It was agreed by both parties that the 1987 year was typical of all the earlier years and that the fiscal consequences in 1987 would, for tax purposes, apply to the other years. Thus, in the 1987 year, FS returned as assessable income an amount of $31,502,614, its apportionable expenses adding up to $5,882,819. Applying the formula using the apportionable expenses as the multiplier, FS thus arrived at an amount of $1,291,408 as apportionable expenditure:

(Assessable income x Apportionable expense)

Total income

= 31,502,614 x 5,882,819

143,505,485

= $1,291,408

Included in the apportionable expenses was the impugned $878,982, identified as "advertising and public relations" expenditure claimed to have been incurred by the various Management Funds of FS.

6. In an amended assessment issued 1 February 1988, the respondent disallowed the $878,982 on the basis that it was not allowable as a deduction under sec 51(1) or sec 116H of the Act and, deducting this amount from the apportionable expenses as returned ($5,882,819), the Commissioner arrived at adjusted apportionable expenses of $5,003,837. When applying the statutory formula using $5,003,837 as the multiplier, this left the apportionable expenditure as $1,098,452, viz.

$31,502,614 x 5,003,837 = $1,098,452.

$143,505,485

7. It will be seen therefore that the taxpayer's apportionable expenditure was reduced by $192,956 ($1,291,408 - $1,098,452), which had the effect of increasing its taxable income by that amount, i.e. from $30,207,106 to $30,400,062.

8. In its objection to the disallowance of the advertising expenses, FS asserts that "the expenses were incurred in advertising and promoting the Society in order to attract new members, thereby generating premium income, which in turn is used to derive the taxpayer's assessable income".

9. To understand the complexity of this case, one needs to delve into FS's background. Thus the returns for the relevant years disclose that FS operated some 47 odd Funds, each with its separate history and function. In the 1950s it entered into the medical benefits business and over the ensuing years built up income from that source.

10. In a further effort to expand its business operations, FS launched into what is generally referred to as "flexible insurance" in about 1980. This type of insurance involves participants paying the insurer a single "up front" premium. The advantage of such insurance is perceived to be that on the maturity of the policy, the insured not only receives his premium back together with whatever bonuses the premium may have attracted, but these bonuses are given concessional tax treatment pursuant to secs 26AH and 160AAB.

11. In order to obtain that concessional treatment, it is, of course, necessary for the investment to constitute "life insurance". This is achieved by so structuring these kind of policies that on the death of the assured before maturity, the original sum invested is returned to the estate - depending on which species of bond is involved - with an additional two or three per cent; another species of policy involves an ex gratia bonus where a death occurs prior to the bonus declaration period.

12. These life policies are generally regarded as constituting investments, rather than life policies in the traditional sense, and it was conceded in evidence that most, or a large number, of the policies emanate through professional investment advisers and are presented to the public as an investment product. (p 31 tr). However, this has no bearing on the outcome of these applications.

13. I now turn to the advertising, the cost of which has been disallowed. In the main, FS advertises in what was claimed to be three separate categories of advertising, examples of each being made exhibits in this case. Bundle 1 is labelled ADVERTISING IN RELATION TO THE COMPANY'S IMAGE AND MULTIPLE PRODUCTS,

bundle 2 is said to be ADVERTISING DIRECTED TO NON-INVESTMENT PRODUCTS

SPECIFICALLY and bundle 3 claims to be ADVERTISING DIRECTED TO INVESTMENT

PRODUCTS SPECIFICALLY.

14. FS was unable to lay its hands on every advertising invoice, year by year. Taking the "typical" 1987 tax year, the Society succeeded in accounting for an expenditure of some $607,377, said to constitute some 69.1% of the total amount expended on advertising in that year and said to be representative of all the advertising costs incurred. 21.4% is in category 1, 39.6% category 2, and 39% in category 3. The result of this breakdown is set out below:

1. Image/Corporate/Multiple Products 21.40% $188,102

2. Investment Products 39.60% $348,077

3. Non-Investment Products 39.00% $342,803

100.00%

1987 Advertising claimed $878,982

(These figures were tendered as a "broad brush" apportionment and were accepted by the Crown merely for purposes of the argument)

15. It is here that I part company with the applicant's treatment of these expenditures. Having dissected them into the three distinct categories as shown above, it is too late to lump them together again as though they produced the same fiscal consequences. It seems to me that when these ads are examined, it is quite apparent that the advertisements in each category are targeted specifically at one or other of FS's different operations. Category 3 is aimed at attracting members of the public into its the various non-investment products, category 3 is a "mixed bag", containing "plugs" for many of FS's products, with the occasional mention of its "investment products", whilst category 2, as has already been pointed out, is aimed at attracting the one-off flexible bond premiums. A typical ad tells potential customers that:

When you put your money into Holydooley 2 Bonds, an astonishing

thing happens. Not only can your money triple in ten years then free

of tax because they're Friendly Society Assurance Bonds ... So if

you have between $1,000 and $100,000 to invest, send it off where

it will get a great return.

Another ad asks:

IS TAX TAKING A LARGE CHUNK OUT OF YOUR INVESTMENT? If you're

not careful taxation can eat into your investment and devour a lot

of your return. But if you invest in Holydooley Series 2 Bonds ...etc.

In other words, these catchy ads are designed to persuade those members of the public with spare money in the bank or under the mattress to put it into Holydooley Series 2 Bonds (otherwise known as "life policies"). This conclusion receives further support from the FS's magazine for August 1986:

Life Assurance Benefits: The higher profile advertising for

Holydooley Series 2 Bonds has undoubtedly been successful with

new business levels achieving an average of approximately $1

million per week. This clearly demonstrates that the public has

confidence in our Society and are prepared to invest their surplus

funds with us. In return we endeavour to ensure at all times that

we provide a high level of service complemented by acceptable

bonus rates. The introduction of the Instalment Plan has provided

a further alternative within our life assurance products and

therefore provides a greater choice for members and the public to

select the products most suitable to their own particular

circumstances. However, the products which we do offer must be

sold and we cannot expect the public to come to us, particularly

when we note the substantial competition in the marketplace.

Public advertising necessarily assists the selling of our products,

but I urge all active members to participate in the promotion of

our life assurance products and subsequently, the new business

which must result.

16. Against this background the Crown seeks nonetheless to defend the assessments, in the main, on the proposition that the advertisements in category 2 were solely directed to obtaining premiums, i.e. exempt income. In other words, since all the income of the Society (other than the investment income derived from its eligible insurance business), constitutes exempt income, and none of the advertising was directed, so it was said, to the production of eligible insurance business, and therefore the cost of advertising was not apportionable. Mr Fajgenbaum QC, of learned senior counsel for the Crown, sought to prove the point that the applicant's sole aim of all the advertising in this category was to obtain premium income, i.e. exempt income, by rigorous cross-examination of Mr W. who held a senior accounting position with the applicant:

"Mr Fajgenbaum: The only purpose of advertising, so far as the

investment products are concerned, so far as the life assurance

funds generally are concerned, is to attract premium income? Is

that not so?---I would say that that is not the only reason at all.

Well, a person reading the advertisement is being asked to do what,

so far as the Society is concerned, from watching the TV?---To

place their confidence in the Society.

And how do they place that confidence in the Society?---By

placing their money with us for us to invest it safely, wisely, for

them.

And that money which is placed with the Society for the Society to

invest wisely is the premium income?---It is.

And the advertisements have no purpose other than to encourage

citizenry to whom the advertisements are directed to pay

premiums to the Society?---I don't think that is the only reason

because it has a number of other reasons, because we are dealing

in an environment where there are many investment products

available to the public, and we believe that this particular product

has benefits that compete with those other investment products.

Now, in addition to that area, there is the area of encouraging our

existing members to keep their money within the Society, that by

advertising the success of our bonus rate, the continuity of the

availability of a product, that in our view continues to encourage

those who have placed their moneys with us to retain it there.

So there are two bases then; one is the advertisements so far as

the fresh investor is concerned - or a person wanting to make a

new investment is concerned - if he is already a member of the

Society, to get him to place his money for investment with the

Society?---Yes.

And that money so placed for investment with the Society is

premium income and nothing else, so far as the Society is

concerned?---Well, it is received as the premium contribution for

that investment product that they have purchased.

Yes. And the existing contributor, or the existing holder of an

investment product who is being encouraged not to surrender early,

is being encouraged to keep with the Society that which he placed

originally with it, which was premium income in the Society's

hand, together with whatever is approved by way of investment in

the interval?---Yes, he is being encouraged to retain that money

with the Society."

17. Mr Forsyth QC, of learned senior counsel for the applicant, approached the issue with more subtlety. After taking me through all three categories of the advertising material which became exhibits, he submitted at the end of this odyssey that the common strand running through all ads in all categories was that FS is an organisation that is "long established, solid, reliable, trustworthy" which will stick by you through thick and thin, although conceding that the connection between different advertisements and FS's assessable income "might well vary from 5% to 90%". However, this did not faze him ("We are not going to worry about looking at each one in turn because it would be a dreadful business. Instead, we are going to say: once it gets into the formula, it gets apportioned according to the formula ... where you have a friendly society, there is a statutory recognition that it has got all these different activities going on and that precise apportionment and allocation will be a pain in the neck, and accordingly, let us have a formula instead"). It is this argument which, with respect, has not persuaded me. A common theme is not enough, given the nature of the advertisements, to bring them all into the formula.

18. Dealing with the category 2 advertisements, i.e. those directed to the insurance arm of the Society's business, Mr Forsyth sought to support the inclusion of this expenditure by submitting that it was a "public relations exercise" aimed at attracting investors into FS's flexible insurance scheme, and although the premiums thus attracted constituted exempt income, the advertising costs were nonetheless an allowable deduction because the premium income was invested by FS and yielded assessable income (albeit taxed at only 20%). Thus, he submits, the advertising cost is an allowable deduction under either of the two limbs of sub-sec 51(1), and hence an apportionable expenditure pursuant to para (b) of sec 116H.

19. Thus it will be seen that the applicant's principal argument rests predominantly on the proposition that, in considering whether money is expended in the production of assessable income, one can have regard not only to the immediate, direct or "first order" effect, but also to what Mr Forsyth felicitously referred to as the "indirect, second order effect". So far so good. If a business is devoted to the gaining of both assessable income and exempt income, the cost is clearly apportionable. From there, Mr Forsyth proceeded to cite a long line of cases which establish that, in calculating the taxable income of a taxpayer, one starts off with the total assessable income derived in the relevant year, from which one then deducts all losses and outgoings actually incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, not being in the nature of losses and outgoings of capital or of a private or domestic nature. Thus in W Nevill and Co Ltd v F.C. of T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290, Latham C.J. noted:

... it is necessary, for income tax purposes, to look at a business as

a whole set of operations directed towards producing income. No

expenditure, strictly and narrowly considered, in itself actually

gains or produces income. It is an outgoing not an incoming. Its

character can be determined only in relation to the object which

the person making the expenditure has in view. If the actual object

is the conduct of the business on a profitable basis ... then the

expenditure (if not of a capital nature) is an expenditure incurred

in gaining the assessable income. (at p 301)

20. Next came Palvestments Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1965] HCA 47; (1965) 112 CLR 661. In Palvestments, the appellant derived income from dividends and from property other than dividends and was entitled to a deduction for, inter alia, interest paid on an overdraft. Upon the basis that some part of the overdraft had been used in the purchase of shares which yielded dividends in the year of income, the Commissioner of Taxation sought, in reliance upon sec 50(a), to apportion the deduction for interest paid on overdraft as between the income from dividends and the income from property other than dividends in the same proportions as the dividend income and the income from other property bore to the total income, and to deduct the amount so apportioned to the income from dividends as relating directly to such income from the amount of the income from dividends before calculating the rebate of tax to which the appellant was entitled under sec 46 of the Act.

Menzies J held that no part of the deduction for interest paid on overdraft related directly to the income from dividends, and accordingly, the calculation made by the Commissioner, based on the formula which he applied, was arbitrary and unrealistic. The assessment was therefore set aside.

21. Mr Forsyth placed even greater reliance on the decision of Hill J in Kidston Goldmines Ltd v F.C. of T. 91 ATC 4538. Both the facts and his Honour's reasons are succinctly set out in the headnote: The taxpayer in that case acquired an option over a gold mining property in 1978. Financing was initially provided partly through interim loans. However, the taxpayer subsequently entered into an arrangement with a bank for a credit facility to finance the development of the mine, including the repayment of the interim loans. As well as the credit facility, the taxpayer had short-term borrowings with several other financial institutions. The purpose of these borrowings was (i) to provide working capital for use in the business of the taxpayer, (ii) to fund the operation of the mine, (iii) to pay interest and principal on the loans and (iv) to fund dividend payments. The taxpayer incurred interest on the credit facility and the short-term borrowings.

The mine commenced commercial production in 1985. The cash balances of the taxpayer fluctuated considerably due to timing differences between gold sales, production schedules and interest payments. At various times the taxpayer invested surplus cash in the short-term money market and earned interest income on the deposits.

For the 1985/86 and 1986/87 years of income the Commissioner treated the interest income as assessable, and disallowed a deduction for any of the interest incurred.

The taxpayer maintained that the interest income was exempt under sec 23(o) as the income was derived from the working of a mining property. However, if the income derived was held to be assessable, then the taxpayer would claim a deduction under sec 51(1) for a portion of the interest expense incurred.

Hill J allowed the appeal in part, holding that:

If a business is devoted to the gaining of both assessable income and exempt income, some part of the interest outgoing will be deductible and it will be necessary to carry out a fair and reasonable apportionment of that outgoing. His Honour noted that in most cases, the purpose of the borrowing will be ascertained from the use to which the borrowed funds were put. The interest outgoings in the case before him, constituted working expenses of the business of the taxpayer. The interest outlaid was thus a cost of the short-term investments, just as it was a cost of the activity of extracting ore from the ground and selling gold. It was relevant and incidental to the gaining of the interest income, just as it was relevant and incidental to the taxpayer's business which was directed at earning both assessable and exempt income. It followed that a portion of the interest was deductible.

22. However, the most critical aspect of his Honour's decision relevant to this case is to highlight the mistake I made in Case Y5 91 ATC 120, when he stated:

Although it can be said here that the investment on the short term

money market is an incident of the taxpayer's gold mining

business, and represents the investment of moneys' derived from

gold sales, it is not sufficiently proximate from the activities

which more directly generate that income and which are properly

described as the working of the mining property. A similar

conclusion was reached by a Deputy President of the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal, Dr Gerber in Case Y5 91 ATC 120,

on facts not greatly dissimilar to the present. With respect,

although agreeing with the conclusion which the learned Deputy

President reached, I think that in saying that the income must be

derived "directly" from the exploitation of the lease, the learned

Deputy President expressed the test perhaps too favourably in

favour of the Commissioner. I would prefer to say that there must

be found a sufficiently proximate relationship between the income

and the exploitation of the mining property such that it can be

fairly said that the income is an incident of the working of the

mining property. If that relationship exists, the income will be

exempt from tax under s. 23(o). (at p 4543; my italics)

In other words, the heresy I committed in Case Y5 was to search for a "direct" nexus with the derivation of the assessable income of the taxpayer when, in truth, I should have asked myself whether or not there was a sufficiently proximate relationship between the income on the one hand, and the business activity from which it was derived on the other.

23. I agree with the submission by Mr Forsyth that that test applies with equal force mutatis mutandis to the nexus required between an outgoing and assessable income pursuant to sub-sec 51(1). Applying that test to the three categories of advertising in this case, I start by examining category 2. Thus the witness W made it clear that the insurance arm of the Society's business was aimed at attracting premium income as part of its overall activities and to invest the premiums thus attracted to yield assessable income.

24. Once one accepts that fact, as I do, the case (as far as category 2 is concerned) falls squarely into the famous dictum in Ronpibon Tin NL v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1948-1949) 78 CLR 47:

The charges for management and the directors' fees are entire

sums which probably cannot be dissected. But the provision

contained in s.51(1), as has been already said, contemplates

apportionment. The question what expenditure is incurred in

gaining or producing assessable income is reduced to a question of

fact when once the legal standard or criterion is ascertained and

understood. This is particularly true when the problem is to

apportion outgoings which have a double aspect, outgoings that are

in part attributable to the gaining of assessable income and in part

to some other end or activity. It is perhaps desirable to remark

that there are at least two kinds of items of expenditure that

require apportionment. One kind consists in undivided items of

expenditure in respect of things or services of which distinct and

severable parts are devoted to gaining or producing assessable

income and distinct and severable parts to some other cause. In

such cases it may be possible to divide the expenditure in

accordance with the applications which have been made of the

things or services. The other kind of apportionable items consists

in those involving a single outlay or charge which serves both

objects indifferently. Of this directors' fees may be an example.

With the latter kind there must be some fair and reasonable

assessment of the extent of the relation of the outlay to

assessable income. It is an indiscriminate sum apportionable, but

hardly capable of arithmetical or rateable division because it is

common to both objects. (at p 59)

25. Applying that dictum to this case, once a sufficient degree of proximity between the advertising and the derivation of FS's assessable income has been established, the expenditure "does not get into the formula", as submitted by Mr Forsyth, i.e. is not apportioned, but is allowed in full under sub-sec 51(1) (subject to satisfactory substantiation). Where, as here, the one business derives what Mr Forsyth has so felicitously termed first and second order income, there is neither room for the kind of apportionment undertaken on behalf of FS under the formula in its return, nor indeed under sub-sec 51(1), if only because it is not possible "to divide the expenditure in accordance with the applications which have been made of the things or services" (Ronpibon Tin NL (supra at p 59).

26. Mr Fajgenbaum provided a very different interpretation of Division 8A. Thus he submitted that FS never gets to sub-sec 51(1) at all because the deductions referred to in para (a) of sec 116H must be deductions:

"that apart from this section, would be allowable to the

organisation ... being deductions that relate exclusively to the

assessable income of the organisation",

and since the grouped advertising cannot be said to relate exclusively to the assessable income of the organisation, sub-sec 51(1) has no application. Para (b), on the other hand, is said to address itself to:

"so much of any other deductions that, apart from this section (my emphasis) would be allowable to the organization ... (other than deductions that relate exclusively to exempt income of the organization) as bears to the amount of those other deductions the same proportion as the assessable income of the organization of the year of income bears to the total income of the organisation of the year of income".

Examples of "so much of any other deduction" encompassed by para (b) is said by Mr Fajgenbaum to be a gift made by a friendly society which is deductible pursuant to sec 78; likewise employer superannuation contributions. Claims with respect to those outgoings cannot be made under sec 51(1), having no nexus with the production of assessable income. Thus, a gift made by a friendly society to, say, the Sydney Opera House Appeal Fund, being only deductible because specifically provided for by sec 78, and unrelated to derivation of assessable income, becomes, so says the Crown, an apportionable expenditure if made by a friendly society.

27. With all due respect to Mr Fajgenbaum's interesting argument, I find his construction of Div 8A less than wholly persuasive. In the end, I have not been persuaded that a deduction for an expenditure incurred in attracting funds for purposes of investment in order to derive (assessable) income, is fiscally compromised by the fact that the funds thus attracted happen to be tax-free premiums. In other words, I am not convinced that a friendly society advertising life insurance incurs "undivided items of expenditure in respect of things or services of which distinct and severable parts are devoted to gaining or producing assessable income and distinct and severable parts to some other cause" (Ronpibon Tin NL supra at p 59). I ask myself: "What other cause"? Thus once it can be demonstrated that an outgoing is incidental and relevant to the gaining or producing assessable income, I am satisfied that one is immediately catapulted into sub-sec 51(1) and, where appropriate, apportionment; cf Ronpibon Tin NL (supra); John v F.C. of T. [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 166 CLR 417; Fletcher and Ors v F.C. of T. 91 ATC 4950. I do not believe that Div 8A has qualified that position with respect to friendly societies.

28. By parity of reasoning, none of the advertising in category 3 is an allowable expense (and thus apportionable) since it is exclusively directed at non-investment products. The advertising encompassed in category 1 involves a more complex issue, containing both investment and non-investment products. On balance, I have concluded that this "mixed" advertising is an apportionable expense, "being a single outlay or charge which serves both objects indifferently" (Ronpibon Tin NL (supra) at p 59).

29. FS, having dealt with each of the three categories separately and placed each in its appropriate fiscal slot, must "suffer" the fiscal consequences that flow from that separation.

30. For the sake of completeness and in the event this case goes further, I will briefly deal with the other evidence produced and arguments advanced.

31. It was part of Mr Forsyth's case "that there is from every advertisement by FS some spin-off benefit to the investment products", hence the cost of all the advertising comes into the formula. He gave the homely example that if HJ Heinz and Co were to launch an advertising campaign for, say, baked beans "then there is some spin-off to all the other 56 varieties". To gain support for that proposition, Mr Forsyth called as his witness one of Australia's foremost advertising experts, Mr John Clemenger, who headed a large advertising agency before his recent retirement from that agency, as well as being the inaugural chairman of the Advertising Federation of Australia, to name but a few of this witness' impeccable qualifications.

32. It is fair to say that Mr Clemenger was more guarded in his support for the "baked beans selling tomato soup" theory. He stated that one needs to examine that proposition on a case by case basis, adding "but certainly, the advertising of one product in a range is beneficial normally to the brand name or the corporation and does have a beneficial effect". He went on to say that corporate advertising, if well done, "can be extremely advantageous to the company involved over the full range of services and activities and its new business prospects". Mr Clemenger sought support for his views by referring to a number of texts and articles which the applicant sought to tender over the Crown's objection. In the end, after much argument, I admitted an extract (pages 595-602) from a book by Belch and Belch, described by the witness as the leading text on corporate advertising (exh "L").

33. Having studied this material and the various samples of the applicant's advertising, it seems to me that none of the Society's material exhibited can be described as "corporate advertising" as that term appears to be understood in the advertising industry. The evidence suggests that fundamental to corporate advertising is the aim "to promote the organisation itself rather than its products or services" (Belch and Belch p 595). This can consist, according to Belch and Belch, of image advertising, which may accomplish a number of objectives, including that of goodwill, both internally and externally, creating a position for the company, and generating resources - both human and financial. The book highlights an example of this type of corporate advertising at p 597, which reproduces the frontispiece of a brochure depicting a clean cut, early 20s nordic male - Siegfried in a pin stripe suit - gazing wistfully into the distance above the caption:

"If you were creating the ideal firm for the 1990s what would it

look like?"

Discreetly, at the side of the picture, appears the name Deloitte and Touche with no indication who they are or what they do. This ad is said to be an example of corporate advertising "designed to attract college graduates ... if you are a graduating senior considering a career in accounting this brochure would be of interest to you".

34. Then there is a form of corporate advertising described as "Generating financial support". This is what Belch and Belch say:

Some corporate advertising is designed specifically to generate

investments in the corporation. By creating a more favourable

image, the firm will look attractive to potential stock purchasers

and investors. More investments mean more working capital, more

moneys' for research and development, and so on. Thus, in this

instance, corporate image advertising is almost an attempt to

make a sale - the product is the firm itself.

While there is no concrete evidence that corporate image

advertising will lead directly to increased investment, at least one

study does show that a correlation does exist between the price of

stock and the amount of corporate advertising done. That is, that

those firms with higher dollars spent in corporate advertising also

tend to have higher-priced stocks.

35. Fundamental to "corporate advertising", according to Belch and Belch:

"is that the ads do not directly appeal to anyone, are not

understood, and do not promote anything specific ... (T)hey typically

do not ask directly for a purchase; they do not ask for investors;

rather, they present a position or try to create an image".

36. Mr Clemenger was not cross-examined.

37. The evidence of this witness, however relevant to the way the case was conducted, i.e. lumping all three categories of advertising together, can have little bearing on the way I have decided the case. However, if I am wrong, what weight do I attach to this renowned expert on advertising? More to the point: can advertising, which does no more than enhance the Society's image, nevertheless piggyback into the formula on the baked beans/tomato soup theory and/or by emphasising that the taxpayer is a sound, solid and long-established friendly society that cares for its members? Fortunately, none of the ads exhibited in this case was directed to corporate advertising simpliciter, each hawked one or other of this taxpayer's products. If, however, some ads are subsequently discovered which do no more than put forward a corporate image, I will add that Mr Clemenger's evidence has not wholly persuaded me that there is a sufficiently proximate relationship between the income and the outgoing "to come into the formula". Having said that, I should also add that I do not believe it to be the function of the Commissioner (or this Tribunal) to sit in judgement on the commercial effect of corporate advertising. If management holds the belief - however misguided - that there is some elusive "spin-off" from corporate advertising which is incidental and relevant to its assessable income and is part of its business activities, so be it; cf F.C. of T. v Total Holdings 79 ATC 4279. Be that as it may, on the evidence it is sufficient for my purposes to content myself with observing that at this point in time, the baked beans/tomato soup theory of advertising remains unproven.

38. Again, and purely for the sake of completeness the witness W previously referred to informed the Tribunal as to the workings of the various limbs of the applicant's activities. Suffice it to say that the thrust of his evidence was that the moneys' of each of the various Funds operated by the Society are kept strictly separate, each Fund keeping its own set of accounts. It seems that in accordance with the schedule approved by the Registrar of Friendly Societies, FS may, and does, charge each of the various Funds a management fee. In the case of the Holydooley Series 2 Bonds, the renewal management fee was 1% of the total value of the Fund. The Society credits the money it charges the Fund to the Life Assurance Management Fund from which comes the expense to meet advertising, wages and the costs of operating all of the Life Assurance activities of the Society. A similar arrangement operates for the non-investment products, the amount which can be deducted for management being approved by the Government through the Registrar's Office. The amounts can vary in that all the Funds do not have the same percentage (the one exception being the Health Insurance Fund, which is administered through the Health Insurance Advisory Council where the applicable percentage commission must accord with its own guidelines).

39. In summary, the taxpayer controls all the various specific Funds as provided by the rules, which are kept separate and distinct. There are two management funds - the United Management Fund and the Life Assurance Management Fund - i.e. one for investment products, the other for all the Funds which are contributed to by the specific funds at a fixed rate. It is then up to the taxpayer to determine each year how much of the Management Fund will be spent on salaries, advertising, etc and how much will be built up in reserve. Mr W was asked how the Society apportioned its advertising expenditure between the Life Assurance Management Fund and the United Management Fund. He explained that in the 1986/87 year, this was done

on a combination of standing journal entries where the marketing

department had a budget for advertising for the year, and as

expense was incurred, the expense was allocated within the

marketing department to whichever type of advertising it related

to... so each invoice, before it was paid, was classified as to type

of advertising it related to. But the amount that appears in the

accounts of the end of the year was based on the standing journal

entry, plus the transfer of some specific invoices across, so that

for the financial year 86/87, the charge to the Life Insurance

Management Fund was $400,109, the charge to the United

Management Fund was $478,783, which combined gives us the total

of $878,982, which is the figure shown on the supporting schedule

for the income tax return.

40. The witness conceded in cross-examination that if all the invoices totalling $878,982 were analysed, the amount falling into Category 2 (Investment Products) would come "somewhere between $348,000 and $400,000" because the figure of $400,109 "was based partly on the budgeted amount allowed for expense for life assurance advertising for that particular year". In re-examination, when it was pointed out to him that some $188,102 had been expended on Image/Corporate/Multiple Products (Category 1), it followed that this must have been allocated between the two funds, hence the $400,109 includes a share of corporate advertising. The witness conceded that he had overlooked that in answering Mr Fajgenbaum.

41. The moneys' received from the life insurance arm of the Society (the Member Fund) were at the relevant time handed to Messrs Dominguez, Barry, Samuel Montague to be invested subject to the requirements of the Friendly Societies Act and the Trustees Authorised Investments Act and earned a healthy (assessable) income.

42. Finally, a Mr C was called who is the executive director of the taxpayer. He confirmed the reason why the Society thought it necessary to advertise publicly:

Well, our corporate objectives were in fact established in the late

70s, being a very different organisation in the 80s to what we

were up until the early 1980s. We realised that we were very

much in the commercial world, we were competing in the financial

sector, we were competing with all types of organisations and in

our view banks, building societies, life companies anybody that

was seeking a dollar - an investment dollar from the public.

Consequently in 1983, particularly in 1983, we examined our

corporate position and determined that we had to concentrate to a

far greater degree on the commercial aspects of our organisation

than we became a much more vital organisation, we became an

organisation which deliberately corporate-planned each year on a

three year rolling basis. We were in the early stages of budgeting

in the early 80s. Since that time our budgeting and our reporting

to the board has tightened to a very significant degree, to an

extent that we believe that we are a major player in the financial

sector, and particularly in that sector in which friendly societies

operation.

We certainly believe that any advertising that we undertake be it

corporate or product advertising, specific product advertising, it

does highlight the organisation the strength the stability of the

organisation and enables us to pursue not only the product that we

may be specifically advertising but the promotion of other

products.

43. Exhibit "J" is a chart which traces the growth of the Society's assets between 1981 and 1990. In 1981, the Society's assets were $41m. From then onwards its assets grew almost exponentially; by 1990 its assets had risen to $670m.

44. In the result, this taxpayer has demonstrated three species of advertising: one is aimed at deriving its assessable income (category 2), and is allowable in full; one relates exclusively to exempt income (category 3) and is not allowable at all, category 1 "gets into the formula", being directed to both the gaining of assessable income and exempt income. Such a result may well be pain in the neck, as Mr Forsyth put it not without some elegance. If so, the taxpayer will need to look for compensation for this work-related injury to other remedial legislation.

45. Finally, assuming that my conclusion that the general exemption which formerly applied to friendly societies was qualified as from the year of income commencing 1 July 1983, all the advertising incurred by this taxpayer in the month of June 1983 is totally exempt from tax.

46. If the parties accept my decision, I am hopeful that they are ready, willing and able to determine for themselves how much advertising expenditure, year by year, falls into the deductible, non-deductible and apportionable categories. Should this turn out to be wishful thinking, I give the parties liberty to apply to restore the matter to the list on seven days' notice.
