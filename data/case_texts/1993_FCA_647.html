William John Crawford v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 647; (1993) 27 Atr 326 (22 December 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

WILLIAM JOHN CRAWFORD v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. QG134 of 1993

FED No. 964/93

Number of pages - 8

Income Tax

[1993] FCA 647; (1993) 27 ATR 326

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

GUMMOW(1), VON DOUSSA(2) and HILL(3) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - whether losses or outgoings in connection with bill discount facility used by borrower to lend to trustee of a trust at a rate of interest equal to those losses or outgoings are an allowable deduction in the years in which those losses or outgoings were incurred although no interest paid by trustee - relevance of purpose of trustee - whether open to Tribunal to find taxpayer's purpose was to gain or produce assessable income.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936: s.51(1).

Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1; discussed.

Commissioner of Taxation v Roberts [1992] FCA 363; (1992) 37 FCR 246; applied.

Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1981) 81 ATC 4100; distinguished.

HEARING

BRISBANE, 19 November 1993

22:12:1993, SYDNEY

Counsel and Solicitors HL Alexander instructed by Clayton Utz for Appellant:

Counsel and Solicitors G Fryberg QC and PG Bickford

for Respondent: instructed by Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. Appeal allowed.

2. Orders of Neaves J be set aside and lieu thereof it be ordered that:

(i) the application be allowed;

(ii) the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal be

set aside, the objection decisions under review also

be set aside and in lieu thereof the objections of

William John Crawford be allowed;

(iii) the assessments for the three years of income be

remitted to the Commissioner for reassessment in

accordance with law.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

GUMMOW J The facts and circumstances giving rise to this appeal are detailed in the reasons of Hill J. The primary judge had before him an appeal on a question of law under s. 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. His decision is reported (1993) 93 ATC 4545.

2. Before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the AAT") the appellant sought review of decisions disallowing his objections in respect of an amended assessment of income tax for the year ended 30 June 1986, and assessments for the years ended 30 June 1987 and 30 June 1988. The appellant had incurred acceptance fees and other bank charges and outgoings or losses in connection with the discounting of a bill facility with Westpac Banking Corporation ("Westpac").

3. The AAT found that Kymmead Pty Limited ("Kymmead") had obtained finance from Westpac for a real estate development known as the Gladstone Industrial Park Project ("the Project") and that in June 1986 Kymmead's indebtedness under its facility with Westpac was $560,000. Westpac held a guarantee from the appellant for that indebtedness. Kymmead was trustee of what was known as the Mead Trust, and had undertaken the financing of the Project. The appellant and Mr K.F. Harris were directors of Kymmead.

4. The interests of Mr Harris and his family in the Project were bought out by the appellant and his family after the failure of Mr Harris's company. This led in June 1986 to the reorganisation of the finances for the Project. The amount of the indebtedness to Westpac was reduced to $460,000 by the repayment of $100,000 out of a sale of shares belonging to the appellant's wife. The appellant then negotiated with Westpac an arrangement whereby, in effect, the indebtedness of $460,000 was assumed by him in place of Kymmead. Under this arrangement, on 20 June 1986, the appellant drew 90 days bank bills with a face value of $460,000. These were accepted by Westpac and discounted. The proceeds of $441,219.19 were paid to the account of Kymmead. The bills were subsequently rolled over and the draw down amount on the facility was reduced in September 1987 to $360,000 and in May 1988 to $320,000. The guarantee by the appellant to Westpac of the indebtedness of Kymmead was discharged.

5. In taking these steps to refinance the Project, the appellant acted upon the advice of his accountant. This was that by his taking over responsibility for the bill facility, the charges would be an immediate deduction for him, whereas if Kymmead continued to incur the charges they might only become deductible at some indeterminate time in the future as the Project was completed and sold.

6. On 1 November 1986, Kymmead acknowledged that the appellant had negotiated the bill facility with Westpac to enable him to make a loan to Kymmead to clear its bank debt. Kymmead acknowledged that the continuation of the appellant's loan to it was subject to the payment by Kymmead to the appellant of interest "equivalent to the amount of interest which I have incurred and will in the future incur on my bank bill borrowings". Kymmead also resolved that the first $500,000 of profit derived from the Project should be allocated and distributed to the appellant. This was said to be "to enhance the prospect of continuance" of the loan facility to Kymmead by the appellant.

7. Oral evidence was given to the AAT by the appellant and his accountant. Both were cross-examined by counsel for the Commissioner.

8. It was submitted to the AAT that the charges and outgoings incurred by the appellant in relation to the bill facility were deductible under s. 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") because they were incurred in the course of deriving assessable income, namely the interest agreed to be paid by Kymmead. The AAT referred to counsel's reliance upon a passage in the judgment of Lockhart J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Limited (1979) 43 FLR 217 at 224. His Honour there said that if a taxpayer incurs a recurrent liability for interest for the purpose of furthering his present or prospective income-producing activities, whether those activities are properly characterized as the carrying on of a business or not, generally the payment of the interest will be an allowable deduction. The AAT said that these propositions had equal force to the charges incurred by the appellant in respect of the bill facility, adding "while such charges are not correctly described as interest they are of a similar character although the discount is more correctly described as a loss rather than an outgoing" (para. 34).

9. After an extensive reference to Fletcher v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1, which included setting out the passage at 17-19, the AAT concluded (para. 40):

"40. Adopting that guidance from the High Court I must

first decide whether the (appellant) has or may be

expected to derive assessable income. The answer to

that is to my mind quite simple. The trustee decided

on 1 November 1986 that interest would be payable to

the applicant equivalent to the 'interest' which he

incurs on the bank bill facility (Exhibit R).

41. For these reasons I have come to the view that the

(appellant) did incur the charges for the purpose of

deriving assessable income and those charges are

therefore deductible in terms of the first limb of

sub-section 51 (1). I think the principles enunciated

by the High Court in Fletcher's case makes this result

inevitable."

10. Nevertheless, the primary Judge reached the opposite conclusion. The crucial passage appears, 93 ATC at 4554. His Honour held that it was not open to the AAT to conclude that the purpose of the appellant in incurring the losses and outgoings which were claimed as deductions was that of earning interest on the net proceeds of the discounted commercial bills which were lent to Kymmead in order that that company might discharge its obligation to Westpac. The primary Judge said that whilst it must be accepted that "part of the arrangement" between the appellant and Kymmead was that it would reimburse him for the relevant outgoings, "that was not the purpose in making the necessary arrangements to refinance the Gladstone Industrial Park Project". His Honour went on to hold that "the purpose" of the appellant in negotiating the facility in 1986 was to ensure that Kymmead remained firmly in control of the Project in the hope and expectation that it would eventually generate sufficient funds to enable Kymmead to discharge its liabilities, including repayment of its indebtedness to the appellant.

11. During the relevant years of income no payments were made by Kymmead to the appellant. This was because, as a practical matter, the making of such payments had to await the completion and sale of the Project. But s. 51 does not require that the purpose of the expenditure, the fees and charges paid by the appellant to Westpac, shall be the gaining of the income derived in each of the particular years of income.

12. The passage in Fletcher to which the AAT referred included the following propositions:

(i) it is commonly possible to characterize an outgoing as

being wholly of the kind referred to in the first limb

of sub-s. 51 (1) without any need to refer to the

taxpayer's subjective thought processes;

(ii) this ordinarily will be so where the outgoing gives

rise to a receipt of a larger amount of assessable

income because in such a case the objective

relationship between the outgoing actually made and

the greater amount of assessable income actually

earned suffices, without more, to characterize the

whole outgoing as one which was incurred in gaining or

producing assessable income;

(iii) however, at least in a case where the outgoing has been

voluntarily incurred, the end which the taxpayer

subjectively had in view in incurring it may, in the

circumstances of the particular case, constitute an element

and possibly the decisive element in characterization of

either the whole or part of the outgoing for the purposes
of

the sub-section;

(iv) what is in question is the existence of "a genuine and

not colourable relationship" between the expenditure

and the production of the income;

(v) further, when no relevant assessable income can be

identified or where it is less than the amount of the

outgoing, the disproportion involved may give rise to

a need to resolve the problem of characterization of

the outgoing by weighing the various aspects of the

whole set of circumstances, including the direct and

indirect objects and advantages sought by the

taxpayer, so as to determine whether the outgoing is

properly to be characterized "as genuinely and not

colourably incurred in gaining or producing the

assessable income".

13. The reasoning of the AAT and the conclusions which it reached must be understood on the footing that it had these points in mind.

14. A primary submission of the Commissioner to us was, in effect, that this was a case where it had been essential to have regard to what one might call the "subjective" purpose of the taxpayer in making the outgoings. Then it was submitted by the Commissioner that there was in the taxpayer's evidence a lack of direct assertion by him that he incurred the expenditure in respect of the Westpac facility for the purpose of deriving assessable income as interest paid by Kymmead. It was somewhat curious for a submission to that effect to come from the Commissioner's side. Such assertions by the appellant no doubt would have been receivable as evidence in his favour which required consideration on its merits in the light of the circumstances of the case. But one would have to bear in mind the caution sounded by Fullagar J in Pascoe v Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 30 ALJR 402 at 403. That case was an "appeal" heard by the High Court in its original jurisdiction, pursuant to what was then s. 197 of the Act, where the issue concerned the applicability of s. 26 (a). Fullagar J said:

"Where a person's purpose or object or other

state of mind in relation to a given transaction

is in issue, the statements of that person in

the witness box provide, in a sense, the 'best'

evidence, but, for obvious reasons, they must,

as Cussen J, observed in Cox v Smail ((1912)

VLR 274 at 283), 'be tested most closely, and

received with the greatest caution'."

See also Jacob v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth (1971) 45 ALJR 568 at 569-570, McCormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; (1979) 143 CLR 284 at 291, 301-302, 314-315, Minister for Immigration Local Government and Ethnic Affairs v Gugerli [1992] FCA 238; (1992) 36 FCR 68 at 72-73, and Australian Builders' Labourers' Federated Union of Workers Western Australian Branch v J-Corp Pty Ltd [1993] FCA 266; (1993) 114 ALR 551 at 565.

15. The course of the evidence before the AAT is fully considered by Hill J in his reasons for judgment. I agree with his Honour's conclusion that there was ample evidence from which the AAT properly could conclude that the appellant took out the bill facility with Westpac and thereafter incurred the outgoings under it for the purpose of gaining or producing in the future assessable income from Kymmead, being receipts of interest in an amount not less than the outgoings. It was not put to the appellant that any other purpose was present, nor that the arrangements between the appellant and Westpac on the one hand, and the appellant and Kymmead on the other, were such that his outgoings were other than genuinely and not colourably incurred.

16. Upon the footing that, in this case, it was relevant, or even essential, for the AAT to have had regard to the "subjective" purpose of the appellant, there was material before it from which a conclusion favourable to the appellant upon this issue might properly be drawn. On the fair reading of the decision of the AAT, that conclusion was so drawn. It follows that on the hypothesis most favourable to the Commissioner, there was no error of law in the decision of the AAT, and that the decision of the AAT should not have been set aside by the primary Judge.

17. Accordingly, I agree that the appeal to this Court should be disposed of by orders in the terms proposed by Hill J.

VON DOUSSA J For the reasons given by the other members of the Court, I agree with the orders proposed by Hill J.

HILL J The appellant, Dr Crawford, appeals against a decision of a judge of this Court dismissing an appeal brought by him pursuant to s.44(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) from decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. At issue is the deductibility under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) ("the Act") of acceptance fees and other bank charges and losses incurred on discounting bills in the years of income ended 30 June 1986, 1987 and 1988 respectively. There is no dispute between the parties as to when the outgoings or losses, as the case may be, were incurred, cf Coles Myer Finance Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] HCA 29; (1993) 67 ALJR 463.

2. It is important in the resolution of the present appeal to bear in mind that an appeal to this Court from the Administrative Appeals Tribunal is an appeal "on", that is to say, limited to a question of law. Accordingly it is for the Tribunal, and not for the Court, to determine issues of fact. Most of the facts were, however, common ground between the parties.

3. Kymmead Pty Ltd ("Kymmead") was, at all relevant times, the trustee of a discretionary trust known as "the Mead Trust". In 1981 or 1982 Kymmead purchased development land at Gladstone in Queensland for development as an industrial park. The Mead Trust was a vehicle for what was then intended to be a joint venture for the benefit of Dr Crawford and his family on the one part and Mr Harris and his family, on the other. The industrial park, which comprised some 43 acres zoned light industrial, was to be developed in three stages comprising 61 allotments in all, 27 in stage 1, 10 in stage 2 and 24 in stage 3. The sub-division of stage 1 had been substantially achieved, it would seem, by December 1982, although there remained the necessity of further expenditure, for example, expenditure to be incurred in providing electricity to the lots, before sales of stage 1 could, or at least so it turned out in practice, be effected.

4. Funds necessary to enable the project to proceed were progressively loaned to Kymmead by Dr Crawford and KF Harris Pty Ltd, a company associated with Mr Harris. Loans by the Crawford and Harris interests were interest free. Funds were also obtained by Kymmead under a bill discount facility, under which Westpac Banking Corporation ("Westpac") accepted bills drawn by Kymmead and arranged for those bills to be discounted at less than their face value. Kymmead's indebtedness under the bill facility, as at 20 June 1986, was $560,000. It had also, as at that date, an indebtedness to Dr Crawford of $837,677.

5. In April 1983 a provisional liquidator was appointed to KF Harris Pty Ltd and thereafter neither Mr Harris nor KF Harris Pty Ltd were able to maintain contributions to Kymmead to finance the development of the industrial park project.

6. In November 1984 Mr Harris withdrew from control of Kymmead and steps were taken to ensure that the Mead Trust thereafter was for the benefit of the Crawford interests only. The Westpac bill facility, which was secured by a first mortgage over stage 1 of the project as well as other land owned by Dr Crawford and his family, was secured also by a personal guarantee of Mr Harris. After lengthy negotiations agreement was reached whereby the Harris interests were released from responsibility for future financial support of the project and Mr Harris was to be released from his personal guarantee to Westpac on relinquishing any entitlement to future profits. Negotiations with Westpac commenced in March 1986 and continued until June of that year. By June the amount of the indebtedness under the acceptance agreements with Westpac had reduced to $460,000 as a result of further finance injected by the Crawford interests as the result of the sale of some shares belonging to Dr Crawford's wife.

7. It was in this context that Dr Crawford arranged for the acceptance facility to be transferred to himself from Kymmead. He did so after taking advice from his accountant that so to do should enable him to obtain an immediate tax deduction off-setting his other income for the bill discount outgoings and losses. In the result, on 20 June 1986, he drew commercial bills having a face value of $460,000 and a term of 90 days, which were accepted by Westpac and discounted. In the result there was a credit available to him of $441,219.19. That amount was then credited to the account with the bank of Kymmead, the credit reflecting a loan of the same amount by Dr Crawford to Kymmead, together with a further amount which Dr Crawford advanced to Kymmead of $16,000 by way of an interest-free loan. Kymmead was thus enabled to discharge its indebtedness to the bank. Thereafter, from time to time, by way of rollover, further bills initially having face values of $460,000, were drawn by Dr Crawford, accepted by Westpac and discounted with the net proceeds being credited to Dr Crawford's account with the bank. It is the acceptance fees and other charges and the difference between the face value of the bills and the amounts payable on maturity with which the present case is concerned.

8. At some time after 30 June 1986 Westpac released Mr Harris from his personal guarantee.

9. On 1 November 1986 an agreement was recorded between Kymmead and Dr Crawford, whereby Kymmead undertook to pay interest to Dr Crawford of an amount equal to "the amount of interest which (he) incurred and will in the future incur on (his) bank bill borrowings". The reference to interest, inapt to describe the acceptance fees and bill discounts losses was clearly intended by all parties to refer to them. The minute acknowledging the obligation of Kymmead to pay interest to Dr Crawford contained also the following resolution:

"To enhance the prospect of continuance of that

loan facility to the Trust by W.J. Crawford, the

company in its capacity as Trustee of the Mead

Trust hereby RESOLVES that the first $500000 of

profit derived by the Trust shall be allocated

and distributed to William John Crawford."

10. As at 1 November 1986 the bill discount facility with Westpac had been reduced to $360,000 by further payment made on 20 October 1986.

11. Kymmead was not in a position to make any payment to Dr Crawford at any time in the years of income with which the appeal was concerned, as the Mead Trust's assets were tied up in the development of the industrial estate. No payment could physically be made until the development was sold.

12. Dr Crawford continued to make advances, from time to time, to the Trustee, interest-free, to fund outgoings required to be made by the Trustee. Some attempt was made to sell the lots but these attempts were not successful. At least in part the lack of success related to the fact that electricity had not been connected to the site. The Tribunal noted that at the time of hearing that situation was being rectified.

The reasons for decision of the Tribunal

13. The Tribunal was of the view that Dr Crawford was entitled to deduct the losses and outgoings incurred by him in connection with the rearranged Westpac facility in the years in question, but found ultimately for the Commissioner on the basis that Dr Crawford had derived income by way of interest from the Trustee of an equal amount. The finding that interest had been derived was set aside by the judge below and a notice of contention, filed by the Commissioner, directed at this matter was not pursued. Before us the only matter at issue was, therefore, the question of deductibility.

14. Before the Tribunal it was argued, for Dr Crawford, that the losses and outgoings incurred by him had been incurred for the purpose of deriving interest income, which income would not be derived by him, however, until either paid or constructively received. The reference to constructive receipt is presumably a reference to s.19 of the Act. It was also submitted that Dr Crawford's right to receive the first $500,000 of profit arising from the realisation of the industrial estate provided a further nexus between the losses and outgoings incurred by him, on the one hand, and the derivation of assessable income, on the other. As to this last matter the Tribunal said (at 10):

"I think that the connection is both too remote

and furthermore is uncertain. It would always

be open to the applicant, as the controlling

mind of the trust, to change or cancel this

arrangement by having the trustee make another

resolution of different effect."

15. The Tribunal referred to a number of authorities, including Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd (1979) 24 ALR 401 (particularly at 406 per Lockhart J); Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 (particularly at 56-57) and the decision of all seven justices of the High Court in Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1 at 17-19, and continued:

"40. Adopting that guidance from the High Court

I must first decide whether the applicant has or

may be expected to derive assessable income.

The answer to that is to my mind quite simple.

The trustee decided on 1 November 1986 that

interest would be payable to the applicant

equivalent to the 'interest' which he incurs on

the bank bill facility (Exhibit R).

41. For these reasons I have come to the view

that the applicant did incur the charges for the

purpose of deriving assessable income and those

charges are therefore deductible in terms of the

first limb of sub-section 51(1). I think the

principles enunciated by the High Court in

Fletcher's Case make this result inevitable."

16. However, the Tribunal, as I have already indicated, formed the view that the assessment was not excessive because Dr Crawford had, in the same years, derived interest of equal amount.

17. The learned judge below was of the view that it was not open to the Tribunal to conclude on the material before it that the purpose of Dr Crawford in incurring the losses and outgoings which he claimed as deductible was earning interest on the amounts which he had loaned to Kymmead. His Honour said:

"While it must be accepted that part of the

arrangement between the applicant and Kymmead

Pty Ltd was that the company would reimburse him

for the acceptance fees paid to the bank and pay

to him amounts equivalent to the difference

between the face value of the bills and the

discounted proceeds, that was not the purpose in

making the necessary arrangements to refinance

the Gladstone Industrial Park project. The

applicant's evidence provides no support for the

conclusion reached by the Tribunal. Neither

does the evidence of the accountant, Mr Weil.

The applicant's evidence clearly establishes

that the negotiation of the refinancing

arrangements with Westpac Banking Corporation in

1986 was occasioned, at least in part, by his

perception of a reluctance on the part of the

bank to continue to provide finance directly to

Kymmead Pty Ltd. His purpose in negotiating

that facility was to ensure that Kymmead Pty Ltd

remained firmly in control of the Gladstone

Industrial Park project in the hope and

expectation that the project would eventually

generate sufficient funds in the hands of

Kymmead Pty Ltd to enable it to discharge its

liabilities, including the repayment of the

loans made to it by the applicant (which, at 30

June 1986, amounted to $837,677.00 and were

expected to increase), and to make distributions

to the beneficiaries of The Mead Trust."

18. In the result the learned trial judge dismissed the application to the Court and affirmed the decision of the Tribunal. It is from this decision which Dr Crawford appeals.

The applicable principles

19. The parties were not at all in issue as to the applicable principles involved. In particular the Commissioner indicated his acceptance of the principles which I discussed in Commissioner of Taxation v Roberts [1992] FCA 363; (1992) 37 FCR 246 at 254-257.

20. It can scarcely be doubted that interest incurred on funds borrowed to gain assessable income will generally be an allowable deduction: The Texas Company (Australasia) Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 at 468 per Dixon J; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd (supra) at 406 per Lockhart J and generally Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) v Riverside Road Lodge Pty Ltd (in liq) [1990] FCA 205; (1990) 23 FCR 305 at 310-314. The fact that no assessable income is ultimately derived will not necessarily preclude a taxpayer from obtaining a deduction: Commissioner of Taxation v Smith [1981] HCA 10; (1981) 147 CLR 578 at 586. What is significant, where no income is derived, is the connection between the loss or outgoing claimed and the activities which are directed at the gaining or producing of assessable income. The outgoing for interest must be incidental and relevant to the end of gaining or producing assessable income: Ronpibon Tin (supra). In this and subsequent discussion where I refer to "interest", I intend to include losses and outgoings on a bill acceptance facility as holding the same commercial substance as interest for present purposes.

21. In Roberts I discussed the significance of purpose in the resolution of the issue whether interest is deductible. That is a matter authoratively discussed in the Full High Court's judgment in Fletcher. As that and other cases make clear, the question of whether an outgoing is deductible requires there to be identified the essential character of that outgoing. In the process of characterisation, the end which a taxpayer subjectively has in view in incurring an outgoing may, as the High Court points out, constitute an element and possibly the decisive element in the process of characterisation. However, as the Court in that decision also pointed out, the process of characterisation will commonly proceed without reference to the subjective thought processes of the taxpayer. In the normal case the objective relationship between an outgoing and assessable income earned will, without more, suffice to characterise the outgoing as one incurred in gaining or producing assessable income.

22. In the present case, had interest in fact been derived in the years of income in question, there is no doubt but that that interest would have been assessable income and the relationship between outgoings incurred in that year and the assessable income derived, would have been self-evident. Even where no income at all is derived the objective circumstances may serve to characterise an outgoing for interest as having been incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. However, as the High Court pointed out in Fletcher, cases where no assessable income is derived, or assessable income less than an outgoing is derived, may require a closer examination of all relevant circumstances in determining the question of characterisation. As the Court said (at 18-19):

"It is a 'commonsense' or 'practical' weighing

of all the factors which must provide the

ultimate answer."

23. Fletcher involved an artificial, contrived and ultimately unsuccessful tax avoidance scheme. A partnership of taxpayers borrowed money to purchase an annuity payable over a lengthy period of time. The scheme was so structured that initially large deductions for interest were available, but in the latter years of the scheme, were it to continue, large amounts of assessable income would be derived. The whole transaction was accomplished by the shuffling around of bills of exchange and it is hard to see how any real money could ever have been expected to have been derived or paid. What was more significant was that the whole transaction was capable of being collapsed once the time arose for significant amounts of assessable income to be derived. It was perhaps surprising that the case proceeded as it did through to the High Court on more than one occasion without the agreements between the parties having been found to have been a sham. It was in these circumstances that the Court said (at 19):

"If, upon consideration of all those factors, it

appears that, notwithstanding the disproportion

between outgoing and income, the whole outgoing

is properly to be characterized as genuinely and

not colourably incurred in gaining or producing

assessable income, the entire outgoing will fall

within the first limb of s.51(1) unless it is

either somehow excluded by the exception of

'outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private

or domestic nature' or 'incurred in relation to

the gaining or production of exempt income'."

24. Ultimately, the Court referred back to the Tribunal the question whether the arrangement was intended to run its course (in which case the large amounts of assessable income would have been derived in later years) or whether the intention was to terminate the arrangement before this happened. It is interesting to note that the Court did not refer the matter to the Tribunal to find whether or not the arrangement was entered into for a purpose of tax avoidance. It was clear on the face of things that a significant, if not dominant, purpose of the taxpayers entering into the arrangement was the obtaining of a deduction for interest. That fact alone would not have disentitled the partnership and through the partnership the partners, to a deduction if the arrangement were such that in later years the annuity was to continue to be paid, and significant amounts of assessable income in consequence were to be derived.

25. Where moneys are borrowed and onlent at a rate of interest no less than that incurred on the borrowing, it will ordinarily be the case that this objective circumstances without more will serve to characterise the outgoing as incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, even where no assessable income is in fact derived in the year of income. This process of characterisation would necessarily assume that the agreement to pay interest on the funds onlent was a real and not a sham agreement. Such a case stands in contrast to that considered by the Full Court of this Court in Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1981) 81 ATC 4100 where funds borrowed were onlent at a significantly lower rate of interest. One explanation for the need to look no further in the ordinary case will be that even if subjective purpose were relevant it could be inferred from the objective circumstance that the loan made with the borrowed funds was at a rate of interest at least equal to the interest incurred that the taxpayer's subjective purpose was to gain assessable income.

26. The fact that the borrowed funds are used to lend to an entity related to the initial borrower will not of itself necessitate a different conclusion: cf Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Janmor Nominees Pty Ltd (1987) 87 ATC 4813. However, where the funds borrowed are onlent to an entity controlled by the borrower, the circumstances may, in a particular case, raise a question whether the obligation to pay interest was real and genuine or whether the arrangement was likely to be terminated once the end borrower was in a position to pay the interest without interest ultimately being payable. That is to say that in some cases the question will arise, as it arose in Fletcher, whether the interest outgoings sought to be deducted are properly to be characterised as genuinely and not colourably incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. Likewise a case could be imagined where funds borrowed were advanced apparently at interest to an entity which objectively could never pay the interest and in circumstances where subjectively the lender did not intend that any such interest would be paid. Again, such would be a case where the outgoing for interest on the money borrowed was but "colourably" incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and not "genuinely" so incurred.

27. The present, however, is not a case where it is suggested that the loan agreement with Kymmead was a sham, nor was it ever suggested or put to any witness that the interest arrangement was intended to be cancelled when allotments were sold and funds became available to pay interest. Nor was the present case one where there was any evidence to suggest that Kymmead would objectively never be able to fund payment of the interest and Dr Crawford knew that to be the case.

The facts 28. The appellant challenges the conclusion of the learned judge below that it was not open to the Tribunal to hold that Dr Crawford incurred the losses and outgoings on the bill facility for the purpose of deriving assessable income. I turn therefore to consider the evidence from which the Tribunal concluded that Dr Crawford did incur the bank charges and discount losses for the purpose of deriving assessable income and upon which the judge below concluded it was not open so to find.

29. At the very heart of the Commissioner's case on the appeal was the fact that at no time in his evidence did Dr Crawford ever say expressly that he incurred the losses or outgoings here relevant for the purpose of deriving assessable income by way of interest to be paid to him by Kymmead. But it does not follow from the failure of a taxpayer to state his subjective purpose in evidence in chief that the taxpayer did not have that purpose. This must particularly be so where it is not put to the taxpayer that any other purpose existed. A finding of fact as to purpose must be made considering all of the evidence and in particular the objective circumstances in which the transaction proceeded.

30. Absent any suggestion that the loan transaction between Dr Crawford and Kymmead was a sham, the very existence of the loan agreement between them, with its attendant interest obligation, provided material from which purpose could be inferred. There was, however, in the present case more evidence than this.

31. In giving evidence in chief Dr Crawford, while conceding that the ability of Kymmead to pay interest depended upon the sale of the lots in the Gladstone Industrial Park, said that he thought (presumably speaking at the time of the trial) that that was about two years away. Asked of his anticipation about the trust earning income when the agreement was entered into, he said that there was a great future for Gladstone, which he said had now been proven. There had been a collapse of prices at Gladstone after initially it had been seen as a boom town for the 1980s and the market had been slow to recover. He said that as at 1 November 1986 he had been looking forward to a profit from the estate.

32. This and like evidence was criticised on the basis that it referred to the trustee, rather than to Dr Crawford, looking forward to interest. However, the trust estate could not produce a profit until the interest had been paid so that the interest payment to Dr Crawford and profitability to the trust estate were, for present purposes, one and the same thing.

33. Dr Crawford said that he expected that profit now to occur within the next two years and that had the funding not taken place he supposed that Westpac would have ended up as a mortgagee in possession.

34. In cross-examination much was made of Dr Crawford seeking his accountant's advice on the obtaining of an immediate deduction in his hands. In the course of his evidence, however, Dr Crawford said that he put the extra money into the trust (referring presumably to the loan of the funds obtained from the Westpac facility) "because I considered I was going to make a profit and I still consider that to be the case". The passage was capable of perhaps being read as obtaining a profit through the trust, but even so again the reference to the "profit" leads to the conclusion that the interest outgoing must first have been paid, before a profit would arise in the trust. Dr Crawford was asked what his reasons were for approaching the bank and, to use the words for counsel for the Commissioner, he was given the opportunity to say whether he had any reasons other than the hope of gaining some kind of tax advantage. To this question Dr Crawford replied:

"Oh, yes, I had a lot of reasons: one to keep

the project going. There was some question - if

my wife had not sold $100,000 worth of BHP

shares that year and reduced the draw down on

the bill line from 560 to 460,000 would have -

the bank would have approved the loan in June."

35. Still later in the cross-examination, Dr Crawford made it clear that he now believed there to be no problem at all for the company to make a profit. The following interchange then occurred:

"Right, but - well you believed at all times

that eventually the company or the trust would

become profitable because you would be able to

sell the land - the trust to be able to sell the

land and there would be money to pay out all of

these debts?---

Well, let us say in those days I probably hoped.

What has happened in Gladstone in the last 12

months I now know.

OK. Well, you regard the moneys owing to you as

part of the debt that comprises the loan and the

interest?---

No.

Well, how do you regard it?---

How do I regard it? That the money I have

expended on behalf of Kymmead to keep the

project going so that I will eventually attain a

profit.

Yes, but the company - you fully expect to be

repaid?---

Yes.

And you regard it is owed to you as a debt?---

I do now, yes."

36. When asked why he expected the trust to have interest in two years, Dr Crawford referred to capital works taking place at Gladstone for the Wallumbilla to Gladstone gas pipeline, the building of a chlorine plant and other capital projects in the area. These projects he thought would result in a demand for industrial land. The saleability of the land had been improved by virtue of the fact that the land had been resurveyed and pegged and the electricity connected. Dr Crawford said that, inter alia, in paying the interest on the bills he was "supporting Gladstone", by which no doubt he meant he was supporting the trustee's investment in the Gladstone land.

37. In cross-examination Dr Crawford ventured the view that at the time he had acquired the Harris interests, including acquiring a debt owed to KF Harris Pty Ltd for $360,000, that he expected that had all the land been sold up to that time the $360,000 would have been repaid. That $360,000 was deferred to the initial Westpac security. At that time, that is to say at the time the bill facility with Westpac was restructured, he expressed the view that in his opinion the land was worth in the vicinity of $3,000,000, although he qualified that by saying that at that time nobody wanted to buy it. There was certainly no independent evidence of valuation, save that in December 1985 Westpac had made a valuation for bank security purposes of only $406,000.

38. In re-examination Dr Crawford was asked whether he had any other reasons for the refinancing, other than those given in cross-examination. The following interchange (including a very leading question) took place:

"You - did you - were you going to earn any

income as a result of the refinancing?---

Oh most certainly. I mean, before I - it was my

intention to earn income from it as I had from

other properties."

39. There was no attempt in cross-examination in any way to attack Dr Crawford's credit. Nor was any attempt made to suggest to him that the trustee would be unable to pay the interest, that he intended that the trustee would not pay the interest, that he intended some variation of the arrangement with the trustee concerning interest, or otherwise that interest was not going to be paid. Indeed he specifically denied that any variation of the loan arrangements was contemplated.

40. It is apparent from the evidence which I have summarised above that not only was there the fact of the loan agreement (not a sham) pursuant to which the funds borrowed were onlent at an interest rate not less than the rate payable on the funds borrowed from which Dr Crawford's purpose could be inferred, but also there was a great deal of other evidence from which it could be concluded by the Tribunal that Dr Crawford took out the bill facility with Westpac and thereafter incurred the bank fees and discount losses under it for the purpose of gaining or producing in the future assessable income by way of interest in an amount not less than those charges and losses from Kymmead.

41. With respect to the learned judge below it may well be that his Honour confused the question of the taxpayer's purpose in incurring interest, a question to be considered from year to year as the interest is incurred, with the taxpayer's motivation in rearranging the Gladstone Industrial Park finance. No doubt it is true that the refinancing was occasioned by a desire on Dr Crawford's part to remain firmly in control of the Gladstone Industrial Park project through Kymmead, rather than to see the bank exercise its power as mortgagee under the power of sale. No doubt part of his motivation was also to rid himself of the Harris interests. No doubt a further part of Dr Crawford's motivation was related to the tax advice which he had received. But none of those matters, in the context of the evidence as a whole, required the conclusion that it was not open to the Tribunal to find that Dr Crawford's purpose in incurring the charges and losses was other than to gain or produce assessable income in the future.

42. It being open to the Tribunal to find on the evidence, as it did, that Dr Crawford's purpose in incurring the charges and losses was to gain or produce assessable income by way of interest, it follows that no error of law was committed by the Tribunal and accordingly the appeal from the decision below must be allowed.

43. I would accordingly propose that the order of the Court be as follows:

1. Appeal allowed.

2. Orders of Neaves J be set aside and lieu thereof it be

ordered that:

(i) the application be allowed;

(ii) the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal be

set aside, the objection decisions under review also

be set aside and in lieu thereof the objections of

William John Crawford be allowed;

(iii) the assessments for the three years of income be

remitted to the Commissioner for reassessment in

accordance with law.

44. As no argument was addressed to costs, I would propose that leave be given to the parties to file written submissions, within 14 days of judgment being delivered, as to how the costs of the appeal and the application below should be borne.
