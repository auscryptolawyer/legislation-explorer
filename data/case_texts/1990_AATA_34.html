REASONS FOR DECISION



16 February 1990 Deputy President R K Todd



1. This application for review involves the applicability of s 23(g)(iii) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA"), which provides as follows:



"23. The following income shall be exempt from income tax:



(g) the income of a society, association or club which is not carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to its individual members and is -



(i) ...

(ii) ...

(iii) a society, association or club established for the encouragement or promotion of an athletic game or athletic sport in which human beings are the sole participants; or

..."



2. In the findings of fact that I now proceed to set out, apart from the history of the Club up to the commencement of the relevant years, I use the present tense for convenience, but I have sought, save where the contrary is stated, to set out findings of fact as they existed in the relevant years.



3. The applicant, which I shall call "the Club", was founded in 1975, with the moves to set it up having started in the previous year. Clauses 3(a) and (b) of the objects set out in the Memorandum of Association provided:



"3. The objects for which the Club is established are:



(a) The primary object of the Club is to be an athletic Club and to that end to construct, establish, provide and maintain such courses, playing areas and sporting grounds as the Club may determine and, secondarily, to construct, provide, establish, furnish and maintain clubhouses, pavilions and other buildings containing such amenities, conveniences and accommodation, either residential or otherwise, as the Club may from time to time determine.



(b) To promote and conduct in ... and neighbouring districts such sports, games, amusements and entertainment, pastimes and recreations, indoor and outdoor, as the Club may deem expedient."



4. There had been a golf course on the site of the Club from 1966 to 1969, and the intention was to re-open the golf course and to promote tennis and other sports. The small town where this was happening had been built in conjunction with the Snowy Mountains Hydro Electric Scheme. Construction of the town by the Snowy Mountains Hydro Electric Authority ("the SMA") had finished in 1973-74, and it had become an "operation and maintenance" town. The SMA had a workforce of 300-400 men at the town and much skilled talent was available. The SMA provided assistance with plant etc. as part of its policy of supporting sporting interests in its regional town. To this assistance was added, I was told, some five thousand hours of voluntary work. There was, I was told, even an element of "voluntary compulsion", the jest being that an SMA operative was said to have had a tendency to check those who applied for increments of one kind or another against their contribution of voluntary work hours to the Club. The golf course was officially opened on 30 May 1975, and regular weekend golf competitions were programmed from that date.



5. The land for the golf course, tennis courts and clubhouse was originally leased from the SMA. It was purchased by the Club ten years later. Building of the clubhouse started after licensing requirements and the obtaining of a certificate of registration had been completed at the end of 1976. The SMA provided part of what had originally been a field office, and also provided help with transporting this structure to the site and with erecting roof trusses by the medium of a mobile crane. Plumbing and electrical work was carried out by voluntary labour. Apart from some brickwork and carpentry the building was built by voluntary working bees. A loan of $80,000 was obtained to purchase and fit all necessary interior equipment.



6. The Club was run by voluntary labour from its opening in December 1977 until 1982, when casual bar staff were employed. In fact directors of the Club continued to help with bar duties until 1987. They are still rostered on duty when the secretary manager is not on duty. This secretary manager, who is provided by the Club with a house in the town, is the only full time employee. There are six casual bar staff and two part-time greenkeepers.



7. The golf course has progressed over the years from "a modest four sand greens" to eight grass greens and one sand green. There are two tennis courts.



8. Apart from golf and tennis the Club provides facilities for indoor bowls, darts and pool, and embodies a fishing club. There are regular inter-club golf competitions played with four towns in the same area. The Club hosts annual SMA golf competitions. There are 20-30 regular golfing members. Many visitors play golf during holiday time. The secretary manager, who came to the town in 1986, that is to say just after the end of the years in question but obviously speaking of a continuity of behaviour, said that he had been "surprised that for such a small club that everyone seemed to get behind all the sports. While they have not a strong lot of golfers they are keen". There is a Friday afternoon "chicken run" after work, nine holes in summertime, six in winter. There is also a Sunday afternoon 18 hole competition.



9. The town where the Club is situated has a population of between 500 and 600. Holiday cottages are owned in the town by people from both near and distant areas because of its proximity to various sporting and general holiday activities. Most residents of the town are members of the Club because its Constitution limits persons residing within 40 km of the town to three visits per year unless they are members. Most holiday home owners are also members. Members are basically those who take part in regular competitions in golf, tennis and indoor bowls. A core group of about 40-60 members attend the Club. There are others who come down only to play pool or darts. At least 50 members attend the Club on three or more evenings in each week and at least 20 members are in attendance on most nights. The average membership of the Club is 350. Because of the cost of hiring a band, the Club holds only about three social dances a year.



10. The Club expends substantial sums of money on maintaining the golf course and generally in promoting the sporting activities in which it is involved. The Club has a good deal of equipment for maintaining the golf course. It has tractors, including a tractor for cutting around small trees; three greens mowers, a green surround mower and spraying gear. Many thousands of voluntary hours have been in the past, and continue to be, spent in assisting with maintenance. In the course of its existence the Club has effected only one extension to the Clubhouse.



11. I am satisfied from the evidence, although the amounts in question could not be identified separately in the accounts, that the Club has in the past, although not necessarily in the years in question, helped out with various sports in the town. There has been support for indoor soccer and cricket by the purchase of equipment, and for Little Athletics, children attending which have been sponsored. In particular help was given to a girl runner, whose family had slender means, who had been selected to run at an overseas event.



12. In respect of the three relevant years of income (1983, 1984 and 1985 on the basis of a year ending on 31 December) the Club was assessed on its non-member income, the non-member proportion being calculated from information provided by the Club. There is no dispute that if the Club is not entitled to the exemption under s.23(g)(iii) of the ITAA the proportion determined by the Commissioner was appropriate. It was arrived at by applying a basic formula for calculating the ratio of "spending" visitors to the total attendance of members and visitors in any given period of time. That formula was based on the assumption that, in normal circumstances, 25 per cent of visitors to a club do not contribute to its assessable income, and there is a regular attendance of 20 per cent of the total membership. These percentages may be varied depending upon the particular circumstances of an individual club, if satisfactory evidence can be provided. But as I have already stated, there is no dispute about the proportion in this case.



13. What the Commissioner submits is this: The bulk of the Club's income comes from "social" receipts, that is to say bar sales and poker machines, of which there are 12 in number, expressed in percentages as follows:



1983 1984 1985

Bar 47.24 54.86 52.13

Poker machines 44.36 3 7.69 41.58

Percentage of total income: 91.6 92.55 93.71



The income from sporting activities, that is to say from members' subscriptions, levies, fees and competitions expressed as a percentage of total income was:



1983 1984 1985

4.82 5.27 4.7



The percentage of income represented by green fees and tennis fees would be about two per cent.



14. The Commissioner's representative pointed out that the golf course is open at all times for members and visitors to use. Members can pay a golf or tennis levy in advance. Otherwise golf players place their money in a box at the first green before they hit off. It is an unsupervised "honour" system.



15. The Commissioner took the view, having regard to the times at which the Club trades, in the sense I take it of the club house, that it does not cater for the golfer but is more concerned with social activities after it opens for business. The trading hours of the Club house were:



Weekdays 5 pm - 10 pm

Saturdays 12 noon - 10 pm

Sundays and

Public Holidays 12 noon - 9 pm



16. The opening hours of the dining room or restaurant, which is operated under a kind of franchise agreement were from 6 pm to 9 pm on Wednesday to Monday. It has a takeaway facility. It is subsidised by the Club to the extent that its operator pays no rent.



17. The Commissioner contended that from a consideration of the accounts of the Club, and from the extent of the enjoyment of the facilities of the Club by visitors, it generated its income predominantly from social activities. The majority of the funds so generated were said to have been expended on running expenses and capital expenditure, the latter being in respect of improvements to the clubhouse such as a new kitchen for the restaurant, and an extension of the dining room.



18. Since the conclusion of the hearing in this matter two important decisions have been given, namely the decision of Hill J in the Federal Court in Cronulla-Sutherland Leagues Club Limited v F C T 89 ATC 4936, and that of Mr P M Roach (Senior Member) in Grand United Port Macquarie West Bowling Club and F C of T 89 ATC 891. These have added further guidance to that given by earlier cases, particularly the decision of Waddell J in "The Waratahs" Rugby Union Football Club v F C of T 79 ATC 4337. It is as well to refer immediately to what Waddell J said in that case at 4345, namely:



"The relevance of the information contained in the financial statements is, in my opinion, that it provides a measure of the extent of the two principal activities of the appellant. But no conclusion can be based on this information alone. It is also necessary to take into account the other evidence dealing with the nature and extent of the sporting activities and the social club activities."



19. His Honour went on to find against the taxpayer because on the whole of the evidence a conclusion was not justified that the promotion and encouragement of the requisite sporting activities was the "main or real object" of the appellant during the years in question. But his Honour was dealing with a fairly substantial version of the familiar city football club, as was Hill J in the Cronulla-Sutherland Case. The latter had some 13,000 members. It is bordering on the fanciful to consider that the activities of such a club, with all the ramifications of large scale operations of poker machines, bars and various entertainments could have been seen as primarily for the promotion of the football club. Indeed that was what his Honour said in the following passage at 4955:



"It would in my view be quite unreal in the present case to regard the social activities of the Club with its dining rooms, bars, television room, auditorium and poker machine facilities as being activities engaged in solely for the purpose of providing funds for the Football Club. In my view the social activities became, if they were not originally, ultimately an end in themselves. They took on a life of their own notwithstanding that it was necessary that these activities be pursued to enable profits to be channelled to the Football Club so that that Club could continue. No mere disinterested observer would describe the social activities of the applicant, its trading as a members' club, as being merely concomitant and incidental to its activities of promoting or encouraging sport. At the very least these activities were engaged in the pursuit of a collateral or independent purpose. Indeed there is much to be said for the view that it is the social activities of the applicant as a members' club which give to the club its essential character, which stamp it as a club which in the relevant years was established to provide for the social needs of its members rather than the promotion or encouragement of football."



20. In the Port Macquarie West Case Mr Roach had before him evidence of club facilities far larger and far more complex than those with which I am here dealing; but looking at the situation as a whole he thought that a disinterested observer would still be left with the impression that what was in view before him or her was a bowling club. If Mr Roach had in that case looked merely at the sources of income as the touchstone, he would, I believe, have had to have come to a different conclusion. If in this case I followed that pathway I do not think that it would be likely that the applicant would succeed. But it is more complicated than that.



21. In order to support the maintenance of a nine hole golf course it is really inconceivable that the Club could rely merely on members' subscriptions, players' levies, as described above, and on green fees. The kind of equipment, and the work hours, involved make it imperative that there is another source of revenue. Even a newly built permanent surface tennis court needs care and maintenance, but such a court could probably be kept in service by members' contributions plus some voluntary work. But a golf course is another thing. When a nine hole golf course and two tennis courts are brought into existence a facility is created that needs back-up revenue. That is what I consider that this Club provides. The Club's auditor put it well in evidence in the following passage. The Commissioner's representative had suggested to him that the income from sporting activities was a rather small proportion of the total turnover and he said:



"Well, I would not agree to that as an assumption. But if you were talking about a golf club, it can never be self-supporting. I mean to charge people an amount of money to play golf sufficient to cover the running costs of a golf links, you would be like Japan, you would be paying something like about $3,000 a round."



Even if there be some exaggeration in the figure offered by the witness, the point is in my opinion well taken.



22. What it comes down to is that, while no doubt people go to the Club who have no interest in golf or tennis, and while people enjoy themselves there eating and drinking, playing poker machines, and playing games such as darts that could hardly qualify as athletic games or sports, the raison d'etre of this Club, and the continuing activities of it, were at the relevant times still predominantly focused on the provision of facilities for, and thus the encouragement or promotion of, games or sports falling within the contemplation of s.23(g)(iii). This is a country town club in a small country town. As a result of the two recent authorities mentioned above, which were handed down after I reserved my decision herein, I took further time to consider the matter. The more however I have done so, the more it has seemed to me clear that the present applicant is entitled to the exemption conferred by s23(g)(iii). I may say that if it is not, the protection of s23(g)(iii) is virtually illusory. It would have to be seen as really applicable only to bodies such as tennis or athletics clubs that provide the most meagre kind of shelter, in perhaps the proverbial tin shed, beside the court or field where the activities are carried on. This Club, as it seems to me, has existed fundamentally to make possible facilities for golf and tennis and also incidentally to help some sporting endeavours in the little town where it is situated.



23. The Tribunal will set aside the objection decision under review and remit the matter for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the applicant was in the years in question entitled in respect of its income to the exemption conferred by s23(g)(iii) of the ITAA.
