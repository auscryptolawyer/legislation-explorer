ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX CLAIM for deduction - Agreement entitling applicant to "interest in a prospecting venture for the purpose of discovering petroleum" - ISSUE : Whether the amount claimed fell within any of the provisions of Division 10AA

The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Emmakell Pty Ltd the Trustee of the W K Stevenson Family Trust Unreported Wilcox, Burchett and Ryan JJ Federal Court Melbourne 1 May 1990

Income Tax Assessment Act Division 10AA, sub-division D of Division 3 of Part III, ss 51(1), 260, 124AA, 124AH(1) & (7), 124AJ(2),(a),(b),(c),(d),(3)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT85/5607 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Mr B J McMahon (Deputy President)

Date : 29 May 1990

Place : Sydney

Decision : 1. The decision of the Commissioner of Taxation is set aside.

2. The matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that the applicant be granted the deduction claimed in his return of income under Item 23 for the year ended 30 June 1979.

B J McMahon

Signed.....................

B J McMahon

(Deputy President)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT85/5607 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 May 1990 Mr B J McMahon (Deputy President)

1. In his return of income for the year ended 30 June 1979, the applicant claimed a deduction of $10,000 in these words

"During the year the taxpayer entered into an agreement whereby he became entitled to an interest in a prospecting venture for the purpose of discovering petroleum."

The claim was disallowed. This application is brought to review the subsequent objection decision.

2. In the statutory reasons given by the respondent for disallowing the claim, reference was made to 5 grounds. They were firstly that no part of the $10,000 was deductible under the provisions of Division 10AA of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"), secondly, that no part of the deduction was deductible in terms of sub-section 51(1) of the Act, thirdly, that s 260 of the Act operated to void the agreement as against the respondent, fourthly, and in the alternative, that any such agreement was to be disregarded as a fiscal nullity and fifthly, that by reason of sub-Division D of Division 3 of Part III of the Act, a deduction was not allowable. On the hearing of this application, reliance was placed by the respondent only on the first of these objections. The issues, therefore, were whether the amount claimed fell within any of the provisions of Division 10AA. The applicant was not legally represented. The respondent was represented by counsel. In an endeavour to confine the issues, it was agreed that the respondent's objection to the claim was ultimately founded on 4 bases and it was incumbent upon the applicant to address these bases of objection, if he wished to succeed.

3. The first objection was that the amount paid was of a capital nature. The second objection was that Ministerial consent provided for in the agreement (to which reference will later be made) had not been received prior to the end of the financial year. The third objection was that the amount claimed was in excess of the value of the interest received and the fourth objection was that the moneys, the subject of the claim, had not been fully expended on exploration activities prior to the end of the financial year.

4. The relevant sections of the Act are as follows -

"sl24AH(1) Subject to this section, expenditure incurred by the taxpayer during the year of income on exploration or prospecting in Australia for the purpose of discovering petroleum is an allowable deduction.

(7) In this section, "exploration or prospecting" includes geological, geophysical and geochemical surveys, exploration drilling and appraisal drilling but does not include development drilling or operations in the course of working a petroleum field.

S124AJ(2) Where a person who derives income from the sale of petroleum obtained from mining operations carried on by him in an area in Australia, or from the sale of products of petroleum so obtained, pays to another person a share of the income so derived in pursuance of an agreement under which -

(a) that other person has carried on prescribed petroleum operations in that area or has engaged in that area in exploration or prospecting for the purpose of discovering petroleum or

(b) the first-mentioned person has acquired, or has agreed or has an option to acquire, from that other person a petroleum prospecting or mining right or petroleum prospecting or mining information in relation to that area,

the amount so paid to that other person shall, for the purposes of this Division -

(c) be deemed to be income derived by that other person from the sale of petroleum obtained from the carrying on by him of mining operations in that area and

(d) be deemed not to be expenditure of a kind in respect of which deductions are or have been allowable under this Division incurred by the first-mentioned person.

(3) Notwithstanding section 21, where a person has assigned or sub-let a petroleum prospecting or mining right in respect of an area in Australia to another person in pursuance of an agreement under which that other person has carried on, or is carrying on, in that area or in another area in respect of which the first-Mentioned person holds or has held a petroleum prospecting or mining right, prescribed petroleum operations or has engaged, or is engaging. in that area in exploration or prospecting for the purpose of discovering petroleum, the first-mentioned person shall not be deemed, by virtue of the assignment or sub-lease, to have incurred for the purposes of this Divison expenditure of a kind in respect of which deductions are or have been allowable under this Division."

5. The applicant gave evidence that he was a stockbroker, a. partner in a firm with considerable experience in fund raising for companies involved in mining and oil ventures. Through this underwriting relationship with various clients, he developed personal associations.

6. In March or April 1979, he was informed by one of these associates of an opportunity to participate in an oil exploration venture in Queensland. He agreed to do so. On 15 May 1979 he signed a letter of commitment to the contractor. This indicated his wish to take part in 2 exploration projects in Queensland in relation to 2 specifically named authority to prospect areas, which will be referred to as ATP 1 and ATP 2. Each of these was held by a subsidiary of 2 well-known, but unrelated, listed public mining and oil companies. The letter (which was Exhibit L) acknowledged that the contractor would act as trustee on the applicant's behalf in relation to both projects and that formal deeds or deeds of trust would be executed in due course. The applicant acknowledged that he would not be entitled to call upon the Queensland Department of Mines or any other party to have his name entered on the title. The letter attached a schedule which set out the beneficial interest to which he would be entitled and the management fee that would be payable to the contractor. The schedule called for payment of $4,000 directly on demand to the tenement holder and the balance to the contractor.

  1. The applicant gave evidence that on the same day that this letter was signed, or soon after, he sent $5,000 directly to the tenement holder in respect of ATP 2. He was informed that the money was to be used for seismic surveys and that the work would be concluded before 30 June. A circular letter (Exhibit M) sent to the subscribers by the associate, and confirmed in conversations with the associate, verified that the seismic survey had been completed. It is not clear from the circular however, whether completion took place prior to 30 June.
  2. On 29 June 1979, formal agreements were signed between the contractor and various subscribers, referred to as "explorers". The applicant. in evidence, said that his understanding was that these documents were to regularise the letters of commitment and the payment of $5,000. A further payment of $4,000 was made to the tenement holder of ATP 1, together with a further $600 to the contractor for management fees and $400 to the contractor for interest on a loan. These payments were effected by sending a bank cheque for $5,000 on 26 June 1979 to the trust account of a firm of solicitors in Perth with instructions on the manner in which the moneys were to be disbursed.
  3. Two wells were subsequently drilled in ATP 1 in August 1979 and one well was drilled in ATP 2 about February 1980. Neither produced any successful results.
  4. The agreements with the contractor provided for loans of $4,000 from the contractor to the applicant on which the above once only interest payment was payable. After the explorations proved unsuccessful, the contractor wrote to the applicant asking him his intentions concerning repayment of the loan. In accordance with his rights under the agreement, he elected to relinquish half of his equity interest in favour of the contractor in full satisfaction of payment of the loan. He understood that the contractor later relinquished its interest to the holders of the tenements.
  5. Counsel for the respondent examined the documentation in some detail and made submissions in relation to it. Each ATP was held by a tenement holder quite unrelated to the applicant. In each case the tenement holder had entered into a head agreement with a group of contractors. in relation to ATP 1 it was called a farm-out agreement. In relation to ATP 2 it was called a farm-in agreement. They were however essentially the same. In both agreements the contractors were offered a percentage interest in the relevant ATP area, entitling the holder to a corresponding percentage in the petroleum production to be derived from the area, subject to a corresponding obligation to meet a relevant percentage of costs and expenses attributable to subsequent production. In return, the contractors (awkwardly called farmlnors in the ATP 1 agreement and farminees in the ATP 2 agreement) undertook to drill wells and to carry out certain specified testing works. A joint operator was appointed. Timetables for payment were set, an authority was conferred upon the contractors to sell all or any part of their interests under the agreement, subject to certain arrangements then provided for between the selling contractor and its fellow contractors.

12. The contractor with which the applicant dealt then entered into the 2 agreements dated 29 June 1979 to which reference has already been made. Each of the agreements dealt separately with each ATP and was made between the contractor on the one hand and a group of 7 individuals (called the explorers) who were to supply some of the funds that would be needed by the contractor in carrying out its obligations. In return for these funds, the contractor assigned to each explorer portion of its entitlement under the head agreement to earn a beneficial interest in the relevant ATP area. The applicant's percentage was 0.121. The sub-agreements for both ATP areas were expressed to be "subject to any necessary approval of the Honourable Minister for Mines and Energy of the State of Queensland". That condition had been expressed somewhat differently in the head agreements, to which of course the applicant was not a party. The head agreement for ATP 1 was said to be "subject to the approval of the Minister for Mines and Energy being granted within 90 days of the date hereof or such extended time as the parties may agree". The head agreement for ATP 2 refers to the condition in these terms -

"Upon receipt of consent from the Minister under clause 2 hereof the parties will hold Area A in the following undivided interests ..."

  1. Evidence was tendered from the tenement holders as to expenditure. A letter from the holder of ATP 1 showed that $1,006,825 was spent between 1 April 1979 and 31 March 1980 (the financial year of the tenement holder). This was for 2 wells, both drilled in August 1979 and for associated technical and administration charges. The author of the letter adds : "The seismic acquired to define these prospects was recorded in October - November 1978 and I am uncertain whether this expenditure was included in the above figure". The holder of ATP 2 confirmed that its subsidiary carried out the seismic survey during the period May - July 1979. The subsequent well drilling took place in August 1980. A hand written note on a copy of official records supplied by the Queensland Department of Mines notes that $548,636 was spent on ATP 1 between 1 March 1979 and 29 February 1980 and an amount of $571,264 on ATP 2 between 1 March 1979 and 29 February 1980. No break up of either figure was available with respect to financial years.
  2. Counsel for the respondent was unable to refer me to any decisions concerning the interpretation of the above quoted sections and I have been unable to find any myself. The conclusions to which I have come. in the absence of the assistance of any authority, are based on the language of the statute and the manifest legislative intention to encourage taxpayers to invest in the risky enterprise of oil exploration. It seems to me that a number of reasons relied upon by the respondent for rejecting the applicant's claim are not based on these sections, but are based on sub-section 51(1) and related sections. Although the applicant argued entitlement in the alternative to a deduction under s 51, in ay view, he could not possibly succeed if this was all he had upon which to rely. The moneys could not be said to have been expenses incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or be said to be necessarily incurred in carrying on a business. If the moneys are deductible, it will be because of the statutory provisions in Division 10AA and because of no other provisions.

15. The first reason relied upon by the respondent for rejecting the claim is that the moneys were capital in nature. It is true that the consideration for the payment of the moneys was the acquisition of a capital percentage interest in the future earnings of the field, if it proved productive. To my mind however this is an objection more appropriate to a s 51 claim than to a s 124AH claim. There is nothing in the language of sub-section 124AH(1) excluding expenditure of a capital nature, unless it is in respect of the capital works specifically excluded by sub-section (7) from the definition of "exploration or prospecting". Capital expenditure incurred on items specifically excluded from the definition would qualify as allowable capital expenditure within s 124AA and be deductible over the estimated life of the petroleum field. The fact that it is necessary to exclude development drilling or operations in the course of working a petroleum field, from the definition of exploration or prospecting indicates, to my mind, the acceptance of the possibility that investment in exploration or prospecting activities may be of a capital nature. Sub-section 124AJ(2) relates to profit sharing. Joint ventures and fans-out arrangements. These are widely recognised as classes of arrangenents enabling a taxpayer to carry out exploration or prospecting on a right held by another taxpayer on the basis of an entitlement to a share of the income derived if petroleum is produced commercially in consequence of the operations or exploration. That section does not discriminate against such payments purely on the grounds that they are capital payments. Having regard to the manifest legislative intention of the Act, it seems to me that expenditure should not lose its quality of deductibility simply because it has some characteristics of a capital payment.

16. The second ground on which the respondent objected related to the fact that the consent of the relevant State Minister was not obtained until after the end of the financial year. Obviously this consent would not be obtained unless sufficient moneys had been expended and the conditions of the original grant of the tenements complied with. This would have been known to all parties. In my opinion, the obtaining of the consent of the Minister, whilst clearly a condition governing the validity of the agreement. is not a pre-condition. Failure to meet that requirement does not in any way affect the characterisation of the payment. If expenditure is incurred during the year of income on exploration or prospecting, the fact alone that the relevant State Minister did not indicate his acceptance of compliance with grant conditions until some time later does not, in my view, extend the period of the expenditure beyond the financial year.

  1. The third basis for rejecting the applicant's claim was the discrepancy said to exist between the amount paid by the applicant and the percentage share he acquired. The contractor finished up with a disproportionate interest. It was said that as trustee, the contractor could not make a profit out of the situation. Once again. it seems to me that even if this argument was correct, it could do nothing to alter the character of the expenditure from that of exploration or prospecting expenditure. The fact that the contractor was in breach of trust or that the applicant obtained a bad bargain is a matter that may or may not concern the relationship between the contractor and the applicant. It does not seem to me to alter the character of the expenditure. In a different context. it was recently observed by a Full Bench of the Federal Court that the characterisation of a payment does not depend on its legal effectiveness, in the sense that its contractual setting was valid in law. (Commissioner of Taxation v Emmakell Pty Ltd unreported, Wilcox, Burchett and Ryan JJ 1 May 1990).
  2. The fourth reason for rejecting the applicant's claim was that there was not sufficient evidence that the moneys had been spent on exploration or prospecting in the financial year in question. It is true that the evidence from the 2 tenement owners is equivocal in that there is no break up of the timing of their expenditure. The evidence from the Queensland Department of Mines has similar defects. Nevertheless there is clear evidence from Exhibit M and from the conversations which the applicant had with his associate (which I accept) that the seismic survey, to which the first $5,000 contributed, was carried out in or about May 1979. The evidence indicates that the balance of expenditure on drilling wells and associated work was probably carried out after 30 June 1979.

19. Nevertheless, it seems to me that the section is concerned with the taxpayer's expenditure and not with the expenditure of the contractor (said to be his trustee) and even less, with expenditure disbursed by the tenement holder, over which the applicant would have no control whatever. So far as the applicant is concerned, he expended the money in a clear and irrevocable way by paying it in accordance with directions given to him by the contractor. The expenditure was "on" exploration or prospecting in Australia for the purpose of discovering petroleum. It was for no other purpose. There is no suggestion that s 124AK, dealing with transactions between persons not at arms length, should be invoked. This was a commercial transaction with reputable, unrelated companies. The taxpayer's expenditure was incurred during the year of income, whatever may be said of the disbursements of those funds (together with other funds from other taxpayers) by the contractor or the tenement holder. As 1 read the legislation, the applicant has complied with the section in all respects and is entitled to his deduction.

20. The objection decision under review is therefore set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that the applicant be granted the deduction claimed in his return of income under item 23 for the year ended 30 June 1979.
