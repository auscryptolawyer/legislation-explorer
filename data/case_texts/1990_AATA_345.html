REASONS FOR DECISION



31 January 1990 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)



1. This application arises under the provisions of the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 ["the T(UCT)A Act"]. It arises by way of a promoter's recoupment assessment. That being so, the issues are founded in assessments of income tax and Division VII tax against a target company: Naidor Pty Limited ("Naidor"). Those assessments are said by the Commissioner to have been beyond challenge by the present Applicant Peter Simons - a Sydney solicitor whom I shall refer to as "Simons". These proceedings have been conducted on the basis that that assertion by the Commissioner is correct. The assessments against Simons having been raised under the T(UCT)A Act, and being raised more particularly as promoter recoupment assessments, the issues to be determined are quite unlike the issues arising following assessments under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act"). Under the Assessment Act persons are assessed to tax on the taxable income they have personally and individually derived. Derivation is personal to the taxpayer and liability to tax is the liability of the taxpayer as an individual. Therefore, ordinarily in determining applications to this Division under the Assessment Act the issue for determination in relation to each Applicant is what is his liability in relation to his income. But with promoter's recoupment tax the position is quite different. Liability cannot arise unless taxes due by another person - in this instance Naidor as the target company - have not been paid. But the liability which then arises under the T(UCT)A Act is a function of that unpaid tax.



2. The legislation to be considered is on any view remarkable. Moreover as JCS Burchett QC (now Burchett J. of the Federal Court of Australia), and SW Gibb of Counsel said in their book "The Tax Company Recoupment Law":



"The Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 had an unnaturally short gestation period, and a premature birth, by reason of what was seen as the insistence of an impatient public opinion. In the result, it suffers from the defects of haste. There are mistakes and anomalies which the draftsman would undoubtedly have removed, had he been given more time".



As if those difficulties were not enough, the manner in which the Commissioner has elected to act in carrying out his duties in the circumstances giving rise to the present proceedings has caused not inconsiderable added complications.



3. The liability for what is described as "promoter's recoupment tax" is not confined to the "promoter" whose activities within the meaning of the T(UCT)A Act caused the taxes of the target company to be unpaid. It extends to include other classes of person, comprehensively described as the members of the "eligible promoters class". Many persons within that class need have had no association with the events giving rise to default by the target company beyond some connection of status (e.g. a director) or of property (e.g. as a shareholder or beneficiary in a trust). Furthermore, assessments may issue not only to individuals, as with income tax assessments, but to more than one person - to all members of the eligible promoters class - even to thousands of persons. In addition, whether assessments are directed to one or more persons, or to all members of the class, or to some only of the "eligible promoters class", the liability of all members of the class is joint and several arising from the issue of an assessment to promoter's recoupment tax to any member of the class liable. Persons could be insolvent and liable to bankruptcy without even being aware of their liability or being aware of any circumstances which would put them on notice as to their possible liability. Further, unless relieved of that liability by an order of the Federal Court of Australia, that liability will remain until barred by the passage of time.



4. On 29 May 1984 the Commissioner assessed Simons as liable to pay $7,076.93 and $1,246.07 by way of promoter's recoupment tax. His liability is said to arise from the inability of the Commissioner to recover $38,844.24 and $6,889.50 in relation to income tax and Division VII tax respectively due and payable by Naidor pursuant to assessments made against Naidor. The amounts assessed are less than 20% of taxes due by the company because of the application of s7(3) of the T(UCT)A Act. In all the Applicant has received assessments of promoters recoupment tax in relation to some eleven target companies. The assessments together amount to some $80,000.



5. The validity of the T(UCT)A Act was the subject of a challenge to its constitutional validity in MacCormick v. FCT [1984] HCA 20; (1984) 158 CLR 622. On 10 April 1984 the Court unanimously upheld the legislation as a valid exercise of the power of the Parliament of the Commonwealth to levy tax. I adopt from the joint decision of Gibbs CJ, Wilson, Deane and Dawson JJ, the following description of "Promoters Recoupment Tax". Their Honours said (at pp632-633):-



"A promoters taxable amount exists where shares in a company were sold in circumstances which are the equivalent of those giving rise to a primary taxable amount. The promoters taxable amount is equal to 20 per cent of the overdue company tax or, where no vendors recoupment tax has become payable or will, in the opinion of the Commissioner, become payable, equal to the overdue company tax. The promoters taxable amount is not, however, to exceed the value of the assets of the company before it was stripped, less the sum of its liabilities including company tax and the total consideration given for the sale of the relevant shares. This limit upon the promoters taxable amount appears to be designed to ensure that a promoter is not liable to be taxed upon an amount which is greater than the amount he received by way of remuneration for his participation in the scheme.



The persons liable to pay tax in relation to a promoters taxable amount are those who constitute the eligible promoters class. That class comprises each person who purchased the relevant shares, each person who acted as agent for the purchaser or broker, each person who was an associate of the purchaser at the time of the purchase and a number of other specified persons.



Where a promoters taxable amount exists, a tax is imposed upon that amount equal to the whole amount by the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax - Promoters) Act. This tax is called promoters recoupment tax.



The persons included in an eligible promoters class are jointly and severally liable to pay the promoters recoupment tax in relation to which the class exists and, if one such person pays any of that tax, he may recover by way of contribution from any of the other persons included in the class such part of the amount paid as a court considers just and equitable. He may also join as co-defendants the other persons included in the class in any proceedings for recovery of promoters recoupment tax. Where proceedings are taken against a person for recovery of promoters recoupment tax, that person may apply to the Federal Court for a declaration that, by reason of special circumstances, it is not just and equitable that he be included in the eligible promoters class to which the promoters recoupment tax relates. Where such a declaration is made, the person concerned is deemed not to have been included in the relevant eligible promoters class."



6. In what follows it will appear that Simons protests strongly the action of the Commissioner in selecting him as the only member of the eligible promoters class to be the subject of an assessment to promoters recoupment tax. There is much substance in the objection. However, as a Director of the promoter company - Jensub - he must accept responsibility for the actions of that company during the period of his appointment. It is no sufficient answer for him to say that he was not aware of the endeavours of the company. As a Director, it was his responsibility to be aware of the activities of the company. But he was not alone in that for there was another Director at the relevant time who was equally responsible. However, recognising the responsibility of Simons as a Director at the material time, it is appropriate to recognise that his protest is to be considered on the basis that what was allegedly unreasonable in conduct of the Commissioner was his decision not to assess Citistate - the holding company of Jensub - and, in view of the insolvency of Jensub, more appropriate to be assessed than the Applicant.



7. Consideration of the submissions for the Applicant will lead to findings being made in relation to Citistate and to its Directors as to which they have not been heard. That being so it is appropriate to acknowledge that the findings to be expressed have been reached without the benefit of the Tribunal having to forward any evidence or submissions which might have been put in the interests of those other persons. Nonetheless it is necessary to make findings upon the evidence available in order to fairly adjudge the submissions for the Applicant.



8. I accept the evidence of Simons that he had no knowledge of any association between himself and Naidor at any time except such as came to be known to him in the course of these proceedings and except also in so far as he had at some time past been aware of, or should have been aware of, Naidor when executing documents. I also accept that, prior to the issue of the assessment in dispute, he had long since forgotten what he had previously known. Further, I accept that even now he is only conscious that there must have been some association by reason of documents able to be now shown to him. I also accept that such association as had existed, existed by reason only of his association with Jensen Mining and Investments Limited – a public company listed on the Stock Exchange and now known as Citistate Limited ("Citistate").



9. That association with Citistate had arisen at a time when Simons, in partnership with one Baffsky, had practised as solicitors under the firm name of Simons & Baffsky. Both men came to be Directors of Citistate in c1971 following acquisition through the Stock Exchange of substantial interests in Citistate by companies in which their respective families were interested. Simons had no interest directly or indirectly in Citistate as a shareholder.



10. Citistate had a wholly-owned subsidiary, Jensub Trading Pty Limited ("Jensub"). Corporate Affairs records showed that the issued capital of Jensub of 10 $1 shares, with the exception of one share, was held by Citistate. The exception was a share held in the name of Simons. I find that that share was held by Simons in trust for Citistate and not beneficially for himself or for any person other than Citistate. Baffsky at all material times was Chairman of Directors of Citistate. Simons was a Director of Citistate and of Jensub until August 1981. Thereafter Baffsky became one of the two Directors of Jensub. By the date at which the assessments under consideration issued, on the face of the record there was no connection between Simons and either Jensub or Citistate.



11. So it was that the issue of the assessments against him indicated that the Commissioner alleged that, by reason of the inability of the Commissioner to recover tax from Naidor, Simons was liable to pay promoter recoupment tax. The assessment served on him did not provide him with any information which would enable him to determine:-



(a) whether Naidor had been correctly assessed to tax;

(b) whether Naidor was unable to pay the tax as assessed;

(c) why, assuming that it was unable to pay, Naidor was unable to pay the tax assessed;

(d) whether his liability to promoter's recoupment tax was alleged to arise because of his activities as a promoter;

(e) whether his liability to promoter's recoupment tax arose because he was associated with the promoter in one or more of the several ways provided for in the T(UCT)A Act;

(f) the identity of the promoter; or

(g) the identity of all the persons who, by the issue of the assessment, became jointly and severally liable with him to the tax assessed.



12. Simons did not respond to the demands made by the Commissioner by immediately accepting the Commissioner's assertions as accurate. Having regard to his lack of awareness of the facts giving rise to the alleged liability, that is hardly surprising. Not unreasonably he sought information as to the basis of his liability and he instructed his solicitor to prepare objections to the Commissioner's assessments. He had but sixty days from service of the notices of assessment to prepare objections. That task had to be undertaken without the benefit of knowing how Courts would come to judge the T(UCT)A legislation and also without the benefit of knowing with any particularity how it was that the Commissioner alleged that he was liable to promoter's recoupment tax at all. The objections also had to be prepared in the knowledge that there could be no amendment of the objections at any time no matter what might later come to light and no matter how unreasonable it might be to refuse amendments. The task was carried out by the solicitor to the Applicant and the objections were prepared and presented to the Commissioner as documents comprising over 70 pages and some 40,000 words.



13. In the following months - a period of nearly four years - Simons was to slowly and laboriously extract from the Commissioner information as to the facts relied on by the Commissioner to justify his assessment. Those disclosures will be the subject of later comments. For the moment it is appropriate to record the following findings as to what was known, or available to be known, to the Commissioner from his own records and from records of other public officers before issuing the assessment. From the Commissioner's own records it appeared:-



(a) Naidor had not lodged any income tax return for the year of income ended 30 June 1979 or for any subsequent year:

(b) the "Naidor/Dalziell Cattle Trading partnership" in which Naidor was a partner had lodged a partnership return to 30 June 1979 as a first and final return, claiming cattle trading losses of $11,831 apportioned as to $8,873 (75%) to Naidor and $2,958 (25%) to one David Dalziell.



14. From the records of the Director of Public Prosecutions it appeared:-



(a) that David Dalziell and Diana Dalziell had each sold one of the two shares in Naidor to Jensub on 29 June 1979 for a price of $14,494;

(b) that transfers of those shares at those prices had been effected on 29 June 1979; and

(c) that a Profit and Loss account for Naidor prepared to 29 June 1979 had shown a profit to that date of $30,251; unappropriated profits in all of $37,308; and had shown the net worth of the company to be $37,312.



15. From the records of the Corporate Affairs Office it appeared:-



(a) Simons had been a director of Jensub from at least 22 December 1978 to 21 August 1981;

(b) McDonald had been a director of Jensub from at least 22 December 1978 to 21 August 1981;

(c) no other persons had been recorded as directors of Jensub in that period;

(d) Jensub was a wholly-owned subsidiary of Citistate;

(e) that the one share held by Simons in Jensub was probably held beneficially for Citistate;

(f) that Simons had been a director of Citistate from at least June 1978 to 21 August 1981;

(g) that the Chairman of Directors of Citistate had held that office at all material times and continued in that office;

(h) that the number of shareholders in Citistate exceeded 500; and

(i) that, in terms of the requirements of the Companies Code, Naidor had become a wholly-owned subsidiary of Citistate during the year of income ended 30 June 1979 and had been wholly disposed of during the same year.

(Later Jensub applied to the Commissioner on 2 September 1986 to be struck off the Register; and the order was made on 10 September 1986).



16. I also find:



(a) that Simons had derived no director's fees or other benefits from Jensub except in so far as he may have shared in professional fees paid to Simons & Baffsky;

(b) that, in relation to Citistate, in the absence of being a shareholder in Citistate - something which could not be determined by reference only to the records of the Corporate Affairs Office - that the only benefits which might have been available to Simons would have come by way of Director's fees or through participation in professional fees derived through Simons & Baffsky; and

(c) that it was improbable at date of assessment that Simons would any longer have any capacity to exercise control over or to have influence in the affairs of Citistate or Jensub.



17. Armed with the foregoing information the Commissioner determined on 7 April 1983 to assess Simons to promoter's recoupment tax rather than any other person. His first step towards doing so was to seek to assess the taxable income of Naidor for the year ended 30 June 1979 at $84,444 and that the undistributed amount for the purposes of Division 7 of the Assessment Act was $13,679. The former amount was made up as follows:-



Profit as per Profit and Loss Account $30,252

Add: Adjustment as a result of s36A election $54,192

Taxable income $84,444



18. On 7 April 1983, well after the passage of the T(UCT)A Act, the Commissioner assessed Naidor and endeavoured to effect service of the notices of assessment. Whether those endeavours were effective or not will be considered later. On 7 April 1983 he also gave notice to David Dalziell and Diana Dalziell of the assessments against Naidor and further gave them notice that he saw in prospect the issue against them of vendor recoupment assessments. The amounts assessed against Naidor were as follows:



Income Tax $38,844.24

Additional tax for late return $19,422

__________

$58,266.24

Undistributed profits tax $ 6,839.50

__________

$65,105.74



Additional tax for late return levied pursuant to s222 of the Assessment Act is not recoverable under the T(UCT)A Act. At the same time prior to the issue of a recoupment tax assessment he gave notice that, if arrangements were made for payment of $45,033.20 by instalments over twelve months, he would accept the amounts in full settlement of all claims he might have to recover vendor recoupment tax.



19. On 20 April 1983 David Dalziell made an offer to the Commissioner to pay $45,033.50 by eleven monthly instalments of $3,000 and a final instalment equal to the balance. The proposal was accepted. The amount proposed to be paid was in fact paid although the Commissioner was unable to say who paid or how. His records only show that he had received instalments totalling $45,033.20 to the credit of Naidor. (Problems may arise in identifying the person entitled to any refund which may be due, but that is of no present moment). But the amount so paid was not paid strictly in accordance with what had been proposed. It was proposed that I should not be persuaded to find that the payments were in fact made by or on account of David Dalziell, but I am not so persuaded. However, the evidence does persuade me that the payments due on or before the 9th day of the months of October and November 1983 and January, February and March 1984, were not paid on time and were in fact only paid on the 10th, 11th, 12th, 13th, and 16th days of the said months respectively. 9th October 1983 was a Sunday. I am persuaded that the Commissioner has waived the late payments in so far as both Dalziell's are concerned.



20. The next matter considered by the Commissioner was the issue of assessments to promoter's recoupment tax. I am satisfied that the members of the eligible promoters' class as it stood at that time comprised:-



(a) Jensub;

(b) the two directors of Jensub;

(c) Simons and Citistate as the registered shareholders in Jensub;

(d) Citistate as the financier of Jensub;

(e) the Directors of Citistate;

(f) the shareholders in Citistate;

(g) (probably) shareholders in any companies which were shareholders in Citistate; and

(h) (probably) beneficiaries of any trusts which held shares in Citistate.



21. The evidence before me, placed in evidence by the Applicant and obtained by him from the Commissioner, pursuant to the Freedom of Information Act 1982, establishes that an officer of the Commissioner recorded that "date of strip" was 29 June 1979. It was also recorded in notes on the Commissioner's file that, in relation to Jensub, the "eligible promoter" was the Applicant and, in relation to Citistate, that the "eligible promoter" was Baffsky.



22. Armed with that information the Commissioner proceeded to issue an assessment against Simons. Allowing for the moment that the Commissioner had an unlimited discretion in choosing whom to assess, why it was that he assessed Simons rather than Jensub (as the promoter) or Citistate (as the holding company of Jensub with a capacity to completely control its affairs and whose interests alone Jensub existed to serve) was not explained. Instead the Commissioner by his Counsel simply contended that unreasonableness was not something which was of any concern to the Tribunal.



23. But for Simons, at the date of his assessment knowledge of many of those matters lay in the future. Recognizing that he would only be liable if assessed in accordance with the Act he sought all relevant information. Having severed his association with Citistate and Jensub in August 1981 he had no right to information from either source and his requests for information from those quarters met with no response. Thereafter he concentrated on seeking information from the Commissioner. It is hard to imagine how his task might have been made more difficult.



THE ISSUES



24. The first issue raised by the solicitor to the Applicant is whether the Commissioner was empowered, or indeed ever will be empowered, at any time to assess Simons as a person liable to pay promoter's recoupment tax. Should that be answered in the affirmative, the second question for determination will be whether the conditions precedent to the issue of a valid assessment of promoter's recoupment tax against Simons had been fulfilled when the assessment under consideration issued. If that too is answered in favour of the Commissioner, the third question for determination will be whether the assessment which issued was excessive.



25. The assumption that the assessments against Naidor are beyond challenge by Simons is one of considerable significance. Its importance would be of little or no practical significance if, as has commonly been the case, the Applicant had been the promoter and had remained in a position whereby he could control the affairs of the target company and thereby ensure that no assessment would lie against the target company until every possibility of a successful defence by the target company to the assessments had been exhausted. As Simons never had control of the target company and neither controlled nor had access to the information necessary to enable it to make out any defence which might have been available to it, he may have been in a position of great disadvantage. How great that disadvantage that might be is impossible to say. But two considerations suggest that it might be a matter of considerable importance. The first is to observe that some of the very few T(UCT)A Act cases which have been heard illustrate how it may be that proceedings under the T(UCT)A legislation may fail. In Case U142 (87 ATC 818), in proceedings conducted by the nominee of persons prospectively liable under the T(UCT)A legislation as vendors, an applicant was successful in establishing that the target company had not been liable to assessment at all. In Case U205 (87 ATC 1,159), the taxpayers assessed as liable to vendor recoupment tax were successful in establishing that the T(UCT)A legislation had no application to the transactions with which they have been associated.



26. The second point to be made is relevant if a view I expressed in Case U204 (87 ATC 1,138) is correct. In that decision I expressed the view that, by the enactment of the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act, the Parliament did not intend that assessments arising out the same circumstances and raised pursuant to Section 260 of the Assessment Act on the one hand and the T(UCT)A legislation on the other, should co-exist. In the circumstances of that case, where Section 260 assessments had co-existed with vendor recoupment assessments, I held that the vendor recoupment assessments prevailed. But that view was expressed in light of the state of authorities at the time that decision was delivered. Subsequently a full bench of the Federal Court of Australia handed down its decision in FCT v Gregrhon Investments Pty. Limited (87 ATC 4988): a decision as to which the High Court of Australia later refused special leave to appeal (88 ATC 4221). It may well be that, applying the principles expressed in Gregrhon to the transaction whereby the vendors sold their shares in Naidor, there was a liability in the vendors, independently of any exercise of discretion by or election by the Commissioner, pursuant to Section 260 of the Assessment Act. If that were so and, if the view I expressed in Case U204 is correct, it may well be that the T(UCT)A legislation had no application to any aspect of these transactions. (The decision in Case U204 was the subject of an appeal to the Federal Court of Australia, but the appeal was determined without his Honour expressing any views on that point FCT v Swift & Ors (89 ATC 5,101)).



27. In the circumstances of a case such as this, with the limited information and resources available to the Applicant, not only does it become difficult if not impossible, for a person such as the Applicant to raise all defences which might be available to him, but the action of the Commissioner in selecting the Applicant rather than Citistate as the entity at all times controlling the promoter, reduces the possibility of excessive assessments being successfully defended. Once such possibility was raised during argument: it was based on such records as the Commissioner had produced. It suggested that the "stripping" which resulted in Naidor being unable to pay the tax assessed may have only been carried out after Jensub had "on-sold" Naidor to third parties. It may have been so. On the other hand it may have been that the further sale was an element in the stripping operation. I am not persuaded that it was not so and in consequence the argument has no bearing on the ultimate result. But nonetheless I acknowledge the possibility as indicating how an injustice may have been done.



28. There is one further consideration which should be mentioned. Despite claims of the Commissioner that the same circumstances may give rise to liability in vendors under both the T(UCT)A and in Section 260 of the Assessment Act - contentions that the Parliament has so declared its will that taxpayers may be what is imprecisely referred to as "doubly liable", it seems that rather than enforce the liabilities so created, the Commissioner, in consultation with the Treasurer, has decided without reference to the Parliament to resolve that problem by offering terms of settlement which have found favour with most taxpayers to whom they are offered. As a practical matter, such action on the part of the Commissioner has in many cases, and similar action in this case has, almost certainly has ensured that questions as to whether the vendors were liable pursuant to Section 260 of the Assessment Act and whether the effect of such liability was to leave no scope for the application of the T(UCT)A Act will never come to be determined.



THE FIRST ISSUE: UNREASONABLENESS



29. In order to identify the first issue precisely it is appropriate to acknowledge that by force of s8(2) of the T(UCT)A Act all members of the "eligible promoters' class" became jointly and severally liable to pay promoter's recoupment tax; and that Simons is a member of that "eligible promoters' class". The Applicant contends that that circumstance alone will not of itself justify the action of the Commissioner in selecting Simons as the person against whom to issue the assessment of promoter's recoupment tax. Furthermore, by his solicitor, he contends that, on the evidence, the issue of an assessment against Simons is so unreasonable that it cannot be justified at all and that it is therefore to be set aside. Counsel for the Commissioner responds by contending that, once a person is established to be a member of the relevant eligible promoters' class, he cannot under any circumstances successfully protest the issue of an assessment for promoter's recoupment tax against him in relation to that target company. More particularly, Counsel for the Commissioner contends:-



(a) that the Commissioner has no duty to the members of the eligible promoters' class as to the selection of one member of the class rather than another (or others) as the person to be assessed; and

(b) that, even if such a duty existed, this Tribunal has no authority to review the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection on any such basis.



30. The decision of the Full Federal Court in Briggs v DFC of T & Ors; ex parte Briggs (86 ATC 4748) establishes that the processes of assessment may be undertaken so arbitrarily as to result in "no assessment at all", notwithstanding the issue of a "notice of assessment". But the contention of the Applicant is not based on any argument that there was arbitrariness in the calculation of the taxable amount. His contention is that the arbitrariness came in the selection of the Applicant as the only person to be assessed. Fair consideration of the issue so posed requires that one should first assess the evidence of "unreasonableness" relied on by the Applicant and only then proceed to examine the provisions of the T(UCT)A Act to determine to what extent, if any, such considerations should have been taken into account.



31. I am satisfied upon the evidence that the legal entity of Jensub, and Jensub alone, was the promoter and alone profited directly from the undertaking. Of all the numerous members of the eligible promoters class Jensub therefore stood at the forefront of those responsible. Had Jensub continued in existence as a solvent company, it would have been unreasonable for the assessment of promoter recoupment tax to have issued against any other member of the class; and it would have been unreasonable for the Federal Court to have ordered any of the other members of the eligible promoters class to contribute to the liability of Jensub. However, as the evidence indicates that Jensub was insolvent long before May 1984, it was only prudent that the Commissioner should have chosen to assess another member or members of the eligible promoters class.



32. I find that Simons had a responsibility for the actions of Jensub in so far as he was a director of Jensub and a director of Citistate. I also find that Simons derived nothing from the undertaking except in so far as the undertaking may have contributed to the derivation by him of Director's fees from Citistate or profit as a partner in Simons and Baffsky in consequence of any professional work undertaken by that firm for Jensub or Citistate.



33. I find that all profit (if any) from the transaction passed to Citistate - a publicly listed company. I find that at all material times Citistate had a capacity to control and direct the affairs of its subsidiary Jensub and to direct, or at least control, the actions of Simons as a Director of Jensub.



34. On the basis of the evidence presented before me, I am well satisfied that, had the assessment in question been raised against Citistate rather than Simons, it is most unlikely that Citistate could have successfully claimed contribution from the Applicant or, indeed, from any other member of the eligible promoters class. I so find in relation to the Applicant whatever is considered to be the basis for his liability.



35. Of all the categories of persons going to make up the eligible promoters class, the Applicant was only at risk of liability as a Director of Jensub; a Director of Citistate; and as a financial adviser to Jensub; but in all those instances his responsibility was equal in degree with others of that rank.



36. I find nothing unreasonable in the action of the Commissioner by reason only of the failure of Citistate and Jensub to co-operate with the Applicant, or to lend any support to him, either in dealing with questions of contribution to, or undertakings to contribute to, any liability there might be; or in failing to assist or co-operate with the Applicant in his preparation of this litigation, whether in the matter of costs, or the voluntary production of documents, or the provision of other information. I so find because it is not to be expected that, prior to assessment, the Commissioner would have had knowledge of those matters. But the possibility of such non-co-operation should have been recognised and, being recognised, should have led responsible officers, such as the Commissioner, to assess the member or members of the eligible promoters class most nearly and directly concerned with the promotion; the member or members most likely to be held liable as contributories; the persons least likely to be released from liability; and those least likely to succeed in recovering contribution from members of the same rank or more remote ranks within the eligible promoters class.



37. I am satisfied that in the circumstances that it was most unreasonable that, out of all those constituted as jointly and severally liable for promoters recoupment tax by the provisions of the T(UCT)A legislation, the Commissioner should have singled out the Applicant.



38. The Applicant argues that the conduct of the Commissioner in the circumstances was so unreasonable as to constitute an abuse of administrative power and responsibility; and as such to invalidate the notice of assessment. When one considers the experience he suffered as reported in Simons v FCT (88 ATC 2055) - a case in which Citistate was the promoter - that he should hold that view is understandable. But such an experience has no relevance. In considering whether the decision to assess the Applicant was valid, only the facts to the date of decision are relevant. Subsequent acts and events may be relevant to other questions, but not to the present issue.



39. But such findings will have no legal effect unless the Tribunal is empowered to grant relief such as is sought. Whether such power exists is primarily a question of the construction of the T(UCT)A legislation - legislation which is to be construed in the light of the general development of administrative law in Australia.



40. The T(UCT)A legislation is clearly so structured as to make all members of a very broadly defined eligible promoters class jointly and severally liable to promoters recoupment tax, without regard to degrees of association with, or participation in, or responsibility for, or knowledge of, relevant events. That that might produce grossly unreasonable results necessarily flows from the fact that the legislation clearly provides for such joint and several liability on the part of persons who could not know, and who would not have had any opportunity to know of, any relevant facts. Recognition of that possible unreasonableness is to be found in the circumstance that provision is expressly made by Section 11 of the T(UCT)A Act for persons to be deemed not to be, and never to have been, included in the eligible promoters class, whenever by reason of special circumstances, it is not just and equitable that they should be included in the class. The point is also implicitly recognised in the circumstance that the right of any member of the class to recover contribution from any other member of the class is limited to such claim to contribution as is considered "just and equitable". (s10).



41. I have carefully considered all of the arguments for the Applicant pointing to the harshness and injustice of the action against him. That a person such as the Applicant might be assessed as primarily liable; and at the unreasonableness of the Commissioner in electing to assess the Applicant rather than others who might, by any reasonable standard of fairness, have been more appropriately held to be primarily liable. I also bear it in mind as a possibility that the Commissioner might have issued an assessment against the least resolute and least substantial member of the eligible promoters class who might, by failing to present available defences - possibly because, as might be the case with this Applicant, he was not sufficiently well informed to know of available defences. But for all that I conclude that the Tribunal has no general mandate to correct an unreasonable exercise by the Commissioner of a power expressly conferred by the Parliament.



42. The Act expressly provides that decisions granting relief and authorising contribution are matters reserved for the Federal Court of Australia. That being so I am satisfied that, just as this Tribunal has no power to grant relief in relation to any person not an Applicant before it, neither does it have power to relieve the Applicant from liability under the assessment raised against him, provided only that he is a member of the eligible promoters class.



THE SECOND ISSUE: THE ASSESSMENT OF NAIDOR



43. Before any liability under the T(UCT)A Act in any vendor or promoter may arise there must have been a tax liability in the target company. Such a liability of a target company can only arise following assessment in accordance with the relevant provisions of the law. That process involves (inter-alia) service of the notice of assessment in the manner provided for. Under the provisions of the Assessment Act, the requirements as to the service of a notice of assessment will be satisfied if theprovisions of Section 174 of the Assessment Act are satisfied. Section 174(1) of the Assessment Act simply provides:



"As soon as conveniently may be after any assessment is made, the Commissioner shall serve notice thereof in writing by post or otherwise upon the person liable to pay the tax".



Until such service is effected no tax becomes payable under the provisions of the Assessment Act. That is because Section 204(1)of the Assessment Act provides:



"Subject to the provisions of this Part, any income tax assessed shall be due and payable by the person liable to pay the tax on the date specified in the notice as the date upon which tax is due and payable, not being less than 30 days after the service of the notice, or, if no date is so specified, on the thirtieth day after the service of the notice".



Although that due date for payment may be accelerated (Section 205 Assessment Act) the concept of service as a pre-requisite to any tax becoming due and payable remains. Until something is stipulated to become due and payable as tax, there is no assessment. (Batagol v FCT [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243).



44. The raison d'etre for the T(UCT)A legislation is an expectation that the taxpayer - in this case Naidor - will be unable to pay the tax assessed. That being so it is not surprising that the T(UCT)A Act has express provisions dealing with problems as to service. Among the problems it addresses is that of how to make tax due and payable by companies still in existence which had not, prior to the date of the T(UCT)A legislation, been assessed as liable to tax.



45. So it is that Section 18 of the T(UCT)A legislation provides:



"(1) Where -



(a) under the Assessment Act, the Commissioner is required or permitted to serve a notice of assessment on a company in relation to the liability of the company to pay ordinary company tax or undistributed profits tax in relation to a year of income; and



(b) vendors recoupment tax is payable, or the Commissioner is of the opinion that vendors recoupment tax is likely to become payable, by a person or persons in relation to ordinary company tax or undistributed profits tax, as the case may be, that is payable or may become payable by the company in relation to that year of income,



the notice shall, notwithstanding section 174 of the Assessment Act, be served on the company by being served on -



(c) where the vendors recoupment tax is payable, or in the opinion of the Commissioner, is likely to become payable, by only one person - that person; and



(d) in any other case - any of the persons referred to in paragraph (b), and, where the notice is served in accordance with paragraph (d), the Commissioner shall serve a copy of the notice on each person (other than the person on whom the notice was served) who is included in the representative class in relation to the vendors recoupment tax.



(2) .....



(3) Where a notice of assessment is served under sub-section (1) in relation to the liability of a company to pay company tax in relation to a year of income -



(a) if the notice was served under paragraph (1)(c) - the person on whom the notice was served; and



(b) if the notice was served under paragraph (1)(d) - one person who -



(i) is included in the representative class in relation to the recoupment tax referred to in paragraph (1)(b); and



(ii) is nominated, by notice signed by more than one-half of the persons included in that class and lodged with the Commissioner,



has the same rights under Division 2 of Part V of the Assessment Act in relation to that liability as the company has and, for the purpose of the exercise of those rights by the person, the provisions of that Division apply in like manner as those provisions would apply in relation to the exercise of those rights by the company.



(4) ......



(5) Where -



(a) in a case to which sub-section (1) did not apply, a notice of assessment in respect of the liability of a company to pay ordinary company tax or undistributed profits tax in relation to a year of income has been served, or purported to have been served, on the company, whether before or after the commencement of this Act; and



(b) the Commissioner is of the opinion that vendors recoupment tax is likely to become payable by a person or persons in relation to ordinary company tax or undistributed profits tax, as the case may be, that is payable or may become payable by the company in relation to that year of income,



the following provisions have effect:



(c) the Commissioner shall serve a copy of the notice of assessment on -



(i) where, in the opinion of the Commissioner, the vendors recoupment tax is likely to become payable by only one person - that person; and



(ii) in any other case - any of the persons referred to in paragraph (b);



(d) where the copy of the notice of assessment is served in accordance with sub-paragraph (c)(ii) - the Commissioner shall serve a copy of the copy of the notice of assessment on each person (other than the person on whom the copy of the notice of assessment was served) who is included in the representative class in relation to the vendors recoupment tax; and



(e) if the notice of assessment was not validly served on the company, the service of the copy of the notice in accordance with paragraph (c) shall be deemed to be service on the company of the notice of assessment.



(6) Sub-section (5) does not apply in relation to a notice that has been served or purported to have been served on a company if the Commissioner has reason to believe that the service or purported service brought the contents of the notice to the attention of the person, or any of the persons, as the case may be, referred to in paragraph (5)(b).



(7) .....



(8) .....



(9) .....



(10) .....



(11) ....."



46. Section 18 of the T(UCT)A Act has as its subject companies which still exist. It stands in contrast to Section 15 of the T(UCT)A Act which deals with companies which have ceased to exist. As the Full Federal Court said in Cripps & Jones (ante at page 4983):



"In a Section 18 case, there is no difficulty about the making of an assessment as the company still exists to be assessed. The service of the notice of assessment can be effected, in accordance with Section 174 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, pursuant to Reg. 59 ..... The difficulty with which Section 18 deals is not a difficulty of assessing the company .... If there is any prospect that recoupment tax will become payable, it is in accordance with the policy of Section 18 that provision should be made for notification to be given to a former shareholder. The Commissioner may be assessing a still existing company with some hope of receiving payment from it, but if he is "of the opinion that vendors recoupment tax is likely to become payable",

he must effect the service of the notice of assessment in the manner required by Section 18 (of the T(UCT)A Act), whether or not he has already effected service of a notice of assessment on a company in accordance with Section 174 of the Income Tax Assessment Act and under Reg. 59 (see Section 18(1) and (5))".



47. In approaching the task of evaluating the evidence presented in relation to this issue, it is appropriate to acknowledge confusions and difficulties which could so easily have arisen in April 1983 in distinguishing concepts of "notices" and of "copies of notices"; and thereafter in endeavouring to maintain records so as to distinguish newly created duplicates or copies of, or certified copies of, earlier documents which may have been "notices" or "copies of notices" and which, if "copies" in their original form, may have been so described on the face of them or not.



48. I am satisfied upon the evidence that at 7 April 1983 Naidor was still a subsisting company. It could have been assessed as liable to tax by service of notice of assessment pursuant to Section 174 of the Assessment Act, but only if the Commissioner was not of the opinion that vendors recoupment tax was likely to be payable (Cripps & Jones - ante). I am satisfied that at 7 April 1983 the Commissioner was well satisfied that vendors recoupment tax was likely to become payable. That being so he was obliged to comply with the Section 18 regimen of the T(UCT)A Act in order to create a liability to tax in Naidor.



49. I am satisfied that on 7 April 1983:



(a) the Commissioner served notices of assessment on Naidor by posting them to its address for service "12th Fl 99 York Street 2000";



(b) under cover of letters which were not in identical terms, the Commissioner forwarded to Mr. Dalziell and to Mrs. Dalziell copies of the notices so directed to Naidor;



(c) that the notices so directed to Mr. and Mrs. Dalziell differed from standard form original notices of assessment only in that, where such documents are ordinarily identified as "notice of assessment", they were identified as "copy of notice of assessment";



(d) in the case of the notice so communicated to Mrs. Dalziell it was clearly intended that the notice be in that form for the covering letter stated that in accordance with the provisions of the T(UCT)A legislation:



"enclosed is a copy of the abovenamed company's income tax notice of assessment for the year ended 30 June 1979 together with a copy of a notice of assessment in respect of undistributed profits tax for the year ended 30 June 1979"; and



(e) in relation to Mr. Dalziell it may not have been so intended for in his case the covering letter stated that in accordance with the provisions of the T(UCT)A legislation:



"enclosed is the abovenamed company's income tax notice of assessment for the year ended 30 June 1979 together with a notice of assessment in respect of undistributed profits tax for the year ended 30 June 1979".



50. In reaching those conclusions I have taken into account the possibility that the document actually forwarded to Mr. Dalziell was, as the covering letter indicated it should have been, an "original" in the sense of it being the document the service of which would constitute Naidor as a taxpayer liable for the payment of tax. But I have concluded on the balance of probabilities that it was not so, for such a finding would run counter to the finding that notices of assessment were directed to Naidor at its address for service - actions wholly unnecessary to bring into existence any liability in Naidor and, by reason of the circumstance that the Commissioner was of opinion that vendor's recoupment tax would be likely to become payable, wholly inappropriate and quite ineffective to bring into existence any liability in tax in Naidor. I find that, as was stated on behalf of the Commissioner by his solicitor in answer on 28 January 1988 to a request for particulars:



"The original notices of assessment of Naidor Pty. Limited for the income year ended 30 June 1979 were served upon the company at its address for service as indicated in copies of those notices which are now enclosed, service being effected by pre-paid letter post or on about 7 April 1983. In addition, copies of the said notices of assessment were served on Mr. and Mrs. Dalziell at their respective addresses for service as indicated upon the copy notices now enclosed, service being effected by pre-paid letter post on or about 7 April 1983".



51. Following the decision of the Federal Court in Cripps and Jones (ante) the Commissioner reversed his previous decision not to provide information as to the service of notices of assessment, doing so before the Tribunal could adjudicate upon the issue. So it was that by letter of 15 December 1987, the Australian Government Solicitor acting for the Commissioner, advised the Solicitor for the Applicant:



"I advise that pursuant to Section 18(1) of the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982, copies of the company assessments were served on David Dalziell and Dianne M. Dalziell on 7 April 1983. Copies of relevant documents will be made available shortly".



52. That letter was followed by another of 28 January 1988 in which it was said:



"With regard to your request for particulars numbered 7, I advise that the reference in my letter dated 15 September 1987 to Section 18(1) of the Act should have been a reference to Section 18(5) of the Act and my letter should be read accordingly. The original notices of assessment of Naidor Pty. Limited for the income year ended 30 June 1979 were served upon the company at its address for service as indicated in copies of those notices which are now enclosed, service being effected by pre-paid letter post on or about 7 April 1983. In addition, copies of the said notices of assessment were served on Mr. and Mrs. Dalziell at their respective addresses for service as indicated upon the copy notices now enclosed, service being effected by pre-paid letter post on or about 7 April 1983".



53. I make no finding as to what views might have been held at other times. But, as to the change of position so notified, I consider it to be an ex-post facto rationalisation offered to explain, and to seek to justify and to render effective, what had been done. Although there may have been admissions, the assertions cannot determine which sub-section was relevant or whether the requirements of that sub-section were satisfied.



54. I now proceed to consider the relevant provisions of sub-sections 18(1) and (5) of the T(UCT)A Act. Section 18(5) only applies when Section 18(1) does not. Section 18(1) operates whenever the Commissioner is required or permitted to serve a notice of assessment on a company; and the Commissioner considers that vendors recoupment tax is likely to become payable. Those two conditions were satisfied and, being satisfied, it follows that, notwithstanding Section 174 of the Assessment Act, Section 18(1) of the T(UCT)A Act applied (Cripps & Jones ante). Since Naidor prior to the enactment of the T(UCT)A Act had neither been served, nor the subject of any attempt at service, no liability in tax could arise in Naidor until it was effectively served. It could only be effectively served if service was effected in accordance with Section 18(1) of the T(UCT)A Act. That would only arise if the notice of assessment of Naidor (as distinct from a copy of the notice of assessment) was served on one of the persons by whom vendor's recoupment tax might become payable.



55. I recognise that by the terms of the correspondence to David Dalziell and Mrs. Dalziell, the Commissioner was conscious of the provisions of Section 18(1) of the T(UCT)A Act and of the need to distinguish the process of serving the company by service upon a person prospectively liable as a vendor and of the need to provide other vendors prospectively liable with copies of the notice of assessment so served on the company. But the evidence of the service of the notice of assessment on Naidor itself at its address for service, coupled with the admission of 28 January 1988 persuades me that, on the balance of probabilities, service on Naidor was not effected in accordance with Section 18(1) of the T(UCT)A Act, but in accordance with Section 174 of the Assessment Act. Since I take the view that the T(UCT)A Act in these respects prevails over the Assessment Act - as indeed is expressly provided for in Section 18(1) – I conclude that there has not been such an effective service on Naidor as has brought into existence any liability in Naidor to pay tax. In consequence, there can be no liability in any other person by reason of the provisions of the T(UCT)A Act in relation to any default by Naidor.



THE THIRD ISSUE: THE ARRANGEMENT



56. The next issue to be considered revolves around the inter-relationship between vendor recoupment tax and promoter recoupment tax and the circumstance that the Act provides that in certain circumstances moneys recovered from the promoter are to be paid on by the Commissioner to a vendor. The concept of overdue company tax and of undistributed profit tax provided for by the T(UCT)A legislation includes the primary tax and additional tax pursuant to Section 207 of the Assessment Act but not additional tax for late return. Once the target company is assessed liability to additional tax accrues against the company. Since Section 5 of the T(UCT)A Act makes provision for vendors to be liable for overdue company tax and Section 7 of the T(UCT)A Act makes provision for promoters to be liable for 20% of the overdue company tax, one possible result is that the revenue will be reimbursed to the extent of 120% of overdue company tax.



57. Section 9(7) of the T(UCT)A Act provides that amounts paid by promoters shall be deemed to have been applied in reduction of the ordinary company tax or undistributed profits tax payable by the company which remains unpaid at the time when the promoters payment is paid. Thereby it would seem that whatever is paid by a promoter reduces the amount for which vendors were intended to be liable.



58. It is against that imprecisely expressed general background that Sections 20 and 21 fall to be considered. Section 20(1) of the T(UCT)A Act provides that before any assessment is made of vendor's recoupment tax, the Commissioner may enter into an arrangement in relation to:



"the payment of all the ordinary company tax and undistributed profits tax that is payable by the company".



It goes on to provide by Section 20(2) that payments so made "in accordance with the arrangement" are not to be taken into account in determining for the purposes of Sections 7 and 9(7) the amount remaining unpaid by the company.



59. Section 21 of the T(UCT)A Act goes further. It provides that:



"the Commissioner may enter into an arrangement with a person or persons in relation to the payment by instalments, over a period of twelve months or such longer period as the Commissioner, in special circumstances, determines, of all the ordinary company tax and undistributed profits tax that is payable by the company in relation to the year of income in which the last sale time occured and each preceding year of income.... (and)



... where such an arrangement is entered into ...



.....



(h) if



(i) all payments that are required to be made under the arrangement are made in accordance with the arrangement; and



(ii) after the time when the last payment is made in accordance with the arrangement, an amount that is paid to the Commissioner is deemed, under sub-section 9(7), to have been applied in reduction of company tax payable by the company in relation to the year of income in which the last sale time occurred or a preceding year of income,



the Commissioner shall refund to the person who made the payments under the arrangement .... so much of the aggregate of the payments made under the arrangements as is equal to the amount that is deemed to have been so applied under sub-section 9(7)".



60. I find upon the evidence that when on 7 April 1983 the Commissioner gave notice to both Mr. and Mrs. Dalziell of the assessment of Naidor; of their prospective liability to vendor recoupment tax; and of their rights of objection and appeal. He also gave notice that:



"the raising of the recoupment tax assessment will be obviated by payment of the company's outstanding ordinary and undistributed tax liability (after allowing any carry forward losses) which amounts to $45,033.50. Should this amount remain unpaid beyond thirty days from the date of service of this notice, additional tax for late payment, that will form part of any later recoupment tax assessment that may be raised, will begin to accrue".



He also gave the following further notice:



"INSTALMENT ARRANGEMENTS FOR PAYMENT OF COMPANY TAX -



If you so desire you may, prior to the issue of a recoupment tax assessment, enter into an arrangement with this office to pay all of the outstanding ordinary company and undistributed profits tax of $45,033.50 by instalments over a period of twelve months. If such an arrangement is entered into prior to the due date of company assessment and the subsequent arrangement is complied with, no additional tax for late payment will be imposed. If however the arrangement is not entered into before the due date of the company assessment, additional tax for late payment will be imposed from the due date of the company assessment up until the date the arrangement is entered into".



61. On 20 April 1983 David Dalziell responded to the proposal with an offer made (without admitting liability):



"to pay the amount of $45,033.50 by eleven monthly payments of $3,000 and the final monthly payment of the balance".



The Commissioner responded on 26 April 1983 stating (inter-alia):



"Consideration has been given to your request in terms of Section 21 of the Act to meet the unpaid ordinary company tax and undistributed profits tax of Naidor amounting to $45,033.50 by instalments".



It is agreed that this amount can be paid on the basis requested, i.e. -



Eleven payments of $3,000 made on or before the 9th of each month, commencing on the 9 May 1983, with a final payment of $12,033.50 made on or before the 9th April 1984 ....



Under the terms of this arrangement no additional tax for late payment will be imposed.



This arrangement is conditional upon strict compliance with the agreed terms. All payments must be made in sufficient time to reach this office on or before the relevant date. Should an instalment not be received in this office by the relevant date, the arrangement will be terminated forthwith.



......



62. I am satisfied that as a result of those communications an "arrangement" was formed. I find that the concept of an "arrangement" as to payment of moneys such as was contemplated by the Act is nothing more than a concept of a proposal from one party which is acceptable to the other. It is quite unnecessary to consider whether or not a contractual relationship arose, or what consequences would flow or might flow in the event of a failure to comply with the terms of the arrangement.



63. I am satisfied that the sum of $45,033.50 was in fact paid by David Dalziell to the credit of Naidor. But the payments were not made quite strictly in accordance with the "arrangement". Some payments were late. However, I am satisfied that, notwithstanding the lateness of some payments, the payments were still effective as payments made "in accordance with the arrangement" in that it was open to the Commissioner to waive late payment - as I find that he did - just as it would have been proper to say that payments had been made "in accordance with the arrangement" if the Commissioner had received early payment.



64. However, as the evidence stands before me, I am not persuaded that the arrangement was an "arrangement" which satisfied the requirements of Section 21 of the T(UCT)A Act. Section 21 authorised arrangements to be made in relation to "all the ordinary company tax and undistributed profits tax" referredto (emphasis added). The certified copies of the relevant notices of assessment in evidence before me show that the tax outstanding by Naidor at 7 April 1983 was made up as follows:



Company tax $38,844.24

Undistributed profits tax $ 6,839.50

$45,783.74



- a sum exceeding by $650.24 the amount in relation to which the arrangement was made.



65. I find that at 7 April 1983, $45,683.74 was the amount owing by Naidor (exclusive of additional tax for late lodgement $19,422); that at that date Naidor had no funds and on and from that date made no payment to the Commissioner; and that the only payments subsequently made to the Commissioner on account of Naidor were those payments made by David Dalziell. I so find notwithstanding the fact that, when notice of assessment of promoters recoupment tax issued against the Applicant, the figures were presented in that notice of assessment as follows:



Tax Amounts Amounts Unpaid Company

Debited Credited Tax

Income Tax $38,844.24 $553.24 $38,291



Undistributed

Profits Tax $ 6,839.50 $97 $6,742.50

__________________________________________



$45,683.74 $650.24 $45,033.50



66. That being so I find that it is more probable than not that the credits so raised were not raised by reason of any action on the part of Naidor or any other person conferring an entitlement in Naidor or anyone else to the credit but rather that the Commissioner, without obligation to do so, purported to remit portion of the tax liability of Naidor. One consequence of that remission was to make it appear that the amount claimed of the Dalziells and in fact paid by Mr. Dalziell was "all" of the relevant tax due by Naidor. I find that it was not. I conclude that the payments so made were not payments made "in accordance with" the provisions of Section 21 of the T(UCT)A Act and that, in consequence, the payments so made to the credit of Naidor are to be taken into account in determining the unpaid company tax and unpaid undistributed profits tax obligations of Naidor from time to time.



67. The significance of that finding lies in the circumstance that the question which then arises in relation to the Applicant is whether at 29 May 1984, the date upon which the assessment of promoters recoupment tax issued, the amount owing by Naidor in relevant taxes was:



(a) $45,683.74 - being the relevant amounts originally assessed without regard to any moneys paid by David Dalziell;



(b) $45,033.50 - being the amount represented to Mr. and Mrs. Dalziell at 7 April 1983 as being the total of the relevant indebtedness of Naidor;



(c) $650.24 - being the balance of the amounts so assessed against Naidor reduced by the payments made to its credit by David Dalziell; or



(d) nil - being the last mentioned amount, further reduced by the act of the Commissioner in remitting the remaining $650.24 before issuing the assessments of promoters recoupment tax.



68. In my view the amount owing by Naidor at the relevant date - the date of assessment of promoters recoupment tax – was $650.24. I reach that conclusion on the basis that the amounts paid pursuant to the "arrangement" were amounts paid in and towards satisfaction of the liability of Naidor, but that there was no authority in the Commissioner to remit the $650.24 remaining.



69. For the foregoing reasons the order of the Tribunal will be that the determination of the Commissioner under review be varied and that the objection of the Applicant be allowed.
