Re Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Paul Alvaro; Rosina Alvaro and Giuseppe Alvaro Re Paul Alvaro; Rosina Alvaro; Cosimo Alvaro; Francesca Alvaro and Giuseppe Alvaro v Commissioner of Taxation; Sg147-159 and Sg166-178 [1990] FCA 303 (17 August 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

And: PAUL ALVARO; ROSINA ALVARO and GIUSEPPE ALVARO

Re: PAUL ALVARO; ROSINA ALVARO; COSIMO ALVARO; FRANCESCA ALVARO and GIUSEPPE

ALVARO

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. SG25-26 and SG30 of 1990; SG147-159 and SG166-178 of 1989

FED No. 446

Practice and Procedure

21 ATR 726

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

SOUTH AUSTRALIAN DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

O'Loughlin J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Practice and Procedure - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - allegations that taxpayers had fraudulently understated their income - proceedings for the recovery tax and concurrent proceedings by way of referrals of disallowed objections - taxpayers also facing conspiracy charges - application by the Commissioner of Taxation for judgment in the recovery proceedings - applications by the taxpayers to stay the recovery proceedings and to stay the objection proceedings - issue of abuse of process raised but not argued by the taxpayers as to do so would destroy their "right of silence" - onus of taxpayers to establish abuse - consideration of the "felony-tort" rule.

HEARING

ADELAIDE

17:8:1990

Counsel for Paul Alvaro and Ors. Mr. M. Abbott QC with

Mr. H. Patsouris

Solicitors for Giuseppe Alvaro P.N. Waye and Associates

Solicitors for Paul, Rosina,

Cosimo and Francesca Alvaro Mahony's

Counsel for the Commissioner Mr. Uren QC with

and Deputy Commissioner of Ms. A. Vicic

Taxation

Solicitors for the Commissioner Australian Government

and Deputy Commissioner of Solicitor

Taxation

ORDER

1. Applications for stay of recovery proceedings and objection proceedings dismissed.

2. Commissioner entitled to enter judgment in each of the recovery proceedings.

3. Questions of consequential orders and costs reserved.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

It would appear from information that is before the Court that in about 1983 officers of the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") commenced investigations into the financial affairs of certain taxpayers, some of whom included Paul Alvaro and his wife Rosina, Giuseppe Alvaro and Cosimo Alvaro and his wife Francesca. Ultimately, these investigations lead to the compilation of "Betterment Statements" and the issue of assessments or amended assessments of income tax over a period of several years, the earliest of which was 1978. I have been told, and for the purposes of these reasons I accept, that all assessments have been disputed. I am also aware that there are already in this Court twenty six (26) referrals under s.187 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) ("the ITA Act") consequential upon the Commissioner's disallowance of various objections that have been lodged in the names of one or other of the five taxpayers whom I have identified. (I understand that further referrals are to be made in respect of other disallowed objections). I will hereafter refer to the existing 26 sets of proceedings as "the objection proceedings".

2. The history of this matter, and in particular, the time taken by the Commissioner to determine the earliest of the objections that were lodged on behalf of certain of the taxpayers in about April 1985, was the subject of criticism by Mr. Abbott QC; he appeared as counsel together with Mr. Patsouris and they collectively represented the five taxpayers. Mr. Patsouris deposed in his affidavit sworn on 11 May 1990 in action No. SG25 of 1990 that the first of the disputed assessments of tax issued on 4 February 1985 and that on 14 February 1989 members of the Alvaro family "filed mandamus proceedings in the Federal Court to compel the Commissioner of Taxation to make a decision...". There has obviously been a flurry of activity since then; the affidavit of Mr. Patsouris and the affidavit of Ms. Macdonald of the Australian Government Solicitor's office sworn on 14 June 1990 in the same proceedings show that objections were disallowed and amended and further amended assessments were issued in the months of March and April 1989. The momentum continued until Sept-Oct 1989 when the requests pursuant to s.187 of the ITA Act were filed in Court.

3. I turn next to an associated but most important matter. On 25 September 1989 an Information was taken and sworn by an officer of the National Crime Authority which alleged (inter alia) that the five tax-payers and other named defendants had conspired to defraud the Commonwealth, namely the Commissioner of Taxation: contrary to the provisions of s.86A of the Crimes Act 1914. Committal proceedings commenced in the Adelaide Magistrates' Court in May 1990 and are presently part heard and adjourned to later this year. It is expected that the balance of the committal proceedings will take some weeks and if the five tax-payers or any of them are committed for trial, the present Criminal lists and the likely length of the trial suggest that it might be mid-1991 before any trial might be reached. I will call the present committal proceedings "the criminal proceedings" and that term is extended to include any criminal trial or trials that may take place as a consequence.

4. In December 1989 the Commissioner instituted separate proceedings in the Supreme Court of South Australia against Paul, Rosina and Giuseppe Alvaro seeking orders for the recovery of income tax as assessed together with additional tax pursuant to s.207 of the ITA Act. I will refer to these three sets of proceedings as "the recovery proceedings". A defence has been filed by each of the named defendants in the recovery proceedings. By separate orders dated 16 March 1990, the Supreme Court ordered (pursuant to sub.s.5(1) of the Jurisdiction of Courts (Cross- Vesting) Act 1987) that "all documents" in the relevant action "be transferred to the Federal Court of Australia".

5. Following the removal of the recovery proceedings into this Court, the Commissioner, in April 1990, moved in each action for summary judgment. Each of the three respondents, Paul, Rosina and Giuseppe countered in the following month, seeking orders that the recovery proceedings "be stayed as they are an abuse of the Court process". At the same time those three members of the family together with Cosimo and Francesca sought orders in the objection proceedings that "the hearing of the substantial question involved in these taxation appeals be stayed".

6. By arrangement with counsel I heard contemporaneous arguments upon, and must now decide, the following issues:-

(1) The Commissioner's applications for summary judgment in the

three recovery proceedings;

(2) The applications by Paul, Rosina and Giuseppe in the recovery

proceedings which, in each case, having regard to the way in

which argument was presented amounted to an application that the

Commissioner be restrained from obtaining summary judgment or,

alternatively, if he obtained summary judgments, that he be

restrained from executing or otherwise enforcing any judgment so

obtained;

(3) The applications by all five members of the Alvaro family

that their respective appeals in the objection proceedings be

stayed.

7. Central to all arguments that were advanced on behalf of the five taxpayers was the existence of the part-heard criminal proceedings. Although it will be necessary to refer in detail to certain of the arguments that were advanced on their behalf, the taxpayers argued that the recovery proceedings and the objection proceedings should be deferred until the completion of the criminal proceedings.

The Commissioner's Applications for summary judgment

8. Section 204 of the ITA Act stated that tax is due and payable "on the date specified in the notice" and the combined effect of ss.208 and 209 allows the Commissioner to sue for and recover any tax that is unpaid. Section 201(1) specifically addressed the question of a pending review or appeal against an assessment by stating:-

"The fact that a review or appeal is pending in relation

to an assessment does not in the meantime interfere

with, or affect, the assessment and income tax may be

recovered as if no review or appeal were pending".

9. In my opinion the Commissioner has complied with all statutory and procedural requirements that are necessary to support his separate applications for summary judgment. Although he put the Commissioner to strict proof, Mr. Abbott did not argue a contrary cause.

10. The Commissioner has pleaded the due making of the relevant assessments, their service pursuant to the ITA Act and the Regulations, the separate specific amounts that were payable as and for income tax, the due dates for payment and details of certain moneys paid by or credited to the respective taxpayers. In each case, each statement of claim alleged the non-payment of the balance due and sought recovery of the outstanding amount together with additional tax for late payment pursuant to s.207 of the Act up to and including the date of judgment. In each of the recovery proceedings, the application for summary judgment was supported by the affidavit of Fatima Wai Han McHugh, the manager of the Debt Management section of the Australian Taxation Office, Adelaide. She exhibited documents which she stated were true copies of the relevant Notices of Assessment and Notices of Amended Assessment. Each of these documents was, according to her affidavit, certified under the hand of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation pursuant to s.177 of the Act. She also exhibited certificates signed by the Deputy Commissioner pursuant to Regulation 53 which certificates constitute prima facie evidence of their contents.

11. Although Paul, Rosina and Giuseppe Alvaro each filed a defence in the recovery proceedings I agree with Mr. Uren QC, counsel for the Commissioner, that nothing turns on their contents. Giuseppe admitted all matters averred save only for the prayer for relief whilst Paul and Rosina limited themselves to "non-admissions" coupled with a denial that the Commissioner was entitled to recover the amounts claimed in the Statement of Claim. In short, none of the defences disclosed any sustainable defence having regard to the statutory aids that are available to the Commissioner.

12. The Commissioner is entitled, in each of the three recovery proceedings, (subject only to the question of a stay) to summary judgment and thereafter to take such steps as he may be advised to enforce each judgment.

Application to stay entry, or alternatively, enforcement of judgments in the recovery proceedings.

13. The question then arises whether there is any material before the Court that would justify an order staying the entry, or alternatively, the enforcement of judgments.

14. It is, on the one hand, clear that there is jurisdiction in the Court to grant a stay in recovery proceedings such as these (Snow v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1986-87) 14 FCR 119). But, on the other hand, the decision of the High Court in D.F.C. of T. v Australian Machinery and Investment Co. Pty. Ltd. (1945) VIII ATD 133 at p 135 points out that:-

"... in considering any application for a stay the

policy of the Act as stated in s.201 is a matter to

which great weight should be attached".

15. A stay would be granted in a case where the Commissioner has abused his position or fundamentally breached the provisions of the ITA Act in the assessment process: Re Deputy Commissioner of Taxation; Ex Parte Briggs (1986-87) 69 ALR 185. And this is so notwithstanding the conclusive nature of the provisions contained in s.177 of the Act. That section provides as follows:-

"177(1) The production of a notice of assessment, or

of a document under the hand of the Commissioner, a

Second Commissioner, or a Deputy Commissioner,

purporting to be a copy of a notice of assessment, shall

be conclusive evidence of the due making of the

assessment and, except in proceedings under Part V on a

review or appeal relating to the assessment, that the

amount and all the particulars of the assessment are correct.

177(2) The production of a Gazette containing a notice

purporting to be issued by the Commissioner shall be

conclusive evidence that the notice was so issued.

177(3) The production of a document under the hand of

the Commissioner, a Second Commissioner, or a Deputy

Commissioner, purporting to be a copy of a document

issued by either the Commissioner, a Second

Commissioner, or a Deputy Commissioner, shall be

conclusive evidence that the document was so issued.

177(4) The production of a document under the hand of

the Commissioner, a Second Commissioner, or a Deputy

Commissioner, purporting to be a copy of or extract from

any return or notice of assessment shall be evidence of

the matter therein set forth to the same extent as the

original would be if it were produced."

16. However, it has not been suggested in these proceedings that the Commissioner failed to comply with his statutory obligations when he, through his officers, engaged in the process of issuing the assessments and amended assessments of tax.

17. A Court might be disposed to grant a stay of recovery proceedings if the taxpayer could show what Hutley J.A. described as "extreme personal hardship": D.F.C. of T v Mackey 82 ATC 4571 at p 4575. However, none of the taxpayers sought to invoke this avenue of relief. In fact, very little factual information was placed before the Court on behalf of the Alvaros because, as Mr. Abbott explained, each of them wished to invoke "the right of silence".

18. The first of Mr. Abbott's arguments to which I turn is his complaint of delay on the part of the Commissioner. The investigations into the financial affairs of the taxpayers appear to have taken a long time. But that does not therefore mean that the Commissioner or his officers have been dilatory in the exercise of their duties. There may be justifiable explanations for the apparent delay; there was virtually no evidence before the Court on this subject. In any event, the delays have reacted, to some extent, in favour of the taxpayers. Section 204 provides that any income tax that is assessed against a taxpayer becomes due and payable on "the date specified in the notice as the date upon which tax is due and payable..." As the earliest assessments were due and payable in early 1985, the Commissioner might well have commenced recovery proceedings at an earlier date than that on which such proceedings were actually instituted. It seems incongruous that the taxpayers should now claim that a delay on the part of the Commissioner constitutes a ground for further delaying the recovery proceedings.

19. Mr. Abbott next argued that all current proceedings, that is, the criminal proceedings, the recovery proceedings and the objection proceedings relate to the same subject matter (he described it as "the purported liability to pay tax"). He said that it was a matter of importance to assess the nature and content of the recovery proceedings and of the objection proceedings in order to ascertain whether the continuation of one or other or both of them would or might result in an unfair trial in the criminal proceedings. Although Mr. Uren disputed the factual assertion that all proceedings related to the same subject matter, I believe that any such dispute goes more to form than to substance. At Mr. Abbott's invitation, I have read the opening of the prosecutor in the Magistrates' Court and from my knowledge of the contents of the proceedings in this Court it is quite likely that much of the ground that will be covered in the criminal proceedings will, be substantially covered in the objection proceedings. However, subject to addressing some further arguments that were advanced by Mr. Abbott, the same proposition is not so readily apparent when considering the recovery proceedings.

20. Mr. Abbott asked, rhetorically, whether it was fair to allow the recovery proceedings to continue whilst the criminal proceedings were unresolved. He developed this question of "fairness" in a unique manner; he pointed to each notice of motion wherein it was claimed that the recovery proceedings should be stayed as they were an abuse of the Court's process. But he then explained that no argument would or could be mounted in support of this claim: to do so would necessitate each taxpayer giving evidence and submitting himself or herself to cross-examination. This, he said, was impossible because the issues that were relevant to the question of "abuse of process" were the same issues that had given rise to the criminal proceedings. Hence, as I understand his argument, he was asking the Court to accept a bald statement, without explanation or particularity, that the Commissioner's conduct in prosecuting the recovery proceedings was an abuse of the Court's process and that the Court should accept this proposition without evidence from the taxpayers because the giving of evidence would cause them to lose their "right of silence".

21. The difficulties confronting such an argument are self-evident. In the first place, the Commissioner was, as I have found, entitled to institute the recovery proceedings. Secondly, nothing appears on the face of the record that suggests that the recovery proceedings constitute or might constitute an abuse of the Court's process. Finally, no challenge was made to the methodology employed by the Commissioner in raising the various assessments and hence there is no scope to argue that the Commissioner has abused his position.

22. Mr. Abbott's remaining arguments in support of a stay of the recovery proceedings may be summarily stated. He pointed to the fact that the criminal proceedings and the recovery proceedings had been commenced within a few months of each other claiming that this showed that the Commissioner was using "every weapon in his armoury" against the taxpayers. A rejoinder to this statement (if, as Mr. Abbott claimed the crown was the de facto prosecutor in each case) is that it is wholly understandable and, indeed proper, for all appropriate proceedings to be commenced at or about the same time. Indeed, a prosecutor might be otherwise subjected to criticism and allegations of harassment if different proceedings were instituted at different dates. Mr. Uren's answer to this complaint was that the criminal proceedings were instituted at the suit of the Director of Public Prosecutions who is not to be identified with the Commissioner:

"The two offices are separate and distinct and the

Commissioner certainly has no control or ability to

influence the Director in deciding whether or not there

should be any criminal prosecution."

(Baker: Re Flatwash Pty. Ltd. and Another v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4626 per Sheppard J. at p 4627).

23. Whichever interpretation is correct (and I respectfully see the force of the comments of Sheppard J.) there is no substance in this ground of complaint.

24. The next argument was that the entry of judgment against any of the taxpayers would be a fact that could be used by the prosecution during the course of any criminal trial and that such a fact would be detrimental (presumably, in the eyes of a jury) to the interests of a defendant. The immediate answer to such a proposition is that the obtaining of such a judgment in a civil action would have no relevance whatsoever in a criminal prosecution for conspiracy. Even if I am wrong on the question of relevance, I would venture to suggest that any trial Judge would rule that evidence of the obtaining of such a judgment would be inadmissible because any probative value that it might have would be outweighed by its prejudicial effect. Finally, if all else failed, any competent defence counsel would be able to explain to a jury, briefly and succinctly, the relevant provisions of the ITA Act which enabled the Commissioner to obtain a judgment in recovery proceedings, leaving questions of merit to be resolved in objection proceedings.

25. Mr. Abbott raised a tentative proposition that the rule in Smith v Selwyn (1914) 3 KB 98 may constitute an absolute bar to the confirmation of the recovery proceedings that had been commenced by the Commissioner. I assume he excluded the objection proceedings from this issue for the reason that they had been commenced by the taxpayers. The rule, which has come to be known as the felony-tort rule, is best recognised from the passage in the judgment of Phillimore L.J. at 106:

"... a plaintiff against whom a felony has been

committed by the defendant cannot make that felony the

foundation of a cause of action unless the defendant has

been prosecuted or a reasonable excuse has been shewn

for his not having been prosecuted."

26. I am satisfied that the felony-tort rule does not fall to be considered in these proceedings. The rigidity of its application (in New South Wales at least, and probably throughout Australia) has been severely curtailed by virtue of the decision of the Court of Appeal in Halabi v Westpac Banking Corporation (1989) 17 NSWLR 26. But in any event, the rule applies exclusively to felonies. The present criminal proceedings that the taxpayers are facing relate to alleged statutory offences. Such conflicts as might arise as a consequence of concurrent criminal and civil proceedings are best left for resolution under the general principles; those general principles give the court power to stay civil proceedings in appropriate cases so as to ensure fairness to any defendant and "to achieve justice between the competing rights of the plaintiff and the defendant": (Halabi's case per McHugh J.A. (as he then was) at p 58).

27. In McMahon v Gould (1982) 1 ACLC 98 a liquidator sought to recover certain money from a former director of the company. The director was also facing criminal charges arising out of the same allegations of misappropriation. In a judgment which has since been followed in this Court on several occasions, Wooten J. said that he approached the application for a stay of the civil proceedings with the following guidelines (p 101):

"(a) Prima facie a plaintiff is entitled to have his

action tried in the ordinary course of the procedure and

business of the Court (Rochfort v. John Fairfax and Sons

Ltd. at p 19);

(b) It is a grave matter to interfere with this

entitlement by a stay of proceedings, which requires

justification on proper grounds (ibid.);

(c) The burden is on the defendant in a civil action to

show that it is just and convenient that the plaintiff's

ordinary rights should be interfered with (Jefferson

Ltd. v. Bhetcha at p 905);

(d) Neither an accused (ibid.) nor the Crown (Rochfort

v. John Fairfax and Sons Ltd. at p 21) are entitled as of

right to have a civil proceeding stayed because of a

pending or possible criminal proceeding;

(e) The Court's task is one of 'the balancing of

justice between the parties' (Jefferson Ltd. v. Bhetcha

at p 904), taking account of all relevant factors (ibid.

p 905);

(f) Each case must be judged on its own merits, and it

would be wrong and undesirable to attempt to define in

the abstract what are the relevant factors (ibid. p 905);

(g) One factor to take into account where there are

pending or possible criminal proceedings is what is

sometimes referred to as the accused's 'right of

silence', and the reason why that right, under the law

as it stands, is a right of a defendant in a criminal

proceeding (ibid. p 904). I return to this subject below;

(h) However, the so-called 'right of silence' does not

extend to give such a defendant as a matter of right the

same protection in contemporaneous civil proceedings.

The plaintiff in a civil action is not debarred from

pursuing action in accordance with the normal rules

merely because to do so would, or might, result in the

defendant, if he wished to defend the action, having to

disclose, in resisting an application for summary

judgment, in the pleading of his defence, or by way of

discovery or otherwise, what his defence is likely to be

in the criminal proceeding (ibid. pp 904-905);

(i) The Court should consider whether there is a real

and not merely notional danger of injustice in the

criminal proceedings (ibid. p 905);

(j) In this regard factors which may be relevant include:

(i) the possibility of publicity that

might reach and influence jurors in the civil

proceedings (ibid. p 905);

(ii) the proximity of the criminal hearing (ibid. p 905;

(iii) the possibility of miscarriage of justice e.g.

by disclosure of a defence enabling the fabrication of

evidence by prosecution witnesses, or interference

with defence witnesses (ibid. p 905);

(iv) the burden on the defendant of preparing for both sets

of proceedings concurrently (Beecee Group v. Barton);

(v) whether the defendant has already disclosed his

defence to the allegations (Caesar v. Sommer at p 932,

Re Saltergate Insurance Co. Ltd. at CLC p 34,131;

ACLR p 736);

(vi) the conduct of the defendant, including

his own prior invocation of civil process when

it suited him (cf. Re Saltergate Insurance Co.

Ltd. at CLC p 34,131; ACLR pp 735-736);

(k) The effect on the plaintiff must also be considered

and weighed against the effect on the defendant. In

this connection I suggest below that it may be relevant

to consider the nature of the defendant's obligation to

the plaintiff;

(l) In an appropriate case the proceedings may be

allowed to proceed to a certain stage e.g. setting down

for trial, and then stayed (Beecee Group v. Barton) ".

28. There is some affidavit evidence before me from the taxpayers or their solicitors in which concerns and fears are expressed. It will be sufficient to refer to the affidavit of Giuseppe Alvaro in Action SG30 of 1990 (one of the recovery proceedings) as an example. He said that he had been advised by his legal representatives that he was entitled

"to claim privilege against self incrimination and that

in claiming that privilege I may decline:-

(a) to answer questions the answers to which might tend

to incriminate me in a criminal offence.

(b) to file any affidavit or document or make discovery

of any documents which may have a similar effect."

He also said that it was his belief:-

"(a) that it would be impossible to argue the abuse

of process proceedings unless I gave evidence on

oath as to the financial hardship I would suffer

and as to my financial position generally.

(b) that the giving of such evidence on oath would

require me to waive my right against self

incrimination and that the answers thereto would be

likely to incriminate me in the offences with which

I am presumably charged."

29. Accepting, as I do for the purposes of these proceedings, that those statements accurately represent the beliefs of the deponent, they fall far short of satisfying the burden that the taxpayer carries or satisfying the Court that it is "just and convenient that the plaintiff's ordinary rights should be interfered with": (McMahon v Gould para (c) of the guidelines). The taxpayer has merely advanced assertions that are devoid of particularity. He has denied the Court the opportunity of investigating their sufficiency. At this stage, I cannot comment on his subjective assessment of what might constitute "financial hardship" nor do I know whether he is referring to the magnitude or the paucity of his "financial position".

30. Bearing in mind the preferred treatment that is afforded to the Commissioner in the recovery proceedings by force of Statute and having regard to the limited opportunities that the Courts have given to taxpayers to erode away those statutory rights, I have concluded that there is nothing before the Court which would justify the granting of a stay in the recovery proceedings. Whether and to what extent the Commissioner may hereafter seek to execute his judgments are matters that are not presently before me. Hence the taxpayers still have the opportunity to reflect upon the matter. They retain the right, at any subsequent stage of the recovery proceedings, to seek the intervention of the Court. But it need hardly be said that any further application would have to be grounded in detailed factual material.

Application to stay the objection proceedings

31. Normally, it is a defendant who seeks a stay of civil proceedings when he is facing concurrent criminal proceedings. In fact, the five taxpayers in the objection proceedings are appellants bearing the onus of convincing the Court that the challenged assessments are bad. However, I respectfully agree with the views of Sheppard J. in Baker: Re Flatwash Pty. Ltd (supra) who, faced with a similar situation, said:

"I think, however, that this difference is not a

difference of substance or, if it be so, not such a

difference of substance as to make the ordinary

principles inapplicable." (p 4628)

32. I do not consider that it is necessary to make a detailed examination of the numerous objections that have been lodged on behalf of the five taxpayers. It is sufficient to say that a cursory examination shows that each taxpayer maintains that his or her tax returns were correct as lodged. Explanations, designed no doubt to reconcile the contents of the returns with the results of the Commissioner's investigations include tax free betting wins, gifts from relatives, claims that amounts attributed by the Commissioner to certain items of expenditure were excessive and claims that the Commissioner has wrongly attributed ownership of assets to a particular taxpayer. It is sufficient to say that the contents of the objections raise arguable issues of fact and it is important to recognise that no taxpayer, advancing factual issues of the type that I have identified, could hope to succeed in objection proceedings without giving evidence on oath. It should also be borne in mind that the gravamen of the criminal proceedings is the fraudulent understatement of taxable income and that the objection proceedings will also address the issue of understated income but without being compelled to address the issue of fraud.

33. Contrary to the view that I have formed with respect to the recovery proceedings, I find myself more readily able to apprehend that the stage might be reached, at some stage in the future, when principles of fairness would call for the intervention of the Court and the grant of a stay of the objection proceedings. Indeed Mr. Uren was prepared to make such a concession in principle whilst nevertheless arguing (in my opinion correctly) that it would be premature at this stage for the Court to interfere. There is much that is yet to be done in the interlocutory process. I am not impressed by Mr. Abbott's global assertion that his clients could not engage in preparation for the hearing of the objection proceedings without throwing away their shield of silence. Gibbs C.J. explained the position in Sorby v Commonwealth [1983] HCA 10; (1982-83) 46 ALR 237 in these terms at p 241:-

"The mere fact that the witness swears that he believes

that the answer will incriminate him is not sufficient;

'to entitle a party called as a witness to the privilege

of silence, the court must see, from the circumstances

of the case and the nature of the evidence which the

witness is called to give, that there is reasonable

ground to apprehend danger to the witness from his being

compelled to answer': R v Boyes (1861) 1 B and S 311 at

329-30; [1861] EngR 626; 121 ER 730 at 738. That statement of the law

has frequently been approved."

34. In the ultimate, it is for the Court to determine whether the issue of self-incrimination does or does not truly arise and this holds good during the interlocutory process as much as during the hearing of the trial. I am of the opinion that it is premature for the Court to intervene in the objection proceedings. That is not to say that at some later stage the situation would not change. However the present motions to stay the objection proceedings must be dismissed.

35. I will hear from the parties on questions of consequential orders and costs.
