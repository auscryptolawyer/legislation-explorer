VT87/1102 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 175; (1990) 21 ATR 3581; 90 ATC 492 (6 August 1990)

Last Updated: 1 June 2009



INCOME TAX - Validity of default assessment - Whether Commissioner formed bona fide judgment of taxable income - Default assessment prepared upon no intelligible basis.



Customs Act 1901 secs 233B(1), 243B(1), 243E

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 secs 167, 177(1), 190(b)

Judiciary Act 1903



Bailey v FC of T 77 ATC 4097

FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088

Trautwein v FC of T [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63

Derry v Peek [1889] UKHL 1; (1889) 14 App Cas 337

McAndrew v FC of T [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263



AAT No 6089



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. VT87/1102

TAXAION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT



Before: Dr P. Gerber, Deputy President

Date: 6 August 1990



DECISION



The assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1982 is set aside and the matter remitted to the Commissioner to deal with the applicant in accordance with the Act.

(Sgd. P. Gerber)

DEPUTY PRESIDENT



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. VT87/1102

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT



Before: Dr P. Gerber, Deputy President



Date: 6 August 1990



REASONS FOR DECISION



This case involves an important issue: in raising a default assessment, what are the requirements in the formation of a judgment in arriving at the income on which tax ought to be levied; cf sec 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act")? Perhaps with more valour than discretion, I handed down my decision orally, setting the assessment aside and remitting it to the Commissioner on terms which are outlined below. I indicated at the time that I would issue my decision in writing if requested to do so by either party, with such textual emendation as I saw fit. In the result, I was requested for written reasons by the Commissioner.



2. The taxpayer, who is presently in gaol was, in his enforced absence, ably represented by his accountant, Mr McCready.



3. It was not seriously contested that the applicant was a heroin addict who "fed" his habit by the illegal importation of narcotics. Suffice it for present purposes that the applicant, during the relevant time, undertook at least 14 trips to Europe via Bangkok, making use of false passports and bank accounts in fictitious names. These journeys began in June 1979 and ended with the applicant's arrest in January 1982 on charges, inter alia, of conspiracy to import a prohibited narcotic substance in contravention of sec 233B (1)(cb) of the Customs Act 1901. He was convicted and, as noted before, sentenced to a lengthy term of imprisonment.



4. When arrested, certain personal and real property valued at $363,799 was identified as belonging, in whole or in part, to the applicant. In addition, various bank deposits previously referred to were located and cash in Australian currency in the sum of $77,963 was seized.



5. Ill-gotten gains of such dimension persuaded the Commissioner of Police to apply to the Federal Court for an Order that the taxpayer pay a pecuniary penalty to the Commonwealth in such sum as the Court may assess in respect of the various offences with which the taxpayer was charged. This was purportedly done pursuant to sec 243B(1) of the Customs Act. In addition, the Federal Court was asked to make an Order directing the Official Trustee to take control of the property of the taxpayer pursuant to sec 243E of the Customs Act.



6. These proceedings, having commenced in the Federal Court (coram Smithers J) on 18 January 1982, were adjourned when the Court was informed by counsel that the applicant had applied to the High Court for a writ of prohibition, directed both to Smithers J and the Commissioner of Police to prohibit them from continuing with the hearing. On the following day, Stephen J made an Order that both the Hon Mr Justice Smithers and the Commonwealth show cause why a writ of prohibition should not issue on the grounds that secs 243B and 243E of the Customs Act were ultra vires. In the result, the Federal Court proceedings were removed to the High Court pursuant to the Judiciary Act 1903. Met with such multiplicity of actions, some challenging the very foundations of the validity of the penal provisions of the Customs Act, the Commissioner of Police became discouraged and, as far as I know, these issues remain unresolved to this day. None of the above was adverted to in argument and has been reconstructed by me from a bundle of documents tendered as one exhibit without explanation. It is thus a reconstruction which can be likened to assembling the Old Testament from the Dead Sea Scrolls.



7. In due course, the Commissioner of Taxation became aware that (i) the applicant had not lodged returns since 1980 and (ii) that there were substantial assets available to meet a potential tax liability, assets which the Commissioner of Police had apparently left unclaimed in the Lost Property Office of the High Court Registry on the basis that, like the Titanic, it lay in waters too deep to make the salvage worth the candle.



8. Once alerted to this potential treasure trove, one J.P., a tax officer attached to the Victoria North Office of the respondent, was instructed to prepare an audit report. This he did with consummate skill. Suffice it for present purposes if I merely set out the conclusions and recommendations J.P. arrived at in his report. (I have attempted to "translate" this Report into recognisable English, correcting the spelling and punctuating it according to ordinary usage. I hope my "translation" has captured its essence).

"Mr M (the applicant) was involved in the importation and sale of heroin in the period 14/6/79 to 4/1/82.



Mr M was arrested on 5/1/82. Since then Mr M has spent approximately half a million dollars on his defence. This has been financed by the sale of property and other assets. The only asset still held on his behalf is $77,963. This is held by the Australian Federal Police and Mr M has requested that this money be refunded to pay for an appeal to the High Court.



An estimation of income is somewhat difficult, but in the financial years ended 1980, 1981 and 1982, Mr M has acquired assets of $363,799, an average of $121,266 per year. On top of this, expenditure of $106,703 has been incurred. This private expenditure includes airfares (which may be a deductible item) and the value of heroin used, which would more likely be assessed at the value of goods for his own use rather than at street value. Mr M has no non-assessable source of funds.



Given that it is not likely that there will be any more than $77,963 to pay any assessments issued, it is proposed to issue assessments for the 1980, 1981 and 1982 years on an income of $121,266 in each year, i.e. the increase in the value of assets held.



Submit:



Melbourne Investigation Unit has not found any record of lodgement for Mr M. It is proposed that:



(i) returns be "dummied" for the years 1980, 1981 and 1982;



(ii) assessments showing an income of $121,266 be prepared in respect of 1980, 1981 and 1982 pursuant to s.167;



(iii) s.218 action be taken against the Federal Police by serving notice on the Officer-in-Charge, Federal Police, Melbourne, in respect of monies held by them, i.e. $77,963."



9. This recommendation was rejected by one J.G., a supervisor, who noted:



"In the circumstances I do not agree with the raising of assessments for 1980 to 1982 inclusive on incomes of $121,266. If this were carried out, a large debt of tax would remain, which would eventually have to be wiped out by Recovery Section. At this stage, "dummy" a return for the 1982 year only and work out an income which will elicit tax plus a 40% penalty for non-lodgement amounting to the amount of money held by the Federal Police, namely $77,963 and serve a sec. 218 notice on the Federal Police."



10. In the events that occurred, the supervisor's instruction was put into effect and a default assessment was issued for the 1982 year only, claiming a tax liability in the sum of $77,963.32. To reach that target amount, an arbitrary "income" was "dummied" in the amount of $107,577, being the exact amount which, with a 40% culpability component, would yield the amount available in cash.



11. As will already have been noted, the Commissioner did not - as in my view he should have done - calculate the assessable income for the years of default; he chose instead to calculate for only one year (1982) what sum, which, when taxed at the appropriate rate together with a culpability component of 40% would yield the exact amount that he could readily lay his hands on. One might likewise say of this Charge of the Lightfingered Brigade: "It is magnificent, but it isn't war".



12. In the result, I have some sympathy with the ground raised by the applicant in his objection derived from Derry v Peek [1889] UKHL 1; (1889) 14 App Cas 337; viz "that the assessment was made recklessly, not caring whether it was true or false".



13. Sec 167 of the Act provides that:



"if any person makes default in furnishing a return, the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his judgment income tax ought to be levied, and that amount shall be the taxable income of that person ...".



14. Not surprisingly, Mr Davies, of learned counsel for the Commissioner (who had not been instructed as to the manner in which the default assessment was cobbled together), relied on sec 190(b) of the Act, which places the burden of proving an assessment is excessive upon the taxpayer, and, since the taxpayer did not appear, so counsel's submission went, he has failed to discharge that burden. Hence the only proper decision open to the Tribunal is to confirm the decision on the objection. Mr Davies referred me to Trautwein v FC of T [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 and FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088 in support of his submission.



15. I must decline so tempting an invitation. I am satisfied that if it can be shown, as here, that the process of assessment has no statutory warrant, the default assessment is "excessive" by default, even though, prima facie, it is evident that had the Commissioner properly exercised his judgment in compliance with his statutory obligation, the income thus arrived at may well have exceeded the income as assessed (the burden of proving that the travel expenses were of a business nature -and hence deductible - would, given the recommended method of assessment, rest on the applicant). Whatever else the Commissioner may do, he may not pluck a figure out of the air by looking at entrails or having recourse to osmosis, astrology or the amount of tax he can recover. In other words, the Commissioner cannot do a "break and enter" to repossess what he believes to be his property.



16. I do not believe that the cases relied on by Mr Davies compel any different conclusion.



17. Trautwein involved a taxpayer who had not kept proper records or books of account. He objected to assessments made in respect of his income during each of seven consecutive years. His liability to tax was reviewed by the Commissioner in the light of a report by the taxpayer's accountants, which showed that, as between the beginning and the end of the seven year period, there had been a large unaccounted for accretion of assets which the Commissioner regarded as of an income and not of a capital nature. The Commissioner added a seventh part of the amount of this accretion to the assessable income of each of the seven years. The assessments were then amended. In six of the assessments the amount of assessable income was increased. Three days later the Commissioner notified the taxpayer that his objections to the previous assessments had been allowed to the extent shown in the amended assessments. On appeal against the disallowance of his objections, the taxpayer was unable to prove during what year or years the accretion was earned, or the precise amount of assessable income in each year, but it could not have been the case that in fact the annual increment in his wealth was throughout the seven years always equal in amount. In the result, the Court held that the addition, in equal proportions, to the assessable income of each year within a period of years of income derived during that period, but which could not be accurately apportioned, did not render the assessments invalid. Furthermore, the burden was upon the taxpayer of establishing (i) that the amount or some other particulars of each separate assessment were incorrect and (ii) that that incorrectness operated to his prejudice. In dealing with the provision of the Act which provided that the production of any notice of assessment shall be conclusive evidence that the assessment had been duly made and that the amount and all particulars of the assessment are correct, Latham CJ stated (at p 88):



"The application of sec. 39 (now sec 177(1)) is not, in my opinion, excluded as soon as it is shown that an element in the assessment is a guess and that it is therefore very probably wrong. It is prima facie right - and remains right until the appellant shows that it is wrong. If it were necessary to decide the point I would, as at present advised, be prepared to hold that the taxpayer must, at least as a general rule, go further and show, not only negatively that the assessment is wrong, but also positively what correction should be made in order to make it right or more nearly right. I say 'as a general rule' because, conceivably, there might be a case where it appeared that the assessment had been made upon no intelligible basis even as an approximation, and the court would then set aside the assessment and remit it to the Commissioner for further consideration."



18. Mr Davies placed particular emphasis on the various judgments in Dalco and indeed, at first blush, they seem to support Mr Davies' forceful argument. In that case (taking the facts from the headnote) the taxpayer received amended assessments for the years ended 30 June 1976, 1977, 1978 and 1980 which significantly increased his taxable income. The taxpayer's appeals to the Supreme Court of New South Wales consequent on the Commissioner's disallowance of his objections to the amended assessments were dismissed by Yeldham J (88 ATC 4131) on the ground that the taxpayer had failed to show that the amended assessments were excessive because he had failed to show the sources of his income and to exclude all sources of income other than those he admitted. The taxpayer had sought to show that the Commissioner had wrongly treated the income of companies or trusts which the taxpayer or his family company acquired or controlled as assessable income of the taxpayer. Yeldham J found that in each of the relevant years there was a derivation of income by the taxpayer that was dealt with at his discretion in disregard of corporate rights of companies with which he was involved. His decision was reversed

(by majority) on appeal to the Federal Court (88 ATC 4649). On appeal to the High Court, the majority decision of the Federal Court was itself reversed. After an exhaustive analysis of the authorities, the Court held:



  1. Mere error by the Commissioner in forming a judgment as to the amount on which tax ought to be levied does not warrant the setting aside of the amount assessed. The amounts assessed represent the Commissioner's bona fide judgment as to the amount of the taxpayer's taxable income and the power to make the assessments was validly exercised. The assessments being valid, the burden was on the taxpayer to prove the amounts assessed were excessive.

  2. "Excessive" refers to the amount of the assessment, not to any unauthorised step in the process of its calculation. A taxpayer does not succeed in establishing that an amount is excessive unless he or she can challenge the substantive liability imposed by the assessment.

  3. The majority of the Full Federal Court treated the error, which they held to infect the Commissioner's assessment of the amount of the taxpayer's taxable income, as concluding the question whether the amount was excessive. It did not.

  4. For the taxpayer to demonstrate that in some respects the Commissioner erred in the way in which he attributed income to the taxpayer or otherwise dealt with the material available to him does not prove that an assessment was excessive. It does not prove that the taxable income of the taxpayer was less than the amount of taxable income shown in any of the assessments.

  5. It was necessary for the taxpayer to discharge the burden of proving that his taxable income was in truth less than the amount assessed. This he failed to do in respect of any of the assessments.

19. On the question of the burden of proof resting on taxpayers who appeal decisions on objections, Brennan J quoted a passage from Bailey v FC of T 77 ATC 4097 where, at p 4098, Barwick CJ stated:



"It is that process of assessment which, by virtue of sec. 190(b), an appellant taxpayer must satisfy ... an appellate court is 'excessive'. If some step in that process which affects the amount of tax lacks the authority of the Act the assessment is 'excessive': and the powers of sec. 195 or of sec. 199, as the case may be, become available."



Brennan J, in dealing with the passage cited above, stated:



"It may be that his Honour had in mind a case where all the material facts were known and the only question was the legal complexion to be attributed to them: cf his observations in Henderson v FC of T 70 ATC 4016 at p 4018; [1969] HCA 14; (1968-1970) 119 CLR 612 at p 648. But if his Honour did not intend the cited passage to be so understood, I must respectfully disagree with it. Since McAndrew's case [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263, it has been generally accepted that 'excessive' refers to the amount of the assessment, not to any unauthorised step in the process of its calculation." (my italics)



Toohey J likewise examined the Commissioner's argument in rebuttal of the Federal Court's majority finding that the assessments were "excessive" if it could be shown that they were not warranted by law.



"The Commissioner's starting point was sec. 166 of the Act which empowers, indeed directs, him to 'make an assessment of the amount of the taxable income of any taxpayer' from the returns 'and from any other information in his possession, or from any one or more of these sources'.



Section 166 is to be read with sec. 167 (the latter is not an independent power but is 'epexegetical to' the former - George v FC of T [1952] HCA 21; (1952) 86 CLR 183 at p. 204). In the circumstances of the present appeals it is para (b) of sec. 167 on which the Commissioner relies. That is, he says that he was not satisfied with the returns furnished by the taxpayer and was therefore empowered to 'make an assessment of the amount upon which in his judgment income tax ought to be levied'. Section 6(1) of the Act relevantly defines 'assessment' to mean 'the ascertainment of the amount of taxable income and of the tax payable thereon'. The view of Kitto J in Batagol v FC of T [1963] HCA 51; (1962-1963) 109 CLR 243 at p. 252, that 'assessment' means 'the completion of the process by which the provisions of the Act relating to liability to tax are given concrete application in a particular case with the consequence that a specified amount of money will become due and payable as the proper tax in that case' was generally shared by the other members of the Court in that case and was endorsed by Mason and Wilson JJ in FJ Bloemen Pty Ltd v FC of T at 81 ATC 4280 at pp. 4285-4286; [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 CLR 360 at pp. 371-372.



Section 175 provides that the validity of any assessment 'shall not be affected by reason that any of the provisions of this Act have not been complied with'. The section 'does not relieve the Commissioner from the necessity of performing his duty to make an assessment' but it does protect 'the validity of an assessment, once made, from the consequences which might otherwise flow from the Commissioner's failure to comply with any provisions of the Act': Bloemen at ATC p. 4285; CLR p. 371. In the present appeals, counsel for the taxpayer eschewed any suggestion that the assessments were invalid.



Section 177(1) then provided that the production of a notice of assessment 'shall be conclusive evidence of the due making of the assessment and (except in proceedings on appeal against the assessment) that the amount and all the particulars of the assessment are correct'. The effect of sec. 177(1) is that, on production of an assessment, 'the taxpayer is precluded from contesting that the Commissioner has made an assessment or that in making the assessment he has complied with the statutory formalities. The taxpayer is entitled to dispute his substantive liability to tax in proceedings under Pt V': Bloemen at ATC p. 4288; CLR p. 375.



Finally, in this concatenation of sections, sec. 190(b) casts 'the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive' upon the taxpayer. It is with the meaning and scope of this provision that these appeals are mainly concerned.



In McAndrew at p. 271, Dixon CJ, McTiernan and Webb JJ referred to the term 'excessive' in sec. 190(b) as 'the word chosen to correspond with the word 'amount' in s. 177(1)', adding 'It is perhaps not a good choice'. At the same time their Honours had no doubt that 'excessive' relates to the amount of the substantive liability'. McAndrew was concerned with an appeal against an amended assessment. The Court regarded the term 'excessive' as extending over the area in which the conditions precedent to the power to amend mentioned in sec. 170(2) found a place. It held that, once a regular notice of assessment was produced, the burden was on the taxpayer to show that he had made a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment or that there had not been an avoidance of tax (at p. 269).



I agree with Wilcox J in the Federal Court that 'the task for the taxpayer, upon an appeal or a review under Pt V of the Act, is to show that the amount of money for which tax is levied by a particular notice of assessment exceeds the actual substantive liability of the taxpayer'. As his Honour points out, a taxpayer will generally discharge that onus by satisfying the court or tribunal that his or her true taxable income is less than that appearing in the assessment. He or she may also do so by pointing to some error of computation or, as suggested by McAndrew, by showing non-compliance with statutory conditions precedent to the imposition of liability, in that case arising by reason of an amended assessment." (at pp 4096-7)



Deane J came to a like conclusion; Dawson J concurring with both Brennan and Toohey JJ.



20. Formidable as these decisions are, I have not been persuaded that they cover a case where it has been demonstrated that the assessment has been made "upon no intelligible basis even as an approximation", per Latham CJ; Trautwein (supra at p 88). Fundamental to the validity of a default assessment is the condition precedent that the Commissioner has formed a bona fide "judgment" ("the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his judgment (my italics) income tax ought to be levied"; cf sec 167). In no conceivable sense can what occurred here be said to constitute a "judgment", any more than my own decision in this case could be said to be a "judgment" if it consisted merely of phrases selected at random from the Book of Job. I am satisfied that in this case, the authority conferred upon the Commissioner by the Act to raise default assessments has been invalidly exercised, and that the observation by Brennan J in Dalco, to the effect that "since McAndrew's case, it has been generally accepted that 'excessive' refers to the amount of the assessment, not to any unauthorised step in the process of its calculation" (at p 4094) was not intended to cover a case, where, as here, the very foundation for the due making of the assessment has been demonstrated to be lacking.



21. I therefore find that the process of assessment has been such that it is open to the applicant to challenge its validity on the ground that he has been assessed on a basis unknown to the law; cf sec 177(1). In these circumstances, the question whether the amount assessed is "excessive" does not arise.



22. In the result, I find that, on this occasion the Crown was more conscious of its responsibilities than capable of discharging them.



23. The assessment is set aside and the matter remitted to the Commissioner to deal with the applicant in accordance with the Act.
