REASONS FOR DECISION

30 April 1999 J Block, Senior Member

1. The objection decision in this matter is the disallowance by the Respondent of an objection dated 24 December 1997 against amended assessments by the Respondent dated 29 October 1997 in respect of the years ending 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1994 (collectively the "relevant years" and each a "relevant year").

2. ) The Applicant was represented by Mr Bernard Glennon of counsel while Mr Roger Gormley an officer of the Respondent represented the Respondent. The Tribunal had before it the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 together with the exhibits set out in some detail in subclause (c).

(b) Before listing the exhibits in subclause (c), the Tribunal notes that it determined, in accordance with a suggestion by Mr Gormley at the end of the hearing, that this matter should be afforded confidentiality in accordance with section 35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The T Documents and the exhibits consist, to a considerable extent, of lengthy extracts from evidence given before the Royal Commission ("the Commission") into the NSW Police Service during 1996. The attention of the Tribunal was drawn to the fact that, although the evidence in question consists of extracts only, those extracts contain a number of references to suppression orders, made by the presiding judge at the Commission, as to the identities of certain witnesses. Those extracts do not indicate (and the Tribunal is not aware of) the extent or duration of the protection afforded by those suppression orders. It seemed to the Tribunal that it would be safest in the circumstances (and notwithstanding that this matter was advertised in the press and heard on the basis that it was a public hearing) to direct that the matter be treated as if it had been heard confidentially under section 35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, and these Reasons are thus framed accordingly. To make them more readily comprehensible, the Tribunal intends to utilise pseudonyms and other name alterations as follows:

* "Larkin" is the name given to the witness who gave evidence before the Commission as MV1 and who was the deponent to the statutory declaration which is Annexure C to Exhibit R1.

* "Warner" is the name given to the witness who gave evidence before the Commission as MV6 and who was the deponent to the statutory declaration which is Annexure D to Exhibit R1.

* "Marks" is the name of the witness who gave evidence before the Commission as to the theft of moneys from him, and who was sentenced to a term of imprisonment; his evidence before the Commission is set out in Annexure G to Exhibit R1.

* "Southside" refers to the suburb in Sydney in which the police station at which Larkin, Warner and the Applicant were serving officers at the relevant time was located; (it is unnecessary to allot a pseudonym to the Applicant who is referred to as the "Applicant" and bracketed references to the Applicant indicate that the term is used instead of his name; it is, however, relevant to note that the Applicant and Marks share a common ethnic origin). Similarly it is unnecessary to allot pseudonyms to Mr A. D. Crowe who was the deponent to the affidavit which is Exhibit R1 and to Mr Ian Marriott who was the deponent to the affidavit which is Exhibit R2, since both of them are officers of the Respondent who are regularly involved in matters of this nature.

* "Bender" refers to a female detective sergeant who gave evidence before the Commission and who served at Southside Police Station at the relevant time.

* "Ikin" refers to the person who is alleged to have made the payment which gave rise to the bribe assessment.

* "Kitchen" refers to the person who is alleged to have made the payment which gave rise to the gratuity assessment.

* "Lamb" refers to a somewhat mysterious policeman who may or may not have been present (at some time) at the search of Marks' apartment.

* "Barnesworth" refers to a police officer who gave evidence before the Commission and in respect of whom a short extract from his evidence was tendered as Exhibit A4.

(c) The Exhibits accepted into evidence by the Tribunal are as follows:

* Exhibit A1 is an affidavit by the Applicant dated 7 April, 1999.

* Exhibit A2 is a statement prepared by the Applicant and entitled "Statement of [Applicant]".

* Exhibit A3 is a statement prepared by the Applicant entitled "Points re Marks".

* Exhibit A4 is a short extract (1 page) from the evidence given by Barnesworth to the Commission on 16 July 1996.

* Exhibit A5 is an extract (15 pages) of evidence given by Bender to the Commission on 16 July 1996.

* Exhibit R1 is an affidavit by Mr A. D. Crowe, an officer of the Respondent, dated 26 March 1999 and to which were attached the following annexures:

- Annexure A is a copy of the notice of amended assessment for the first relevant year;

- Annexure B is a copy of the notice of amended assessment for the second relevant year;

- Annexure C is a statutory declaration by Larkin dated 31 May, 1996;

- Annexure D is a statutory declaration by Warner dated 19 July, 1996;

- Annexure E is an extract (207 pages) from the evidence given by Larkin to the Commission on 24 May 1996, 25 July 1996 and 18 August 1996;

- Annexure F is an extract (167 pages) from the evidence given by Marks to the Commission on 25 July, 1996;

- Annexure G is an extract (38 pages) from the evidence given by Marks to the Commission on 25 July, 1996;

* Exhibit R2 is an affidavit by Mr Ian Marriott dated 1 April, 1999.

* Exhibit R3 is a letter from Mr A.M. Champion of the Internal Affairs Command of the NSW Police Service and addressed to the Respondent which refers to adverse allegations made before the Commission in respect of the Applicant and the Applicant's subsequent resignation from the NSW Police Service after he had been served with notice to show cause why his service should not be terminated.

3. ) The Annexures to Exhibit R1 consist in large part of extracts from evidence given to the Commission and lengthy statutory declarations of Larkin and Warner which were prepared before they gave evidence before the Commission. The Tribunal was advised that each of Larkin and Warner had agreed to assist the Commission with its enquiries.

(b) At the beginning of the hearing Mr Gormley sought to tender the T Documents and in addition Exhibits R1 and R2. Exhibit R2 makes it clear that Larkin and Marks were not prepared to give evidence before the Tribunal, and that Warner, whose whereabouts were not known, would not be available to give evidence before the Tribunal. The term "relevant evidence" as used in these Reasons refers to those parts of Exhibit R1 which consist of extracts from the evidence of Larkin, Warner and Marks before the Commission, and in the case of Larkin and Warner, their statutory declarations; the term "relevant witnesses" refers collectively to Larkin, Warner and Marks.

(c) It was noted by the Tribunal that the transcript evidence before the Commission indicated that each of the relevant witnesses (and also the Applicant himself) had given evidence before the Commission unwillingly. The commission was constituted under the Royal Commission (Police Service) Act 1994 (NSW) ("1994 Act"), which is in its terms related to the Royal Commission Act 1923 (NSW) ("1923 Act"). Section 5 of the 1994 Act which is entitled "Relationship with 1923 Act" reads as follows:

"5(1) This Act is to be read as if it formed part of the 1923 Act (but only for the purposes of the operation of this Act in relation to a Royal Commission to which this Act applies). A reference in any other Act to the 1923 Act does not include a reference to this Act, except as expressly provided by this Act.

(2) The provisions of this Act are in addition to and not in derogation of any provisions of the 1923 Act, except as specifically provided by this Act. In particular, Part 2 of this Act does not limit or otherwise affect the provisions of the 1923 Act with respect to a person summoned to attend or appearing as a witness before the Commission.

(3) Nothing in the Royal Commission Act operates to constrain or restrict the exercise of a function conferred or imposed by or under this Act."

[The 1923 Act referred to in the above extract is defined in s4(1) of the 1994 Act as meaning the Royal Commission Act 1923]. Section 17 of the 1923 Act in turn reads as follows:

"17(1) A witness summoned to attend or appearing before the commission shall not be excused from answering any question or producing any book, document, or writing on the ground that the answer or production may criminate or tend to criminate him, or on the ground of privilege or on any other ground.

(2) An answer made, or book, document, or writing produced by a witness to or before the commission shall not, except as otherwise provided in this section, be admissible in evidence against that person in any civil or criminal proceedings.

(3) Nothing in this section shall be deemed to render inadmissible-

(a) any answer, book, document, or writing in proceedings for an offence against this Act;

(b) any answer, book, document, or writing in any civil or criminal proceedings if the witness was willing to give the answer or produce the book, document, or writing irrespective of the provisions of subsection one of this section."

(c) ..." (Emphasis added by the Tribunal)

Accordingly, and in respect of each of the relevant witnesses (and also the Applicant himself) the fact that he gave evidence unwillingly entitled him to require that that evidence could not (subject to certain exceptions not here relevant) be used against him "in any civil or criminal proceedings". In each case the Commission itself made specific mention of this aspect; see by way of example, and in relation to Larkin, the following passage from Annexure E to Exhibit R1 at page 3228:

"THE COMMISSIONER: Q. Although you may already have been informed of it, you will now hear it from me and then I will ask you a question about that which I am now going to say to you. The first thing is: You have to answer all of the questions that are asked of you here.

A. Yes Sir.

Q. You have an entitlement to indicate, if you wish, that you will answer those questions unwillingly. If you do that, evidence that you give cannot be used against you in any civil or criminal proceedings, except a prosecution for perjury, should you knowingly give false evidence to me, or a prosecution for an offence which you may have committed or you may commit under the legislation which governs this Royal Commission. Have you understood what I have said?

A. Yes, I do.

Q. Are you going to answer willingly or unwillingly?

A. I'll answer the questions unwillingly.

THE COMMISSION: Very well. I make a declaration to the effect that the evidence of this witness is given unwillingly ..."

In respect of the other relevant witnesses and the Applicant the formula used was much the same.

(d) Mr Glennon objected to the admission of the relevant evidence on the basis that the evidence of the relevant witnesses could not be tested by cross-examination, due to their unavailability to appear before the Tribunal, and so that the admission of that evidence would be unfair to the Applicant. (Mr Glennon contended also that the copy of the statutory declaration of Larkin which is Annexure E to Exhibit R1 did not on its face indicate that it was signed by Larkin; that contention was not persisted with, and indeed the Tribunal has no reason to doubt that it was in fact attested.)

(e) Mr Glennon cited in support of his contention that the relevant evidence should be rendered inadmissible by reference to the decision of the House of Lords in R v Hull Prison Board of Visitors [1979] 3 All ER 545. In that case, a number of prisoners challenged the findings of a disciplinary proceeding on the grounds that the disciplinary board had failed to observe the rules of natural justice in that it had, inter alia, admitted and then acted on statements made during the hearing by the governor which were based on reports by prison officers who had not given oral evidence. The findings of the House of Lords (per Lord Widgery CJ, Geoffrey Lane LJ and Ackner J) are best summarised (in respect of their relevance to Mr Glennon's submission) by reference to headnote (iii) which reads as follows:

"(iii) The board was not bound by the technical rules of evidence which applied in courts in criminal cases. It could admit hearsay evidence but its right to do so was subject to the overriding obligation to provide the accused with a fair hearing. Depending on the facts of the particular case, and the nature of the hearsay evidence provided, the obligation to give the accused a fair hearing might require the board not only to inform the accused of the evidence but also to give him a sufficient opportunity to deal with it, which could involve the cross-examination of witnesses whose evidence was initially before the board in the form of hearsay. Where a prisoner wished to dispute hearsay evidence, and for that purpose to question a witness, the board should, if there were insuperable or very grave difficulties in arranging for the witness's attendance, refuse to admit the evidence, or, if it had already come to its notice expressly dismiss it from its consideration ..."

The evidence in question in Hull's case was, as the headnote indicates, hearsay evidence.

(f) It is clear that under section 33(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence. Furthermore, the Tribunal may accept evidence which is not the best evidence; the Tribunal refers in this context to the decision in Barbaro & Minister for Immigration & Ethnic Affairs, Re (1980) 3 ALD 1 at page 5 where Davies J as President of this Tribunal stated:

"In informing itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate, the Tribunal endeavours to be fair to the parties. It endeavours not to put the parties to unnecessary expense and may admit into evidence evidentiary material of a logically probative nature notwithstanding that the material is not the best evidence of the matter which it tends to prove. But the Tribunal does not lightly receive into evidence challenged evidentiary material concerning a matter of importance of which there is or should be better evidence. And the requirement of a hearing and the provision of a right to appear and be represented carries with it an implication that, so far as is possible and consistent with the function of the Tribunal, a party should be given the opportunity of testing prejudicial evidentiary material tendered against him. It is generally appropriate that a party should have an opportunity to do more than give evidence to the contrary of the evidence adduced on behalf of the other party. He should be given an opportunity to test the evidence tendered against him provided that the testing of the evidence seems appropriate in the circumstances and does not conflict with the obligation laid upon the Tribunal to proceed with as little formality and technicality and with as much expedition as the matter before the Tribunal permits.

It is not in every case fair to all parties or otherwise appropriate that the Tribunal should insist upon the best evidence of disputed facts, even critical facts. There are occasions when it is inappropriate to insist upon such evidence and when it is appropriate to receive evidence which, though of a logically probative nature, cannot be tested by one or more to the parties. In such a case, the party adversely affected by the evidence is given a fair hearing by his attendance at the hearing, his awareness of the evidence adduced against him and his ability to adduce evidence to the contrary if he sees fit to do so. The fair hearing in this instance takes account of the function of the Tribunal including the fact that it is an administrative tribunal and not a court of law. As an administrative tribunal, it has the duty to review an administrative decision and to exercise the decision making power. It is unnecessary that the nature of the procedures adopted at the hearing and the nature of the evidence which is received by the Tribunal be adapted to the function which it performs." (Emphasis added by the Tribunal)

(g) The relevant evidence could not be described as hearsay evidence, in contrast with the evidence to which objection was made in the House of Lords decision in Hull. On the contrary, the relevant evidence sets out in graphic details (particularly in the case of Larkin and Warner) the extent of their own conduct. Nor, in the view of the Tribunal would the admission of that evidence be unfair (having regard to the decision of Davies J in Barbaro's case), having regard to the fact that the Applicant was present and able to give evidence in rebuttal. The Tribunal noted, in admitting the relevant evidence, that the weight to be attached to it would necessarily be affected by the fact that that evidence could not be tested in cross-examination.

(h) However, and initially, the Tribunal rejected Document T8 of the T Documents which is an extract of the Applicant's own evidence before the Commission. He gave that evidence unwillingly, and thus became entitled to the statutory protection referred to previously. The Tribunal for this purpose was of the view that the proceedings before it were "civil or criminal proceedings", noting in this regard Brett Geoffrey Hartmann v Commission of Police (1997) 91 AcrimR 141 (a decision of the Court of Criminal Appeal of New South Wales; Cole JA with whom the other members of the Appeal Court agreed) at page 147:

"The words 'in any civil or criminal proceedings' in section 17 are not to be narrowly construed but are to be read as expressing the legislature's intention that statements of evidence, or documents, or oral evidence produced to or given to the Police Royal Commission are not available as evidence against the person providing or giving the statement, document or evidence in any future proceedings, howsoever they are categorised. The expression 'any civil or criminal proceedings' was intended to encompass the full category of possible future proceedings."

(i) At a later stage of the hearing and with the express consent of the Applicant, and having regard to the fact that Document T8 of the T Documents is consistent (for the most part) with other evidence given by the Applicant, Document T8 of the T Documents was accepted into evidence, and so that as set out previously, all of the T Documents were accepted in evidence. Mr Gormley was in consequence afforded an opportunity to cross-examine the Applicant in respect of the evidence contained in Document T8 of the T Documents.

4. The amended assessment in respect of the first relevant year seeks to bring to tax an amount of $20,000 which is alleged to be the Applicant's share of $60,000 stolen from the apartment of Marks during a search of that apartment by Larkin, Warner and the Applicant for illicit drugs, and an amount of $1,250 which is alleged to be the Applicant's share of a bribe paid by Ikin. The assessment for the second year seeks to bring to tax an amount of $1,500 which is alleged to have been received by the Applicant as a gratuity, although it formed part of a larger amount received by Larkin as a bribe from Kitchen.

5. The evidence of each relevant witness before the Commission was given on oath, as also were the statutory declarations of Larkin and Warner and being Annexures C and D to Exhibit R1. Although the Commission extracts indicate that a number of lawyers were present and that questions were asked by them of witnesses, and including in this context a lawyer briefed by the Police Commissioner, that evidence was not tested by cross-examination on behalf of the Applicant who was not present, or invited to be present, when that evidence was given. It may be noted that Larkin and Warner were both senior detectives at Southside (and Larkin was the senior of the two) while the Applicant was at the time an "apprentice" detective (as Mr Glennon aptly described him) and had been transferred (some months previously) from the uniformed branch, in order to be trained with a view to his possible confirmation as a plain-clothes detective. The Applicant was not partnered with either Larkin or Warner, although he did on occasion work with them.

6. ) The evidence of Larkin as given in his affidavit (which is Annexure C to Exhibit R1) is lengthy and detailed; it is disconcerting in that it includes, inter alia, a long list of crimes committed by Larkin consistently over a lengthy period and including in particular repeated assaults, bribery, theft and other corrupt behaviour. It is disconcerting in its matter-of-factness; it was clearly, so far as Larkin was concerned, the manner in which he conducted his duties on a day-to-day basis. The following extract from Larkin's affidavit serves as an example of one aspect only of Larkin's behaviour; that paragraph reads as follows:

"8. In early 1994 a person by the name of [Hamman] was arrested by Police Officers at [Southside]. He was arrested for assaulting a pregnant woman on a platform at the [Southside] Railway Station the previous evening. He was brought to the [Southside] Police Station and interviewed by me in the presence of a young uniformed police officer. I subsequently sent that young police officer out of the room and then I assaulted [Hamman] by giving him a couple of backhanders across the back of the head. I think I hit him on two separate occasions. In one of the assaults I hit [Hamman] over the back of the head resulting with him being thrown towards a wall and hitting his head on that wall resulting in a lump on his forehead. I subsequently charged [Hamman] with assault and he was eventually dealt with by a magistrate at [Children's Court]. This matter was not dealt with at a hearing stage until about twelve months afterwards whilst I was then at [Bannon] Police Station serving as a detective Sergeant. I gave evidence at the hearing before a magistrate. I cannot remember the name of the magistrate or the day on which the matter came on for hearing. I was questioned by [Hamman's] Solicitor ... as to whether or not I had assaulted [Hamman] at the time of the interview prior to him being charged. I committed perjury on that occasion by denying that I had assaulted [Hamman]." (Square brackets in respect of this and other quoted passages from Commission extracts indicate edits to preserve confidentiality.)

Larkin's evidence, both before the Commission and in his statutory declaration (which is Annexure C to Exhibit R1) indicates that the behaviour referred to by way of example (and other equally reprehensible behaviour) occurred repeatedly and regularly.

(b) The statutory declaration of Larkin and his evidence before the Commission can be summarised, insofar as it affects the Applicant, in that it alleges that Larkin, Warner and the Applicant searched the apartment of Marks for drugs, found a large sum of money (and also drugs) and that of that money $60,000 was stolen and shared between Warner, Larkin and the Applicant. (Warner said that $40,000 was retained by Larkin, which may have been shared by Larkin with the Applicant; para 81 and 88 of Annexure D; however, the allegation is inconsistent with another statement by Warner (referred to later in these Reasons) that the Applicant may not have known that the money was misappropriated. Larkin's evidence deals also with the Ikin bribe and the Kitchen gratuity.

(c) Larkin, during the course of his evidence before the Commission, rejected suggestions of violent conduct towards his police subordinates or juniors and in particular the Applicant and Bender. In particular see Annexure E (at page 5795) where the following exchange took place under cross-examination:

"Q. Did you also assault other police officers?

A. Assault other police officers? No.

Q. Never?

A. No.

Q. No other police officers have you ever assaulted in your career; is that right?

A. Not a physical assault, no. There may have been a bit of mucking around in the office, but no blows have ever been exchanged or anything like that, no.

Q. What about mucking around in the office with Detective [Bender]; did you ever strike her?

A. That's a load of rubbish.

Q. Is that incorrect?

A. This is incorrect.

Q. Anybody else that you might have played around with and struck in a joking manner?

A. [The Applicant]. He's a big guy. We worked together for some time. If he made a mistake, I might punch him on the shoulder or something like that, but nothing of anything that would hurt him. He's a fairly big guy."

However, Bender's evidence (Exhibit A5) contradicts the evidence of Larkin in this regard. See in particular the following evidence at page 5359:

"Q. How were you treated by [Larkin] on arrival and in the period that you were attached to the [Southside] detectives?

A. In the most, I suppose, condescending manner. He appeared to me - he had a really poor attitude to women in policing. He certainly didn't like me being senior to him and I had been at the patrol no more than a week and I was standing there at the detectives office, the bosses had gone, Mr [Barnesworth] and Mr [Harner] had gone for the day. I was just chatting to a few of the other detectives and [Larkin] whacked me with a car diary and in full force hit me on the bottom as he was walking past and I just turned to him, 'You'll keep' with a swear word attached to it and he just laughed and walked out of the office. Those bully tactics from [Larkin] continued for about twelve months until I managed to stop him from hitting me and the other constables.

Q. Apart from this occasion when he hit you with a car diary, do you say there were other instances when he physically assaulted you?

A. He hit me a number of times, but I do recall two bruises that he left because I'm not a very big sort of person and [Larkin] is over six foot tall and is a footballer and a joking punch in the arm from him to me was like a full force punch to me on my arms, left a bruise and I had from another punch a bruise on my ribcage.

Q. Did you observe him assaulting other members of the staff at [Southside]?

A. Initially I observed him assaulting them. Then I observed him attempting to assault them while I stood in between him and his alleged victim who was normally cowering in the corner with his hands over his head."

At page 5368 Bender stated further:

"Q. Generally, what observations would you make about [Southside] at the time you were there?

A. Well, it seemed to me that morale was pretty low at [Southside]. It was low amongst the uniformed police too. There was a lot of grown men there, I think that were physically scared of this [Larkin] and when he used to hit them, but I don't think ever would have the courage because they're men, A, to stand up to him, or, B, to tell anyone how frightened they were of him physically.

..."

The evidence of Bender before the Commission is supported to some extent by the evidence of Barnesworth (Exhibit A4) before the Commission:

"Ms Bell: Q. ... Were there any amongst the detectives staff who, in your view, intimidated junior officers?

A. Yes, I would indicate to the commission that [Larkin] came over as being a person of very strong personality and possessed perhaps a domineering manner and that in itself, I believe, did intimidate some of the uniformed staff as well as the detectives."

In the view of the Tribunal, the evidence of Bender is to be preferred to that of Larkin in respect of Larkin's conduct towards the Applicant, Bender and others.

(d) The Applicant in his evidence before the Tribunal made mention of Larkin's violence towards him and of his fear of him.

(e) The evidence of both the Applicant and Bender indicated that Larkin was a very large man who was capable of inspiring and did in fact inspire very real fear; there is a reference in Bender's evidence to police "cowering" under his threats. In general terms, the evidence of Larkin both in his statutory declaration and before the Commission is indicative of a person who (in general terms) was both corrupt and violent.

7. ) The evidence of Warner both on affidavit and before the Commission indicates similarly that Warner was corrupt and dishonest, and committed crimes (particularly assault, perjury and theft) as if they were a part of his ordinary duties. See paragraphs 36, 43-45, 48, 50, 56, 58-59, 61-62 of his affidavit which is Annexure D to Exhibit R1. In particular and as one example of Warner's violent conduct see paragraph 59 of his affidavit, which contains the following statement:

"There was one incident when there was an Internal Affairs complaint in relation to a Vietnamese young boy who was affected by drugs and when my partner went out of the room, I punched him on several occasions to the stomach in order to calm him down."

The statutory declaration does not then go on to elaborate as to how an assault can possibly be calculated to calm a person.

(b) The evidence of Warner indicated that sharing of corrupt moneys was by no means invariable and that there were occasions on which corrupt moneys were obtained and where other policemen involved in the investigation received either little or no share at all. See for example paragraphs 43, 110 and 169-171 of Warner's statutory declaration.

(c) Warner's evidence in relation to the Applicant was not as incriminating as that of Larkin; indeed in clause 85 of his statutory declaration he stated that the Applicant may not even have known of the theft of the moneys from Marks. That statement is significant in that it conflicts the evidence of Larkin (in relation to the largest by far of the three amounts which are the subjects of the amended assessments) that the Applicant received an equal share of the stolen moneys.

(d) The Tribunal's general comments as regards Larkin (in clause 6(e)) apply also (albeit to a lesser extent) to Warner.

8. The evidence of Marks before the Commission was that the Applicant was involved in the theft of moneys from him. (The Applicant, in his own evidence, said that notwithstanding the fact that he endeavoured to be conciliatory towards Marks, speaking to him in their common language, Marks may have taken the view that all three policemen were to be regarded in the same adverse light; put in other words, Marks having had a substantial amount stolen from him and having served a prison term, did not distinguish between the three policemen as to degrees of guilt or innocence. Put colloquially, they were, so far as Marks was concerned, "tarred with the same brush".) The evidence of Marks does not in its terms furnish any detail of how the Applicant came into possession of the stolen moneys, and in particular whether he took it out of the bag or received it from Larkin or Warner or both.

9. ) The Applicant was cross-examined at length as to the events surrounding the alleged theft from Marks of $60,000. His evidence was at times confused, both as to the time taken in relation to certain events, and also as to precisely where he was at any relevant time; in particular:

) the Applicant gave evidence before the Tribunal as to one bag of money; that evidence was not consistent with his evidence before the Commission as to two bags. He said that he saw a bag containing a large sum of money but did not know how much that sum was and that it was never counted in his presence.

(2) The Applicant was also questioned at some length as to the whereabouts of Larkin during the period in Marks' house and, in particular, as to whether he had seen Larkin leave the premises for a period of time. The Applicant was unsure about when exactly Larkin left the Marks apartment and the duration of his absence.

(3) The Applicant was also unsure as to the exact period of time at the apartment stating that it may have been anywhere from 45 minutes to an hour and a half.

(b) Some time after the Applicant had given evidence before the Commission, he was called upon to show cause as to why he should not be dismissed from the Police Force; see Exhibit R3. His evidence was that he consulted his police association who according to him, advised him that he had little prospect of success. He decided to resign from the Police Force voluntarily, giving at different times during his evidence various reasons for his decision. He said that his reasons included the fact that voluntary resignation would preserve the whole of his superannuation entitlement in contrast with dismissal, which would result in loss of a portion of that entitlement. At a different time, he said that he had no real hope of further promotion and would never be promoted higher than senior constable; he had on his return to work, after being suspended for a period of months, been returned to the uniform branch. Later in his evidence he explained his resignation on the basis that he had a young child, did not want to carry a gun any longer and be exposed to danger, and in any event that his wife wanted him out of the Police Force. The evidence of the Applicant in this regard while in some respects inconsistent, is perhaps explicable on the basis that all of these motives figured in his decision to some, although not necessarily to the same, extent.

(c) The Applicant gave evidence as to his real fear of Larkin and the contemptuous way in which Larkin treated him, referring to him in very uncomplimentary terms and in particular as a "Monaro man", "the wog with the Monaro" and "the Big Palooka"; see Exhibit A2. The term "Monaro man" is apparently a derogatory term applied to persons of ethnic origin. He was cross-examined as to the fact that he nonetheless invited Larkin (together with every other officer at Southside police station) to his engagement party, that he had lent $500 to Larkin (repaid at the time of the engagement party, the repayment supposedly being the Applicant's engagement present), and that before the Commission occurred, he solicited Larkin's assistance in relation to a personal problem. The Applicant was cross-examined also as to why he stayed at Southside where he was treated contemptuously and aggressively and did not seek a transfer to another station. He said that he was comfortable at Southside where he had friends and where he had been a uniformed policeman, and that much of his social life involved socialising with his colleagues at Southside.

(d) The Tribunal does not consider that the Applicant's evidence was unacceptable. Although he is a very large man, he speaks very softly, and does not indicate any great self-confidence. The Tribunal accepts that he was afraid of Larkin; the Tribunal believes moreover that the truth may be that the Applicant regarded Larkin with fear, but at the same time with a certain respect; he certainly appears to have been cowed by the presence of Larkin. The Applicant went to meals with Larkin (and a person referred to as a police informant) and made entries in his records at the direction of Larkin where those entries were either fictitious or, intended to record something different (to a greater or lesser degree) from the events which actually occurred. The Applicant admitted that he received free meals and liquor but consistently denied receiving any corrupt moneys.

(e) Nor is it surprising to the Tribunal that the Applicant invited Larkin to his engagement party; to have failed to do so when he invited every other member of the Southside police station would have been most imprudent. Moreover, that the Applicant elected to stay at Southside is not, in the view of the Tribunal, to be held against him. His bad treatment at the hands of Larkin occurred on a number of occasions, but given that he was not partnered with Larkin, may not have been frequent. It would appear that during much of the time, the Applicant was more or less comfortable in his position in a branch which was familiar to him. In general terms, the attitude of the Applicant towards Larkin indicates, at least to some extent, a somewhat complex dependence; while afraid of his superior officer, he was at the same time apparently prepared to accommodate himself to his superior officer's requirements and even to put up with occasional (and perhaps even quite regular) insults and physical aggressiveness.

10. The evidence of the Applicant has been consistent in one important respect; before the Commission, in a subsequent interview with the Respondent (and see Document T9) and before this Tribunal, the Applicant has consistently and repeatedly denied that he ever received any of the moneys sought to be taxed.

11. In general terms the Tribunal considers (subject to some reservations) that the evidence of the Applicant was worthy of credit and should be accepted. It was, as set out previously, and in some respects inconsistent, in that it did, as Mr Gormley contended, change to some extent during the hearing, and moreover did not always accord with his evidence before the Commission. But regarded overall, his evidence was not such as to cause the Tribunal to consider that it should not believe his evidence, and in particular his denials of receipt of the moneys sought to be assessed. The inconsistencies previously referred to are to some extent explicable having regard to the lapse of years between the evidence having been given before the Commission and the evidence having been given before this Tribunal.

12. Mr Gormley argued that each of Larkin and Warner had in effect been granted an indemnity by the Commission, but that the indemnity excluded perjury and so that each of them would not only have had no motive to perjure himself in relation to the Applicant, but rather would have had a real motive not to perjure himself for fear of loss of the relevant indemnity. This being so, so Mr Gormley contended, their evidence as to moneys taken by the Applicant as his share of ill-gotten gains should be taken to be truthful. Such a contention is in the view of the Tribunal far-fetched; persons who were capable of the behaviour set out in the affidavits of Larkin and Warner are unlikely to have baulked at evidence incriminating their seemingly defenceless junior; the prospect of a prosecution for perjury in these circumstances cannot have been perceived as a real threat. Moreover, and as their evidence demonstrated, perjury was a common occurrence so far as they were concerned.

Mr Glennon suggested that the evidence which incriminated the Applicant may have been given to protect a senior officer, and that perhaps that senior officer was Lamb, who may or may not have been at the apartment of Marks at the time of the search. But there was no evidence before the Tribunal as to a senior (or any other) officer being protected in this manner.

13. In Case 68/96 ATC 605 (a decision by me) the Tribunal was concerned with certain questions which were in some respects similar to (or at least analogous with) relevant questions in this matter. The Tribunal ruled in Case 68/96 that evidence by Munro given before the Independent Commission against Corruption should not be admitted having regard to the protection afforded to him by section 37(3) of the Independent Commission Against Corruption Act (NSW) 1988. In that matter too, the Tribunal had to deal with untested evidence by one Schaefer; it found that the evidence of Schaefer was to be preferred to that of Munro as to the facts in question. The evidence in Case 68/96 indicated that Munro had received (albeit very indirectly and through a subscription for redeemable shares in an associated company) an amount of $400,000, in consideration for his relinquishment of a profit entitlement (couched in very wide terms, and wide enough to include profits both of an income nature and profits, or an equity entitlement of a capital nature). The payment was made, presumably at the insistence of Girvan, when it acquired an interest in the relevant trust. The Respondent contended that the payment should be taxed as income derived by Munro (although not paid directly to him) and having regard to section 19 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("Tax Act"); however, the Respondent expressly disclaimed any intention to invoke the capital gains tax provisions contained in Part IIIA of the Tax Act. The evidence before the Tribunal was that the trust had never derived any income. The Tribunal found against the Respondent on the basis that the amount of $400,000 could not be said to be income derived by Munro. Although not specifically so stated, it is implicit in that decision that a finding under Part IIIA of the Tax Act would have been made, had that Part not been disclaimed.

The decision in Case 68/96 was reversed on appeal in FC of T v Munro 97 ATC 5041. His Honour Lockhart J noted that where the evidence of Schaefer was preferred to that of Munro, Munro should fail on the basis that he had not discharged the onus on him under Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. The Tribunal accepts of course that the decision of the Federal Court in Munro is binding on it; the Tribunal notes however that as appears from headnote 1 in Trustee of the Balmain Trust v FC of T 98 ATC 4433, that a "taxpayer is not precluded from receiving a correct assessment by reason of his untruthfulness if the evidence disclosed facts which show that the assessment was wrong."

14. Mr Gormley argued that in respect of the Applicant, there was no real evidence by him other than his denial of receipt of the ill-gotten gains and this being so, any evidence to the contrary, notwithstanding that it may have not been tested, should prevail. This contention was founded on the decision in Munro's case on which Mr Gormley relied. However, and in any event, the facts (and the Tribunal's findings) in this matter are such as to distinguish it from Munro's case. The Tribunal considers (and so finds) that the Applicant's evidence is preferable to the relevant evidence (given by corrupt persons), and where two of the relevant witnesses refused to come before the Tribunal (in the case of Larkin in the profane terms set out in Exhibit R2); the Tribunal again notes that in relation to by far the largest part of the aggregate amount assessed (i.e. the amount of $20,000 stolen from Marks) the evidence of Larkin and Warner was not consistent; while Larkin's evidence was that the Applicant received $20,000, Warner's evidence indicated a clear doubt as to whether the Applicant knew of the stolen moneys. Of course Warner's own relevant evidence at another point contains an inconsistent statement to the effect that he assumed that the Applicant had received his share of the stolen moneys. Treated overall the evidence of Larkin and Warner as to the Applicant's receipt of $20,000 must be treated with suspicion.

The Tribunal finds that the relevant evidence, insofar as it is alleged to assist the Respondent, cannot safely or properly be afforded any significant weight, and moreover could not possibly, on any basis, be preferred to the evidence of the Applicant.

15. ) Mr Gormley criticised the Applicant because he did not bring Bender (and also another police officer) to give evidence as to Larkin's violent conduct, and presumably by way of corroboration of the Applicant's allegations in this regard. The Applicant said that he did not have the means for this purpose. But in any event the fact that Larkin was violent was all too probable; on his own evidence he assaulted persons on a regular basis. No other evidence as to this aspect of Larkin's conduct was necessary, especially in the light of the fact that the Tribunal considers that the evidence of Bender before the Commission is to be preferred.

(b) Mr Glennon pointed out that the only evidence tendered by the Respondent as to the Applicant's receipt of moneys was that of the two corrupt policemen and Marks (a convicted drug dealer); there was in other words no evidence of any financial investigations into the Applicant's affairs or of any audit which, as in many cases, precedes a betterment or default assessment. There was in other words no evidence of possession by the Applicant of moneys for which he could not account.

Nor indeed did the Respondent take steps to compel Larkin or Marks to be present at this hearing; the presence of either or both of them could presumably have been compelled notwithstanding their hostility; if in the result either of them had given evidence which was adverse to the Respondent, it is likely the Tribunal would have been inclined to allow the Respondent to cross-examine them. This is so also of Warner; the Tribunal was not informed as to attempts (if any) made by the Respondent to trace his whereabouts and to compel his presence.

16. In this particular instance, the Applicant when faced with incriminating or adverse evidence denied it consistently. Any adverse evidence has been found to be worthy of slight, if any credit, whereas the denials of the Applicant have been accepted, albeit with some reservations. It is hard to see what more could be expected of an Applicant in these circumstances; the onus on him (and see in this regard Munro's case and the case authorities referred to in the judgment of Lockhart J in Munro's case) oblige him, in certain circumstances, to establish what the correct figures should be. In a matter such as this, and where the Respondent did not obtain any evidence of a financial nature as to the receipt by the Applicant of moneys for which he could not account, the Applicant, who denied the receipt of the relevant moneys, cannot be required to furnish evidence as to the correct figures, since his evidence is (and can in these circumstances only be) that the correct figure is nil, or in other words that he did not receive the payments alleged to have been made to him. It may be noted that the Applicant gave evidence (which was not questioned) as to difficult financial times experienced by him following his resignation from the Police Force, and including a period during which he was on the dole. That the Respondent himself had doubts as to his course of action in issuing the relevant amended assessments appears from Document T10, which is an internal report by a senior officer of the Respondent; in that document the Respondent noted that the matter was not free from doubt; (see page 109 of the T Documents). The nature of the relevant evidence is such that those doubts were well-founded.

17. The Tribunal finds as a matter of fact that the Applicant has discharged the onus on him under s14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, and accordingly the objection decision under review must be set aside. In these circumstances it is unnecessary for the Tribunal to deal with arguments submitted, in the alternative and on behalf of the Applicant, that in any event the moneys in question were not capable of characterisation as income in accordance with ordinary concepts.
