Re Perron Investments Pty Ltd; Century Finance Pty Ltd; Prestige Motors Pty Ltd; Geoffrey John Hartigan and Carnegie Richmond Hallett v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation,Perth and Sydney [1989] FCA 70 (15 March 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: PERRON INVESTMENTS PTY LTD; CENTURY FINANCE PTY LTD; PRESTIGE MOTORS

PTY LTD.; GEOFFREY JOHN HARTIGAN AND CARNEGIE RICHMOND HALLETT

And: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION,PERTH AND SYDNEY

Nos. G175-177 and 218, 219 of 1986

FED No. 127

Administrative Law

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Einfeld J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Law - Application to review the decision of the Commissioner of Taxation to issue notices under section 264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act - interpretation of sections 264(1)(a) and 264(1)(b) - whether Commissioner can require copies to be made and provided - whether a reasonable time for compliance was allowed - whether the power to issue section 264 notices was properly delegated - legal professional privilege - privilege against self-incrimination - severance.

Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 - ss 5(1)(d), 5(1)(e), 5(1)(f) and 5(1)(j)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s 264

HEARING

SYDNEY

15:3:1989

Counsel and solicitors for Mr D.H. Bloom, QC and

the applicant companies Dr G.A. Flick

and Mr Fieldhouse instructed by

C.R. Fieldhouse

Solicitor

Counsel and solicitors for Mr R.F. Edmonds

the applicant Mr Hartigan instructed by

Minter Simpson

Solicitors

Counsel and solicitors for Mr A. Emmett, QC and

the respondents Miss M.J. Beazley

instructed by the

Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The applications are dismissed.

The applicants are to pay the respondent's costs.

DECISION

On 21 October 1988 I handed down my conclusions on various matters of law submitted for decision concerning notices issued under section 264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 to each of the applicants. I formally adopt those conclusions as part of these reasons for final judgment. During the hearing of the matter it was decided that I should not make final decisions on the relief to be granted until those conclusions were available for consideration.

2. On 9 March 1989 the parties argued the orders which I should now make. The applicants submitted that I should now make a virtual line by line examination of the questions or matters contained in each of the notices. I should then rule that several of them were ambiguous, uncertain or too wide, and should for that reason be severed, and that certain questions or requests which clearly sought information that was covered by legal professional privilege should be struck out or declared to be invalid. The respondent argued that in the light of the conclusions which I handed down on 21 October 1988, I should dismiss the applications for orders of review. The respondent objected to my now analysing the notices as requested by the applicants, as being inconsistent with the earlier conclusions. In view of the attitude I have formed to the attack on the notices by the applicants, it is not necessary that I rule on the approach pressed on me by the respondent.

3. The applicants say that the tests that should be applied in examining notices of this kind are those which were discussed by a Full Court of this Court in Pyneboard Pty Ltd v Trade Practices Commission [1983] HCA 9; (1982) 57 FLR 368. In that case the Court was examining a notice issued under section 155 of the Trade Practices Act requiring the furnishing of information and the production of documents in relation to suspected breaches of certain parts of that Act. In substance the Court (Northrop, Deane, and Fisher JJ) decided that for such a notice to be valid, it must have been issued in good faith, it must clearly tell the person to whom it is addressed what information and documents he is required to furnish, and it must comply with various other formal obligations. Their Honours said that unreasonableness, oppressiveness and harshness were not independent grounds of objection but that invalidity will only follow if those qualities are such as to enable a conclusion to be drawn that the notice was not issued in good faith. It was also held in that case that where possible what was described as "a blue line deletion" was permissible and that a person may refuse to answer any question which should not be asked where blue line deletion was not possible.

4. The applicant further pointed to the serious consequences that follow the failure of a person to answer a notice under section 264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act: see Taxation Administration Act 1953 sections 8K(1) and 8J(2). The applicant also relied upon the fact that these notices were addressed to the public officers of these companies care of the companies' registered offices, which seem to have been in the offices of their accountants. The applicants argued in this respect that the notices should be examined against the likely knowledge of a public officer about the matters comprised in the notices. I was actually told nothing about the particular public officers involved in these notices including for example whether they were, apart from public officers, also directors of the companies or otherwise involved in the companies' operations. In answer to my specific query, senior counsel for the applicants argued that I was not entitled to consider that the applicants might have or might choose to have access to expert legal or accounting advice in respect of any particular questions or requests which they did not completely understand or in relation to which they did not know their rights, despite the fact that the companies had senior and junior counsel appearing for them during the substantive hearing of this matter as well as on this question of the orders that should now be made. In my opinion, these propositions ought not to be accepted. However, in the light of my conclusions on the actual content of the notices, this is not necessary to be finally decided here.

5. I turn to the notices themselves and the attacks made on them by the applicants. The oral argument covered only some examples of the objections raised, while written submissions deal with the objections in detail. The detailed written objections are annexed to this judgment. I shall now deal with only those of them that appear to me to be the most important illustrations for the conclusions which I have drawn. Quite a number of the objections are identical or very similar and involve the same principle. However, I have considered them all and my conclusions apply to all.

6. The notices contain a number of requests to explain or supply details of what is described as "the circumstances". In the notice to Prestige Motors, this occurs as:

Explain the circumstances in which it was decided to amend the Deed

of Trust pursuant to the Deed dated 1 March 1979, including

after which a number of specific details are requested.

7. In the notice to Century Finance Pty Ltd, this is put in the form:

In relation to the issue (of certain shares) provide the

circumstances in which it was decided to issue the

shares . . . . . . . . including

after which there follows a number of specific details sought.

8. In the case of the notice to Perron Investments, a similar attack is made on the request for "a detailed account of the circumstances" which led to the company agreeing to purchase (certain assets) from another entity. It is argued that these types of expression are uncertain or ambiguous and that therefore the whole request should be struck out. In my opinion, there is no merit or substance in the proposition at all. Whatever their form, these notices are in substance seeking information from the directors of the companies concerned and their advisers. These companies are alleged to have set up a number of tax schemes of one kind or another which are suspected of evading or facilitating the evasion of income tax. Reading those parts of the requests under attack in their context, I do not conceive that any difficulty at all would be experienced in handling them all without the slightest embarrassment or difficulty.

9. Another series of criticisms on behalf of the applicants arise out of the use in the notices on a number of occasions of the word "evidence", in the context of documents or acts which are said to "evidence" agreements, negotiations or the handling of monies. It is said that this word can mean either "being evidence of" the particular subject or "constitute" or "manifest" in the sense of being the subject itself. There is substance to the observation that the word can have more than one meaning but the very fact that a word can have different meanings does not portend that its use gives rise to an ambiguity any time it is used. Many words have more than one meaning. The meaning intended in a particular use will be determined by the context and circumstances. On all the occasions where this word has been used in these notices, I think it is perfectly clear what the notices are seeking.

10. A number of questions ask why certain things were done or not done sometimes in the form of seeking the reasons for certain actions or inactions. An example of this is to be found in the notice to Prestige Motors which seeks the reasons why certain units in a unit trust were issued at par followed by the words:

given that the new owners of these units would become

entitled to substantial distributions being covered in any

one year, which, going on past performance of the same

business, was likely to return the owners the cost of the

purchase of the units within a short period of time . . . . .

11. In principle, I can see no objection at all under section 264 to requests for reasons why certain actions were undertaken or things done. Here the applicants say that the question is based on at least two premises, perhaps more, none of which have been established beforehand. It is true that this particular request might have been more felicitously expressed and is a little clumsy, but notices under section 264 cannot be approached as if they were interrogatories or cross examination for which completely different rules apply. I can see no reason for concluding that this question was not asked in good faith and none for concluding that a reasonable company director with knowledge of his company's activities as required under the Companies Code would have any difficulty in understanding what was being asked. The applicants argued that the premises on which this particular request is based may be challenged. If that is so, it would be most unlikely that a successful prosecution for failing to answer could follow a refusal to supply reasons for the particular activity addressed, if the answer was given that the premises on which the question was based were false and there was a frank disclosure of the correct position.

12. Another example of this type was a request for an explanation, in the event that certain identified calculations had not been made, as to why it was not thought necessary to consider a particular matter. This was also said to be ambiguous and uncertain but I do not agree that it can correctly be so described. Yet another group of objections concerns the use in some requests of what were described as "terms of art" meaning that words have been used that appear in one or more statutes. These requests are therefore said to involve two things. One is the need to seek legal advice about what the words mean, and the other is, perhaps alternatively, that the requests require conclusions of law by lay persons. In one particular case, the words concerned include such words as "reimbursed" and "paid". These are words in common usage, albeit that they also appear in relevant legislation. I can see no restriction in section 264 on requests which include the use of words that appear in statutes.

13. Another category of objections concerns requests for such things as "a detailed description" of various activities, sometimes called "full details" or "a detailed account" of some action or document. I have carefully examined the occasions when such expressions have been used but cannot imagine that on any of the occasions in these notices, those expressions would cause the slightest difficulty of comprehension as to what was sought.

14. In my opinion, none of these objections have merit. These submissions are really attacks on the style of questioning of the author of the notices, rather than on any matter of principle. There is in my opinion no reason for concluding that any sensible director or officer of a company applying reasonable intelligence to the matters being sought would have any difficulty in fully complying with what was required and certainly with avoiding successful prosecution under the relevant penal legislation.

15. The respondent concedes that no information or document can be sought which is subject to legal professional privilege. The applicants point to certain requests which they say are in that category and, as I understand the submission, seek an order or declaration invalidating those requests. In my opinion, that application should also be rejected. It is not for me, hearing an application for judicial review, to declare that a certain question or questions which might give rise to a valid claim for legal professional privilege must necessarily do so without my seeing the documents or information concerned and considering the circumstances in which they came into existence. This would be necessary because of the decisions of the High Court in Grant v Downs [1976] HCA 63; (1976) 135 CLR 674; Baker v Campbell [1983] HCA 39; (1983) 153 CLR 52; Arno v Forsyth (1986) 9 FCR 576; and other cases in recent years. They are all referred to in my conclusions of 21 October 1988. These cases establish expressly or by analogy that a request for documents or information which may be the subject of a privilege cannot affect the validity of notices such as these. I so held in my conclusions: see page 45-47. There is nothing invalid about seeking material subject to privilege: all that follows when such material is sought is that the production or furnishing of material cannot be compelled.

16. For all these reasons it is my opinion that the proper order now to be made is that the applications be dismissed with costs.
