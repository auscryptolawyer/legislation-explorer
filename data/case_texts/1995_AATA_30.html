REASONS FOR DECISION

10 February 1995 Deputy President B.M. Forrest

Mr W.G. McLean, Member

Mr B.H. Pascoe, Member



These proceedings concern applications by two applicants, husband and wife, for review of the decisions by the respondent Commissioner of Taxation to disallow objections lodged by the applicants against amended assessments of income tax based on income for the years ended 30 June 1987, 1988 and 1989. As the issues were identical for both applicants the applications were, by consent, heard together.

The applicants, referred to hereafter as Mr and Mrs H, carry on a business of clothing manufacturing in partnership. The business essentially consists of sewing items of clothing to the specifications requested by customers. Normally the material is provided by the customer, sometimes cut out ready for sewing and sometimes the partnership is required to do the cutting. The evidence of the applicants was that a number of subcontractors are engaged to do the sewing.

An audit of the applicants’ affairs was undertaken by officers of the respondent which resulted in the issue of amended assessments of income tax for each of the three years. The amendments were based upon the “T” account method or excess expenditure method which disclosed substantial amounts of alleged understated income.

The bulk of the understated income was reconciled by the auditors to cheques drawn on the partnership business account and the proceeds deposited into the personal bank accounts of the applicants.

Ms J. Batrouney of counsel appeared for the applicants and Mr T. Murphy of counsel for the respondent. Evidence was given by Mr and Mrs H and by Mr Long Viet Nguyen who has been President of the Vietnamese Community in Victoria since June, 1993. In essence the contention of the applicants was that the alleged understatement of income represented money from family members in Vietnam brought to Australia by various couriers. As this money was always received in cash, it was regularly used to pay subcontractors and equivalent amounts were drawn from the partnership to reimburse the private funds so expended.

The evidence of Mr H was that he left Vietnam in 1983 with his wife and two of his seven children. After arrival in Malaysia they were assessed as refugees and flown to Melbourne. On arrival he arranged for the remaining five children to migrate to Australia. After gaining permanent residency status in 1985 he attempted to sponsor his parents and three younger brothers. Approval was given for the parents to migrate to Australia in 1987. His father died in 1990 prior to migrating and his mother came to Australia in 1991. He stated that his father had been a successful clothing manufacturer and his mother had operated a seafood business in Vietnam. After he arrived in Australia, his parents embarked on a program of realising assets and converting the proceeds into United States (US) dollars. His father would then seek out people who were travelling from Vietnam to Melbourne or Sydney and ask them to act as couriers to deliver money to him. The usual arrangement was that his father would negotiate a commission to be retained by the courier, about 5%, and notify Mr H by way of coded telephone or telegraph message of the amount of money coming. To reduce risk of loss, the maximum amount at any one time would have been approximately A$10,000. The couriers would convert the US dollars into Australian dollars, retain their commission and make contact by telephone with him to arrange delivery. He did not know the names or addresses of any of the couriers and had no further contact with them. On one occasion only he thought that he recognised a courier subsequently in a market. The purpose stated for the transfer of funds by this method was to enable Mr H to purchase a house for his parents near his own home. He established a bank account in his own name in which to deposit the funds received. However, as many of his subcontractors required cash, on many occasions, rather than depositing the money received from couriers into the bank account, he used that money to pay the subcontractors. He would then seek to rectify using his parents money to pay business expenses by drawing cheques on the partnership bank account and depositing those cheques into the separate bank account in his name. He estimated that some A$180,000 to A$190,000 was transferred from Vietnam during the relevant period.

During cross examination Mr H stated that it was possible that on some occasions a courier would bring in diamonds or gold rather than US dollars, sell the article on arrival in Australia and deliver the proceeds less commission to him. He agreed that sometimes the amount delivered was less than that notified by his father but that he trusted the couriers and, if they had kept back more than the prearranged commission to the extent of, say, $1,000, that did not concern him. He insisted that each amount advised by his father was expressed in Australian dollars.

The evidence of Mrs H added very little. Only her husband dealt with couriers and arranged cash payments to subcontractors. She was aware of a number of occasions when couriers visited her husband but she did not deal directly with them nor was she present when any money was handed over. She understood that money had come from her parents-in-law but knew no details. Sometimes she arranged payment by cheque to subcontractors against invoices but had no dealings in cash.

Mr Long Viet Nguyen had no direct knowledge of the circumstances relating to the applicants. His evidence was that arrangements such as that alleged by the applicants were and are entered into as a means of transferring funds out of Vietnam. His knowledge of such arrangements was based on what he had been told rather than by any direct personal involvement. He stated that the Vietnamese culture depends heavily on trust and people are prepared to place their trust in virtual strangers. He stated that, in the majority of cases, the courier would be known to the provider of the funds or there would be some knowledge of his family or associates. He understood that, if the courier did not arrive or did not deliver the amount of money anticipated then this information would be relayed back to the Vietnamese resident “who would make contact with the courier’s relatives still living in Vietnam and any difficulties would be satisfactorily resolved”. The evidence of this witness as to the arrangements for the transfer of money was based on hearsay and was of little, if any assistance to the Tribunal.

The Tribunal was urged by counsel for the applicants to accept Mr Nguyen as an expert witness in relation to the culture underlying the alleged transactions and to take into account that culture in assessing the evidence. Whilst we do not have any great difficulty in acceding to that request, we do not see that it provides any great assistance to the applicants’ case. The question to be determined is whether, on the balance of probabilities, the otherwise unsupported evidence of Mr H is to be believed so as to discharge the onus of proof placed on the applicants by s. 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 as to whether the relevant assessments are excessive.

Unfortunately the evidence of Mr H falls a long way short of discharging this onus of proof. No evidence or any record of funds being brought in by couriers was provided. It was Mr H’s evidence that all the couriers were strangers to him, could not now be located and he was not aware whether they had been known to his father. For the Tribunal to accept the evidence somewhat strains the bounds of credibility. We were asked to accept that the father of Mr H was able to assess accurately and in advance in Vietnam the Australian currency equivalent of the US dollars or valuable commodities to be carried to Australia, converted into Australian currency and delivered, after the deduction of 5%, to Mr H in round thousands of dollars. It was not explained why it was necessary for the courier, who in most cases, was said to be a new immigrant to Australia, to convert the currency or commodities to Australian dollars prior to delivery. The apparent ready acceptance by Mr H of occasional shortfalls in expected deliveries is not easy to understand. No evidence was produced to substantiate the contention that much of the money alleged to have come from Vietnam was used to pay subcontractors in cash. No subcontractors were called to give evidence and no documentary evidence was provided, even though it was said that invoices or receipts were always obtained. Mr H stated that these invoices or receipts were given to his accountant together with other records each year for the preparation of income tax returns. The accountant was not called to give evidence of having prepared the returns based on such records. No evidence was provided to seek to demonstrate that the amounts claimed to be paid to subcontractors were within some industry standard percentage of costs or revenue in the type of business carried on by the partnership. In giving evidence about the various houses purchased and sold by the applicants, Mr H stated that for the final house built in 1990 which ultimately housed himself, his wife, his children and his mother he took $100,000 out of the business. Given that the combined profit of the business as shown in the tax returns lodged for the three years ended 30 June 1989 was $69,614 this again strains credibility.

In her submissions on behalf of the applicant Ms Batrouney urged the Tribunal not to regard the lack of objective evidence as fatal. It was argued that, on the balance of probabilities, the applicants’ explanation for the alleged understatement should be believed. As stated earlier, we are unable to accept the unsupported evidence of the applicant as being so credible as to allow the Tribunal to come to the view urged upon it. Given that the bulk of the alleged understatement was said to be explained by payments to subcontractors, it was further argued that this was an appropriate case for the method of apportionment adopted by Davies J. in Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 5200. That case related to assessments issued as a result of an investigation into the taxpayer’s scrap metal business. The assessments were based on what the Commissioner understood to be the taxpayer’s gross income in each year and did not make allowance for deductions. The taxpayer claimed that the assessable income for each of the relevant years should have been reduced to reflect a profit margin in his business of between 10% and 15%. After considering the evidence His Honour accepted that a dealer in scrap metal would have substantial costs and that there was sufficient evidence that the taxpayer’s taxable income would not have been more than 50% of his identified gross income, notwithstanding that the taxpayer had no records and that his evidence was unreliable. In this case the Tribunal has not been provided with any evidence of reasonable costs of subcontractors so as to allow it to arrive at any apportionment of the total amount of the alleged discrepancy.

A recent summary of the onus of proof provisions in the legislation was provided by Davies J. in Saffron v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 94 ATC 4049 where he said (at pp 4050 and 4051)

“In any review of an objection decision, it is necessary for the Court or the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to keep in mind that s. 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), now s. 14ZZK(H)(i) and s. 14ZZO(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth), provides that the burden of proving that an assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer. In respect of this, Walsh J. said in Krew v. FC of T 71 ATC 4213 at 4216:-

“I think it is clear that the onus of proof in relation to the main issues of fact in the case is on the appellant. Furthermore, it is not sufficient, in my opinion, for the appellant to make it seem probable that the overall discrepancy ... disclosed by the betterment statement is not wholly correct. Each year must be considered separately and it must be shown in respect of a particular year that the challenged assessment was wrong. If it is not proved that the whole of the amount of income which is in dispute has been wrongly included as taxable income, then material must be provided upon which the amount of income which should have been excluded can be ascertained. What I have just stated is subject to the qualification that in some cases it may be enough for a taxpayer to show that the assessment has proceeded upon a wrong basis or upon wrong principles: cf. Henderson v. F.C. of T. (1970) 70 ATC 4016; 44 A.L.J.R. 115.”

In FC of T v. Dalco 90 ATC 4088 at 4093; (1990) 168 C.L.R. 615 at 624-625, Brennan J., with whom Mason C.J., Deane, Dawson, Gaudron and McHugh JJ. expressed agreement, said:-

“The manner in which a taxpayer can discharge that burden varies with the circumstances. If the Commissioner and a taxpayer agree to confine an appeal to a specific point of law or fact on which the amount of the assessment depends, it will suffice for the taxpayer to show that he is entitled to succeed on that point. Absent such a confining of the issues for determination, the Commissioner is entitled to rely upon any deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the amount assessed to uphold the assessment, though the taxpayer is limited to the grounds of his objection. In Gauci v. F.C. of T. 75 ATC 4257; [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 C.L.R. 81, Mason J. said (at ATC p. 4261; C.L.R. p. 89):

‘The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be sustained or supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with sec. 190(b) for its is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.’

That view, expressed in a dissenting judgment, now prevails: Macmine Pty. Ltd. V. F.C. of T. 79 ATC 4133 at pp. 4139, 4146, 4159; (1979) 53 A.L.J.R. 362 at pp. 366, 371, 381: McCormack’s Case [79 ATC 4111; [1979] HCA 18; (1978-1979) 143 C.L.R. 284], at ATC pp. 4121, 4123, 4132; C.L.R. pp. 303, 306, 323.”

However, in proceedings by way of review of an objection decision, the assessment itself is not evidence of the facts upon which the assessment was based. The review authority, the Court or the Tribunal, must come to its own decision on the facts and the issues. Walsh J. said in Krew’s case at 4216:-

“I have a duty to reach my own conclusions on the questions of fact which have to be decided and to give effect to those conclusions. ...I must make my own decisions as to the facts.”

Although the taxpayer may be untruthful and a tax evader, that factor, of itself, is no reason not to undertake a calculation of income tax as contemplated by the Act, if such a calculation can be made. As Walsh J. said in Krew’s case at 4219, “A witness upon whose word one cannot rely may yet give evidence which is true in part”. In that case, although Walsh J. held that he could not accept the appellant himself as a reliable witness, he nevertheless found that several of the facts alleged by the taxpayer were established as a matter of probability and accordingly that four of the amended assessments before the Court should be set aside. Moreover, in the calculation of the taxable incomes of years past, exact proof may not be available. As Burchett J. said in Ma v. FC of T 92 ATC 4373 at 4379:-

“...the making of estimates upon inexact evidence, which is so much a feature of both judicial and administrative decision-making, cannot be uniquely excluded from appeals against betterment assessments.”

If, despite the general untruthfulness of a witness and the lack of records, the Court or the Tribunal nevertheless feels satisfied, as a matter of probability, as to the existence of a relevant fact or factor, effect must be given to that conclusion.”

Given the lack of any objective evidence and the view we have formed as to the lack of credibility of Mr H’s evidence, the Tribunal cannot be satisfied that, as a matter of probability any relevant fact or factor can be given effect in this case so as to reduce the taxable income assessed.

Given the limitations of the evidence on which Ms Batrouney was required to argue the case for the applicants, it was not surprising that she devoted much of her submission to the question of the additional tax imposed under s. 223 of the Act by the relevant amended assessments. In her submission on this question, the Tribunal was urged to find that the failure to discharge the onus of proof that the assessments were excessive did not mean that statements made in the income tax returns lodged were false or misleading in a material particular so as to attract the penalties of s. 223. If this case had been concerned solely with unexplained receipt of funds and the applicants’ explanations of their Vietnamese source then consideration may have been given to remitting additional tax under s. 223. However, the question for consideration here was as much related to the claim for deductibility of alleged payments to subcontractors which were represented by funds transferred from the business account to private accounts. The applicants’ difficulties in providing objective evidence of transfers of funds from Vietnam, given that both the father and mother of Mr H had died, could be understandable. The complete failure to provide any objective evidence as to the alleged cash payments to subcontractors is not. The respondent had levied a culpability penalty under s. 223 at the rate of 45% on the relevant amounts of income tax payable on the omitted income. The Tribunal is not convinced that any further remission of this penalty is warranted.

There was one further matter which was put to the Tribunal as being an error in the assessments based on the year ended 30 June 1987. The respondent had adjusted the income of that year by $2,115 for each of the applicants which was described as an “arithmetic error in calculation of expenses” and was said to be an error by the accountant/tax agent in the addition of expenses in the profit and loss statement. It was said by the applicants that this amount related to the payment of $4,230 as rates and taxes in respect of the business premises which had been inadvertently omitted as a business expense. No evidence was provided of the payment or the details of any reconciliation to accounting records. The Tribunal was left with a statement by Mr H that the applicants were each entitled to a deduction for one-half of the alleged payment. Again, with no evidence, including that of the accountant who prepared the returns, we are not satisfied that the applicants have discharged the onus of proving that the relevant assessments were excessive.

For these reasons the decisions under review will be affirmed.
