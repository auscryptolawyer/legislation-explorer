Re Taxation Appeals [1988] AATA 92 (18 March 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. ST86/801 and ST86/802

AAT No. 4218

Taxation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

R.A. Layton (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - Jurisdiction - whether applications dismissed pursuant to sub-s.42A(2) of A.A.T. Act 1975 could be reinstated in the list of matters for hearing by the Tribunal.

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, ss. 33(2), 37(1A), 41(2), 41(3), 42A(2), 43, 43(4), 43(5A)

Onslow v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (1890) 25 QBD 465

Holtby v Hodgson (1889) 24 QBD 103

In re Harrison's Share Under a Settlement (1955) 1 Ch. 260

Re Saint Nazaire Co. (1879) 12 Ch.D 88

Bailey v Marinoff [1971] HCA 49; (1971) 125 CLR 529

Re Nolan and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs V.87/205, delivered 29 April 1987 (Decision 3557)

HEARING

ADELAIDE

18:3:1988

ORDER

The Tribunal has no jurisdiction to reinstate these applications in the list of matters for hearing by the Tribunal.

DECISION

This matter came on by way of a directions hearing to consider an application made by the applicant for both applications to be reinstated following their dismissal on 16 December 1987 pursuant to sub-s.42A(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the Act") due to non-appearance by or on behalf of the applicant.

2. The directions hearing was first listed on 2 February 1988 but was adjourned to enable legal argument to be adduced as to whether this Tribunal had jurisdiction to reinstate the matters in the circumstances of their dismissal. At the resumed hearing on 16 February 1988, the applicant was represented by counsel, Mr Berveling and the respondent was represented by Mr Fisher.

3. The facts which gave rise to this application for reinstatement are as follows.

4. By letter dated 13 January 1986 the Registry advised the parties of a callover of the matters which was listed for 16 February 1987.

5. By letter dated 10 February 1987, the applicant's agent requested that the matters be adjourned from that callover to a later date.

6. At the callover on 16 February 1987, there was no attendance for or on behalf of the applicant.

7. On 19 February 1987, a letter was sent by the Registry to the applicant's agent advising that a preliminary conferencewould be held in relation to both matters on 1 December 1987.

8. On 1 December 1987, there was no attendance for or on behalf of the applicant at the preliminary conference and at that time the member conducting the conference telephoned the applicant's agent. The agent was not available; a message was left but the agent did not return the call. As a result of that non-attendance, the Registry sent a further letter dated 7 December 1987 to the applicant's agent advising that a directions hearing would be conducted on 16 December 1987, and the letter included a notation that failure to attend that directions hearing may result in the matters being dismissed.

9. On 9 December 1987, the applicant instructed solicitors to act on her behalf and toattend the directions hearing on 16 December 1987. An affidavit of the solicitor tendered at the directions hearing on 16 February 1988 deposed that an error was made in the diary entry whereby the directions hearing had been noted as being at 10.00 am on 17 December 1987 rather than at 10.00 am on 16 December 1987. I accept that this error occurred.

10. As a consequence, on 16 December 1987, there was no attendance by or on behalf of the applicant at the directions hearing and the member presiding therefore dismissed both matters for non-attendance of the applicant pursuant to sub-s.42A(2) of the Act which is expressed in the following terms:

"42A. (2) If a party to a proceeding before the

Tribunal in respect of an application for the review of

a decision (not being the person who made the decision)

fails either to appear in person or to appear by a

representative at a preliminary conference held in

relation to the application under section 34 or at the

hearing of the proceeding, the Tribunal may -

(a) where the only other party to the proceeding is

the person who made the decision - dismiss the

application without proceeding to review the

decision; or

(b) in any other case - direct that the person who

failed to appear shall cease to be a party to the

proceeding."

11. On that same day, a formal direction was drawn up and signed by the member in the following terms:

"The applicant in this application for review having

failed to appear in person or to appear by a

representative at the conference held on 1 December 1987 and the

directions hearing held on 16 December 1987 respectively, the

Tribunal, pursuant to section 42A(2) of the Administrative Appeals

Tribunal Act 1975, dismisses the application for review herein

without proceeding to review the decision under review."

A copy of that direction was forwarded to the applicant's agent and the respondent in a letter from the Registry dated 16 December 1987.

12. The Tribunal accepts that at some time during the afternoon of 16 December 1987 the applicant's solicitor telephoned the Registry of the Tribunal to ascertain what had occurred at the directions hearing and was informed that the matters had been dismissed.

13. By letter dated 17 December 1987, the applicant's solicitor made the present application for reinstatement of the matters.

14. At the hearing, counsel for the applicant, Mr Berveling, sought to argue not only that the application should be reinstated but, in the alternative, if such application were to be unsuccessful, that the applicant should be given an extension of time in which to file a fresh application. With regard to this alternative claim, counsel for the respondent, Mr Fisher, submitted that only the first matter should be dealt with. I agree with this submission and consider it premature and inappropriate to deal with any application for extension of time to file a "fresh application" as there are a number of options which the applicant may wish to consider if the present application is unsuccessful.

15. As to the reinstatement of the actions, Mr Berveling submitted that the dismissal of the matters pursuant to sub-s.42A(2) of the Act did not constitute a dismissal on the merits; it was not a dismissal for failure to attend a review hearing but was a dismissal for failure to comply with attendances at two procedural hearings, a preliminary conference and a directions hearing. It was also argued that the "order" for dismissal had not been perfected and that the Tribunal had inherent power to vary the "order" and to reinstate the matters in the interests of natural justice.

16. Mr Fisher, on behalf of the respondent, submitted that the dismissal constituted a finalisation of the matters and that therefore this Tribunal had no jurisdiction to entertain the present application.

17. In considering these submissions, I turn firstly to consider whether or not the dismissal of the application has been perfected. The Act gives power to the Tribunal to make a "decision" upon a review (sub-s.43(1) of the Act). It also gives power to the Tribunal to make "directions" from time to time (sub-s.33(2) of the Act). In addition, the Tribunal has power to make "orders" (for example, sub-s.37(1A) to shorten the time for lodging s.37 documents; sub-s.41(2), relating to an order to stay the implementation of a decision; sub-s.41(3) relating to a request for an order varying or revoking a stay order). The words "direction" and "order" would appear to be separately used in the Act but both appear to be distinguished from the word "decision" used in s.43 of the Act. To this extent, the words "direction" and "order" would appear to be comparable to the use of the word "order" in common law jurisdictions which, in turn, is to be distinguished from the word "judgement" at common law which is comparable to the word "decision" in the Tribunal. As Lord Esher, Master of the Rolls, in Onslow v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (1890) 25 QBD 465 said:

"A judgement is a decision obtained in an action and

every other decision is an order"

18. Sub-section 43(5A) of the Act provides what is in effect the common law position with regard to judgements, namely that a decision of the Tribunal comes into operation forthwith upon the giving of the decision, unless a later date is specified. There is no similar statutory provision in the Act with regard to the coming into operation of an order or direction but in my view the common law position would apply and any order or direction would operate from the time when the direction or order is given.

19. Sub-section 43(4) of the Act provides that proof of a decision or order, prima facie, consists of a document purporting to be a copy of "such a decision or order . . . certified by the Registrar or Deputy Registrar to be a true copy of the decision or order".

20. At common law, a judgement takes effect from the time it is pronounced and the entry of it is mere form in obedience to the direction (Holtby v Hodgson (1889) 24 QBD 103). In my view, the same would apply so far as a decision, direction or order of this Tribunal is concerned.

21. A court of superior jurisdiction has inherent jurisdiction to alter, review or vary an order so long as it has not been perfected or entered (Re Harrison's Shares Under a Settlement (1955) 1 Ch 260; Re Saint Nazaire Co. (1879) 12 ChD 88). It is doubtful whether this Tribunal, as an administrative Tribunal and a creature of statute, has such similar inherent jurisdiction to vary, alter or review an order which had not been entered or perfected, however, be that as it may, in these matters the direction to dismiss the applications has been drawn up and copies of that direction have been provided to the applicant.

22. Further, courts of superior jurisdiction do not have inherent power to change an order after it has been drawn up and perfected. As Barwick, CJ. stated in Bailey v Marinoff [1971] HCA 49; (1971) 125 CLR 529 at 530-531:

"Once an order disposing of a proceeding has been

perfected by being drawn up as the record of a court,

that proceeding apart from any specific and relevant

statutory provision is at an end in that court and is

in its substance, in my opinion, beyond recall by that

court. It would, in my opinion, not promote the due

administration of the law or the promotion of justice

for a court to have a power to reinstate a proceeding

of which it has finally disposed. In my opinion, none

of the decided cases lend support to the view that the

Supreme Court in this case had any inherent power or

jurisdiction to make the order it did make, its

earlier order dismissing the appeal having been

perfected by the processes of the Court. I would add

that, however hard the case might seem for the would

be appellant the loss of its right of appeal derived

from its own conduct or from that of persons for whom

it must take responsibility. The finality of the

order dismissing the appeal does not seem to me to

partake of injustice in the circumstances or to call

for any departure from well settled principles,

themselves essential in my opinion to the due

administration of our system of law."

23. Similar observations were made in that case by Walsh, J. at p.535:

"The Court referred to dicta in some of the more

modern cases suggesting that the courts have an

inherent jurisdiction to vary their own orders, in the

interests of justice, to an extent which has not been

and should not be limited by strict definitions.

These observations were regarded as supporting the

conclusion to which the Court of Appeal came. Then

certain earlier decisions and statements of principle,

which appeared to deny any power in the Court to

review its own orders (leaving aside special cases in

which the slip rule could be applied or in which an

order as drawn up failed to express accurately the

intention of the Court), were distinguished upon

various grounds. I think it may fairly be said, in

relation to the judgment of the Court of Appeal, that

none of the cases to which their Honours referred was

a decision which clearly supported their conclusion

and, secondly, that even if each of the decisions

which denied the existence of power in a court to

review its own orders could be distinguished from the

case now under consideration, nevertheless there would

be a substantial body of judicial opinion tending

against the view that the inherent jurisdiction of the

Court enables it to revive by a subsequent order an

appeal which has been dismissed."

24. If a court of superior jurisdiction does not have inherent jurisdiction to review orders or judgements which have been perfected, then I cannot see how this Tribunal could be imbued with such inherent jurisdiction.

25. It is to be noted that the drawn up document has the heading "Direction". There is no mention made in para. 42A(2)(a) of the Act as to whether such dismissal should be made by direction, order or decision, although in the following para. 42A(2)(b), the Tribunal is given power to "direct" that a person cease to be a party. Further, there is no indication in the Act that any particular application or hearing is required before a dismissal is made. The only prerequisite appears to be the failure to attend a preliminary conference or hearing. Bearing in mind that the Tribunal has power to give a direction as to the procedure to be followed under s.33 of the Act, the dismissal of an application or the striking out of a party would appear to be exercising a somewhat different function from the giving of a direction as to procedural matters. Further, a direction may be varied or revoked at any time (sub-s,33(3)).

26. A dismissal of an action affects the substantive rights of the parties and would not normally come within the usual meaning of interlocutory directions. Therefore, it would appear to be inappropriate for a dismissal to be called a "direction" as if it were an exercise of power under s.33 of the Act, but more appropriate to be called an "order".

27. It is clear, however, that a dismissal is not a "decision" made pursuant to s.43 of the Act which may only come into play at a review hearing after the exercise of the powers referred to in that section (see Re Nolan and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs V.87/205, 29 April 1987 (3557)).

28. To summarise this point, in hindsight, it would have been preferable to have expressed the drawn up dismissal document as being an "order" as it is well recognised that orders may affect the substantive rights between parties which distinguish them from from procedural "directions" given under s.33 of the Act. Be that as it may, the terminology "direction" does not invalidate the perfection of the document drawing up the dismissal and, in my view, the order has been entered and perfected and this Tribunal has no jurisdiction to vary that dismissal order.

29. In addition, there is no doubt that a dismissal of an action at common law must be an end to that action unless there is power to set aside such a judgement (as exists in various Supreme Court and Local Court Acts). Such a statutory provision does not exist in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act (supra) and, in the absence of such provision, there is no power to set aside a dismissal. An order for dismissal, therefore, is in contrast to the specific provisions which allow the varying or revoking of an order as contained in sub-s.41(3) of the Act and also the varying or revoking of a direction made pursuant to sub-s.33(3).

30. In these circumstances, I find that this Tribunal has no jurisdiction to entertain any application for reinstatement of the matters by either varying or setting aside the dismissal orders made on 16 December 1987.

31. It remains for another day as to the effect of a dismissal made pursuant to para.42A(2)(a) of the Act. It is to be noted that at common law a distinction has been drawn between the effects of a dismissal for non-appearance at the trial of proceedings which, as Esher M.R. said in Armour v Bate (1891) 2 QB 233 at 235 was "the same as if the action had been dismissed on the merits", and a dismissal for default of pleading or noncompliance with an order of a court. A dismissal in the latter circumstances has not been regarded as a bar to the commencement of a fresh action and that (with the possible exception of a dismissal by consent) the doctrine of res judicata does not apply (see Magnus v National Bank of Scotland (1888) 57 LJ Ch. 902; Pople v Evans (1969) 2 Ch. 255; Hart v Hall and Pickles Ltd (1969) 1 QB 405 at 411; Kinch v Walcott (1929) AC 482).

32. Deputy President Thompson has expressed the view in Re Nolan (supra) that a fresh application could be made if there was a dismissal for non-appearance at a hearing. Senior Member Ballard also reached a similar conclusion in Re Morgan and Department of Social Security 10 ALN N187. In view of the decisions referred to above, I have doubts as to the accuracy of the conclusions which my fellow Tribunal members have reached. The further complication is that these matters come before the Tribunal pursuant to the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 with its own set of provisions regarding reference of objections to this Tribunal.

33. The question which may arise at some future time is whether a dismissal for failure to attend either a preliminary conference or a directions hearing may be treated differently from the failure to attend at a review hearing and, in the further alternative, whether a dismissal for failure to attend a review hearing should be treated as if the action had been dismissed on the merits bearing in mind the provisions of the Act in sub-s.42A(2) which specifically provide for a dismissal "without proceeding to review the decision".

34. I make no further comment other than to allude to these difficulties in view of the fact that this matter is not before me, and find that this Tribunal has no jurisdiction to reinstate the applications.
