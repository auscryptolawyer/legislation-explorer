TT93/29 and Commissioner of Taxation [1994] AATA 571; (1994) 30 ATR 1001; 95 ATC 126 (13 July 1994)

Last Updated: 15 May 2009

INCOME TAX - Allowable deductions - Partnership involved in land development, subdivision and sale - Whether expenditure on legal costs and damages arising in relation to breach of contract under which purchased land to be developed etc. is deductible under sub-ss 51(1) and/or 52(1) -Whether appropriate case to dispense with need for "s 52 notice" - Relationship between ss 25(1) and 26(a) - When were expenditures "incurred" - Whether required nexus existed between expenditures and partnership business in circumstances where that business ceased years before expenditures made - Whether expenditures of a capital nature - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss 25(1), 26(a), 51(1), 52(1) and 64A

DEPUTY PRESIDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. The applicant ("A") in this reference has participated for many years in subdivisional land developments in North Queensland as a member of the ABC partnership ("P") which consisted of in addition to A, Messrs B and C; A and B each holding a 40% interest, whilst C held the remaining 20%. A invariably returned - and was assessed on - the profits from his sub-divisional activities year by year, as well being allowed the occasional loss. C took care of the financial and legal aspects whilst A assumed responsibility for the technical and engineering part. In cross-examination. A could not recall how the partnership was formalised; it appears that it was not reduced to writing, nor did it have a bank account. Be that as it may, A, who "did not handle any of the money involved in the project", struck me as an extremely careful and truthful witness, whose evidence I accept without hesitation, and who has left me in no doubt that, at all relevant times, there was indeed a partnership relating to the particular subdivision forming the backdrop against which the events relating to this reference occurred. The sequence of events has been slightly altered to avoid identifying the taxpayer.

  1. The tactual backdrop relevant to this application began in 1974, when one Richard Roe acquired some 75 hectares in North Queensland south of Mossman, (henceforth referred to as "Blackacre") with the intention of subdividing, developing and selling Blackacre in separate allotments. A local town planner had drawn up a plan of subdivision ("the Roe plan"), which was said to comply with all Mossman Council requirements. In the events that occurred, Roe foiled to proceed with the plan and, instead, sold Blackacre (including the Roe plan) to P in 1981. A deposed that he and his partners acquired the land with the intention of subdividing and selling Blackacre in accordance with the Roe plan. The contract of sale nominated A as the purchaser on behalf of P. Clause 11 of the said contract provided that: "The purchaser shall forthwith proceed to obtain subdivisional approval of the said land from the Mossman Shire Council in accordance with the plan of subdivision set out in Schedule 1 of this Agreement and after obtaining said approval the purchaser shall forthwith reconvey to the vendor some 6.5 hectares of the said land marked in red in Schedule 1 and identified on the said land by four survey pegs, which 6.5 hectares are hereby expressly excluded from this sale". (The 6.5 hectares contained some absolute beachfront).
  2. For reasons which need not concern me, after obtaining approval of the Roe plan of subdivision from the Council, P merely sold one block to a third party, after which it made no attempts to proceed with further sales. In the result, the mortgagee of Blackacre sold the remaining land to a third party in June 1984, including the 6.5 hectares which P was required to reconvey to Roe under the contract.
  3. After some fruitless negotiations for compensation, Roe instituted proceedings for damages for breach of contract. A, who defended the proceedings on behalf of the partnership, was ultimately unsuccessful in his defence and judgment was entered against him in the amount of $23,000, being the agreed value of the land, together with costs of the action.
  4. I am satisfied on the evidence that A:

(i) was aware from the time of the purchase of Blackacre of the partnership's obligation to reconvey the 6.5 hectares to Roe and that a failure to so reconvey the land constituted a breach of contract:

(ii) sometime prior to the hearing (the evidence on this aspect not being specific) had quantified in his mind that the amount of that 'liability" was in the order of $25,000 (only $2,000 more than the amount subsequently awarded) and was prepared to pay that amount into court. Indeed, it was only as a result of his legal adviser, who considered the amount too high, that he paid a mere $13,000 into court. Alas, his judgment was sounder than that of his adviser.

6. To complete the factual narrative, P's Trading, Profit and Loss Statement for the year ended 30 June 1983 (part of exh B) claimed expenses of some $13,358, made up, in the main, of such items as land tax. interest, accountancy, etc, resulting in a net loss for the year of some $9,705.

  1. In January 1985, a return was lodged on behalf of P for the year of income ended 30 June 1984, which was endorsed as a "FINAL RETURN", adding "ALL PROPERTY NOW SOLD". The sale by the mortgagee of the remaining portion of Blackacre was disclosed, and a net loss of some $78,000 was claimed. Contrary to a submission by counsel for the Commissioner that the partnership subsisted until the time of the judgment in 1989, I am satisfied on the evidence that P had ceased business sometime in the 1984 year of income, holding no other land and not being otherwise engaged in any form of business.
  2. Thereafter nothing much of relevance occurred until July 1989, when a return was lodged on behalf of P for the year of income ended 30 June 1989, P describing itself as a "Property Developer" and asserting a loss of $40,854, distributed as follows:

A 40% (16342)

B 40% (16342)

C 20% (8170)

9. Attached to the abovementioned 1989 return was a "Request for Commissioner's

Interpretation of the Law' which stated:

From 1978 to 30th June. 1984, the partnership derived assessable income as a property developer. Notwithstanding that a final return for the partnership was lodged the year ended 30th June, 1984, the partnership settled a legal claim on the 28th February 1989 amounting to $40,554 and which related to the partnership's undertaking in previous years.

The details of this claim are:

Court Ordered Settlement 23,000

Legal costs defending the claim

Costs of barrister 17,329

- Legal costs awarded against

the partnership

Photocopying and miscellaneous 225

$40,554

As the partnership unsuccessfully defended this action, the partnership now claims the outgoing of $40,554 as a loss on outgoing deductible under the provisions of Section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Broadly, the partnership believes that the expenses at $40,554 are losses or outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income.

10. Some eight months later (March 1990), A lodged his return for the 1989 tax year, claiming a deduction of $16,342, being his share of the $40,854 loss claimed by P (which consisted of the claimed $40,554 set out in the previous paragraph and an additional amount of $300 for "income tax return preparation expenses''). In that return he advised:

The 1989 taxable loss sustained by P in relation to the Mossman land is subject to a S.169A Election. The taxpayer's share of the loss [40%] has been included in his taxable income but is subject to the outcome of the election when assessed.

(The evidence disclosed that A received a 40% contribution towards the judgment and costs from B. I have no evidence whether C contributed his share of that liability.)

11. On 8 October 1990, the respondent issued A with a Notice of Assessment with respect to the 1989 tax year, which disallowed the claimed deduction of $16,342. The explanatory document attached to that Notice stated:

No deduction is allowable for the damages settlement as the company is no longer carrying on a business of property development.

There is, in the words of Dixon J, (Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v FCT

[1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 [at 301]) an "entire lack of connexion between the assessable income and [the] expenditure".

12. A objected against the assessment on 6 December 1990, claiming, inter alia, that the loss was an allowable deduction to P under sub-s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), being incurred in gaining or producing P's assessable income; alternatively, it was a loss or outgoing necessarily incurred in carrying on P's business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. In the alternative, it was claimed:

(be) Notwithstanding the abovementioned - and those grounds are not conceded - and given the loss or outgoing amounting to $40,854 consists of two components, namely Damages Settlement and associated costs and the preparation of the 1988 income tax return, which respectively amounted to $40,554 and $300, the partnership is entitled to a portion of the legal expenses claim under the provisions of section 64A of the Act. Further, the proportion of income tax return fees are, alternatively, deductible under the provisions of section 69 of the Act.

13. By letter dated 15 July 1991, the respondent advised A that his objection had been partially allowed and continued:

To be deductible under section 51(1), an expense is allowable to the extent to which it was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or was necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income.

The claim for [damages] and legal expenses fails the first part of section 51(1) as there has been no income received from that partnership since 1984. Accordingly, it cannot be said that the expense was incurred in the gaining or producing of assessable income.

As the business has ceased several years prior to the expense, the claim also fails the second part of section 51(1) as there was no carrying on of a business for the purpose of gaming or producing of assessable income. It has been established that a claim for deductibility under section 51(1) cannot succeed where there is a complete cessation of that business. The expenditure incurred is so remote that the requisite connection between the claimed expenditure and the earlier operations cannot be established.

In any case, claims for expenses incurred in disputing ownership to a title would be considered to be capital in nature as the expense is not incurred in producing profits, but rather in disputing ownership. Accordingly these expenses cannot be allowed.

To be deductible under section 64A, legal expenses can be claimed up to a maximum of $50 if they are incurred in the course of carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income. As the partnership has ceased, with no further income, this claim fails to be allowable under this section.

A tax deduction is allowable under section 69 for the costs of having a tax return prepared by a registered tax agent. Accordingly an amended assessment will issue to allow your share of these costs ($120).

  1. Being dissatisfied with that objection decision, by letter dated 23 Jury 1991, A requested the respondent to refer the matter to this Tribunal for review. The respondent complied with that request on 3 September 1993.
  2. At the hearing of this application, A was granted leave to amend the Notice of Objection by inserting after paragraph (ix) the following:

(x) The aforesaid loss incurred by the partnership was a loss incurred from the carrying on or carrying out of an undertaking or scheme the profit (if any) from which undertaking or scheme would have been included in its assessable income; and

(xi) The said loss flowed directly from the carrying out of such an undertaking or scheme:

the obligations giving rise to the liability were incurred during the carrying out of the undertaking or scheme:

they were obligations incurred only for the purposes of promoting the profitability of the undertaking or scheme;

the obligations were found by the Supreme Court of Queensland to have given rise to a cause of action against the partnership during the year of income ended 30 June 1976;

the obligations were only satisfied during the year of income ended 30 June 1989;

which outgoings increased the loss directly arising from the carrying out of the undertaking or scheme; and

which was not allowable as a loss incurred in any earlier year or in any year other than the year of income ended 30 June 1989.

16. The primary submission made on behalf of A, based upon his grounds of objection as

amended, was that the claimed amount was deductible under sub-s 52(1) of the Act, which

provides:

Any loss incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income upon the sale of any property or from the carrying on or carrying out of any undertaking or scheme, the profit (if any) from which sale, undertaking or scheme would have been included in his assessable income, shall be an allowable deduction:

Provided that, in respect of property acquired by the taxpayer after the date of the commencement of this proviso, no deduction shall be allowable under this section (except where the Commissioner, being satisfied that the property was acquired by the taxpayer for the purpose of profit-making by sale or for the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme, otherwise directs) unless the taxpayer, not later than the date upon which he lodges his first return under this Act after having acquired the property, notifies the Commissioner that the property has been acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale or for the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme.

17. The initial hurdle A faces in relation to that submission is the proviso in sub-s 52( 1) that no deduction is allowable under the section unless the taxpayer gives the respondent the relevant notice. His counsel submitted that "it is clear from the outset, from the time the first return was lodged, [the partnership] had given notice of the acquisition of the land in terms consistent only with development with a view to a profit." I am, however, satisfied that the various statements contained in the partnership's returns for the relevant years, particularly the various profit and loss statements (which became part of exhibit B) which were included in those returns, do not fulfil the notice requirement of sub-s 52(1). However, that is not the end of the matter. As A's counsel rightly pointed out, notwithstanding the lack of relevant notice to the respondent, the Tribunal, standing "in the shoes of the respondent", may still direct that sub-s 52(1) may apply if to quote from the words in brackets in the proviso in sub-s 52(1), it is "satisfied that the property was acquired by the taxpayer [partnership] for the purpose of profit-making by sale or for the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme". Counsel for the Commissioner advised the Tribunal during his address that the respondent's position on this aspect was that "we make no submissions as to whether you [the Tribunal] should make a direction under that proviso'".

  1. Being satisfied on the evidence that P did acquire the property for the carrying on or carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme which was to involve subdivision, development and sale of Blackacre, the taxpayer is thus reprieved from the failure to give the proper notice.
  2. Having come to that conclusion, it is necessary to determine whether in the circumstances the "basic words" of sub-s 52( 1) (as SmitheTS J in FCT v Werchon 82 ATC 4332 at 4335 referred to the words of sub-s 52( 1) which precede the proviso) are satisfied. Firstly, I accept the submission made on behalf of the respondent that on the tacts, the first limb of sub-s 52( 1) cannot apply in these circumstances, that is, the claimed amount cannot be "a loss incurred by the taxpayer [partnership] ... upon the sale of any property ..." since the loss arose upon the judgment; not upon the sale of the property. On the other hand, I accept the applicant's submission that the second limb of sub-s 52(1) applies in the circumstances, that is, the claimed amount is "a loss incurred by the taxpayer [partnership]... from the carrying on or carrying out of any undertaking or scheme ...". In response to that submission, learned counsel for the respondent pointed to the fact established in Werchon and confirmed in Ailesch & Anor v FCT 86 ATC 4043, which noted (at 4045) that:

a deduction pursuant to sec. 52 in relation to a loss incurred in carrying on or carrying out a profit-making scheme is, of course, available only where, had the loss been a profit, that profit would have been assessable pursuant to sec. 26(a) [later replaced by s 25A]

and submitted that any such profit would have been assessable under sub-s 25(1), not s 26(a) and therefore sub-s 52(1) could not apply, adding that "it [the partnership] was accepted by the Commissioner of Taxation as carrying on the business as a property developer". I accept that what P did amounted to the carrying on of a business of land development, subdivision and sale, albeit was largely unsuccessful, but I do not accept that this necessarily excludes the operation of sub-s 52(1). True it is that Gibbs CJ and Mason J expressed the view in FCT v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd 82 ATC 4031, at 4037 and 4046 respectively, that the second limb of s 26(a) applied only to "profits not attributable to gross income that has already been captured by sec. 25", whereas Wilson J (at 4058) did "not regard its [s 26(a)] construction as settled by existing authority." However, in a joint judgment in FCT v Myer Emporium 87 ATC 4363, the Full Court of the High Court stated (at 4372):

What we have said leads to the conclusion that the amount in question formed part of the income of Myer under sec. 25(1) of the Act. A similar chain of reasoning would have led to the conclusion that the amount constituted assessable income under the second limb of sec. 26(a). The relationship between sec. 25(1) and 26(a) has been the subject of much previous discussion involving considerable differences of opinion about the extent (if any) to which the provision of the second limb of sec. 26(a) supplemented the provision of sec. 25(1) (see eg., Whitfords Beach, at ATC pp. 4044-4045; CLE. pp. 379-381). It is, however, unnecessary that we examine that question here since, as we have indicated, we consider that the amount in question in the present appeal constituted income of the taxpayer pursuant to both sec. 25(1) and 26(a).

  1. It would seem to me that, implicit in that statement is a non-acceptance of the views of Gibbs CJ and Mason J in Whitfords Beach, if only because it is not easy to reconcile those views with the conclusion in Myer that "the amount in question ... constituted income of the taxpayer pursuant to both sec. 25(1) and 26(a)". (my emphasis).
  2. In the alternative, it was submitted on behalf of the respondent that if s 52 could apply, it did not in the instant case because the requirement that the loss be incurred 'from the carrying on or carrying out of the relevant undertaking or scheme" imported a requirement that there be "a nexus between the undertaking or scheme and the loss similar to the question that arises in relation to section 51" and, submitted learned counsel, no such nexus existed "because the scheme had come to an end in 1984 at the latest, when the land was finally disposed o£ and a judgment debt some five years later did not arise from the carrying on of the scheme or undertaking."
  3. Whilst I accept that the presence of the word "from" requires some nexus between the incurring of the loss and the carrying on or carrying out of the relevant undertaking or scheme. 1 am satisfied that the judgment debt liability - which arose as a result of a breach of a contract the entry into which was a fundamental part of that undertaking or scheme - can properly be said to be a loss incurred in the 1989 year of income from the carrying on or carrying out of the relevant undertaking or scheme.
  4. Having concluded that the claimed amount is deductible to P pursuant to sub-s 52(1), with the consequential "flow on" effect on the applicant's taxable income, it is strictly unnecessary for me to consider the second submission made on behalf of A, viz that sub-s 51(1) applies with the same result. However, in deference to the research undertaken by both counsel on this aspect and the fact that this was the principal issue prior to A's amendment of his grounds of objection at the hearing, I consider that it is appropriate for me to deal with this aspect.
  5. In his Reasons for Decision, the respondent virtually repeated what he had stated in his Notice of Partial Allowance, dated 15 Jury 1991 (referred to in paragraph 13 above), viz:

1 (a) The deduction claimed by the partnership for damages and legal expenses paid during the year ended 30 June 1989 falls for consideration under subsection 51(1);

(b) Insofar as it is relevant, subsection 51(1) permits deductions in respect of

losses and outgoings either:

(i) incurred in gaining or producing assessable income; or

(ii) necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income, except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of a capital, private or domestic nature;

(c) The damages and legal expenses do not qualify for deduction under sub section 51(1) because:

(i) they were not incurred in carrying on a business;

(ii) they were not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income: and

(iii) they constituted expenditure of a capital nature.

2(a) Alternatively, part of the deduction claimed by the partnership for legal expenses paid during the year ended 30 June 1989 hills for consideration under section 64A;

(b) Insofar as it is relevant, section 64A permits a $50 maximum deduction for certain legal expenses incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income, except to the extent to which they are expenses of a private or domestic nature;

(c) No part of the legal expenses is allowable as a deduction under section 64A because they were not incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income.

25. It is almost trite to note that sub-s 51(1) provides that:

All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.

26. On the facts, are the outgoings on damages and costs incurred by the partnership an

allowable deduction under subs 51(1)? On somewhat similar facts, Deputy President Nicholson of this Tribunal (now his Honour Mr Justice Nicholson of the Supreme Court of Western Australia) said "No" in Case U177 87 ATC 1020, (a case heavily relied upon by the respondent in this case). The facts are sufficiently set out in the headnote:

In 1970 the taxpayer commenced to carry on a business of structural steel trading as a sole trader. During the conduct of the business in 1974, a crane owned by the taxpayer was involved in an accident which injured another employer's worker. As a result of court action instituted in 1976 by the other employer, the other employer obtained judgment against the taxpayer for $11,855 forworkers' compensation paid to the injured worker up to and including 14 January 1977 and the other employer was held to be entitled to indemnity from the taxpayer for any such payments subsequent to that date. From September 1977 the business previously conducted by the taxpayer was sold to a family company which was liquidated in 1980.

In June 1981, a further judgment was entered in favour of the other employer for $16,865 as a consequence of further proceedings under the former judgment to recover workers' compensation paid after 14 January 1977. Pursuant to an agreement between the taxpayer and the other employer, the taxpayer was paying off the judgment debts by instalments. The instalment payments were made by another family company which charged the amounts to the taxpayer's loan account.

In his returns for the 1982 and 1983 income years, the taxpayer claimed deductions for the instalment payments. The Commissioner disallowed the claims and the taxpayer objected.

After an exhaustive review of the law on the subject, including the decisions of the High Court in Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v FCT mdAGC (Advances) Ltd v FCT (1975) 132 CLR 1175; 75ATC4057, the learned Deputy President stated at 1027-1028:

Examination of the nexus

This applicant is precluded from succeeding in the claim for deductibility pursuant to the second limb of sec. 51(1) because there was a complete cessation of the carrying on of the business.

In examining the existence of the nexus pursuant to the first limb of sec. 51(1), it is apparent on the tacts that the applicant cannot succeed on any argument that the outgoings claimed are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income in the years in which those outgoings were claimed. The only question is whether the outgoings claimed in the 1982 and 1983 years of income are said to have been incurred in gaining or producing assessable income at the time at which the accident causing the injury to "C" arose in 1974. The applicant seeks to establish the nexus therefore with the argument that it was in the nature of a contingent liability during that period. Alternatively it was argued that the issue of Supreme Court proceedings in 1976 prior to the cessation of business in 1977 provided the necessary nexus. The submission is that the judgment in December 1977 after cessation of the business merely quantified the liability which had arisen prior to the cessation of business. In the applicant's submission, this was sufficient to establish that the deductions can be truly said to arise from the day-to-day operations of the business whilst it was being actively conducted {Case R65 84 ATC 468 at p 469).

The Tribunal therefore considers each of the claimed bases for the nexus [between the assessable income and the claimed expenditure]:

(1) The accident in 1974; and

(2) The issue or proceedings in 1976.

In the view of the Tribunal it is not correct to say that the required nexus can be found on the basis that some liability of a contingent inchoate nature existed prior to the cessation of business as a consequence of the occurrence of the accident in 1974 and the issue of proceedings in 1976. It was not until the judgment of the Supreme Court of Western Australia on I December 1977 that a judgment debt was created and liability to repayment arose. While there was a contingency prior to cessation of business, there was no liability then in existence. A "contingent debt" refers to a case where there is a doubt if there will be any debt at all - pevMellish LJ, Ex parte Ruffle 8 Ch. 1001.

The mere contingency cannot provide the nexus between the outgoings and the gaining or production of assessable income in those years. The fact that it was merely a contingency prevents it from being incurred in the requisite manner, because as a contingency it is not truly "inclined": cf. AGC (Advances) (supra) at ATC pp. 4072-4073; CLR pp. 197-198 per Mason J. The word 'Hncurred" in sec. 51(1) does not warrant treating a liability which does not "come home" m the year of income in the sense of a pecuniary obligation which has become due, as having been incurred in that year: Nilsen Laboratories v FCT 78 ATC 4031 at pp 4034-4035, per Barwick CJ. An expenditure which is no more than impending, threatened or expected is not within the meaning of the word "incurred": New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v FCT [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at p 207; FCT v James Flood Pty Ltd (1953)88CLR492atp507.

(3) Judgment in December 1977

The judgment in December 1977 was proximate to the cessation of business in September 1977. Furthermore, a connection does appear between the claimed expenditure and the earlier operations - the judgment debt arose from an event during those operations. It is possible that had a claim been raised in the 1978 or 1979 years of income the applicant may have been entitled to succeed. The position is, however, different because the expenditures are claimed in relation to years of income four and five years later, hi the opinion of the Tribunal, it can be properly said the earlier income-producing activities are so remote in time that the requisite connection between the claimed expenditures and the earlier operations cannot be established. It is not that the applicant's claim is defeated by the law or any prior decision but rather that, as a question of fact, he is unable to establish the nexus required by sec. 51(1) in circumstances where so much time has elapsed between the cessation of income-producing activity and the claimed expenditures.

  1. Learned counsel for the applicant submitted that the approach taken by Deputy President Nicholson in that case was "fundamentally unsound and inconsistent with decided cases" and invited me not to follow it. However, he did not enlighten me as to why it was wrong. Be that as it may, in view of developments in the law since Case U177 was decided, it is necessary to re-examine the law in this area, the more so since the respondent placed such heavy reliance on Case UI77 as well as the decision in Amalgamated Zinc.
  2. It would seem to me that there were two issues relating to the interpretation of sub-s 51( 1) which loomed large in Case U177 and which are central to the resolution of the case now before me, (i) the meaning of 'facurred", and (ii) the required nexus between the loss or outgoing and the assessable income or the business carried on for the purpose of producing such income.
  3. The meaning of "incurred" as used in sub-s 51(1) was recently the subject of considerable discussion in Coles Myer Finance Ltd v FCT 93 ATC 4214, a case not relied upon or cited in the instant case. In the circumstances I do not propose to delve too deeply into the issues raised in the various judgments. Suffice it for present purposes if I merely note that in the joint judgment of Mason CJ, Brennan, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ, their Honours made the following observations with respect to the decisions in James Flood and Nilsen at 4221;

The decisions in Flood and Nilsen proceed on the footing that the Court, in determining entitlement to a deduction under s.51(1), accepted the legal or jurisprudential analysis rather than the commercial view as the correct one. In other words, the Court concluded that the liability was the ordinary liability to pay wages to an employee in respect of a period of employment in preference to the commercial view that the liability was a progressive one, being part of the cost of labour employed from day to day. Subsequently, in Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Ltd v FC of T [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314. Barwick CJ, Kitto and Taylor JJ. said of Flood:

"The Court there held that, while commercial and accountancy practice may assist in ascertaining the true nature and incidence of an item as a step towards determining whether the item answers the test laid down in the Act for allowable deductions, it cannot be substituted for the test."

No doubt as a consequence of the Court's adoption of the legal or jurisprudential analysis in determining entitlements to a deduction under s. 51(1) in preference to the commercial view, the parties did not contend in the present case for a different approach to the question.

30. On this point, his Honour. Deane J expressed what would appear to be a slightly different view (at 4224-5):

It is true that, as was pointed out in Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty Ltd & Ors v FCoft, the courts have, for the purposes of deterrnining whether a liability constitutes a loss or outgoing which has been incurred for the purposes of s. 51(1) of the Act, tended to favour a strict legal analysis of the nature of the relevant liability rather than a pragmatic commercial approach. ... It does not follow from those cases [Flood, Nilsen, and FCT v The Northern Timber and Hardware Co Pty Ltd (I960) [1960] HCA 93; 103 CLR 650], however, that the feet that jurisprudential analysis discloses that a particular liability to make a future payment of money is contingent, necessarily means that such a contingent liability cannot constitute or found a "loss or outgoing" which has been "incurred" for the purposes of s. 51(1). To the contrary, the weight of authority supports the conclusion that, depending upon the circumstances, a liability to pay money can constitute, or give rise to, a 'loss or outgoing" which is "incurred" within the meaning of that sub-section, notwithstanding that the money is not payable until a future time (Flood CLR 492 at 506) and that the obligation to pay it is theoretically defeasible (ibid) or contingent (cf Commercial Union Assurance Co v FCT 11 ATC 4186 at p 4193; Texas Co (Aust) Ltd v FCT [1940] HCA 9; 63 CLR 382) in that it is subject to a condition which remains unfulfilled.

In deterrnining whether a liability to make a future payment represents, for the purpose of ascertaining taxable income on an accruals basis, a loss or outgoing which has been ''incurred" as required by s. 51(1), the critical question is not whether, as a matter of legal analysis, the liability is theoretically contingent or defeasible. It is whether the taxpayer is, as a practical matter, "definitively committed" or "completely subjected" to the obligation to make the payment in the nature even though it has not come under "an immediate obligation enforceable at law" to do so (Flood CLR at 506; Commercial Union Assurance Co of Aust Ltd v FCT11 ATC 4186, at pp 4193-4194). ...

  1. (have come to the conclusion that, applying either of the above views as to the meaning of "incurred". Deputy President Nicholson was correct in concluding in Case 1)177 that, on the facts of that case, the liability there in question was not "incurred" for the purposes of sub-s 51(1) until the time of the judgment against the taxpayer in that case. For like reasons, applying a "strict legal or jurisprudential analysis" in the case before me. I am satisfied that the relevant liability was also not "incurred" for the purposes of sub-s 51(1) until the time of the judgment against A (on behalf of the partnership). Therefore, the claimed expenditure was 'Incurred" in 1989 - the income year before me. 1989. It may well be that the same conclusion is reached if I were to apply the more 'liberal" view of Deane J in Coles Myer set out above, that is, the partnership was not "definitively committed" or "completely subjected" to the obligation until the time of payment into court shortly before trial, assuming that it occurred in the same income year as the judgment as to which I have no clear evidence [cftr p 31]. But is there the necessary nexus between those expenditures and assessable income or the partnership business carried on for the purpose of producing such income? To answer that question it is necessary to consider several decisions.
  2. In Amalgamated Zinc, the appellant company had at one time carried on a business of, inter alia, producing zinc concentrates, employing manual labour and contributing to the compensation fund established under the Workmen's Compensation (Broken Hill) Act 1920 (NSW). This business was discontinued in 1924. By 1929 the company had disposed of its plant, machinery and stock; its sole income being henceforth derived from investments and the letting of a hall in Broken Hill, albeit remaining liable to make payments to the fund in the years 1932 and 1933. These payments were claimed by the company as deductions in the relevant years as losses "made in each such year by the company in carrying on the business of a producer and seller of zinc concentrates in Australia" (a business which it had ceased to carry on for some years). The respondent Commissioner refused the company's claim to these deductions, hence the appeal The sections relied on were the (then) sub-ss 23(lXa) and 26(lXa) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1934, the former providing for a deduction from assessable income of "all losses and outgoings (... not being in the nature of losses and outgoings of capital) actually incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income."; the latter provided for a deduction "where a loss is made in any year by any person in carrying on a business in Australia."

34. In/AGC, BarwickCJ stated at 186-189:

In Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, the expression "a continuing business" was used to qualify the occasion when an expenditure not precisely related to the assessable income of a particular year was an allowable deduction. This description was intended, in my opinion, to convey the notion that in the case of an expenditure, the business of the taxpayer in respect of which the expenditure was made, would probably in due course reflect in its income the proceeds or effects of that expenditure, or that it would already have done so where the expenditure was discharging an outstanding liability. I do not regard the expression "continuing" in such a temporal sense that if there were any break in the carrying on of the business for some reason, the business could not be regarded relevantly as continuous.

In any case. Amalgamated Zinc (Be Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation was concerned with an expenditure and not with a loss. Its facts were unusual the appellant company having discontinued its business in connexion with the recovery of metals and having become an investment company, its assessable income in the particular year was derived wholly from investment ... The time interval between the conclusion of the business of recovering minerals and the tax year in question was considerable. The case passed off against the taxpayer on the footing that there was no possible relationship between the expenditure and the assessable income in the tax year in question. Although the case was not concerned with the incurring of a loss, Sir John Latham observed upon the difficulty of regarding a loss as something which could gain or produce income. He suggested a reading of the section which retained the definite article. He decided that if the expenditure in question in the case were to be regarded as a loss, it could not have been made in the course of carrying on a business.

Perhaps it would have been more satisfactory in that case to have read the section there under consideration in a sense which related expenditure or loss to the gaining of assessable income which may have been already gained and returned, or which may yet be gained and returned. See the discussion in Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p 56, where it is suggested that the reference to the assessable income is really a reference to "assessable income" generally. In the case of Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, the liability to make the workers' compensation payments was undoubtedly a liability which was incurred in the gaining of assessable income upon the mineral-recovering activities. That income had already been returned. It may be that, because of the terms of the Income Tax Assessment Act, the current value of the payments to be made in the future under the Workers' Compensation (Broken Hill) Act, 1920 could not have been deducted in determining the assessable income of any one year. But the obligation to make the payments was a business liability which sprang out of the carrying on of the business which had yielded assessable income. On a construction of the section which included expenditures which related to the gaining of assessable income "generally", it might properly have been said that the later payments of compensation had been incurred as a cost of gaining the assessable income which had already been returned and taxed. But an interpretation was adopted which required a relationship of some undefined kind between the expenditure and the assessable income of the year in question. This interpretation, whilst on the one hand not requiring the tracing of the expenditure into the assessable income of the particular year, does require some relationship between the expenditure and the assessable income of that year. No particular logical prescription of that relationship is suggested in the judgment of the Court.

However that may be, I do not regard that case as deciding that, even in the case of expenditure, the business in respect of which the expenditure is made must be or has already been carried on without any substantial break. It seems to me that the most that could be deduced from the construction of the section applied in Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation in relation to an expenditure is that where there has been a break in the carrying on of the business yielding the assessable income of the particular year, that business must in its nature be substantially the same as that which was carried on at the earlier period of time.

But in any case, in my opinion. Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation has nothing to say as to the deduction of losses. It is clear enough, it seems to me, that in order to be a relevant loss, it must be a loss of money which has been put out in order to gain assessable income. It may be, and I have no need to decide that question at the moment, that if a long period of years separated the two events, and meantime the company had started a different business or become an investment company, as in Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, it may be necessary if that decision is followed, in such a case to say that the relationship between the two had ceased to be sufficiently proximate. It would suffice for my present purpose that I am not satisfied that, in order to be deductible, the loss which flows from carrying on a business carried on to gain assessable income need necessarily occur in a year when the company is actively carrying on that business.

Further, in the present case, the facts, in my opinion, satisfy the idea of a "continuing" business except upon the vary narrow view that a business is not relevantly continuing if it has had any substantial break in its continuity, a view which, as I have indicated, I am unable to accept....

Since writing the above, I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment prepared by my brother Mason. Having done so, I am in agreement with his opinion that the construction of s. 51 is not concluded by Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation and with his conclusion that the words 'the assessable income" in that section mean assessable income of the taxpayer generally without regard to division into accounts periods. I am in agreement with the reasons which my brother gives for that opinion and conclusion.

35. Mason J stated in that case (at 195-198):

In Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

the Court considered that the expression ["the assessable income"] should be read as referring to assessable income of the year in which the expenditure was incurred, though the Court qualified the narrow effect of this interpretation by observing that the words 'Incurred in gaining or producing" should be understood as "in the course of gaining or producing". On this view Latham CJ said that an expenditure designed to produce income in a past or future year was deductible where it was "of such a character that, in a continuing business, it must be met from time to time as a part of the process of gaining assessable income" (54 CLR at pp 303-304). See also Dixon J.(54CLRatp309)....

When the Court was subsequently called upon to consider the similar question of interpretation which arises under the provisions of s. 51( 1) in the Ronpibon Case, it indicated that a different view might be taken of the provisions in their new setting. Latham CJ, Rich, Dixon, McTieman and Webb JJ there said of the second alternative under s. 51(1) (78 CLRatp 56):

'The word 'business' is defined by s. 6(1) to include profession, trade, employment, vocation or calling, but not occupation as an employee. The alternative in s. 51(1) therefore covers a wide description of activities. But in actual working it can add but little to the operation of the leading words, 'losses or outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income'. No doubt the expression 'in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing' lays down a test that is different from that implied by the words'm gaining or producing'. But these latter words have a very wide operation and will cover almost all the ground occupied by the alternative. The words 'such income' mean 'income of that description or kind' and perhaps they should be understood to refer not to the assessable income of the accounting period but to assessable income generally. If they were so interpreted, they would cover a case where the business had not yet produced or had railed to produce assessable income and the alternative would then itself suffice to authorize the deduction of a loss made in a distinct business."

The passage indicates that the question of construction is not foreclosed by Be Bavay's Case and that this Court is at liberty to reach its own conclusion unfettered by what was said in De Bavay's Case. Support for this view is in my opinion supplied by the observations of Dixon CJ in Commissioner of Taxation v Finn ((1961) [1961] HCA 61; 106 CLR 60, at p 68), where his Honour said: 'The better view, however, is that s. 51 as now drawn does not in either limb require a rigid restriction to the gaining or production of assessable income of the current year."

Looking at the question de novo, the case for saying that "the assessable income" in s. 51(1) means assessable income of the taxpayer generally without regard to division into accounting periods is to my mind irresistible. There is every reason for thinking that the definite article was used so as to designate the income of the taxpayer generally rather than the income of the taxpayer in the year in question. It is inconceivable that Parliament intended to confine deductions to losses and outgoings incurred in connexion with the production of income in the year in question and to exclude losses and outgoings incurred in connexion with the production of income in preceding or succeeding years. True it is that the expression "in gaining or producing" as it applies to assessable income may allow some expansion in the relationship which it would otherwise prescribe between the loss or outgoing and the production of income in the year in which the loss or outgoing was incurred, but the expanded relationship thereby suggested is hinged upon the notion that the taxpayer is conducting a continuing business, a concept which finds no expression in the first limb of s. 51(1) for the ascertainment of the allowance of a deduction. The preferable course, so it seems to me. is to read the reference to assessable income in the first limb of s. 51( 1) as a reference to the assessable income of taxpayer generally.

This conclusion is not an answer to all the problems of construction presented by the subsection. In the Ronpibon Case, the Court suggested in the passage to which I have already referred that the second limb may have a slightly wider operation than the first limb and that it may authorize the deduction of losses incurred in a distinct business. At first glance it may be thought that these observations overlook the possible limitations inherent in the words 'incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income", viz assessable income generally. It may be argued that if the taxpayer has ceased to carry on a particular business, a loss subsequently sustained in relation to that business cannot be described accurately as a loss incurred in carrying on that business, or at any rate one incurred in carrying it on for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. But the soundness of the argument depends on what is meant by "incurred". A loss constituted by the writing off of a bad debt is no doubt incurred, in the sense that it is sustained, at the time when the debt is written off and that may occur in a given case after the taxpayer has ceased to carry on as a going concern the business in which the debt was created. Yet even in such a case it may be correct to speak of the loss as having been incurred in the carrying on of the business. This is because the occasion for the loss is to be found in a transaction entered into the carrying on of the business for the purpose of producing assessable income, that is, in the agreement by which the debt was created. Because the loss had its origin in such a transaction, the loss may be said to be one which was incurred in the carrying on of the business for the purpose of producing assessable income notwithstanding that its true character as a loss is not finally ascertained until the debt is written off. (my emphasis)

What I have said is, I think, in accord with the observations made with respect to s. 51(1) in the later cases. Thus in the Ronpibon Case, the Court stated (at p 57) that "to come within the initial part of the subsection it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income'1. So also it may be said that it is enough to satisfy the second part of the subsection that the occasion of the loss or outgoing is to be found in the carrying on of a business for the production of assessable income. In my opinion, therefore, the outstanding terms charges not brought to account as assessable income iall certainly within the second limb of s. 51(1); it may be that they fall within the first limb, but this is not a question which needs to be pursued.

If this conclusion involves a departure from the literal meaning of the words used, it is to be justified by reference to the context and to the unlikely and arbitrary consequence which would flow from the adoption of a literal interpretation. That a loss having its origin in the course of carrying on a business for profit should only be deductible whilst the business is still in operation, though the loss may not be ascertained until a later date, seems to me to be a strange result; so strange indeed that an intention to bring it about should not be imputed to the legislature when the statute is susceptible to a sensible alternative interpretation, (my emphasis) On the view which I have thus far expressed it is unnecessary to decide whether the business carried on when the debts were written off was the same as the business which was carried on when the relevant hire-purchase agreements were entered into. However, having considered the question. I should state as an additional ground of decision that on the facts recited in the stated case I am satisfied that the business carried on by the appellant after its share capital was acquired by Australian Guarantee Corporation Ltd was the same business as that which the appellant formerly carried on....

Whether interest incurred by a taxpayer will, in a particular case, satisfy the test of deductibility depends upon the facts of a particular case: FCT v Total Holdings (Aust) Pty Ltd (79 ATC 4279) at p 4283 and the cases there cited. Lockhart J there said:

"If a taxpayer with a continuing business incurs a liability for interest being incidental to or connected with the operations or activities regularly carried on for the production of income, the interest is an allowable deduction. The circumstance that each item of expenditure cannot be traced to a particular hem of income does not prevent the deduction of the expenditure. See Ward & Co Ltd v Commr of Taxes (NZ) [1923] AC 145 at p 148; De Bavay's Case at p 307; W Nevill & Co Ltd v FC of T [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290 at p. 305 and The Texas Co (A'sia) Ltd v FCofT[1940] HCA 9; (1939-1940) 63 CLR 382 per Starke J at p 451."

The language of the section introduces, as Professor Parsons points out in his work Income Taxation in Australia, Law Book Company, 1985 (para 5-37 to 5-48), an element of contemporaneity. Thus an outgoing may be precluded from deductibility if it be incurred at a point too soon before the commencement of the business or income-producing activity (FC of T v Maddalena 71 ATC 4161; (1971) 45 ALJR 426; Lodge v FCofT11 ATC 4174; [1972] HCA 49; (1972) 128 CLR 171), or if it is incurred after the business or activity has ceased (eg. Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's)), at least in the latter case where a considerable time had passed and the taxpayer had given up all thought of mining. However, the subsequent case of AGC (Advances) Pty Ltd, while not a case where the business had wholly ceased, although operations had been suspended, suggests that there should be some qualification to the absolute principle suggested in De Bavay's Case. Mason J in AGC (Advances) Ltd at ATC p 4072; CLR pp 197-198, discussing a loss incurred by writing off a debt after a company had ceased to carry on as a going concern the business in which the debt was created, remarked that:

"even in such a case it may be correct to speak of the loss as having been incurred in the carrying on of the business. This is because the occasion for the loss is to be found in a transaction entered into in the carrying on of the business for the purpose of producing assessable income..."
