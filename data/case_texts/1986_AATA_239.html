Re "Aa" (No 2) and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 239 (29 August 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: "AA" (No. 2)

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A85/37

AAT No. 2846

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

C.J. Stevens (Members)

N.J. Attwood (Members)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - invalidity benefit - final annual rate of salary - salary - higher duties allowance.

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 3, 5, 7, 66, 70, 71, 72 126A, 157, 183

Superannuation (Salary) Regulations regs. 4, 5, 6

Acts Interpretation Act 1901 s. 13

Re AA and Commissioner for Superannuation (A85/37, 6 November 1985)

Re McLindin and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 261

Commissioner for Superannuation v Carpenter [1983] FCA 148; (1983) 48 ALR 230

Great Fingall Consolidated v Sheehan [1905] HCA 43; (1905) 3 CLR 176

Webster v McIntosh (1980) AC 854

Re Nelson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 681

Re Hoare's Casings Pty Ltd and Export Development Grants Board (1980) 3 ALN N15

HEARING

CANBERRA

29:8:1986

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

In this application for review the sole question before the Tribunal is whether the respondent has correctly calculated the applicant's entitlement to invalidity benefit under s. 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"): See Re AA and Commissioner for Superannuation (A85/37, 6 November 1985).

2. By virtue of s. 7(2) of the Act, the applicant was deemed to have been retired from the Commonwealth Public Service on the grounds of physical or mental incapacity on 30 June 1977. As a result of an earlier decision of this Tribunal, an initial election by the applicant under s. 72(1) of the Act to receive a lump sum benefit in lieu of a pension was cancelled pursuant to s. 157(3) of the Act. The effect of that cancellation was to make it as if no election had been made, thereby giving to the applicant all the superannuation options ordinarily open to a person retiring in the applicant's circumstances. In a letter dated 28 May 1984, the respondent provided the applicant with advice of his entitlement should he not make an election under s. 71, and of his entitlement should he make an election under s. 71. The letter included information relating to the calculation of the different rates of pension and it was stated that the applicant's "salary at retirement" was $17,217 per annum. In a subsequent letter this figure was amended to $18,091 per annum. Very briefly, the question now before the Tribunal turns upon the answer to the question whether, in calculating the applicant's pension, the respondent was correct in basing those calculations upon the salary of a Clerk Class 9 or whether it should instead have been based upon the income which the applicant received as an Acting Class 11.

3. On 19 July 1973 the applicant was promoted to a particular Division in a certain Department and took up duty accordingly on 11 October 1973. The position was a Class 9 position, and this remained his substantive position until the cessation of his service, which took effect from 30 June 1977. Over the whole period of his service at the Class 9 level, amounting to some three years and eight months, the applicant acted in higher duties, and received higher duties allowance accordingly, for about forty per cent of the time. He so acted during the following periods:

17 January 1974 - 4 February 1974

24 May 1974 - 12 June 1974

19 August 1974 - 11 October 1974

28 October 1974 - 8 November 1974

1 April 1974 - 23 April 1975

25 August 1975 - 29 August 1975

7 November 1975 - 7 October 1976

6 December 1976 - 17 December 1976

12 January 1977 - 3 March 1977

Thus between 1 July 1976 and 30 June 1977, his last year of service, the applicant acted on higher duties during the following periods:

1 July 1976 to 7 October 1976

6 December 1976 to 17 December 1976

12 January 1977 to 3 March 1977

4. Section 66(2) of the Act is the provision which governs the invalidity benefits available to the applicant. Very briefly, under that sub-section a person in the applicant's circumstances may elect to receive either an invalidity pension and lump sum benefit under s. 71, or a lump sum benefit under s. 72. If no such election is made the person is entitled to an invalidity pension in accordance with s. 70. Sections 70, 71 and 72 and the relevant schedules, in declaring the amount of entitlement, all refer to the person's final annual rate of salary".

Section 3 provides:

"'final annual rate of salary', in relation to a

person who has ceased to be an eligible

employee, means -

(a) if paragraph (b) or (c) does not apply in

relation to him - his annual rate of salary

on his last day of service;

(b) if the annual rate of salary by reference to

which his final basic contribution was

calculated is higher than the rate specified

in paragraph (a) - that higher rate; or

(c) if his annual rate of salary was higher than

the rate referred to in paragraph (a) or (b)

at any time on or after -

(i) the anniversary of his birth last

preceding his last day of service; or

(ii) where, on or after than anniversary, he

made an election under section 47 in

respect of a reduction in his annual rate

of salary - the date of the election,

that higher rate;"

Section 5 provides:

"5(1) In this section, 'salary' means salary or

wages, and includes -

(a) any allowance, or the value of any allowance,

or any fee, that is an allowance or fee of a

kind that, under the regulations, is to be

treated as salary for the purposes of this

Act; and

(b) partial invalidity pension,

but does not include any part of any salary or

wages that, under the regulations, is not to be

treated as salary for the purposes of this Act.

(2) For the purposes of this Act but subject to

sub-section (3), the annual rate of salary of an

eligible employee on a particular day is an amount

equal to the amount per annum of the salary payable

to him on that day.

(3) The regulations may provide that, in a case

specified in the regulations, the annual rate of

salary of an eligible employee on a particular day

shall, for the purposes of this Act or a provision

of this Act specified in the regulations, be an

amount equal to such amount per annum as is

ascertained under the regulations."

5. Reference must also be made to the Superannuation (Salary) Regulations which took effect from 1 July 1976. The relevant regulations provide:

"4. In this Part, a reference to an allowance of an

prescribed kind shall be read as a reference to an

allowance of one of the following kinds:

(a) an allowance that is payable to an eligible

employee by reason that he temporarily

performs the whole or a part of the duties of

an office of a higher classification than the

classification of the office occupied by him;

(b)-(g) . . . .

5. Each of the following kinds of allowance is a

kind of allowance that is to be treated as salary

for the purposes of the Act:

(a)-(k) . . . .

(1) an allowance of a prescribed kind that is

payable on a regular basis to an eligible

employee.

6. (1) An allowance of a prescribed kind shall not,

for the purposes of paragraph 5(1), be taken to be

payable to an eligible employee on a regular basis

unless the allowance is payable in respect of

duties or work performed by the eligible employee

for a continuous period that commences -

(a) immediately after the eligible employee has

performed for a continuous period of 12 months

duties or work in respect of the performance

of which an allowance of the same kind as that

allowance has been paid or is payable to the

eligible employee; or

(b) where a certificate in respect of the eligible

employee has been given under sub-regulation

(2) - on the day specified in the certificate.

(2) If a person authorised by the Commissioner

for the purposes of this regulation is of the

opinion that there is a likelihood that, from and

including a particular day -

(a) an eligible employee will, for a continuous

period of not less than 12 months, perform

duties or work in respect of the performance

of which an allowance of a prescribed kind

will be payable to him; or

(b) where the eligible employee has, for a

continuous period of less than 12 months

immediately preceding the particular day,

performed duties or work in respect of the

performance of which an allowance of a

prescribed kind has been paid or is payable to

him - the eligible employee will, for a

continuous period of less than 12 months that

together with the first-mentioned period will

not be less than a period of 12 months,

perform duties or work in respect of the

performance of which an allowance of the same

kind as that allowance will be payable to him,

the authorised person shall give to the

Commissioner a certificate to that effect

specifying the day (not being a day earlier than

the day on which the certificate is given) on which

the period referred to in paragraph (a) or (b), as

the case may be, in relation to the eligible

employee will commence.

(3)-(4) . . . . "

The heading to regulation 7, although not part of the regulation (see s. 13(3) Acts Interpretation Act 1901), describes the regulation as dealing with "Part of salary or wages not to be treated as salary in certain circumstances", but the provisions themselves are not directly relevant.

6. Mr Anforth, representing the respondent, submitted that in order for the applicant's acting Class 11 income to be treated as salary for the purposes of the Act the applicant must either (a) have performed for a continuous period of 12 months in that position; or (b) have had issued in respect of him, pursuant to Regulation 6(2), a certificate in relation to that position. The applicant, Mr Anforth contended, fulfilled neither requirement. He further submitted that a decision not to issue a certificate was not a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation himself, and was therefore not reviewable by this Tribunal. Moreover, even were the Commissioner now to issue a certificate, under Reg. 6, he said, such a certificate would operate only from the date of the certificate. He relied on Re McLindin and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 261

7. The applicant conceded that there was no continuous period of 12 months or more in which he had served as an Acting Class 11 but contended that he satisfied both of the requirements of regulation 6(2). At the initial hearing it was agreed by the parties that the Tribunal should decide the jurisdictional points (namely whether the Tribunal has power to issue a certificate under regulation 6 and, if so, whether its operation could be made to antedate his retirement) before dealing with the question of the likelihood of the applicant falling within either paragraph of reg. 6(2), as failure by the applicant on the jurisdictional point would obviate the need to proceed further.

8. In our opinion the jurisdictional point must clearly be decided against the applicant on the basis of the decision in Re McLindin. The Tribunal was there presided over by the President, but in any event we respectfully agree with that decision and with the reasons given therefor. There was however another issue which gave the Tribunal cause for concern, and to that we now pass.

9. The definition of "salary" in s. 5(1) of the Act was the subject of close consideration by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Carpenter [1983] FCA 148; (1983) 48 ALR 230, to which Mr Anforth referred us. In that case the employee performed shift work on a regular basis for some 30 years before his retirement. The employee received the prescribed rate of pay only when he was on day shift, receiving shift allowances for night and afternoon shifts. The shifts, each of which lasted for approximately a week, were worked on a roster basis so that while the employees' earnings varied from week to week, they were constant for each cycle of the roster. In holding that continuing percentage variations to a salary resulting from regular roster shifts fell within the ordinary meaning of the word "salary", the Court rejected a submission from the Commissioner that "salary or wages" in s. 5(1) should be given a restricted meaning which could only be extended by regulation. The majority of the Court summed up its conclusions as follows (at 232-3):

"(1) When sub-s 5(1) says 'salary means salary and

wages' it covers everything within the ordinary

meaning of salary and wages.

(2) When sub-s 5(1) adds that salary includes

'partial invalidity pension' (para (b)) it is

extending the definition to something which would

not be within the ordinary meaning of salary and

wages.

(3) When sub-s 5(1) also adds that salary includes

'any allowance or the value of any allowance or any

fee that is an allowance or fee of a kind that

under the regulations is to be treated as salary

for the purposes of this Act' it is not enumerating

an exhaustive list of things which are to be

regarded as within the meaning of 'salary'; on the

contrary, it is stating no list at all; Parliament

is delegating to the Executive the power to

prescribe by regulation what further allowances or

fees shall be included. These are clearly words of

extension.

(4) The use of 'means' and 'includes' in the one

definition is a contrasting use of these two words.

In this collocation of words it is difficult, if

not impossible, to read the word 'includes' as in

effect covering the same ground as if the word

'means' had been used.

(5) When sub-s 5(1) says 'but does not include any

part of salary or wages that, under the

regulations, is not to be treated as salary for the

purposes of the Act', Parliament is delegating to

the Executive the power to exclude by regulation

certain things otherwise included in the definition

by the words 'salary or wages'.

(6) The power delegated to exclude by regulation

is a power to exclude 'any part of salary or wages'

thus picking up general words first used in the

definition. This appears to require the words

'salary and wages' to be read in their ordinary

sense and not in any sense confined by reading

'includes' where it is earlier used as the

practical equivalent of 'means'.

(7) In its ordinary sense the phrase 'salary or

wages' would cover the remuneration regularly

received by Mr Carpenter over the normal four-week

structure of his duties."

10. A fundamental distinction between the present case and Carpenter is that in the present case it was not disputed, nor in the Tribunal's opinion could it successfully have been disputed, that the difference between the salary of a Clerk Class 11 and a Clerk Class 9 was an allowance of a prescribed kind under regulation 4. The Federal Court in Carpenter found that the employee's shift work, which constituted his normal hours of duty, did not fall within regulation 4(b): See now Part IIA of the Regulations, inserted by SR 1985 No. 326. The issue which is now raised for the Tribunal is whether it is possible for the applicant's additional salary to fall within the ordinary concept of salary and wages notwithstanding that the Regulations deal with higher duties allowance as an allowance of a prescribed kind that is payable on a regular basis (reg. 5(1)).

11. There appears in the Tribunal's opinion to be a problem as to whether the combined effect of s. 5(1) and of the Regulations is such as to exclude a consideration of whether the allowance received by the applicant was "salary or wages" within the ordinary meaning of those words. It can no longer be contended (as was argued in Carpenter) that "salary or wages" covers only such allowances as the Regulations provide are to be treated as such. Indeed at the hearing Mr Anforth specifically and very properly stated that the respondent did not seek to argue that higher duties allowance would not normally constitute salary. He conceded that it could be salary within ordinary concepts but submitted that, the Regulations having dealt with higher duties allowance in a particular fashion, any higher duties allowance lying outside the ambit of those regulations could not be considered as salary for the purposes of the Act. It was said that higher duties allowances could be either of two kinds: those that satisfy the 'regular basis' test in regulation 6; and those that do not satisfy the 'regular basis' test in regulation 6. Between them, the two alternatives covered all possible allowances within regulation 4(a). Regulation 5 having specifically included the first class, by application of the maxim expressio unius est exclusio alterius it had been intended to exclude the second category. It was also submitted that s. 5 empowered the making of regulations which modified the Act and that it was an example of a "Henry VIII clause". Finally it was submitted that the decision in Carpenter turned upon the particular wording of regulation 4(b) and was not directly transportable to regulation 4(a).

12. We shall deal with the submissions in reverse order. While it is true that the particular facts in Carpenter turned upon regulation 4(b), the Federal Court in reaching its conclusion was required to tread a path which, in part, involved the same enactments, superior and subordinate, as are involved in the present case. Most importantly, the Court directed its attention specifically to s. 5(1) of the Act, and its observations on that section cannot be ignored by this Tribunal. It is quite apparent from the passage at 48 ALR pp. 232-3 which has been quoted above, that the reasoning of the Court in that case has a direct bearing upon the problem presently before us, and to that extent we are bound by the Federal Court's reasons. Mr Anforth's final submission as mentioned above is therefore rejected.

13. We are unable to accept the submission that s. 5 of the Act is a "Henry VIII" clause. It is said that such clauses enable regulations made under an Act to amend or repeal parts of that Act. Thus, for example, s. 126A(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 provides:

"The regulations may make provision for modifying

this Act, or a specified provision of this Act, in

the application of this Act or that provision to or

in respect of a person to whom this section

applies, or to or in respect of persons included in

a specified class of persons to whom this section

applies."

Section 183 of the Superannuation Act is another very clear example. Whatever might be the status of such clauses, it is plain that s. 5 is not couched in terms allowing regulations made under it to repeal, amend or add to any provisions of the Superannuation Act 1976 itself. History may still argue the merits and demerits of the second Tudor King of England, but at least it can be said that he was not a King who hid round corners or in cupboards. The legislative clauses that have been given his name have been much criticized by learned writers, and valid as they no doubt are we do not consider that an intention to confer such a power can be left to inference. If a power to amend an Act by regulation is to be conferred, it should be conferred openly and with precision.

14. The remaining submission is one of much greater difficulty. It is that, because the regulations have dealt with the issue of higher duties allowance, it no longer remains to consider whether such an allowance falls within the ordinary concept of the word "salary". Notwithstanding the breadth with which the regulations may have been drafted, it is clearly stated in the legislation, and has so been interpreted by the Federal Court, that the provisions in the subordinate legislation are in addition to what is encompassed by the ordinary meaning of the words. Moreover the respondent's submissions effectively involved using the regulations to ascertain and circumscribe the meaning of the principal legislation. Such a technique must be resisted for it is a firmly established principle that regulations may not be relied upon as an aid to the construction of the Act under which they are made: Great Fingall Consolidated v. Sheehan [1905] HCA 43; (1905) 3 CLR 176 at 184; Webster v McIntosh [1980] FCA 128; (1980) 32 ALR 603 at 606; Jackson v. Hall (1980) AC 854 at 884, 889 To accept the respondent's argument could be said to be to convert, by a back door means, the character of the Regulations from inclusive and exclusive to exhaustive. The concluding words of the definition in s. 5(1) provide a specific means of excluding any part of any salary or wages from the definition of salary and unless that means is used, ought anything which falls within the ordinary concept of salary or wages and which has not been specifically excluded remain capable of being regarded as outside the power to exclude conferred by s. 5(1)?

15. Upon a plain reading of the concluding words of the definition of "salary" in s. 5(1), for any part of any salary or wages not to fall within the definition the regulations must specify that that part is not to be treated as salary for the purposes of the Act. It is relevant that the definition does not say "any part of any salary or wages that, under the regulations, is not treated as salary . . .". The inclusion of the words "to be" indicates that in order for a payment that falls within the ordinary definition of salary or wages not to be treated as salary, the regulations should specifically stipulate that it is not to be so treated. In other words, it is not to be "not treated as salary" by implication. The Salary Regulations as they are drafted appear to accord with this interpretation: Regulation 5 directly specifies kinds of allowance that are to be treated as salary for the purposes of the Act; Regulation 7 specifies parts of salary or wages that are not to be treated as salary in certain circumstances. Regulation 7 deals specifically with parts of salary or wages and it may have needed careful drafting to add to that regulation a clause dealing with the residue of higher duties allowances not specifically dealt with elsewhere. Yet with hindsight it would have been better to have made the attempt. On one view regulation 6 does not provide that certain salary or wages are not to be treated as salary: All that it does is to restrict the types of allowances which fall within regulation 5(1); regulation 5 is devoted to listing allowances that are to be treated as salary for the purposes of the Act. Is it concerned with listing matters that are not to be treated as salary for the purposes of the Act? Can it be that regulation 6, in refining the operation of regulation 5(1), has the effect of giving regulation 5(1) a dual character of requiring certain payments to be treated as salary but requiring others not to be treated as salary?

16. Mr Anforth referred us to a number of earlier Tribunal decisions which, he said, accorded with his submissions. In Re McLindin and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 261, the applicant took up a position for which he was entitled to a higher duties allowance on 2 August 1976. The Tribunal accepted that as at that date he was expected to remain at that level for at least 12 months either because:

(a) the person whose position he was filling was

expected to be away for 12 months; or

(b) it was expected that before the person whose

position he was filling returned, the applicant

would have received a permanent appointment at that

level.

However, on 4 August 1976 the applicant himself went on 3 months sick leave. The Tribunal was largely concerned with whether a regulation 6(2) certificate could be issued or whether the Tribunal could review a decision not to grant such a certificate. The Tribunal held that a decision not to grant a certificate was not "a decision of the Commissioner" and was therefore not a reviewable decision under s. 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976. In Re McLindin the Tribunal had, in truth, no cause to consider whether the higher duties allowance fell within the ordinary concepts of salary because the allowance had been paid for such a short period of time that, on any reasonable view, the question could not be said to arise. The only way in which the applicant could hope to have his allowance considered in McLindin was by way of a certificate under regulation 6(2). The remark on p. 263-7 of the report should be seen as relating to the facts of the case being dealt with, not as a statement of universal application and there is nothing to suggest that it was so intended.

17. In Re Nelson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 681, the applicant took up a higher position on 7 October 1976, was admitted to hospital on 20 February 1977 and did not resume active service thereafter. He was formally retired from the service on 3 June 1977 with effect from 31 March 1977. As in McLindin, the Tribunal did not consider the question whether the applicant's higher duties allowance fell within the ordinary concept of salary: it merely considered whether the applicant was in receipt of an allowance of a prescribed kind within regulation 4. In Re Nelson a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation had declared, on 31 May 1977, that the allowance be regarded as salary following a recommendation to him that the applicant was likely to occupy the higher position for a period of not less than 12 months. The Tribunal held that the declaration of 31 May 1977 did not meet the requirements of a certificate under regulation 6(2) and so affirmed a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation not taking into account the higher duties allowances. The Tribunal in Re Nelson did not consider or advert to the question of whether the applicant's higher duties allowance could be salary according to ordinary concepts. We do not consider that their silence can be taken as a statement by the Tribunal that only higher duties allowances within the meaning of reg. 4(a) are "salary" for the purposes of s. 5.

18. We have taken all of these arguments into consideration, and have found the question before us very difficult to resolve. We confess that we have wavered in coming to a conclusion. Having however given the matter anxious consideration we have come to the conclusion that, s. 5(1) having empowered the Governor-General to include, by regulation, certain allowances within the concept of salary, and likewise effectively to exclude any salary or wages by not treating such as salary for the purposes of the Act, the Regulations have, although admittedly with some drafting obscurity, achieved that object. It is odd that the exclusionary aspect of the matter, that is to say that part of the drafting of the regulations the function of which was to secure the result that certain salary or wages was not to be treated as such, was drawn in such a way as to require reliance upon the maxim expressio unius est exclusio alterius and/or upon the notion of the covering of a field. On the whole however, we consider that the better view is that that is what has been achieved. In essence, we consider:

(i) that the concluding words of s. 5(1) should be read

for present purposes as having the sense "but does

not include any part of any salary . . . of a kind

dealt with under the regulations and not in those

regulations treated as salary for the purposes of

this Act", and

(ii) that reg. 6 has dealt with the field of higher

duties allowance and has, to the extent that it has

excluded higher duties allowance from being treated

as salary for the purposes of the Act, effectively

provided that it "is not to be treated as salary".

19. It follows that in the result we do not have to deal with the question whether the applicant's higher duties allowance should be treated as salary and enter into the calculation of his final annual rate of salary.

20. We should make it plain that, whatever difficulties we have had with the matter, we have reached our conclusion, whether it be right or wrong, according to principles of statutory interpretation as we understand them to be. We have not given any weight to a submission that was made to us by Mr Anforth near the conclusion of the resumed hearing. This was that the Tribunal should, in coming to its conclusion, have regard to the economic consequences of its decision. It was said that a contrary conclusion to that contended for by the Commission would have enormous financial consequences and would also create a vast workload in recalculating entitlements of others. The submission was courteously, if not diffidently, put, but we should explain why we could not have given effect to it.

21. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal, while an administrative, not a judicial, arm of government, has no lesser obligation to observe the law than has a Court. That does not mean, human frailty being what it is, that the Tribunal will always be correct. The fact that all court systems within the jurisprudence of the common law, and presumably of most other legal systems, include an appellate structure, indicates that the stated frailty is not confined to the AAT. The critical fact is that there is a right of appeal from a decision of the AAT on a question of law to the Federal Court. Is it to be supposed that the AAT should apply a construction of a statute based not on legal analysis but on its supposition of serious economic consequences to government? This would be a mode of statutory construction that would take brave counsel indeed to put, in support of an AAT decision so based, before the Federal Court on appeal. Is it suggested that that Court should so proceed? The fact is that with all its faults the law is one system, and it cannot be administered in different ways according to different concepts by different segments of the administrative and judicial system. The same rules apply to Courts, Tribunals, and administrators: See Re Hoare's Casings Pty Ltd and Export Development Grants Board (1980) 3 ALN N15 especially at N17 for an exposition of how this proposition applies in relation to statutory interpretation.

22. None of what we have said is intended to convey that Tribunals, or for that matter the Courts, should not be aware of the economic consequences of their decisions. The point is that such consequences are not a proper part of the rules for construction of legislation. The only possible exception to this would be in some areas of the exercise of discretionary power. It can nevertheless be accepted that Courts and Tribunals should indeed be conscious of the background against which the problem in question has arisen and of the consequences of their decisions. Such consequences are not however simply economic consequences for government. There are consequences for the underprivileged and the distressed, consequences for the individual, for whom the result of a particular case may mean the difference between abject penury and a reasonable if lowly standard of living.

23. For reasons earlier expressed, we shall affirm the decision under review.
