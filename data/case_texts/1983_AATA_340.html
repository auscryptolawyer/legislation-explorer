Re Cheryl Ann Hendy and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1983] AATA 340 (13 October 1983)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CHERYL ANN HENDY

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V83/71

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr Justice J.D. Davies (President)

Dr A. Marsh (Member)

Mr B. Lock (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation _ retirement on grounds of invalidity _ chronic anxiety depression _ whether health so restored as to be able to resume duties in the Public Service

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) s.75

Re Grover and Commissioner for Superannuation W.81/29 (delivered 22 July 1983)

HEARING

SYDNEY

13:10:1983

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside.

DECISION

This is a review of a decision under s.75 of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) that '...the health of Miss Hendy had become so restored as to enable her to perform duties of a kind ... suitable to be performed by her, namely those of a typist or clerical assistant' and that the Regional Director of the Public Service be informed with a view to finding her suitable employment. Section 75(1) reads :

'75.(1) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, after receiving the report or reports of a medical practitioner or medical practitioners with respect to the health of a person to whom invalidity pension is payable, that the health of the person has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are, in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant), the Commissioner shall so inform such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner."

Miss Hendy, who was born on 23 September 1948, is 35 years of age. She entered the Public Service as a secretary on 24 February 1969. She subsequently, in 1973, resigned, with a view to taking employment outside the Public Service. A few months later she rejoined the Public Service, principally because, she believed, she desired the security which the Public Service could give her. She was competent in her field and it is somewhat surprising that, at that stage in her life, she was looking for security.

By about 1976, Miss Hendy had become a Steno-secretary Grade 2 on the staff of a Minister. She was competent in her work, but obviously she had difficulty relating to other people. Miss Hendy gave this evidence :

'What was the physical environment like?-Very bad.

In what way?-For a period of time we had clashes of personality.

When you say 'we', who are you talking about?-The female secretaries with one particular male secretary, the private secretary, which lasted for some time.

Was that a simple personality matter, or what was it?-He was working for a Liberal minister and he was a labour voter and he resented the whole environment, consequently took it out on the female staff by name-calling and general bad treatment.

Did that have any impact on you?-Yes, it had a great impact.

.....

Did things improve after he departed?-They did for a short while, but then we went into opposition and our offices changed.

The physical environment?-The work situation, we were moved out of the office, into the opposition offices and I was working on the press side of things because I was a faster typist than the other two girls. There again, I was in a strange environment because the press secretary he had at the time was involved in bisexual relationships and he used to bring his boyfriend into the office and I would be sitting there facing the boyfriend all night. I did try desperately to get away from Canberra at that time.

Because of the press secretary or the private secretary, or for some other reason?-The hours were getting too much, the work also, I was getting the work load because the other two girls _ one was related to .... and the other had a terrific list of qualifications behind her of colleges she had been to in England _ neither could type very well so I had all the typing of the office. It was also this situation of working with this man and having his boy friend sitting in front of me and, just a whole lot of things at the time.

.....

Do you attribute your being distressed and being upset to any particular factor?-Mainly the press secretary I was working with, I could not cope with that situation and there was no choice, I was put onto the press side of work and that was it, and the travelling and the long hours. It was extremely tiring.

.....

You mentioned there was pressure and a lifestyle which was not one you participated in. Was there any reason for you not participating in the lifestyle?-I was not interested in that lifestyle.

Why were not you interested? Was it because you disapproved of it?-Yes, I totally disapproved of it.

In what way?-There is a lot of _ I do not know how to describe it _ we had two girls on the staff that used to go into Parliament House and sit there and pick out the parliamentarians they wanted to sleep with, and this type of thing. This type of thing was going on all around you. As far as I was concerned with it was a job and I was there to do it. That type of thing did not enter my head. I did not know it went on until I got the job.

Was that also a factor which influenced you wanting to get away?-Yes.'

Ultimately, Miss Hendy felt that the pressures upon her were so great that she could no longer work in that situation and had to leave Canberra. She sought a position elsewhere and, when the Prices Justification Tribunal was established, she obtained a position as secretary to the Registrar of that Tribunal. Miss Hendy held that position for four years and her present recollection is that the Registrar was an excellent person to work for. However, as time went by, Miss Hendy again had problems relating to the other people in the office. Miss Hendy gave this evidence :

'What was your health like, at that stage, when you first started at the PJT?-Quite good actually, after a while I really settled into it, it was fine, it was just what I wanted.

How long did that situation continue?-Up until the last six months it was about six months before I was actually superannuated out. I felt depressed and just generally sick and I started going to my GP.

Did you experience any other symptom, you mentioned feeling sick?-No, I think just a general frustration.

Did you have any other ailments at this time?-I was not sleeping, I would be awake all night, crying a lot. I think I cried for six months continuously. In the work environment I could not face people because of the way I felt and the way I looked, because I was in tears even sitting in the office. I felt too sick to work.

Did you attribute that feeling to any factor in particular? Why do you think that happened at that time, feeling the way you did?-I do not know, I really do not know.'

Miss Hendy commenced sick leave on 20 December 1977. On 17 March 1978, she saw a Commonwealth Medical Officer with a view to seeking extension of her sick leave. The Commonwealth Medical Officer recorded the fact that Miss Hendy was suffering from chronic anxiety depression and recorded the following history :

'For the past 2 years, this lady has been feeling anxious and depressed. She doesn't know what is causing her to feel like this. She admits to having heavy financial commitments and minor personal problems, but she doesn't think these are causing her to feel this way. She is unhappy at her present place of work and would not consent to return there even if well. She cannot sleep, think clearly, concentrate and feels tired all the time. Medical treatment so far has not been helpful.'

As Miss Hendy's leave entitlements were then only three weeks and two days on half pay, the Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended that Miss Hendy be retired on the grounds of invalidity, but recorded the information that Miss Hendy 'may, in due course, partially or completely recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment'.

Although the issue is not presently before us and we have not heard from the Commonwealth Medical Officer with regard to this matter, we think it proper to record our view that the step of retiring Miss Hendy at that time may have been unfortunate. Experience in the rehabilitation of workers shows that, generally, the sooner a person who has suffered injury or ill-health can be got back to work in some form or another, the more likely it is that rehabilitation will be effected. The longer the time off work, the less likely it is that rehabilitation will be successful. Miss Hendy retired from the Commonwealth Public Service, received a pension and has not since been employed. At the present time, almost six years have passed since she has earned a living and worked with other people.

Towards the end of her employment at the Prices Justification Tribunal, Miss Hendy put on 3 or 4 stone in weight, a circumstance which is likely to have been a response to her anxiety and depression. Once she was retired, she sought a good deal of help but did not find that assistance overcame her problems. Miss Hendy has seen a number of persons and clinics about her weight problem, but she has not managed to lose weight in the long-term. She has seen psychiatrists but has not been able to afford any long-term treatment. She has seen one or more psychologists but again has not been able to afford long-term psychotherapy. She has regularly consulted a general medical practitioner. She has consulted medical practitioners in relation to vaginal discharge, she has consulted experts in relation to excessive perspiration, boils, rashes and thinning of the hair. But, as most of Miss Hendy's problems have their basis in her inadequate personality, she has not found alleviation of her problems.

In the past year, Miss Hendy, who had been living with her mother, moved into her own home. Her general medical practitioner, Dr B.H. Bignell, is of the view that she has improved since she has commenced to live on her own, for in his opinion, one of the contributing causes to Miss Hendy's problems has been her relationship with her mother. Miss Hendy continues to see her mother regularly and to care for her, but otherwise she leads a quiet life. She receives visits from a few friends but she rarely goes out and she avoids crowds, such as a wedding group, for she finds that she perspires unduly when she mixes with a large group.

In 1980, the Commissioner for Superannuation, who had been faced with the prospect of attempting to get back into the Public Service a number of persons who had been prematurely retired, requested that Miss Hendy attend a medical examination. Miss Hendy, on 17 March 1980, completed the following information :

'My problem is bad nerves and deep depression. I have had many medical problems and each time I've had them checked out by doctors and specialists they have all said each problem was caused by bad nerves. I don't seem to have improved at all.

In fact I feel more depressed and unable to cope with every day living.

But even the writing of this report I felt would have been better coming from my doctor. So, therefore, I asked him to write a short note to send along with this. So it is, therefore, attached."

The note from Dr Bignell which was attached read as follows :

'This is to certify that I saw Miss Hendy today.

She has persisting problems with anxiety and organic problems.

She is anxious about her weight and persisting sweating problems. She has great trouble coping with life in general and really is unable to handle work because of these problems.'

After seeing a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Miss Hendy was referred by the office of the Commissioner for Superannuation to Dr Geoffrey Conron, psychiatrist, for his report. Dr Conron reported, inter alia :

'In March 1978 she was medically retired from the Public Service, and for the next few months she felt low, but has recently felt less depressed than she did. She lives with her mother who relies on her a lot for advice and support, but her mother in fact does all the house keeping. She has plenty of friends and goes out with them for lunch. She is currently having a course of treatment for her excessive perspiration but is on no psychotropic medication.

At interview Miss Hendy seemed tense and expressed some anger toward some of her previous bosses, and some of her medical attendants. She is over weight, but does care for her appearance, and shows no behavioural evidence of depression, although she does say that she is an unhappy person who rarely enjoys herself socially.

I think that she shows evidence of a mild anxiety depressive reaction and that her perspiration is probably largely of emotional origin. I think that a position as a steno-secretary grade 2 may be beyond her at present, but she is certainly fit for work as a typist which would carry a lesser degree of responsibility. It may be that Miss Hendy would consider working as a typist personally unacceptable to her as it would be lower on the hierarchy. In view of her current attitude toward seeking psychiatric treatment, I don't think that further psychiatric intervention has much to offer at present.'

As a result of that advice from Dr Conron, the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation concluded that Miss Hendy's health had been restored to the extent that she could perform duties such as those of a typist or clerical assistant and he directed that the Regional Director of the Public Service Board, Melbourne, be informed with a view to finding her suitable employment.

Some of the problems with this case may have arisen from the terms of Dr Conron's first report. Dr Conron gave evidence before the Tribunal of his view that it would be better for Miss Hendy if she went back to work. He said that he had had a good deal of success in getting people back to work and that he thought that, if Miss Hendy went back into a sympathetic environment, she may be successfully rehabilitated. Dr Conron did not put his first report precisely in those terms. Dr Conron subsequently reported on 16 December 1982 as follows :

'Thank you for asking me to report on Miss Hendy whom I interviewed on December 15, 1982. In summary her neurotic anxiety depressive symptoms are substantially unchanged since my last report dated 26.5.80.

Miss Hendy is now aged 34 and since last March has been living alone in a West Brunswick townhouse. She told me that she always felt tired and depressed and sometimes nauseated with a grabbing feeling in her stomach. She said that she found everything an effort and got particularly nervous if she was with crowds of people, and on these occasions she would perspire and this perspiration was an embarrassment to her. She said that if she had to go to a wedding she would take a couple of Serepax beforehand and she has been worried about going to her nephew's wedding in three months time. She said that she avoided going to parties or the theatre but did visit friends if she was invited and she has a couple of old friends who drop in on her fairly regularly.

In talking about her depression she said that she 'didn't find anything nice' and that sometimes she felt that life was not worth living but she did not experience suicidal ideation. She is also concerned about her weight.

In talking about treatment, Miss Hendy said she had consulted a psychiatrist, Dr Scott in Kew while she was still working in 1977 and subsequently saw a psychologist, Dr Sandra McNeill for around 6 visits. She said that she had seen a psychologist for hypnotherapy at Doncaster late in 1980 or early 1981 and had six visits. She has not sought psychological treatment since then. She said that she sees a doctor in Kew every month for injections which help prevent boils and she sees her family doctor, Dr Bignell, less often than every few weeks, and he gives her a prescription for Serepax 30 mgs. She averages taking half or one Serepax tablet per day and particularly uses them if she is not sleeping well or if she is going to a social engagement like a wedding.

Miss Hendy told me that she drives over to Northcote for her mother to visit twice a week but she sometimes argues with her mother. She visits her siblings infrequently. She spends her time keeping her flat clean and watching television and would admit to no other daily activities.

In talking about her job Miss Hendy said that she had been inappropriately angry with her boss in her last job as a steno-secretary and she doesn't feel that she could cope with this work. When I asked her if she could handle work of lesser responsibility she said that there was no way she would go back to work as a receptionist or put stamps on letters. She said that if she did this, that the rest of the people in the office would soon learn that she had been off work for a long time and was doing work of lesser responsibility, and that she would find this embarrassing.

I consider that Miss Hendy has chronic anxiety neurotic symptoms with phobic and depressive features in that these symptoms are genuine. She has understandable anxieties about returning to work and also a strong wish which is understandable, not to return to work of a lesser degree of responsibility.

At interview she showed evidence of moderate tension but was composed during our 50 minute talk and showed no evidence of psychosis and did not communicate depressive affect. In my opinion she is not fit to perform the duties of a steno-secretary but is fit to perform the duties of a typist or clerical assistant."

It appears that what Dr Conron actually had in mind was that Miss Hendy could undertake any work that was suitable for a steno-secretary with '...chronic anxiety neurotic symptoms with phobic and depressive features...' who '...has understandable anxieties about returning to work and also a strong wish which is understandable, not to return to work of a lesser degree of responsibility'. What Dr Conron had in mind was that Miss Hendy should be found a position in which she would be treated with a good deal of sympathy and understanding and hopefully would be rehabilitated into the workforce. This was a quite different matter from finding for Miss Hendy simply a job as a typist or clerical assistant. It was one thing to tell the Regional Director to find for Miss Hendy, who had been an excellent Steno-secretary Grade 2, a position as a typist; it was another thing to direct him to find a position suitable for a person with chronic anxiety neurotic symptoms, including phobic and depressive features, who was not motivated to return to work.

We accept that Dr Conron is a highly qualified psychiatrist who was attempting to do his best for Miss Hendy. However, there is a good deal of difference between rehabilitating patients who accept the advice of their psychiatrist and achieving that result for a person who is not the psychiatrist's patient and who is not motivated to return to work. If Miss Hendy could be persuaded to go back to work, of course that would be the best thing she could do. She is a highly qualified steno-secretary and, once motivated to work, rehabilitation could be successful. But the problem at the present time is that Miss Hendy is not persuaded she can go back to work and is not motivated to do so.

Dr Colin Scott, psychiatrist, who treated Miss Hendy in 1977, apparently took a generally similar view to Dr Conron. On 16 July 1982, he reported to Miss Hendy's solicitors :

I first saw Miss Hendy on the 30th of July, 1976 at the request of her General Practitioner, Dr Bignell.

At this stage she seemed to have had quite a number of problems during her life, more particularly over the twelve months prior to seeing me. She was three stone over weight, had quite marked self-deprecatory ideas and low self-esteem. She was angry and resentful about the lack of fulfilment in her life and what she saw as the many injustices in the world about which she felt powerless.

Cheryl left school at the age of fourteen and had worked since that time for various government departments as a typist. She was at this time a secretary to the local director of the Department of Conservation. She felt her job was frustrating and boring and she resented the boss's personal remarks about her and the inconsistencies in his behaviour. Over the previous twelve months she had been irritable and more quarrelsome at her work and seemed to be able to show righteous indignation when pursuing causes for other people, but when she had to protect herself she would burst into tears.

It seemed to me at this time that there were several areas in Cheryl's life which had brought about her unhappy state. She lived alone with her sixty five year old mother, who suffered from angina. Cheryl had developed a hostile dependent relationship with her mother whose care had become Cheryl's responsibility as her three other siblings had successively become married. Her mother pried into her personal life, refused to be left alone in the flat when Cheryl was out on a date, and insisted she should be picked up by Cheryl as late as three or four in the morning whilst being 'babysat' at an aunt's house or with other members of the family. At some level Cheryl recognized her dependency on her mother and was prepared to admit that it was convenient in many ways for her and although she complained about her mother she knew that being on her own without her mother would make her feel insecure. This seemed one obvious area for sorting out.

Cheryl had been going out with a twenty eight year old Greek man for some eighteen months. He was an old world Greek with patriarchal philosphy intact. He saw women as inferior and as objects of convenience. Apart from this Cheryl resented the time he spent with his family and although she felt very strongly for him she realized that if she wanted to marry him then she was going to have to put in a lot of work on changing his attitudes. He did not want to get married.

Cheryl spent a fair bit of time talking about the change in the social order that she had observed over the past ten or fifteen years. She felt that during this time women had fallen from their previous place of high respect and now were objects for men to leer at. She objected to nudity in films, strippers and the uncouth language she observed in night clubs. She said that this was a lot of the reason why she lived a more restrictive social life than previously. My feeling at this stage was that she was an over weight, frustrated, narrow minded young lady who was depressed and anxious as a result of becoming gradually worn down by frustration she felt powerless to confront and resolve. She saw herself as being the victim of other people's wishes.

I saw Miss Hendy for five sessions subsequently advising her to be assessed for group therapy. However she failed to attend for the assessment and was not seen again. She was next seen by me on the 27th of November, 1980, this time she requested assessment for superannuation purposes. She felt she was unable to return to work as she would not be able to cope with the work. She had recently been assessed by a superannuation board doctor who felt she should go back to work. She was appealing against this and was hoping that I would be able to give confirmatory evidence to oppose her return to work.

I was unable to do this as she appeared to me to be in no way distressed. She said that she did experience depression from time to time but otherwise lived quite a full life, seeing a lot of friends and was not consistently depressed.

She is fearful that should she return to work that she would burst into tears and not be able to work effectively. She was worried she would thereby take days off, and would not be paid for this time and be sacked or forced to resign. If this occurred, then she would lose superannuation and be in trouble financially. At this time she was buying a town house which was at that time rented.

My attitude to her was to try to get her to attempt to look at things from the perspective of herself as an individual. I felt that continuation of superannuation was a means of prolonging invalidism which I felt was not in her best interests. I told her that I felt that the real issues were to do with why she had difficulties with personal relationships and why she did not see her life as fulfiling. I told her that I thought that a more realistic attitude would be to attempt to go back to work and make the best of it and at the same time have some form of psychotherapy to cope with the issues as they came up, and to get to understand why she had the sort of difficulties she had.

I arranged to see her again to discuss this whole matter further and she appeared keen to do this. I told her that I thought I could not substantiate her claims to be unfit for work and that if I did so I would be certifying her to chronic invalidism which I did not feel would be in her best interests. However, Miss Hendy failed to attend for her follow up appointment and I have not heard from her since."

It should be noted, however, that, notwithstanding his view, Dr Scott was unsuccessful in keeping Miss Hendy in employment or in getting her to group therapy. It was after treatment by Dr Scott that Miss Hendy took sick leave and was later invalided out of the Public Service.

Before the Tribunal, Dr D.A. Sime, forensic psychiatrist, gave evidence. His view of Miss Hendy's problems coincided to a large extent with those of Dr Conron and Dr Scott, but he expressed the view that it was unlikely that, after this time, Miss Hendy would get back to work and that an attempt to force her back to work at this stage was likely to precipitate an acute depressive state and that it would be unsuccessful. He further expressed the view that Miss Hendy's depression had phobic features and that she had a phobia concerning groups of people and particularly crowds. Dr Bignell, who is Miss Hendy's general practitioner, expressed the same view. He and Dr Sime both thought it unlikely that Miss Hendy would go back to work in a typing pool or in a crowded office, for it was in the area of interpersonal relationships that the problems had previously occurred. Both expressed the view that, if Miss Hendy were to go back to work, it was much more likely that she would go back to work as a personal secretary for a particular person and therefore as a steno-secretary rather than as a typist in a group situation. Both thought it was not the pressure of the work as much as the interpersonal relationships in the office environment that had given rise to Miss Hendy's problems.

Dr Sime gave this evidence :

'If you were treating her are there any steps you would want to undertake in order to rehabilitate her back into the workforce?-The problem is she has been under psychiatrists to an extensive degree. She has seen several psychiatrists and considerable efforts have been made to help her with her complicated emotional problems, other than the work situation. Although she is coping, I think with the help she has been getting it is not at a level of recovery, it is a propping up situation. In many ways she is a very inadequate person and she needs constantly propping up. That is the treatment concept I would envisage _ after all, the doctors have already tried to get her back into the work situation and it has failed. It has become chronic; the longer the time, the worse it is. I cannot see this working now.

Are you saying you cannot see in the foreseeable future any rehabilitation that would be sufficient to get her back into the workforce?-No, I personally cannot.'

We agree with the statement of principle by the Tribunal in Re Grover and Commissioner for Superannuation (delivered 22 July 1983), where the Tribunal said :

'S.75(1) speaks of the health of a person becoming 'restored'.

'Restored' in that provision does not mean completely restored to the same state of health as before. It is a qualified state of restoration. The person's health is to be 'so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind ... suitable to be performed by him'. And what is so suitable is to be determined having regard amongst other things to 'the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement'. The question here is whether the applicant's state of health has been restored to that extent.

We do not agree, as counsel for the applicant suggested, that for the section to apply the applicant must be so restored to health as to be able to resume work so closely identifiable with her former work as to be 'of a similar nature to her pre-accident work'.'

On the facts, however, we think that the view expressed by Dr Sime and Dr Bignell is the correct one. We think that Dr Scott was unsuccessful in overcoming Miss Hendy's problems. Her problems, including the phobic reaction which she has from being with other people, are considerable. We think that Miss Hendy did not accept Dr Scott's suggestion of group therapy because her troubles were too deep-seated for that. She was alienated from society and had an aversion to people. Dr Sime expressed the view that only long-term psychotherapy was likely to provide adequate treatment and he thought that the expense of that was hardly warranted, having regard to the limited prospects of success. We are inclined to think that Dr Sime's view that it is unlikely that Miss Hendy will return to the workforce is correct. Dr Bignell expressed hope that Miss Hendy would, in due course, return to the workforce and thought that he had seen improvement since Miss Hendy had left her mother's home. He hoped that, in due course, she might overcome the problems which he considered to have arisen from the family relationships. However, even on Dr Bignell's view, it would not be proper now at the present time to attempt to force Miss Hendy to return to work.

We observed Miss Hendy during the course of the hearing of the review. She appeared to the Tribunal to be a person with deep-seated anxiety. We think that she would not, at the present time, be a suitable member of an office staff and that any attempt to force her into employment would be likely to precipitate acute depression.

In the circumstances, we think that the proper step is to set aside the decision under review.
