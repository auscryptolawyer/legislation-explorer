Robert Erwood Chalk and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 278; (1993) 30 ALD 537 (2 September 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ROBERT ERWOOD CHALK v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q92/593

AAT No. 8954

Number of pages - 5

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 278; (1993) 30 ALD 537

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

HONOURABLE JUSTICE R.J. BULLEY (Presidential Member), K.L. BEDDOE (Senior Member) AND A.M. BRENNAN (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Preservation of contributor's rights - eligible employee - late election - extension of time - failure to make adequate enquiry

Superannuation Act 1922

Superannuation Act 1976: Ss.17, 41, 30, 137(1), 157(1), Division 3 of Part IX

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Ors v Cohen (1984) 58 ALR 305

Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision 8553).

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307

HEARING

BRISBANE, 22 and 23 June 1993

2:9:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Thompson

Solicitors for the Applicant: Poteri Woods and Co

Solicitors for the Respondent: Ms Munoz

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that the decision under review be affirmed.

DECISION

HONOURABLE JUSTICE R.J. BULLEY, K.L. BEDDOE AND A.M. BRENNAN The applicant seeks review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent Commissioner made on 22 November 1991 (T3) and confirmed on review (T2). That decision was to the effect that it was not desirable to extend time in respect of a late election by the applicant to preserve benefits in the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme ("CSS").

2. Division 3 of Part IX of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") makes provision for preservation of contributors' rights to the Superannuation Fund where the contributor ceases to be an eligible employee. It is not necessary to consider these provisions for the purpose of deciding the present application except to refer to sub-section 137(1).

3. Sub-section 137(1) provides that where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to a pension or invalidity benefits under the Act, that person may, not later than 21 days after ceasing to be an eligible employee make an election, in writing, that Division 3 is to apply. Insofar as is relevant to this case such an election would allow the applicant to preserve his benefits in the CSS after resignation.

4. Sub-section 157(1) of the Act provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in the Act, where an election under the Act is made out of time for making the election and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the late election should be recognised, then the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed for making the election and the election shall have effect accordingly.

5. Mr Thompson of counsel appeared for the applicant and Ms Munoz of the Retirements Benefits Office represented the respondent. Oral evidence was given by the applicant and other witnesses. The documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administration Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were marked as an exhibit and other documents were also put into evidence.

6. At the time of the hearing the applicant was aged 51 years and employed by Telstra Corporation at its Caboolture depot. In 1957 the applicant became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1922. At that time the applicant was an employee of the Postmaster General's Department employed as a technician in training.

7. In August 1976 the applicant resigned his employment with the organisation then known as Telecom Australia. At the time of his resignation the applicant was employed as a technician stationed at a small country depot located at Cooroy in Queensland. We note that Cooroy is served by the main northern railway, is on the Bruce Highway and is approximately 130 km north of Brisbane and 25 km north of Nambour.

8. At the time of his resignation the applicant was living at Wamuran, which is 11 km from Caboolture but had previously been living at Pomona, a town close to Cooroy. The Cooroy depot was attached to Telecom's Nambour office, where administrative staff were employed. However, superannuation matters were handled by the Personnel (or Human Relations) Section of Telecom located at Brisbane.

9. The applicant resigned in 1976 following the birth of his daughter in 1974. We accept that the period following the birth proved to be a most difficult period for the applicant because of continuing problems with the well-being of his wife and his daughter. In the result, and we so find, the applicant decided that the health of his family demanded his full-time attendance at or in the vicinity of his home and he was unable to do this in his employment as a technician with Telcom. The result was that he purchased a farm in the Wamuran district which adjoined a property on which his sister's family resided.

10. We accept, and so find, that the applicant purchased the Wamuran property for the purpose of solving the problem caused by the needs of his family. We also accept, and so find, that the applicant felt an obligation to resign his employment as a technician because of the family situation.

11. Document T27/62 is a copy of a letter sent to the applicant at or about the time of his resignation. It is a pro forma pre-printed letter entitled "Acceptance of Resignation" addressed to the applicant at Wamuran and issued by the Personnel and Industrial Relations Department of Telecom Australia at Brisbane. The indicated relevant part reads:

"Refund of Superannuation.

You are advised in your own interests to complete and return at an

early date the application form for refund of superannuation

contributions which is attached."

Whether a form was attached is not known. If a form was attached, whether that form was completed and returned we do not know. Document T27/69, which is another but different copy of the letter, suggests that the letter was sent to the applicant at his work address at Cooroy. However, the letter is dated sometime in September 1976 i.e. after the applicant terminated his employment.

12. On 12 January 1977 Telecom again wrote to the applicant at Wamuran enclosing a form S2A for completion and apologising if the delay with refund of the superannuation contributions had caused any undue hardship (T27/64). An example of a form S2A is Exhibit 2 in these proceedings.

13. Notwithstanding the apparent absence of a completed form S2A the applicant was sent a refund of his contributions to the Superannuation Fund on or about 5 January 1977. The applicant acknowledged that he received the amount of $1949.41.

14. The applicant said in evidence and we accept that he had not been told about preservation of benefits in the Superannuation Fund. The applicant did not recall any documents being sent to him at the time of his resignation. Prior to the hearing he searched his personnel file in Telecom and could not find any evidence of a form S2A nor could he recall signing such a form. The applicant recollects receiving two cheques from the Superannuation Fund. The first being the cheque for an amount of $1949 and the second cheque being for $597. We accept the applicant's evidence that those funds were used towards paying a bank loan in respect of purchase of the Wamuran property but were not necessary for the purchase of the property. That is, however, contrary to what is stated in document T33/83 where the applicant stated that the amount of $1949 "was used to purchase land at Wamuran as an investment". The amount of $597 was said to have been invested in shares.

15. In 1978 the applicant rejoined Telecom as an assistant technician at Caboolture. Subsequently he was appointed as a permanent officer and thereby again required to contribute to the CSS. The applicant said in evidence that he was not given any information about preservation of benefits at the time of his re-entry into the CSS.

16. He first became aware of the right to preserve benefits in the Fund from a union journal "Teletechnican" issued for August-September 1987 (Exhibit 4) which discussed in some detail the system for preservation of benefits. Nothing was said in that article about the right to apply to preserve benefits on a late election but it did recommend checking with the Retirement Benefits Office.

17. In 1990 the applicant became aware of the right to make a late election to preserve benefits from another magazine article. The article headed

"Superannuation - Resigned and re-employed. Do you know your

rights?"

appeared in the June/July 1990 edition of "Tele Tech" which we infer to be the successor of "Teletechnician". We understand that both magazines were published by the Communications Workers' Union.

18. By letter dated 25 August 1990 the applicant wrote to the respondent referring to his resignation from Telecom in August 1976 and section 157 of the Act, and requesting permission to make a late election to defer benefits. Hence these proceedings.

19. In 1987 the applicant, having become aware of the right to preserve benefits, made what must have been general conversational enquiries of persons working at the Caboolture Exchange. He said that he did not direct any enquiry to the Telecom Office in Brisbane because that would be contrary to the culture in Telecom at that time. He thought that it was appropriate to ask such questions of his immediate superiors who he understood to be more aware of "personnel business" than he was.

20. When he rejoined Telecom in 1978 the applicant asked a different question. He then asked whether he could obtain continuity of service with his previous service in relation to his superannuation. As we understand the applicant's evidence he was given a negative response. However, the applicant's answers on this aspect in cross-examination were evasive and not responsive. We have been left to speculate as to the extent of the enquiries made by the applicant in 1978. In these circumstances we must find that the applicant made informal enquiries about continuity of service but did not pursue the matter with people such as the Human Resources Branch of Telecom, his union or the Retirement Benefits Office - people it may be thought would be in a position to advise the applicant. As we will show later, it is doubtful that two of these organisations were in fact capable of supplying such advice.

21. We cannot escape the conclusion that the applicant did not make any enquiry of persons who might be thought expert as to preservation of benefits on resignation. In our view his clear intention was to make his living from the farm with no real intention of returning to public employment at the time of his resignation. He recognised that the resignation was a "very big step", however, caring for his family had become his first priority.

22. Evidence was given by Mr J T Eves who was the applicant's supervisor at Nambour when the applicant was employed at Cooroy. He said in evidence that he had not been aware of the right to preserve benefits in the CSS. It followed, he said, that such information would not have been passed on to officers in the field such as the applicant. Furthermore Mr Eves had no recollection of receiving circulars on superannuation in the course of his employment but was equivocable as to whether he would have received any such circulars.

23. The applicant also called what can only be described as evidence of similar cases to establish how Telecom dealt with superannuation refunds of other persons. That evidence was apparently led for the purpose of establishing that the procedures followed in other cases would also be likely to have occurred in the applicant's case. Mr Licciardello, who resigned from Telecom in 1979, said he was sent a form S2A (Exhibit 9) in 1979 by Telecom on which two options (lump sum benefits and preservation of rights) were deleted on the form before he received the form. Mr Licciardello had become a contributor to the CSS in June 1978, ceased to be a contributor in 1979, applied for a refund of accumulated contributions in 1979 and was so paid in 1979, resumed employment and again became a contributor in March 1982. He applied to make a late election to preserve benefits in July 1990. After a lengthy consideration the extension of time was refused in August 1992. The reasons are set out in Exhibit 10. We have not obtained any assistance from Mr Licciardello's case.

24. Evidence was given by an industrial officer of the Communications Workers' Union through whom documents relating to two union members were tendered. These union members are a Mr Lillie and a Mr Hamilton. The documents are themselves self-explanatory.

25. A favourable result came about in the case of Mr Lillie, set out in Exhibit 12. In that case the applicant failed to sign and failed to delete any of the three options set out on form S2A. For reasons which are not clear but seem to revolve around the fact that Mr Lillie had placed his refund of contributions into life assurance and he had not in fact made any election in 1980 (Exhibit 11), time to make an election to preserve benefits was extended from April 1980 to July 1990 by the Commissioner's delegate. There is nothing in the reasons to say why it was thought desirable to extend the time as required by sub-section 157(1) but it seems to have been accepted that because Mr Lillie had not signed the Form S2A it may not have been completed by him at all and perhaps never seen by him - on Mr Lillie's assertion as recorded in the Exhibit.

26. In the case of Mr Hamilton, his matter is pending before this Tribunal. It is therefore not appropriate that we make findings in fact in relation to his case except to say that although Mr Hamilton elected on a form S2A to be paid a lump sum benefit he was in fact paid a refund of contributions - one of the alternatives he had deleted on the form S2A.

27. Evidence was also given by Mrs M V Smith, a personnel officer employed by Telecom. We have not gained any assistance from Mrs Smith's evidence except to conclude, as she said in answer to a question from the Tribunal, that she did not have a great interest in superannuation when she was a member of the CSS. She preferred to refer enquiries to the respondent's office.

28. By contrast, evidence of Miss K M Clarke, also employed in the personnel area of Telecom, revealed a considerable understanding of the work undertaken in relation to staff movements. She identified the letter to the applicant in September 1976 accepting the applicant's resignation (T27/62 and T27/69) as having been signed by her. She also acknowledged that there was a practice of sending a "pro forma" form S2A to the respondent in cases where they were unable to obtain a signed form. However, a form would be sent to the contributor as a blank form to be filled in by the contributor. Miss Clarke said it was part of her responsibility to complete the required details on the form S2A after completion by the contributor. It was not her function, however, to advise staff on their rights under the Superannuation Act and she was not expected to have a detailed knowledge of the Act.

29. The applicant also led evidence from a Mr G C Theaker, Assistant Secretary of the Communications Workers' Union in Queensland. That evidence was led to establish that prior to 1987 there was a general lack of understanding of the right to preserve benefits on resignation among Telecom employees. It would seem from the evidence that the Union also had a lack of understanding of the relevant provisions of the Superannuation Act. Further, there was apparently no real understanding of the right to make a late election to preserve benefits in the scheme until around 1990 when the Union published the article which is marked as Exhibit 5.

30. Exhibit 15 is an extract from the Superannuation Manual used by Telecom personnel staff and also by the Union. Paragraph B10/3/12 reads as follows:

"On resignation a contributor is entitled to a refund of

contributions with interest accumulated at the earning rate of the

Fund from time to time."

Paragraph B10/3/14 lists the forms available from the Personnel Officer when applying for benefits. Listed as (c) is the following:

"Applications for Refund of Accumulated )

Contributions Lump Sum Benefit or )

Election for Preservation of Superannuation rights ) Form S2A"

The index to forms, as does the form itself, clearly draws attention to the alternatives available but the text of the manual only describes refund of accumulated contributions.

31. Perhaps even more misleading was a pamphlet issued by Telecom in June 1977 to its employees which contains the equally inadequate statement that on resignation a contributor is refunded all contributions plus interest (T30/80).

32. Mr Theaker said quite frankly that it was the practice of the Union to rely on the Superannuation Manual without recourse to the Superannuation Act.

33. The respondent called Mr P M Scott, a former officer of the Human Resources Branch of Telecom. Mr Scott gave evidence about the work of this branch of Telecom. That evidence included evidence to the effect that his area of the branch relied upon personnel practice manuals prepared by Telecom and also a Superannuation Manual in the course of their work. The problem with Mr Scott's evidence is that it does not connect with the circumstances of the applicant either on his resignation or upon his re-appointment to Telecom in May 1978. The evidence was general and even speculative as to what happened at the relevant times. The Tribunal got little assistance from Mr Scott's evidence. The evidence did establish, however, that it is more likely than not that the applicant would not have been given any advice by Telecom staff about preservation of benefits in the CSS on resignation.

34. Mr Scott said that queries on superannuation were referred to the "Superannuation Board" (sic) and responses to those enquiries were received. There is, however, nothing before us to suggest that any such enquiry was made on the applicant's behalf except document T35/87 which relates to an enquiry about the payment of the refund of contributions requested by the applicant.

35. Mr Scott made it clear that although he knew about rights to preserve benefits in the CSS he did not know about provisions for making a late election to preserve benefits.

36. Mr Thompson of counsel, who appeared for the applicant, contended that the trustees of the Superannuation Fund had an obligation in equity to the applicant through Telecom as the trustees' agent. That included a duty to keep the applicant informed of his rights and due to ignorance Telecom failed to keep the members of the fund properly informed. While we agree that the persons administering the scheme have a duty to keep the contributors informed about the Fund, we have been unable to find any basis in the Act for saying that the trustees appointed under section 30 of the Act had any obligation including any duty to keep contributors informed of their rights under the Act. The trustees managed the Superannuation Fund itself (section 41) but were not responsible for the administration of the Superannuation Act. The Commissioner for Superannuation has the general administration of the Act (section 17). We are not satisfied, for these reasons, that the trustees had any obligation to keep the applicant fully informed of his rights or to apply any discretion in the applicant's favour.

37. We are satisfied, however, that there was a general insufficiency of information promulgated to contributors by the respondent and by the employing agency Telecom. One need only refer to Telecom's own documents such as T27/62 and Exhibit 15 to establish a failure to fully explain rights. The administration of resignations from Telecom in the late 1970's seems to have proceeded on the incorrect assumption that a resigning contributor to the Superannuation Fund was only entitled to a refund of contributions plus interest, and we so find.

38. We accept, and so find, that when the applicant rejoined Telecom in 1978 he was concerned to ensure he maintained continuity of service with his previous employment in Telecom. It is not clear to us, however, that the applicant ever made any concerted effort to ascertain his rights. He seems to have been content to rely on his colleagues and immediate supervisors. It was not suggested that he made enquiries of Telecom's Human Resources Branch in Brisbane. The applicant said that technicians do not do such things. That may be the applicant's perception, and we accept that it may be honestly held, but it is not the attitude of a person intent on ascertaining his rights. In the same context the applicant apparently made no contact with the Union nor the Retirement Benefits Office.

39. Although Telecom seemed to have a communications problem when advising their employees of rights under the Superannuation Act the evidence of Miss Clarke made it clear that the right to preserve benefits was understood by some in the area of Telecom handling separations. The evidence of Mrs Smith points in the other direction as does the evidence of Mr Scott.

40. As Ms Munoz correctly submitted, this Tribunal must be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the late election should be recognised. It is for the applicant to satisfy the Tribunal that the discretion should be exercised in the applicant's favour. In effect the Tribunal must be satisfied that it is desirable to extend the time for making the election. The leading authorities on extensions of time in the context of administrative law are discussed in the judgment of Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Ors v Cohen (1984) 58 ALR 305. The following principles distilled by his Honour from the cited authorities are set out in the judgment at pages 310-311 and can be summarised as follows:

(a) the prescribed period for making an application is not to be

ignored;

(b) the applicant must show an acceptable explanation for the delay;

(c) the applicant must also show that it is fair and equitable in the

circumstances to grant the extension of time sought;

(d) it is relevant to consider whether the applicant has "rested on

his rights" so as to lead the decision-maker to believe that the

matter was finally concluded;

(e) prejudice to the respondent occasioned by the delay is a material

factor mitigating against an extension of time, but the absence

of prejudice will not determine the matter in the applicant's

favour;

(f) the merits of the substantial application are properly to be

taken into account; and

(g) considerations of fairness as between the applicant and other

persons in the same position are relevant.

41. In the context of an application for a late election under section 157 of the Act we must add that this Tribunal must be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the late election should be recognised. In Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307 the Tribunal considered the meaning of "desirable" in circumstances not all that different in some aspects to the circumstances of this case. In that case the Tribunal took the view that "desirable" connotes a "positive aspiration" which is "something worthy of achievement" rather than "merely advisable" (paragraph 51 of D P Forgie's reasons). We note in passing that the cited report refers to "undesirable" but that word does not appear in the Tribunal's reasons (c.f. [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 at 470). The majority decided, because of the particular circumstances in that case, that it was desirable to extend time by nearly ten years.

42. The present case lacks some of the attributes of the facts in Liddle and is more akin to Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision 8553).

43. In particular, although the applicant had to contend with health problems of a serious nature with his family, his own health was apparently good at all relevant times. In contrast, at the time of Mr Liddle's resignation his mental capacity was adversely affected by his anxiety state and his personal circumstances. When taken together with the fact that nothing was done to assist Mr Liddle in relation to the Form S2A or his rights under the Act, it made it desirable to extend the time (paragraph 3 of majority reasons).

44. Taking all these considerations into account we have come to the view, on balance, that the decision under review should be affirmed. Our reasons for this view are that the applicant did nothing effective to discover his rights at the time of resignation and at the time of re- employment. Clearly he was concerned to establish continuity when he returned to employment at Telecom but he did not pursue his rights in the CSS to any reasonable level of enquiry. To suggest that his immediate supervisors, who were technical officers not expected to have specialised knowledge of the Act, were the appropriate persons to approach for advice is, in our view, naive in the extreme. That the applicant chose to rely on the advice of those officers suggests to us that he was not really concerned to ascertain his rights.

45. Certainly it is probable, if the applicant had made enquiries in the Human Resources Branch of Telecom or from his Union, that he would have received inadequate or incorrect advice. The evidence shows that the Union was content to rely on the inadequate manual supplied by Telecom and that the staff of the Human Resources Branch were also likely to rely upon the same manual.

46. However, it has not been suggested to us that the applicant was given inadequate or incorrect advice. He was not given any advice other than that of his immediate colleagues and supervisors. Accepting that advice is akin to accepting advice given in the public bar of an hotel at 7 pm on a Friday night. No reasonable person would rely on that advice and if they do they must bear the consequences.

47. There is nothing in this case that attracts us to the view that it is desirable to grant the late election. We must give weight to the time limit in the Act, that there is no acceptable explanation for the delay - we do not regard ignorance of the law per se to be an acceptable explanation and we are satisfied that the applicant had no real interest in the matter until later in his life when he realised he had made a mistake (whether wittingly or unwittingly) with the benefit of hindsight.

48. We are not satisfied that if the applicant had been given adequate advice about preservation of benefits on the day he resigned that he would have done any differently than accept a refund of his interest in the Superannuation Scheme. Nor can we be satisfied that with adequate advice he may have sought to make a late election to preserve benefits when he returned to employment with Telecom.

49. In these circumstances we are not satisfied that it is desirable to extend time for the purpose of making an election under section 137 of the Act.

50. For these reasons the decision under review will be affirmed.
