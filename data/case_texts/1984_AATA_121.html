Re RM and Commissioner of Superannuation [1984] AATA 121 (16 April 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: R.M.

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A83/28

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr A.N. Hall (Deputy President)

Dr. A.H. Marsh (Member)

Mr. I. Prowse (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - invalidity retirement - less than 20 years' service - failure to disclose history of anxiety and nervous condition at entry medical examination - whether, but for non-disclosure applicant would have been admitted to Provident Account - Benefit Classification Certificate specifying history of nervous disorder, namely anxiety state; paranoid personality - whether paranoid personality a mental condition - whether incapacity causing retirement substantially contributed to by conditions specified in Benefit Classification Certificate

Statutes - on literal interpretation section incapable of application - purposive construction

Constitutional law - s.51(xxxi) - whether applicant had a vested proprietary right to pension under the Superannuation Act 1922 - whether right acquired by the Commonwealth pursuant to Superannuation Act 1976 - inchoate right to pension not property - extinguishment and replacement of right not acquisition

Law Reform - observations on drafting defects in s.184(5) of the Superannuation Act 1976

Acts Interpretation Act 1901, ss.15A, 15AA

The Constitution, s.51(xxxi)

Superannuation Act 1922, ss.38, 48B

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.5, 16(4), 19A, 37(2), 38(2), 66(2), 76A, 82, 184(2), (5), (7)

HEARING

MELBOURNE

16:4:1984

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review,and in substitution therefore now decides:

1. To issue in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate pursuant to s.184(5) of the Act specifying the conditions 'History of nervous disorder, namely anxiety state; paranoid personality'; and

2. In accordance with s.66(2)(c) of the Act, the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant was substantially contributed to by the mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, namely paranoid personality.

DECISION

1. This is an application for review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation the effect of which was to limit the invalidity benefit payable to the applicant in accordance with the provisions of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act") following her retirement on the grounds of invalidity. The substance of the Commissioner's decision was that he was satisfied that, at her medical examination for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1922 (the superseded Act), the applicant had failed to disclose a history of a nervous condition that he considered to be connected with the incapacity that was the ground for her retirement.

2. The applicant commenced employment as a Clerical Assistant, Grade 1, with the Department of Health on 28 September 1970. She was then 31 years old, married and with four young children aged 12, 10 (twins) and 6.

3. On 27 July 1972 she underwent a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") for the purpose of determining her fitness for permanent appointment to the Public Service and for the purpose of determining whether her health and physical fitness were such as to justify her acceptance as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund ("the Fund") under Part III or to the Provident Account established under that Act (see s.5). In connection with her medical examination, the applicant had been required to answer a large number of questions concerning her previous medical history. The questions and her answers, included the following:

'Personal

Have you now or have you previously had any of the following conditions?

Answer 'Yes' or 'No' in space provided.

NOTE: All questions must be answered.

QUestion 1.

(a) Nervous fatigue No (g) Persistent headaches No

(b) Mental or nervous (h) Dizziness or turns No

conditions No

(c) Anxiety state No (i) Attack of unconsciousness or weakness No

(d) Neurasthenia No (j) Migraine No

(e) Fits No (k) Disturbance of sensation

or muscular activity No

(f) Epilepsy No (l) Persistent breathlessness

No'

It is these negative answers and the alleged failure of the applicant to make any reference to her previous history of a nervous condition that are central to the present application for review.

4. Following the medical examination, the Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended that the applicant be accepted for the Fund on the basis that her health was such that she was likely to give efficient and continuous service until the age of 65 years. The medical report and recommendation were signed by Dr Helen S. Turner. The Acting Senior Medical Officer concurred with that recommendation and on 25 August 1972 a delegate of the Superannuation Board certified that the applicant be accepted as a contributor to the Fund.

5. After completing her initial six months probation satisfactorily, the applicant was promoted to a position of Clerical Assistant Grade 2 with effect from 26 March 1973. On 19 October 1973, some seven months later, she was appointed to a position of Clerical Assistant Grade 4 in the Personnel Recruitment Section of the Department. The evidence is that she was recommended for the normal increments on 31 October 1974 and on 29 October 1975.

6. On 2 December 1977, the applicant was advised by her supervising officer that she was to be transferred to another section of the Department. She said that she was told that this was because of 'a complaint they had received from the Public Service Board over some recruitment work' (Transcript p20). Evidence given by Mr T.J. King, who was the applicant's supervisor in the Department of Health at the time, was that the reason for the transfer was because of inaccuracies that had occurred in her work over the previous two and a half years and because of her behaviour which was annoying and disruptive to other employees. Mr King referred to 'temperamental outbursts' and 'boisterous laughter' which were disruptive.

7. The applicant appealed under Regulation 33 of the Public Service Regulations against the transfer. That appeal eventually succeeded on the grounds that the applicant had not been adequately counselled as to her day to day duties nor as to her alleged inaccuracies.

8. On 6 December 1977, the applicant consulted her general practitioner, Dr I.L. Ferguson, complaining of the emotional upset caused by the transfer direction. He certified her unfit for work for four days from 6 to 9 December 1977 (Exhibit T4, pp84-85) and again from 13 to 24 December (Exhibit T4, p83). On her return to work, she found that she could not cope. On 3 January 1978, the applicant advised the Department that she did not wish to resume duties in her substantive position. She was requested to seek a transfer to another Section. She was ultimately placed in a position within the A.D.P. Branch of the Department. She said that she was not given a desk or a chair nor any work to do. She said that the treatment that she received at work had upset her terribly. She was later transferred to another Division. According to her sick leave records (Exhibit T4, p67) the applicant had a further absence from work from 31 January 1978 to 14 February 1978. On 28 February 1978 her doctor advised that she should have three months off work. Her sick leave record shows that she was absent from 27 February 1978 to 30 May 1978.

9. The applicant said that upon her return to work after the prolonged absence the situation was no better. In fact it just got worse and worse. She described herself as 'just not coping at all' (Transcript p22). Thereafter she had further absences from 9 June to 16 June 1978, an absence of some 2 hours and fifty-one minutes on 2 August 1978; she was absent on 10 August and 25 August 1978 and then more or less continuously until the date of her ultimate retirement on 12 February 1979.

10. The medical certificates furnished to the Department by Dr Ferguson covering the initial absences from work during December 1977 indicated that she had suffered an emotional upset 'due to unpleasantness relating to an involuntary transfer at work'. The medical certificates issued thereafter referred either to 'emotional upset', 'nervous disorder' or 'anxiety symptoms'.

11. At a medical examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 22 March 1979, the Commonwealth Medical Officer formed the opinion that the applicant was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the ground of invalidity due to 'anxiety' (see Exhibit T4, p53). The Medical Officer recorded that 'the patient has a history of anxiety neurosis dating back to eighteen months. She has been off work since February 12 1979 because 'I can't cope''. The Commissioner was advised accordingly and steps were then taken to retire the applicant on the grounds of invalidity as from the date on which she had last worked.

12. As the applicant's period of contributory service was less than twenty years, the Commissioner considered it necessary to investigate whether the provisions of s.184(5) of the 1976 Act were applicable (see Exhibit T4, p 62). Although we will need to refer to s.184 in some detail later, it suffices to say for the present that the question which the Commissioner was investigating was whether the applicant had failed to furnish any relevant information concerning any previous history of 'anxiety' at the time of her medical examination on 27 July 1972. It was as a result of these inquiries that the Commissioner ascertained that the applicant had a history of a nervous condition prior to her appointment to the Service as a permanent officer and prior to the date of her acceptance as a contributor to the Fund under the superseded Act.

13. It was not disputed by the applicant that between 21 September and 3 October 1967 she had been admitted to Canberra Hospital and that for part of the time (from 21 September to 26 September 1967) she had been in M Ward (the Psychiatric Ward). The Hospital records (Exhibit 1) disclose that she was discharged from M Ward at 4.00p.m. on 26 September 1967 to return to her home. She was readmitted, however, at 7.30p.m. the following day for further treatment. The final diagnosis recorded on her admission summary was 'anxiety state'.

14. On 6 March 1980, a delegate of the Commissioner wrote to the applicant advising that as a result of the investigations into her medical history he had formed the opinion that in connection with the medical examination in 1972 she had failed to furnish information as required in relation to her history of nervous condition; that the condition that was the ground for her retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by her nervous condition; and that, accordingly, a benefit classification certificate had been issued specifying that condition. As a consequence of this decision the applicant's only entitlement under the Act on retirement was to a lump sum benefit in lieu of a pension (see s.66(2)). On 6 April 1980, the applicant requested reconsideration of that decision pursuant to s.154 of the 1976 Act.

15. With the applicant's consent the Commissioner obtained from Dr Ferguson a summary of her psychological/medical history covering the period from 21 August 1967 to 10 September 1980 (see his letter dated 25 February 1981 - Exhibit T4, p119). That history, insofar as it relates to the period preceding the medical examination on 27 July 1972, was as follows:

Associated with 7 days rheumatism and 4 days infected throat. Nervous conditions (anxiety state) not treated.

16. Extracts from these medical reports were made available to the applicant who wrote to the Commissioner by letter dated 3 May 1982 challenging the accuracy of some of the history taken by Dr Ferguson. In particular, she commented that at no time had she complained of 'anxiety state'. She said that she was totally unaware of this condition until after the events of 1977. She pointed out that the periods of hospitalisation in 1967 were from 21 September to 26 September 1967 and then from 27 September 1967 to to 3 October 1967 - not to 9 October as stated by Dr Ferguson. She denied that she had been in a psychiatric ward; she implied that there was no reason for her original admission to Hospital on 21 September 1967 and that the only reason for her readmission on 27 September 1967 was to be near her husband who "was also in hospital at that time"; and that her husband's doctor had suggested that she might return to hospital to be with her husband.

17. The applicant criticised the questions in the Personal Medical Statement as 'badly worded'. She said that they required consideration of her then present and previous conditions; that her 'No' answers were correct so far as her then present condition was concerned; that she did not consider that she had had a 'mental condition', which she considered to refer to 'unsound mind'; nor did she consider that she had had a 'nervous' condition, which she considered to be an obvious visual condition such as twitching. She added that she understood 'neurasthenia' was a seldom used term and that its meaning was 'not fully appreciated by the medical profession'. Finally, she said, she had never been told that she had suffered from an anxiety state. The question form, she stated quite specifically, 'was required to be completed before attending the medical examination and not in the presence of the CMO'.

18. With respect to the information she had provided at her medical examination concerning the 1967 hospitalisation, the applicant wrote:

'I recall that at the medical examination in 1972 I was most apprehensive and the CMO commented that I appeared very nervous and questioned me in relation to this. I stated, and have consistently stated to all doctors whom I have consulted, that I was 'highly strung'. I told the doctor in the course of quite intense questioning that I had been in hospital in 1967 and was advised that it was 'more than 3 years ago'. It was at the end of this questioning that I was asked to sign the authority on the front of the form to obtain information from Dr Ferguson which I had not previously completed.'

19. At the hearing before the Tribunal the applicant verified under oath the correctness of the factual statements made in her letter dated 3 May 1982. She went on, however, to give evidence in relation to the 1972 medical examination that was at variance with those statements in a number of critical respects.

20. The applicant said in evidence that following her arrival for her medical examination a Sister had taken her blood pressure, a urine test and an eyesight test. A lady doctor then asked her to follow her. Although she did not recall the doctor's name, there appears no doubt on the evidence before us that the lady she described was a Commonwealth Medical Officer named Dr Helen Turner, who was also called to give evidence before us. The applicant said that half-way down the corridor a dark doctor, whom she presumed to be Indian (and who appeared subsequently to have been identified as Dr Crowe) came out from another room. There was a discussion between the two doctors, following which Dr Turner asked the Indian doctor would he mind seeing the applicant. Dr Turner then gave him the applicant's file. The applicant followed the Indian doctor to his room. She was told to strip down to her bra and pants and the Doctor then conducted an examination during which he made a comment evidently related to the stretch marks on her stomach due to childbirth. When the applicant informed the Doctor that she had had twins he commented on the fact that she had no varicose veins. The applicant said that the Doctor noted that her legs were very shaky and commented 'you seem to be very nervy'. She replied 'Yes, I am a highly strung person'. The Doctor then asked her to get down off the examination couch and touch her toes. He commented on her straight back and then requested her to get dressed.

21. The applicant said that she then went over to the table. The Personal Medical Statement that she had completed, which she thought was called a 'PSB 9 Form' and which she described as being a red and white form (and which in fact is a bilious yellow) was evidently on the table. She said that she had not completed the answers to Question 1 'because some of the words I did not understand'. She claimed that she then proceeded to tell the Indian doctor about her hospitalisation in 1967. Her evidence in answer to questions by Mr Higgins was as follows (Transcript pp14 and 15):

'Can you recall now as clearly as you can what you said to the Doctor about that question?-Well, he sort of started to tell me what they meant and he asked me why I had not ticked them and I said to the court orderly (?) that I had been hospitalised in 1967 for a period of seven days and he asked me why I had been hospitalised and I told him.

What did you tell him?-I told him that I had been put into hospital because we had just shifted from Balranald, where my husband was postmaster, and he had a very big bowel operation and I had contacted the - had four little children - and I had contacted the specialist who was operating on my husband and had a talk to him and he had told me that, you know, that he did not know what he was going to find when he operated on my husband, and he sort of talked about cancer, and with that, I, you know, wondered how I was going to cope with four little children on my own in Canberra, and Dr Ferguson - I went and saw Dr Ferguson ---'

The questioning later continued:

'... you told him about your husband's bowel operation?-Yes and I explained to him (that) Dr Ferguson had decided to put me in hospital if I could get someone to mind the children so that I could be near R. (name suppressed)

R. is your husband?-Yes and he just said - the CMO said, 'you look fine now and it is over 3 years so I would not worry about that' and he just flicked through the pages and with that there was a knock on the door and a lady doctor came to the door and he got up and went over and he just said a few words to the lady doctor.

Did you hear what it was he said?-No, I did not hear what he said.

Mr Hall: Was it the same lady doctor?-It was the same lady doctor who took me down the corridor and he said - she then came over to the table where the papers were and flicked through them, signed her name and said 'That will be all for now, thank you, (name suppressed).'

Mr Higgins: Just prior to that time, you have referred to the fact that the document was incomplete at the time when you spoke with the doctor who did the examination?-Yes.

When was it completed in the form that it is now?-It was completed in front of the main doctor.

Did you complete it?-Yes I did.

You completed it following the conversation about you having to go to hospital?-Yes I did.

And did he see what you wrote?-Yes he did.

Later in her evidence, however, the applicant was asked by Mr Higgins:

'Did you tell the doctor anything more about your hospitalisation? Did he ask you about any drugs you were given or anything of that nature?-No he did not - not that I recall. It was just over very quickly.

Did you tell him you had been anxious at that time about your husband's condition?- Well, I told him about why I was put into hospital and why I was upset about it but that is about all I recall at that time.'

22. In cross-examination, however, the applicant said that Dr Ferguson had told her, prior to her first admission, that he was putting her into hospital 'for a rest'. She recalled that she had been admitted on the first occasion to M Ward but that when she was readmitted on 27 September 1967 she had been admitted to another ward. She acknowledged that she knew what was meant by 'nervous fatigue', and 'mental or nervous condition'. She said, however, that she had not completed any of the answers to Question 1 until she had discussed them with the Commonwealth Medical Officer because there were certain terms such as 'neurasthenia' that she did not understand. She acknowledged she had suffered from migraine in the past (cf. Dr Ferguson's report - consultations on 27 August 1968 and 9 April 1969). The applicant said that she had not been told by Dr Ferguson that she had suffered from an 'anxiety state' in 1967. She acknowledged that she was aware that at the time of her admission to hospital she was depressed and agitated. She acknowledged that she shakes continuously. She denied, however, that she would ever have described herself to Dr Ferguson as having had 'bad nerves' or that she had ever contemplated suicide.

23. As the respondent had been unaware prior to the commencement of the hearing that it would be alleged that Dr Turner who signed the report of the medical examination had not in fact conducted the examination, no arrangements had been made for the obtaining of a statement by Dr Crowe nor was it possible, at short notice, to arrange his attendance to give evidence. However, Dr Turner was called. She said that, whilst she had no independent recollection of this particular examination, she was able to identify her signature as Commonwealth Medical Officer at the end of the report. Dr Turner also identified as her handwriting the various notes that had been written under the heading 'Medical Officer's Comments'. In relation to Question 13 on the personal statement in which the applicant had been asked to give details of all accidents, surgical treatment or operations, the applicant had written 'Nil'. Dr Turner had recorded in her own handwriting 'appendicectomy 10 years ago, tonsillectomy in childhood'. Under the heading 'Medical Officer's Report' Dr Turner had answered a series of 12 questions, the majority with 'Yes/No' answers. However, in relation to Question 1 - 'General appearance of candidate: physique, mobility, presence of deformities, etc.' - Dr Turner had written 'healthy average build'. In relation to Question 5b 'Are there any signficant scars, ulcers or enlarged glands?' Dr Turner had written 'No - McBurney's scar'. That scar, she said in her evidence, related to the appendicectomy.

24. There was also in evidence before us (Exhibit T4, p41) a copy of the clinical record card which was completed at the same time as the medical report. On that card Dr Turner had recorded in her own handwriting a series of comments which parallelled those that she had recorded on the medical report.

25. In answer to Question 6(b) on the Medical report - 'Is there any evidence of mental or nervous troubles?' - Dr Turner had recorded 'No'. Dr Turner said that if the applicant had told her of the period of hospitalisation including some days in M Ward in 1967 she would undoubtedly have made a note of it and would have investigated the matter further. She said that previous histories of mental problems were regarded by Commonwealth Medical Officers as very important. She said that statistics had shown that a lot of retirements on the grounds of invalidity were related to nervous disorders. She pointed out that the Medical Handbook which was prepared to guide Commonwealth Medical Officers in the conduct of medical examinations was very specific in requiring thorough investigation of cases grouped under the general heading of 'neuropsychopathies'.

26. The relevant paragraphs of the Medical Handbook, which were in evidence before us as Exhibit No. 3 were as follows:

'100(a) Candidates who have suffered from or who show symptoms of neuropsychopathies, e.g. nervous, emotional or mental illnesses, are not necessarily excluded from contributing for the superannuation pension or to the Provident Account; each case is considered on its merits on the basis of the guidelines in the following paragraphs. Nevertheless, it should be noted that neuropsychopathies are a common cause of prolonged sick leave leading to early retirement; experience in this respect has been most unfavourable.

100(b) In general, in cases of neuropsychopathies representing no more than:-

(i) a single episode which is not of a chronic or recurring nature; or

(ii) a stated episode, the diagnosis of which has not been completely substantiated,

and where there has been freedom from recurrence and absence of signs and symptoms for three years, consideration can be given to a recommendation for acceptance for superannuation benefits after deferment for the maximum period, or for Provident Account immediately.

Note: Cases involving a single episode of minor emotional disturbance resulting from stress, such as may occur after bereavement, marital discord, illness, natural disasters, social disturbances, unemployment and similar changes, should be thoroughly investigated. Such candidates may be considered for the Superannuation Fund, with or without a deferment period, according to the assessment of the severity of the condition by the examining medical officer which must be supported by a full history and documentation.

100(c) Cases of a more complex nature than those in (b) should be thoroughly investigated. If there is clear evidence of satisfactory treatment, freedom from recurrence for at least three years and no current signs and symptoms and no control by drugs, candidates may be recommended for the Provident Account; if there is clear evidence of these requirements being met over a period of five years, candidates may be considered for superannuation pension benefits.

100(d) Applicants suffering from a chronic or recurring psychotic or psychoneurotic illness or who manifest definite evidence of chronic personality disorders should be rejected for all purposes.

100(e) When recommendations are being formulated:-

(i) account should be taken of the nature of the duties to be performed or likely to be performed by the applicant during his or her working life; certain occupations (e.g. telephonist duties) are prone to aggravate any tendency towards neuropsychopathies; and

(ii) where the recommendation is for acceptance for superannuation pension benefits, or if there is doubt concerning acceptance for the Provident Account, specialist advice and tests should be sought, except for those cases covered by the Note in paragraph (b) above.

100(f) The results of investigations and medical reports should be incorporated in and/or attached to the appropriate form."

27. Dr Turner said that on the information since made available by Dr Ferguson as to the applicant's medical history prior to July 1972, she would not have considered that the applicant came with paragraph 100(b) or 100(d) and that paragraph 100(c) was the appropriate paragraph to apply. She said that, had the applicant's prior medical history been made known to her, she would have deferred a decision for the maximum period for eleven months with review at the end of that period having regard to her sick leave record and efficiency report. If they were all satisfactory, and if the applicant had not had any recurrence of her nervous complaints, she would have recommended that she be admitted to the Provident Account. In her evidence in chief, Dr Turner said that she would not have considered the applicant for admission to the Superannuation Fund. In cross-examination, however, Dr Turner acknowledged that in expressing that opinion, she had accepted the accuracy of the matters recorded in Dr Ferguson's report. She said that if a report had been called for from Dr Ferguson and she had been convinced by that report and the evidence as to the applicant's work performance that there had been no recurrence of the 1967 illness for at least three years, she would have recommended the applicant for the Provident Account immediately. She acknowledged that it would have been possible to have recommended the applicant for the Superannuation Fund if she had been free of any recurrence at the end of five years but that at this point of time she found it impossible to say what she would have done. One of the options available to her would have been to have called in a specialist psychiatrist on a consultancy basis.

28. Dr Turner said that the usual practice was that the patient would have completed the personal statement on the medical report before arriving for the medical examination. The person is called by a nurse who examines the patient's eyes, does the urine analysis and records height and weight. All the details are entered on the clinical card, the heading of which (details of the person's name, etc.) is filled in by a clerk at the counter. Dr Turner said that it was her usual practice that whilst the person was getting undressed, she would record onto the medical report the information entered on the clinical card by the nurse.

29. Dr Turner told us that Dr Crowe was a Commonwealth Medical Officer who was working in the Department of Health in July 1972. She could not recall ever having been involved with Dr Crowe in conducting a joint examination of a patient. She said that she had never signed a report of a medical examination where the examination had been conducted by another Commonwealth Medical Officer. She said that if she had asked another doctor to do an examination, she was sure she would leave the whole examination to that doctor. She said that she had never asked another doctor to commence an examination whilst she attended to other matters and then returned and taken over that examination from him.

30. Dr Turner also said that it would be most unusual for any other doctor to be involved in an examination which she conducted. The only circumstances she could imagine would be if there was a special problem such as a skin condition in relation to which one of the other Commonwealth Medical Officers may be a specialist. It would be appropriate in those circumstances to invite a second opinion. She said that she could offer no explanation as to why the applicant could imagine that she had been examined by Dr Crowe.

31. Dr Ferguson was called to give evidence on behalf of the applicant. He said that he had first been consulted by the applicant on 21 August 1967 and that the last consultation recorded was on 10 December 1981. He said that she had first consulted him concerning complaints of nervous symptoms and a throat infection. She had told him that she had suffered from bad nerves for twelve months following influenza. She had also said that she had palpitations and a dreadful feeling all over her body and a feeling that she could not do things quickly enough. Asked whether the term 'bad nerves' was her expression or his, he said that she had used that term. He could not recall her saying that she was 'highly strung'.

32. Dr Ferguson said that he had seen the applicant again on 30 August 1967. The applicant was complaining of two days' shakiness and a dreadful feeling. The children were noisy and messing the house. She felt depressed. He had noted 'with suicidal thoughts'. The depression commenced with a move to Balranald. She did not like Balranald because she was isolated and had influenza on arrival which precipitated the depression. She had told him that she had suffered from depression for six years since she had had twins ... She said her husband was unsympathetic. She had difficulty in handling her children from the age of three onwards. In relation to her family history she said that her mother worried a lot. She told Dr Ferguson that her husband took little part in punishing the children; that she was married when she was eighteen years; and children had been around ever since. She had had no fun since she was married. She never went out. She had had one holiday in ten years. Dr Ferguson said that he had diagnosed that the applicant was suffering from depression due to factors in her environment, i.e. exogenous depression. Dr Ferguson was unable to recall whether he had informed the applicant of his diagnosis.

33. Dr Ferguson said that he again saw the applicant on 20 and 21 September 1967. He had considered that at that stage she was suffering from an illness, namely an anxiety state with depression. He said that on 21 September 1967 her complaint was that she was at the end of her tether, she could not cope, her thoughts were racing and she had difficulty in remembering names. Dr Ferguson said that it was obvious to him that she needed some assistance at that stage. He accordingly recommended hospitalisation. Having regard to the severity of her illness, Dr Ferguson said that he had felt it best not to rely entirely upon his own judgment but to enlist the assistance of a specialist. Accordingly, he had arranged for Dr Merrifield to see the applicant following her admission to hospital.

34. Dr Ferguson confirmed that he had probably said no more to the applicant than that she needed a stay in hospital without explaining to her the reason for that recommendation (cf. his letter to the Commissioner dated 22 September 1982 -Exhibit T4, p145). However, he also confirmed the view that he had expressed in that letter that it was self-evident to the applicant at the time that she had a nervous disorder in the form of excessive nervous tension. In that letter he had gone on to say that it was principally because of her depression with suicidal thoughts that he had decided to hospitalise her.

35. Dr Ferguson said that he had recorded nothing in his notes about the applicant's husband's impending operation. He said that he could vaguely recall that Mr M. was about to have an operation and that this was of concern to the applicant. However, he said that he had not made any note of it because it was not very relevant to her complaints. In his opinion her complaints, at the time, were of matters other than her husband's pending operation. Whilst he had no recollection of the applicant being advised that she could go into hospital to be near her husband, Dr Ferguson said that it could well have been the case because she desperately needed help. He added '... and if she was going to be left at home in a situation where she had no support at all, I would have no reason to object to her going into hospital if that is what she wished, but again, principally because of the complaints other than his illness which she made to me'.

36. Dr Ferguson said that although the applicant had been discharged from hospital on 26 September 1967, he had been obliged to arrange for her readmission the following day. His recollection was that the arrangements were made by telephone. The applicant complained of not being able to cope despite the rosier appearance on the day before and it was agreed that the best solution was for her to go back to hospital. He said that prior to her discharge on 3 October, he had discussed with her why the illness had eventuated, and had explained that it was based on a combination of home matters, some of which could be improved. He said that it was also necessary to explain to her the purpose of the medication which he had prescribed and the side effects which could be dangerous if she was involved in activities such as driving or operating machinery or if she drank alcohol in conjunction with the drugs. He said that he also warned her against isolation when she became depressed. In other words, he said, she should avoid the circumstances where her thinking could become more depressed than if she was in company and the suicidal aspects of the problem might become fact.

37. Dr Ferguson said that, at the time, it was his opinion that the illness from which the applicant suffered had the capacity to recur. After trying Aventyl to which the applicant reacted adversely and Pertofran, Dr Ferguson finally settled on Sparine, an anti-anxiety drug as the appropriate medication for her. Dr Ferguson's clinical notes record that the applicant continued to use Sparine on occasions until at least 13 March 1968, although by that stage it appears that she had substantially recovered from the illness which caused her hospitalisation in September 1967.

38. Dr Ferguson said that if he had been asked to consider the applicant's history as at 27 July 1972 (the date of her medical examination), he would not have considered that she was suffering any mental illness at that point of time and that she had been, in fact, free of any symptoms of mental illness for not less than three years. He said that if he had been asked to express an opinion as at the end of February 1973, he would have considered that she had been free of symptoms for more than five years.

39. Dr Ferguson said that when the applicant consulted him on 6 December 1977, she stated she had been upset regarding her transfer on 2 December 1977. He would not have considered, however, that she was suffering at that time from a mental illness although an illness did develop over a period of several months. Reference to Dr Ferguson's report, dated 25 February 1981 (Exhibit T4, p121), indicates that he first diagnosed an anxiety state on 28 February 1978 when he advised that the applicant should take three months off work.

40. Dr Merrifield was called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent. He said that he had no independent recollection of having seen the applicant in 1967 but the records available from the Canberra Hospital (Exhibit 1) confirmed that he had seen her briefly following her initial admission to M Ward. The history which he recorded was as follows:

'For 18/12 tension, sensations of panic controlled with Valium etc. Last few days uncontrollable. Married at 18 ten years ago. Feels she should have got more out of life than she has. Mother intensely nervous just like her. Depression present but appears secondary to her chronic anxiety state. I have only seen briefly but will see in depth next visit.'

Further notes appearing in Dr Merrifield's handwriting under the date 22 September 1967 are as follows:

'Husband fortuitously in ward and interviewed ...

Despite her views he says, and she had to confirm, that she is a highly obsessional woman. Environmental manipulation will help. Part-time job, more attention from him, more interests for them both. I feel that acute symptoms will settle after which will be important to have close supervision to see that they implement the programme they propose.'

41. The Hospital records relating to the applicant's readmission on 27 September 1967 indicate that on admission the patient appeared agitated and claimed that that agitation was caused 'because my husband is going into hospital tomorrow'. A note dated 28 September 1967, apparently in the handwriting of Dr Ferguson, states that 'readmitted because of exacerbation of shakes, wanting to scream and being at end of tether following discharge'. Upon her ultimate discharge on 3 October 1967, Dr Ferguson recorded that the applicant's prognosis was good. Dr Merrifield said that he had agreed with that prognosis.

42. Dr Merrifield said that he did not see the applicant again until 14 August 1979 when she was referred to him by the Director-General of Health. In a report, dated 8 October 1979 (Exhibit T4, p59), Dr Merrifield recorded the history that he had taken from the applicant relating to the events of December 1977 and subsequently. At the time he wrote his report he was evidently unaware that he had previously seen the applicant in hospital in 1967. Based on the history which he had taken, Dr Merrifield expressed the view that there was no doubt or question in his mind that the applicant had a paranoid personality and that it was a consequence of that personality that she was angry and intensely bitter over the treatment that she had received at work and was unable to cope with stressful situations. He went on to comment that 'the personality is one that developmentally has always been present although it is not necessary that it could manifest itself and in the past she may have appeared a normal, happy contented person'.

43. Subsequently, however, the Commissioner for Superannuation wrote to Dr Merrifield forwarding a copy of Dr Ferguson's reports and of a letter obtained from the Royal Canberra Hospital covering the period of hospitalisation in 1967 (Exhibit T4, p150). Based on that information, Dr Merrifield furnished a further report dated 8 December 1982 (Exhibit T4, p169) in which he said that he believed that a person with the symptoms described by Dr Ferguson and the treatment given would 'have to see themselves as of a nervous diathesis' (i.e. a nervous pre-disposition). Some people, he said, can have a pre-existing condition and be unaware of it or unaware of its significance. He added, however, that he could believe that that would apply on the information presented. He expressed the view that, in all probability, the applicant would have seen herself as being of nervous disposition.

44. Dr Merrifield went on to say in his report that he considered the proper diagnostic label for the applicant condition to be paranoid personality. He added that unquestionably she does exhibit anxiety symptoms and the label of anxiety symptoms is perhaps less than accurate but not inappropriate. The paranoid ideation and anxiety, he said, are derived from the same source and are simply different ways of coping with her problem. Dr Merrifield's report continued:

'People such as (the applicant) who have a neurotic diathesis have to use psychic energy to maintain an equilibrium. Life is harder for them as a consequence. If internal forces become too strong or external forces operate then the equilibrium is distended and symptoms develop. In the pre-government employment era, the evidence is that the problems of family life appear to have tipped the balance and then anxiety depressive symptoms dominated. In the work situation, external factors clearly were inter-personal interactions and the symptoms became more definitely of the paranoid personality variety. Paranoid symptoms may have been present in the earlier manifestation but are certainly not documented and admittedly are more unusual when family difficulties are the precipitants. They are commonly derived from conflicts outside the family scene. (The applicant's) nervous diathesis must be seen as being the root cause of the paranoid personality.

Dr Merrifield concluded that in the applicant's case there had been shown evidence of neurotic symptoms in the 1967 to 1972 period and given that these come from a nervous diathesis they were seen 'as the fertile soil for the development of the retirement condition". The circumstances at work overwhelmed her substantially more because of the lack of an adequate 'reservoir of psychic energy' (see Exhibit T4, p169-70).

45. In his evidence, Dr Merrifield explained that a person with a nervous diathesis was simply a person 'perhaps we do not see as strong as other people'. Dr Merrifield emphasised that there was a difference between a paranoid personality and paranoia. He said that a paranoid personality was simply one of the normal variants of personality. Some of us, he said, are obsessional, some are tense, some are depressed. He did not regard a paranoid personality as abnormal nor was it an illness. He said that a paranoid personality did not describe a medical condition. He said that people who are nervous have to cope with their nervousness in some way or other. Probably from a very early time the applicant was capable of blaming other people for her nervousness. Other people might blame themselves and become depressed, or people might just go around in rages or with anxiety. In the case of the applicant she had a nervous predisposition and, in Dr Merrifield's opinion, she handled it by projecting things on to the outside world with her paranoid personality.

46. Dr Merrifield agreed that by the end of February 1968 he would have expected the applicant 'possibly to be in a state of quiescence at that stage' (Transcript p142). He said that between that date and 14 April 1972 there was nothing to indicate a recurrence of the anxiety condition for which she had been hospitalised.

47. Asked whether there was any relationship between the events of 1967 and those of 1977 to 1979, Dr Merrifield said that the applicant was showing stress on both occasions. In an analogy he appeared ultimately to regret, he likened her episodes of stress reaction to the humps of the Loch Ness monster. He agreed with Mr Higgins that the cause of the stress reaction in 1967 (which appeared to be related to family circumstances) was entirely different from the cause of the stress reaction in 1977 (employment difficulties). He was then asked by Mr Higgins: - 'So the two illnesses are entirely unrelated as illnesses, are they not?-There is no connecting link between A and B, no' (Transcript p143). Asked by the Presiding Member to explain this answer more fully, Dr Merrifield said 'I mean the anxiety or the psychological symptoms are manifesting at various periods over (name suppressed) life, that the symptoms of one episode do not necessarily have a connection with any other episode'. Q. 'You mean the cause of the symptoms?-The cause of the symptoms.' Q. 'But are you saying that the symptoms that are manifested are really not manifestations of the same condition, or that they are manifestations of the same condition?-I have said that they are manifestations of the underlying sort of paranoid personality. When the stress comes it reacts in that way and the stress is totally different in these two situations that were described." Q. '... that which causes the stress might be quite different?-Yes.' 'But are you saying that the reaction to the stress is the same or similar?-I say it is similar, yes.' Q. 'And are you saying, as I understand what you have just put to me, that it is the underlying condition that manifests itself in response to these different stressful situations?-Yes.'

48. In further questioning Mr Higgins elicited from Dr Merrifield that the 'underlying condition' that he had in mind when answering the above questions was the nervous predisposition and the paranoid personality - conditions which he had earlier said were neither abnormal nor a 'medical condition'. Dr Merrifield added, however, that people with a nervous predisposition, are more prone to exhibit symptoms than (as we understood him) others. In re-examination, Dr Merrifield restated his position that, in his opinion, the applicant has a personality that responds to stress. He saw the incidents in 1967 and from 1977 to 1979 as not connected in the sense that he saw them as being two episodes in her life. Asked by Mr Whithear whether he saw any connecting links, Dr Merrifield replied 'only her basic personality' (Transcript p149).

49. We also heard evidence from Mr T.J. King to which we do not feel the need to refer any further than we already have. We do not propose to try to unravel the rights and wrongs of the circumstances surrounding the applicant's ultimate retirement. Mr M. gave evidence confirming that prior to their arrival in Canberra in January 1967, he and his wife and family had resided for nine months at Balranald. He had then been transferred to Canberra. He said that prior to his admission to hospital in September 1967 his wife had appeared to be worried about his condition. He confirmed that he went into hospital towards the latter part of her stay in hospital. Other evidence before us indicates that in fact he went into hospital on 28 September 1967 a week after his wife was first admitted (see Exhibit 1). He said that he could not recall any other signs of her being upset at about the time he was to go into hospital. He could not recall having any discussion with Dr Ferguson prior to his wife's admission, nor could he recall any manifestations of depression on the part of his wife following her discharge from hospital. He had not heard his wife express any suicidal thoughts. He agreed that he had always considered his wife to be highly strung - by which he meant that she was 'anxious to get on with things, probably a bit impetuous'. He could not recall any incidents of shakiness on her part prior to 1967. He confirmed that she had been very upset by the events that had occurred at work in December 1977. Insofar as Mr M.'s evidence related to his wife's medical history in 1967 it presents, in our view, a somewhat rosier picture than she herself presented and in particular is inconsistent with the evidence of Dr Ferguson. In the circumstances, his evidence does not assist us greatly. Finally, we heard evidence from Dr R.K. Gupta, a practising psychiatrist, concerning the importance of the history taken by a medical practitioner. Having regard to the views that we have formed as to the merits of this matter, we do not feel it necessary to refer to that evidence.

Non-disclosure of History of Nervous Condition

50. Having considered most carefully the evidence before us and the able submissions advanced by Mr Higgins on behalf of the applicant, we are nevertheless forced to the conclusion that at her medical examination on 27 July 1972, the applicant either failed completely to disclose her history of nervous condition and the critical events of September-October 1967, or alternatively, that she presented such a false account of those events as to cause the Commonwealth Medical Officer to dismiss them as of no consequence.

51. In her letter dated 3 May 1982 (Exhibit T4, p127) the applicant presented a significantly different version of the critical events surrounding her admission to hospital and the disclosures that she allegedly made at the time of her medical examination to that which she gave in her evidence at the hearing. Yet both stories were verified on oath as being the truth. Not the least of these variations was the extremely serious allegation, involving, we would have thought, strong inferences of professional misconduct on the part of Dr Turner, that Dr Turner completed and signed a report of a medical examination that she did not conduct. If those allegations were true, we would have expected them to have been made to the Commissioner for Superannuation much earlier than they were.

52. We reject the allegations not only because of their inconsistency with the statements made by the applicant in her letter of 3 May 1982, but more importantly because they are completely inconsistent with the handwritten notations made on the medical report and the clinical record card. If the sworn evidence given by the applicant at the hearing is correct, the whole of the medical examination was conducted by Dr Crowe. Yet there is no record made by him on either the clinical record card or the medical report. Dr Turner was said to have taken no part in that examination whatsoever. She allegedly returned at the end of the examination, spoke briefly to Dr Crowe, 'flicked through' the papers and then signed her name. Nevertheless the writing under the medical officer's comments is that of Dr Turner and the information elicited with respect to the applicant's appendicectomy and tonsillectomy and the observation of a McBurney's scar were not disputed to be accurate records of the applicant's past medical history. Those details were not disclosed by the applicant in the answers to her personal medical history. They must have been elicited by questioning during the medical examination. Although the applicant gave a very detailed account of her recollection of the medical examination, of the colour of the form she had completed (which was incorrect), and of the comments made to her by the examining medical officer and of comments made in reply, she made no mention of discussing her previous operations. Indeed, when asked in cross-examination whether she had had an operation for an appendix or tonsils she answered 'the CMO did not pick that either'. When shown the form, however, she was obliged to acknowledge that the operations were noted.

53. It is, in our view, highly improbable that Dr Turner would have been prepared to accept the responsibility for completing the medical report based on information subsequently conveyed to her by Dr Crowe when her only contact with the applicant had been to escort her from the waiting room down the corridor and then to see her briefly when she was again fully dressed in Dr Crowe's rooms following the completion of his examination. If those had been the circumstances of the medical examination, we can see no reason why Dr Crowe would not himself have signed the medical report.

54. In our view, therefore, the probabilities are that the medical examination was conducted by Dr Turner. We reject the allegations to the contrary by the applicant. That conclusion, of course, casts such doubt upon the applicant's credibility as to place the rest of her evidence as to the critical events that occurred during the medical examination in jeopardy. We find it difficult to accept that a Commonwealth Medical Officer who went to the trouble of recording such relatively inconsequential details as a tonsillectomy carried out in childhood, an appendicectomy carried out ten years prior to the medical examination, and a 'McBurney's scar' would fail to make any notation if told of an admission as recently as five years previously to M Ward at the Canberra Hospital. The significance of such an admission could not have failed to impress itself upon a Commonwealth Medical Officer familiar with Canberra Hospital. We have no hesitation in accepting Dr Turner's evidence that had such a disclosure been made she would have investigated it most carefully.

55. However, even if we were wrong as to who conducted the medical examination, we are not prepared to accept that the applicant made a full and truthful disclosure as to knowledge of the circumstances surrounding her hospitalisation in 1967 (cf. Re Watson and the Commissioner of Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD 267). There is, firstly, the serious inconsistency between the allegations made in her letter of 3 May 1982 (Exhibit T4, p127) and her evidence before the Tribunal. In her evidence the applicant made no mention of any 'quite intense questioning' about her hospitalisation in 1967 (see her letter dated 3 May 1982). On the contrary, she said that the questioning was quite brief. Her evidence about not understanding Question 1 and having completed it only after discussion with the Commonwealth Medical Officer was quite inconsistent with the statements in her letter. Finally, even on her own evidence, she was aware that she had been admitted to M Ward 'for a rest' on 21 September 1967. Mr M. was not admitted to hospital until 28 September 1967, the day after the applicant's readmission on 27 September 1967. To have said, as she allegedly did, that the only reason for her hospitalisation on the first occasion was to be near her husband was simply not true. In our view, she must have known that there was more to her hospitalisation than that.

56. Although the accuracy of Dr Ferguson's clinical notes was challenged in a number of respects by the applicant, we are nevertheless prepared to accept the substantial accuracy of those notes and to accept his evidence as that of a competent and careful general practitioner. We do not think there is any doubt having regard to Dr Ferguson's evidence and indeed the evidence of the applicant herself, that the applicant was, at all material times, well aware that in September 1967 when she consulted Dr Ferguson she was a person who was excessively nervous; that she was seriously depressed and in need of medical treatment. We are satisfied from the history that the applicant gave to Dr Ferguson and to Dr Merrifield that an accumulation of domestic circumstances (of which her husband's impending operation was but a small part) had overwhelmed the applicant to the point where she felt at the end of her tether and unable to cope. It was in response to the severity of her condition that Dr Ferguson arranged, in her own interests, for her admission to M Ward.

57. There is no doubt from the evidence of Dr Ferguson, confirmed as it is by the specialist opinion of Dr Merrifield, that on her first admission to Canberra Hospital on 21 September 1967 the applicant was suffering from an anxiety state with depression and that on her readmission on 27 September 1967 she was suffering from an anxiety state. We are satisfied that Dr Ferguson did not inform the applicant that that was his diagnosis. However, we agree with the view expressed by Dr Ferguson that it must have been self-evident to her that she had a nervous disorder in the form of excessive nervous tension (see Exhibit T4, p145). Whilst it is probably a manifestation of her paranoid personality that the applicant should seek to justify in her own mind her admission to hospital in 1967 in the way that she has, we cannot accept that she was unaware on 27 July 1972 that the events of September-October 1967 constituted a serious episode of nervous disorder and that there was more to her admission to hospital on 21 September 1967 than simply to be near her husband (who was not admitted till one week later). Even if she was unaware of the precise diagnosis of her condition, the circumstances surrounding her admission should have been fully and truthfully disclosed. In our view, on her own evidence, she seriously misrepresented the facts which must have been within her knowledge at that time.

58. If the applicant had not failed in her duty of proper disclosure it is our view, having regard to the evidence of Dr Turner and to the relevant Medical Standards that the applicant would not have been granted entrance to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the 1922 Act. Although the episode of nervous disorder in 1967 would probably have been seen as a single episode within the meaning of paragraph 100(b) of the Medical Standard, it was, on the evidence, a condition that was likely to recur. In our view, having regard to Dr Turner's evidence, the most that would have occurred would have been that the applicant would have been admitted immediately to the Provident Account. It may be that some further consideration would have been given to the question of admission to the Fund after a period of deferral but, in our view, it is entirely speculative at this stage whether the applicant would ever have been recommended for admission to the Superannuation Fund. In the circumstances of this case we are not prepared to make any finding other than that, had the applicant made full and true disclosure of the 1967 episode, she would have been admitted to the Provident Account.

60. This section needs to be seen against the background of the new superannuation scheme introduced by the 1976 Act. Public servants who had been contributors either to the Fund established by the superseded Act or to the former Provident Account and who had not retired before 1 July 1976 became liable to pay contributions in accordance with Part IV of the 1976 Act and became entitled to retirement benefits in accordance with Part V of that Act. Simultaneously, the obligation to make contributions under the 1922 Act ceased (see s.19A and s.79A) and any entitlement under that Act to a pension or to a payment from the Provident Account on retirement on or after 1 July 1976 was also brought to an end (see ss.37(2) and 38(3) and s.82).

61. One other important change brought about by the 1976 Act was in the way that Act dealt with persons who, by reason of some physical or mental condition, were considered unlikely to continue in the Service until maximum retiring age. Under the 1922 Act such persons would, in the majority of cases, be accepted into and remain in the Provident Account, with consequentially limited entitlements on retirement. The scheme of the 1976 Act was that, in respect of such persons who became eligible employees after 1 July 1976, a benefit classification certificate (BCC) is to be issued specifying the condition or conditions considered relevant for the purposes of the Act (see s.16(4)). If, while the BCC is still in force, the person is retired on the ground of invalidity and before reaching his maximum retiring age and the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the person's retirement was caused or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition or conditions, the retirement benefits payable are limited (see s.66(2)(d)). Essentially, however, a BCC ceases to have any effect after 20 years contributory service and, in the event of invalidity retirement thereafter, full retirement benefits are payable. There are special provisions empowering the Commissioner to issue a BCC with retrospective operation in respect of persons who became eligible employees after 1 July 1976 who are retired within 20 years on the grounds of invalidity and who failed to make a relevant disclosure at their entry medical examination (see s.16(11)).

Contributors to the Previous Fund or Provident Account

62. Section 184, in its present form, deals with two categories of contributors whose period of contributory service is less than 20 years and who have died or retired on the ground of invalidity after the commencement of the 1976 Act before attaining their maximum retiring age:

(i) Eligible employees who were contributors to the former Provident Account (see s.184(2)); and

(ii) Eligible employees who were contributors either to the previous Fund or the former Provident Account established by the superseded Act who their entry medical examination failed to disclose a relevant physical or mental condition or furnished false information at that examination(see s.184(5)).

Where the physical or mental condition that caused the death or retirement was related in one of the specified ways to the pre-employment condition by reason of which the person was accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account (category (i) above) or by reason of which he would have been so accepted but for his failure to make a proper disclosure (category (ii) above), the evident intention is that a BCC is to be issued with consequently limiting effects upon the person's retirement benefits (cf. s16(11)). Of course, in the present case, we are not directly concerned with s.184(2) of the Act as the applicant was in the former Fund, not the Provident Account. However, as will become apparent, consideration of s.184(5), which is at the heart of these proceedings, necessarily involves some consideration of s.184(2).

63. Mr Higgins submitted, that even though the intended thrust of s.184(5) could be gleaned from paragraphs (a), (b), (c) and (d)(i) of that sub-section, there were so many deficiencies in the drafting of paragraph (d)(ii) and the operative words at the conclusion of the sub-section that, in the final analysis, the sub-section as a whole was incapable of any sensible application to anyone. Alternatively, in his submission, if the sub-section was capable of an intelligible construction, it could not constitutionally apply to a person such as the applicant who, at the time of her retirement, had completed more than three years' service after the date on which her" obligation to contribute tot he Fund established by the superseded Act had expired (see s.48B of the 1922 Act). We will return to this alternative submission later.

In our view, it is because the draftsman has attempted to deal, in the one sub-section, with all the various circumstances in which a relevant non-disclosure might have occurred, that the drafting has become so complex and, at first impression, baffling. However, the structure of the sub-section is such that it should, we think, be read distributively. Reference to paragraph 5(c) of s.184(5) discloses that the sub-section was intended to apply not only to a case such as the present where the person who failed at her medical examination to make a relevant disclosure was a contributor to the previous Fund, but also in respect of a person who was a contributor to the Provident Account established by that Act. In the latter case, what s.184(5) is concerned with is a person who, by reason of a physical or mental condition made known or otherwise detected at the entry medical examination for the purposes of the 1922 Act, was admitted to the Provident Account, but whose retirement within 20 years thereafter was found to be related to another physical or mental condition that the person had failed to disclose or in relation to which the person had furnished false information at the entry medical examination. That, of course, is not the present case. We propose therefore, to concentrate our attention upon the proper construction of the sub-section in its application to the circumstances of the present case.

There was no dispute that, within the meaning of s.184(5)(a), the applicant was a person to whom s.184 applied (see s.184(1) and the definition of 'existing contributor' in s.5(1) ); that her period of contributory service was less than 20 years and that, on the day on which she ceased to be an eligible employee,she had not attained her maximum retiring age (see s.184(5)(b) ); and that within the meaning of s.184(5)(c), she was a person, immediately before the commencing day ( 1 July 1976) was a contributor to the Fund established under the superseded Act (see definition in s.184(7)(a)). It was denied that the applicant had failed to furnish any required information or that she had furnished false information at her entry medical examination (s.184(5)(d)(i)) but, as we have said, we are satisfied that she did.

66. Some of the difficulties with sub-paragraph (d)(ii) tend to dissipate when the paragraph is read distributively in its application to a person such as the applicant who, immediately before the commencing date, was a contributor to the former Fund and when one identifies the words in the sub-paragraph the sense of which is best conveyed by putting them in parenthesis. Reading the sub-paragraph in that way, its main thrust emerges as follows:

'(5) Where

...

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied

(i) ...

(ii) that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information and (in a case where the person would not have been a contributor to ... the Fund immediately before the commencing day but for the failure to furnish that information or the furnishing of that false information) the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day, there would have been deemed to have been in force, in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement, a benefit classification certificate in which there would have been specified etc.'

67. In our view, the proper construction of sub-paragraph (d)(ii) is that the words which we have identified by parenthesis introduce an additional substantive condition precedent to the operation of sub-paragraph (d). Once this is recognised, the meaning of sub-paragraph (d) as a whole becomes clearer. What it requires is:

(i) that, at or in connexion with a medical examination which the person underwent for the purposes of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information;

(ii) that but for that failure or for the furnishing of that false information the person would not have been a contributor to ... the Fund immediately before the commencing day; and

(iii) that if the person had not so failed or had not so furnished false information and the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day there would have been deemed to be in force, in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement, a benefit classification certificate in which there would have been specified the physical or mental condition or conditions which caused, or substantially contributed to, the death or retirement or a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions.

Although, for the sake of exposition, we have done that which is impermissible, namely to rewrite sub-paragraph (d)(ii), we believe that the sub-paragraph as rewritten, exposes its proper construction.

68. We come now to the concluding portion of sub-section 184(5) commencing with the words 'the Commissioner shall issue'. Again, for the sake of clarity, we have isolated in parenthesis, the words that are identical with those which are placed in parenthesis in sub-paragraph (d)(ii). Thus, where all the requirements of paragraphs (a) to (d) are satisfied, the Commissioner is required to issue in respect of the person, a BCC in which 'there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified in the benefit classification certificate that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would have been the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified in the benefit classification certificate that the Commissioner would have issued ... in respect of the person under sub-section (2) if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information and (in a case where the person would not have been a contributor to the ... Fund immediately before the commencing day but for the failure to furnish that information or the furnishing of that false information) the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day ...'.

69. As before, the complexity of this portion of s.184(5) is considerably reduced if the elements of the provision are separated out more clearly. Essentially, as we understand the intended application of paragraph (d) in the circumstances of a case such as the present, the Commissioner is required to issue a BCC specifying the conditions that in his opinion would have been the conditions specified in the BCC that the Commissioner would have issued under sub-section (2) if:

(a) the person had not failed to furnish information, or had not furnished false information;

(b) the person would not have been a contributor to the Fund immediately before the commencing day but for that failure or the furnishing that false information; and

(c) the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day.

The certificate so issued is 'deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(11) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement'.

70. Mr Higgins submitted that even if it was possible to extract a meaning, such as we have attempted to do, out of sub-section (5), there remained a difficulty which he contended was insurmountable. Shortly stated, his submission was that the certificate authorised to be issued by the Commissioner under sub-section (2) is a certificate specifying, in respect of a person who was in fact a contributor to the Provident Account, the physical or mental condition or conditions that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act. By virtue of the definition in sub-section (7)(c), so far as relevant in the present case, that condition or those conditions were the physical or mental condition or conditions that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the condition or conditions of the person by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as justify his acceptance as a contributor to the previous Fund. In Mr Higgins' submission, as these events never in fact occurred in relation to the applicant, and as the Superannuation Board never applied its collective mind to the question of the applicant's entry to the Provident Account, there is no way that the Commissioner or, on review, this Tribunal could determine what was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the applicant that was or were 'relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act'.

71. On a literal reading of s.184(5), it must be conceded that these submissions carry considerable weight. But the consequence that follows, if they are correct, is that the whole of s.184(5) is an exercise in futility. The sub-section would, as Mr Higgins submitted, be incapable of application to anyone, because in none of the cases to which the section was evidently intended to apply would the physical or mental condition or conditions that are relevant for the purposes of sub-section (5) have been considered by the Superannuation Board.

72. Where the result produced by a literal interpretation of a section was plainly not intended, the Courts have been prepared to depart from that literal meaning in order to give effect to Parliament's clear intention (see, for example, Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1980) 147 CLR 297). Whilst one must be mindful of judicial strictures against 'a naked usurpation of the legislative function under the thin guise of interpretation' (see per Viscount Simon in Magor and St Mellons R.D.C. v Newport Corporation (1952) AC 189 at 190; and see the discussion in Pearce on Statutory Interpretation, 2nd Edn p184), the Federal Parliament itself has lent its support to the adoption of a purposive approach in the construction of Commonwealth statutes by s.15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 which provides as follows:

'(1) In the interpretation of a provision of an Act, a construction that would promote the purpose or object underlying the Act (whether that purpose or object is expressly stated in the Act or not) shall be preferred to a construction that would not promote that purpose or object.'

In our view, the interpretation of s.184(5) must be approached in this manner.

73. The major problem with s.184(5), which Mr Higgins' analysis so painfully exposed, is in the draftsman's attempt to adapt to a hypothetical situation, a power that, as framed, can only be exercised by reference to the physical or mental condition of a contributor that in fact resulted in the contributor being accepted by the Superannuation Board into the Provident Account (see s.184(2) and 184(7)(c)). However, a careful consideration of sub-paragraph (d)(ii) discloses, as we have already indicated, that, in order to be satisfied on the matters referred to in that sub-paragraph, the Commissioner is required to apply his mind, hypothetically, to what would have been the position under s.184(2) if the particular physical or mental condition or conditions had been disclosed (or if false information in relation to them had not been furnished) and the person had, as a consequence, been admitted to the Provident Account. The reference in sub-paragraph (d)(ii) to the benefit classification certificate that, on those assumptions, 'would have been deemed to be in force in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement' can only refer to a certificate that would have been deemed to be in force by virtue of s.184(2) but for the non-disclosure etc. Thus, for the purposes of sub-paragraph (5)(d)(ii) the Commissioner must have been intended to ask himself what would have been the physical or mental conditions relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act if a proper disclosure had been made and the person had been admitted to the Provident Account. The answer to the question entailed consideration not only of what would have been the condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board would have accepted the person into the Provident Account, but also of the questions of causation and the like posed by s.184(2)(c).

74. In our view, therefore, it follows that the certificate required to be issued pursuant to s.184(5), is the certificate that would have issued under s.184(2), on the assumptions referred to above. The physical or mental condition or conditions required to be specified is or are the condition or conditions that, pursuant to sub-paragraph (d)(ii), the Commissioner is satisfied would have been specified in the certificate deemed to be in force under s.184(2) in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement but for the failure to furnish relevant information or the furnishing of false information. Tortuous though this interpretation may be, it appears to us to accord with the language used in s.184(5) and to be entirely in accord with the apparent intention of Parliament.

75. Applying this construction of s.184(5) to the facts of this case, we have already found as a fact that the applicant failed to furnish information at her entry medical examination concerning her previous history of nervous condition (see paragraph 58). But for that non-disclosure she would, in our opinion, have been admitted to the former Provident Account rather than to the Superannuation Fund because of her history of nervous condition which, on proper investigation, would have been established as an anxiety state. Insofar as the evidence enables us to identify the cause of her anxiety state in 1967, it was due to her paranoid personality and nervous diathesis reacting under stress.

76. It was common ground between the parties that the anxiety state that was the ground for the applicant's retirement was neither caused by, nor was it substantially contributed to by the applicant's anxiety state in 1967. The evidence is that the anxiety states suffered by the applicant in 1967 and 1977 emerged out of quite different circumstances - the one out of domestic pressures - the other substantially out of a work environment. However, Dr Merrifield's evidence establishes that the two episodes of anxiety state were separate manifestations of the reaction of the applicant's paranoid personality and nervous diathesis to stress. The question is, therefore, whether those 'underlying' conditions, or either of them, can be characterised as a 'mental condition' for the purposes of the Act, so as to connect the two episodes of anxiety state. It was the Commissioner's submission that they could. Mr Higgins, on the other hand, strongly disputed any such contention. He relied on the evidence of Dr Merrifield that both conditions were simply part of the applicant's personality.

77. A paranoid personality, Dr Merrifield had said, was not abnormal - rather it was one of the normal variants of personality. It was not an illness. Dr Merrifield was not prepared to describe either condition as a 'medical condition'.

78. Whilst paying due regard to Dr Merrifield's evidence on this point, reference to some of the major medical publications on the classification of disease (including mental disorders) exposes the difficulties, and to some extent the differences of viewpoint, regarding classification of personality disorders. In Chapter 5 of World Health Organisation, International Classification of Diseases: Manual of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases Injuries and Causes of Death, 9th Revision, Geneva, 1977, a publication by the World Health Organisation dealing with the classification of disease (including mental disorders), paranoid personality is classified as a type of personality disorder. 82. It is a matter of common experience that personality characteristics vary considerably between individuals. In one sense, it is clearly correct, as Dr Merrifield says, that paranoid personality is one of the normal variants of personality. Many people probably display such characteristics at some stage of their lives without being characterised either as mentally ill or as suffering from a 'mental condition'. But it appears to us, having regard both to Dr Merrifield's evidence and to the publications to which we have referred, that it is ultimately a question of degree. Where, as in the present case, the evidence establishes that the applicant's personality characteristics are such that she has been prone to suffer an adverse reaction to her mental health when under stress, those characteristics, in our view, are properly described as a disordered personality and, in that respect, as a 'mental condition' for the purposes of the Act. We find, therefore, that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement, namely her anxiety state, was substantially contributed to by the mental condition that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act, namely the applicant's paranoid personality.

83. Subject to the alternative argument presented by Mr Higgins, it follows that, in our view, the Commissioner was bound to issue a BCC in respect of the applicant. On the evidence before us, we think that the conditions to be specified in the certificate should be 'history of nervous condition, namely anxiety state; paranoid personality' and it will be necessary, therefore, to vary the BCC issued by the Commissioner accordingly.

The Constitutional Question

84. That brings us, finally, to the alternative argument advanced by Mr Higgins on behalf of the applicant. As earlier indicated, his argument was based on the provisions of s.48B of the 1922 Act which provided as follows:

'48B. Where -

(a) within three years after the obligation of a contributor to contribute to the Fund under Part III of this Act came into existence, the contributor dies or is retired on the ground of invalidity or physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties;

(b) the Board is satisfied that the death, invalidity or incapacity was caused, or substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition that, to the knowledge of the contributor, existed at the time when he underwent the medical examination referred to in sub-section (1) or sub-section (2) of section seventy-nine, of this Act; and

(c) the contributor did not disclose the existence of the condition to the person who made the examination or made or furnished a false or misleading statement to that person in respect of the condition,

pension is not payable to or in respect of the contributor but there is payable to him or to his personal representatives, as the case may be, anamount equal to the amount of the contributions paid by him under this Act."

By Act No.32 of 1976, s.48B was amended (so far as presently relevant) by adding the following sub-section:

'(3) This section does not apply in respect of a contributor who dies or is retired on or after 1 July 1976.'

85. In a detailed written submission lodged with the Tribunal after the hearing, Mr Higgins developed a carefully reasoned argument, the substance of which was that:

(i) having regard to the terms of s.48B, the applicant was a person who, had she been retired on the ground of invalidity immediately prior to 1 July 1976, would have had an absolute and unqualified entitlement to a pension pursuant to s.38 of the superseded Act notwithstanding any alleged non-disclosure;

(ii) that the entitlement to a pension was a proprietary right;

(iii) that the entitlement was 'acquired' by Act No.32 of 1976 which provided that s.38 no longer applied 'to a contributor who is retired on or after 1 July 1976' (see s.38(3));

(iv) that, in respect of a person such as the applicant, the entitlement which s.184(5) purported to substitute in place of the entitlement 'acquired by the Commonwealth', was substantially less favourable to her than her previous entitlement and thus did not provide 'just terms' for the acquisition of her proprietary right (see s.51(xxxi) of The Constitution);

(v) that s.184(5) should be read down so not to exceed the legislative power of the Commonwealth (see s.15A of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901);

(vi) that, so read down, s.184(5) could not apply to the applicant.

86. In an equally detailed submission prepared on behalf of the Commissioner by the Attorney-General's Department, the principal submissions were that:

(i) the applicant had no property by virtue of the Superannuation Act 1922. The most that she had was 'a mere expectation' of a right to a pension;

(ii) in any event, there was no 'acquisition' of property by virtue of the operation of s.184(5). Even if that sub-section does result in the extinguishment of 'property', that property did not pass to the Commonwealth or to any other person or body: see Commonwealth v Tasmania [1983] HCA 21; (1982-3) 46 ALR 625 (The Dam Case) per Mason J. at 708; Murphy J. at 738 and Brennan J. at 795;

(iii) that if there was an 'acquisition' of 'property' it was not on 'just terms'.

87. There is, in our view, an important threshold question to which the parties did not address any submissions, namely, the extent to which (if at all) it is proper for this Tribunal to consider the constitutional question raised by the applicant. As the first President of the Tribunal, Brennan J. (as he then was), said in Re Adams and The Tax Agents' Board (1976) 1 ALD 251 at 255:

'When an administrative body declines to exercise a power in consequence of its opinion as to the limits of the authority conferred upon it by statute, the administrative body thereby seeks to conform with the expressed will of the legislature. But if an administrative body declines to exercise a power in consequence of its opinion that the legislature could not confer the relevant authority upon it, the will of the legislature expressly conferring that authority would, at least for a time, be overriden. Moreover, that will would be overriden without judicial consideration. A consequence of such gravity throws doubt upon the proposition that an administrative body ought to consider the constitutional validity of a statute affecting its power.'

88. In the present case, we are not faced with an argument that s.184(5) is wholly unconstitutional. What is said is that the sub-section cannot constitutionally apply to a person such as the applicant who immediately before the date of commencement of the 1976 Act had acquired an unqualified 'right' to a pension under s.38 of the 1922 Act. In Adams, by contrast, the constitutional issue that was sought to be raised would, if sustained, have denied both the Tax Agents' Board and the Tribunal any relevant power to deal with the matter. It was in that context that the President said (at 257):

'As at present advised, it appears to me that, when a decision-maker acts in conformity with his statutory authority, a person whose interests are affected by his act may not obtain relief from this Tribunal upon the ground that the statute is ultra vires the Parliament. This Tribunal has no powers of review which it might exercise to give effect to such a ground. It has no judicial power. The relief must be sought, if at all, from a court in which the judicial power of the Commonwealth is vested."

89. Where, however, the constitutional question that is sought to be argued before the Tribunal does not go to the basic constitutionality of the legislation from which the powers of review arise (as in Adams) but rather is directed to confirming within proper constitutional limits a statutory power of apparently broad application, we are satisfied that it is within the Tribunal's jurisdiction to consider the question. How the Tribunal deals with such a question will require careful consideration in each case.

90. Bowen C.J. touched upon the point in Collector of Customs (NSW) v Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd [1979] FCA 21; (1979) 24 ALR 307 at 317 when he said, in respect of the Tribunal's jurisdiction to review a 'decision' taken by the Collector in the purported exercsie of a 'power' that did not in law exist:

'As I have said, in my opinion an applicant to the Tribunal has standing and the Tribunal has jurisdiction provided there is a decision in fact and provided further that the decision purports to have been made in exercise of powers conferred by an enactment whether or not as a matter of law it was validly made and whether or not action on the basis there was power to make the decision was right or wrong.

It may be that the nature of the legal question raised will be such that the Tribunal, although it has jurisdiction, may consider it proper that the applicant should first approach a court for decision of the question. It may, in its discretion, decide to defer hearing the application until this is done. An appeal involving a constitutional question might well be such a case. However, this would not be because the Tribunal lacked jurisdiction.'

91. The American experience in this regard is also instructive. In Davis, Administrative Law Text, 3rd Edn at 388-9, the learned author says.

'A fundamental distinction must be recognized between constitutional applicability of legislation to particular facts and constitutionality of the legislation. When a tribunal passes upon constitutional applicability, it is carrying out the legislative intent, either express or implied or presumed. When a tribunal passes upon constitutionality of the legislation, the question is whether it shall take action which runs counter to the legislative intent. We commit to administrative agencies the power to determine constitutional applicability, but we do not commit to administrative agencies the power to determine constitutionality of legislation. Only the courts have authority to take action which runs counter to the expressed will of the legislative body.'

92. In the circumstances, we have considered whether we should refer the constitutional question to the Federal Court for its decision pursuant to s.45 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (cf. Re Zimmax Trading Co Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (1978) 2 ALD 120 at 126) or whether we should invite the applicant to approach a court for a decision on that question (as Bowen CJ. suggested in Lawlor). In our view, however, neither course of action would be justified in this case. The constitutional question is only one of a number of difficult questions of law that arise in these proceedings. As we do not consider the constitutional argument to be sustainable, we think that it is preferable that we should express our views briefly on the constitutional question and leave the applicant to exercise her rights of appeal to the Federal Court if considered appropriate.

93. In our view, the applicant's submissions founder on the argument that the applicant had a proprietary 'right' to a pension under s.38 of the superseded Act that was 'acquired' by the Commonwealth. For the purposes of s.51(xxxi) of the Constitution, 'property' has certainly not been construed in any narrow or restrictive sense. Dixon J. (as he then was) said in Bank of N.S.W. v The Commonwealth [1948] HCA 7; (1948) 76 CLR 1 at 349:

'I take Minister of State for the Army v Dalziel [1944] HCA 4; (1944) 68 CLR 261 to mean that s.51(xxxi) is not to be confined pedantically to the taking of title by the Commonwealth to some specific estate or interest in land recognized at law or in equity and to some specific form of property in a chattel or chose in action similarly recognized, but that it extends to innominate and anomalous interests and includes the assumption and indefinite continuance of exclusive possession and control for the purposes of the Commonwealth of any subject of property.'

94. Nevertheless, we do not understand the notion of 'property' for the purposes of s.51(xxxi) of the Constitution as extending to an inchoate right to a pension under an enactment which is repealed and replaced by other rights before any entitlement to the pension has vested in interest. We agree with the submission on behalf of the Commissioner that, putting the applicant's case at its highest, all that was lost was a 'mere expectation of a right', not 'property' within the meaning of s.51(xxxi).

95. If, contrary to that view, the expectation of a right to a pension was 'property' for the purposes of s.51(xxxi) of the Constitution, we do not consider that the applicant's right was 'acquired' by the Commonwealth. As Mason J. said in Commonwealth v Tasmania (1983) 46 ALR 265 at 708:

'The emphasis in s.51(xxxi) is not on a 'taking' of private property but on the acquisition of property for purposes of the Commonwealth. To bring the constitutional provision into play it is not enough that legislation adversely affects or terminates a pre-existing right that an owner enjoys in relation to his property; there must be an acquisition whereby the Commonwealth or another acquires an interest in property, however slight or insubstantial it may be.'

The similar effect were the following observations by Brennan J. at 795:

'Where neither the Commonwealth nor any other person acquires proprietary rights under a law of the Commonwealth, there is no acquisition upon which para (xxxi) may fasten. And so, in Trade Practices Commission v Tooth & Co Ltd [1979] HCA 47; (1979) 142 CLR 397 at 408; [1979] HCA 47; 26 ALR 185 at 192-3, Gibbs J observed that 'not every compulsory divesting of property is an acquisition within s

51(xxxi)'.'

96. There is nothing in the amendments to the 1922 Act or in the provisions of the 1976 Act as amended, to which we were referred, which could be construed as effecting an 'acquisition' of property in the required sense. There is, in our view, no more than an extinguishment of one entitlement and the substitution in its place of another entitlement. At no point of time was the former 'entitlement' to a pension divested from the applicant and vested in the Commonwealth, the Commissioner for Superannuation or any other person or agency on behalf of the Commonwealth.

97. As, in our view, there was no relevant 'acquisition' of

'property', we find it unnecessary to consider the question of 'just terms'. We can see no constitutional reason why s.184(5) should be read down as the applicant has contended and we propose therefore to apply the sub-section in accordance with the construction that we earlier indicated.
