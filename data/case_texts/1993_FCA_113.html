Re SP Investments Pty Limited (As Trustee of the LM Brennan Trust) and Perron Investments Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 113; (1993) 93 Atc 4170 (1993) 25 Atr 165, (1993) 112 A (25 March 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: S.P. INVESTMENTS PTY LIMITED (AS TRUSTEE OF THE L.M. BRENNAN TRUST) and

PERRON INVESTMENTS PTY LIMITED

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. N G686-G688 of 1992

FED No. 153

Number of pages - 46

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Burchett(1). Hill(2) and O'Loughlin(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - assessable income - whether consideration received for assignments of part of an interest in a royalty for a limited period income derived by the assignor - question not to be determined by subtleties of drafting but by total effect - whether consideration received in substitution for income stream and thus having the character of income.

Income Tax - tax avoidance - whether assignments of part of an interest in a royalty for a term to be characterised as arrangements altering the incidence of income tax - relevance of subjective purpose or motive to question arising under s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 of purpose of arrangement.

Income Tax - alienation of income - assignments of part of interest in a royalty for a term - relevance of specific provision in Division 6A dealing with short term assignments.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth): s.25(1), s.26(1), s.102A, s.102B, s.260.

Conveyancing Act NSW 1912: s.12.

Commissioner of Taxation v. Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199; discussed.

Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Purcell [1921] HCA 59; (1921) 29 CLR 464; discussed.

DECISION

BURCHETT J. In my opinion, the orders should be made which are proposed by Hill J., for the reasons given by him.

HILL J. S.P. Investments Pty Ltd ("S P Investments"), the trustee of the L.M. Brennan Trust ("the Brennan Trust"), appeals from the judgment of a judge of this Court (Lockhart J.) confirming the disallowance by the Commissioner of Taxation of objections lodged by it in respect of income tax assessed by the Commissioner for the year of income ended 30 June 1980 ("the year of income"). Related to this appeal are two further appeals brought by Perron Investments Pty Limited referred to later in the judgment which, for present purposes, raise no different issues.

2. The relevant facts are not in dispute. The Brennan Trust is a discretionary trust established pursuant to a trust deed dated 30 June 1953, as amended by later deeds. As at 12 February 1962, the trustee of that trust was Mr Perron, who was replaced by L.S.P. Pty Ltd ("LSP") on 11 April 1979. The appellant became trustee of that trust after the year of income in question and was trustee when the notice of assessment issued.

3. The background to the dispute between the parties commences in March 1959 when in two letters, dated 16 March and 20 March 1959 respectively, it was acknowledged that Perron Bros Pty Ltd ("Perron Bros"), a company controlled by Mr Perron, had acquired from Wright Prospecting Pty Ltd, Hancock Prospecting Pty Ltd, Langley George Hancock and Ernest Archibald Wright (sometimes referred to in the documents as the partnership of "Hancock and Wright") ("the prospectors") for 500 pounds, a fifteen percent interest in an iron ore venture at Hamersley in Western Australia, or any other iron ore venture instituted by the prospectors if the venture then on foot did not reach the stage where mineral claims were granted to the prospectors within ten years.

4. By deed of assignment dated 29 January 1962, between Perron Bros as assignor and Mr Perron as assignee, Perron Bros assigned to Mr Perron all its right, title and interest in the agreement constituted and confirmed by the letters of 16 March and 20 March 1959.

5. By declaration of trust made 12 February 1962, Mr Perron then declared that he held his interest under the deed of assignment of 29 January 1962 as the then trustee of the Brennan Trust.

6. Subsequently, on 12 December 1962, the prospectors agreed to sell to Hamersley Iron Pty Ltd ("Hamersley Iron"), as purchaser, all the right, title and interest of the vendors in and to various reserves and land comprised therein and rights to prospect or mine relating thereto, comprising the Hamersley Iron ore venture and other iron ore ventures. Under that agreement, and as part of the consideration for the sale, Hamersley Iron agreed to pay to the vendors in respect of all iron ore produced by it from the "Temporary Reserve land", as defined in the agreement and which iron ore should thereafter be sold or disposed of, an amount equivalent to two and a half percent of the amount received on sale or other disposal of that iron ore in unrefined and unmanufactured from (sic) FOB the first point of shipment thereof, subject to certain provisos not presently relevant.

7. Thereafter, by deed dated 22 October 1964 between Mr Perron of the one part and the prospectors of the other, it was noted that it had been agreed that Mr Perron might at any time up to 15 March 1969, make an election as to which of the iron ore ventures of the prospectors would be the subject of his fifteen percent interest. By the deed, Mr Perron irrevocably elected to have a fifteen percent interest in the agreement with Hamersley Iron and thereby became entitled to have assigned to him a fifteen percent interest in the 12 December 1962 agreement, that is to say, he became entitled as trustee in equity to a fifteen percent interest in the royalty agreement and in consequence to fifteen percent of the two and a half percent of the sale proceeds of iron ore sales in the Hamersley project. It does not seem that any written assignment was thereafter made, but nothing turns upon this. By force of changes of trustees, LSP became entitled, in its capacity as trustee of the Brennan Trust, to the fifteen percent interest in the agreement between the prospectors and Hamersley Iron and, in consequence, to the fifteen percent interest in the royalty.

8. It is against this background that two arrangements were entered into involving the National Mutual Life Association of Australasia Ltd ("National Mutual"), arrangements giving rise to the payments which the Commissioner contends formed part of the net income of the trust estate of the Brennan Family Trust in the year of income.

9. So far as is relevant to the present appeal, the first of the two arrangements was constituted by two separate deeds. The first was a deed dated 25 October 1979 between LSP and National Mutual. That deed recited much of the background which I have already set out and an agreement between the parties that, in consideration of the payment by National Mutual to LSP, LSP would assign to National Mutual:

twenty fourth day of January 1987... including

12. The second deed of significance to the first commercial arrangement with National Mutual is a document, dated 24 October 1979, referred to as a bond pursuant to which Stan Perron Pty Ltd, Phoenix Holdings Pty Ltd and Mr Perron, as obligors, acknowledged themselves jointly and severally bound to National Mutual to pay $4,350,000 within fourteen days of demand, that obligation in essence to be reduced to the extent that National Mutual received from Hamersley Iron, pursuant to the first deed referred to above, the $50,000 per month referred to in that deed during the seven year three month period commencing on 24 October 1979. Each of Stan Perron Pty Ltd and Phoenix Holdings Pty Ltd was a company controlled by Mr Perron. That bond was secured by mortgage.

13. A second and similar arrangement was entered into between LSP and National Mutual on 30 June 1980, that arrangement again being constituted by two deeds. Under the first deed which, so far as is presently material, was in substantially similar form and terms to that of 25 October 1979, LSP released to National Mutual all its beneficial interest, to the extent of $25,000 per month, in the amounts held in trust by National Mutual for LSP pursuant to the terms of the first deed and purported to assign, subject to cl.2 of the second deed, its right, title, interest and benefit in "the assignor's interest" to National Mutual for a further period commencing on the termination of the period of assignment under the first deed and ending at the expiration of a period of seven years and three months from 30 June 1980, for a consideration of $1,321,300. Pursuant to cl.2 of the second deed, National Mutual agreed to hold in trust for the use and benefit absolutely of LSP all or any amounts received by National Mutual pursuant to the second deed during the further period, over and above the amount of $25,000 per month.

As part of this arrangement, there was again executed a bond, the parties to which were Stan Perron Pty Ltd, Phoenix Holdings Pty Ltd and Lloyd Stanley Perron, in favour of National Mutual whereby the obligors bound themselves to pay to National Mutual $6,525,000, that obligation being "conditioned to be void" provided National Mutual was paid the sum of $6,525,000 by Hamersley Iron or LSP or any of the obligors, by monthly instalments of (i) $50,000 each during the period from 24 October 1979 to 30 June 1980; (ii) $75,000 each during the period from 30 June 1980 to 24 January 1987 and (iii) $25,000 each during the period from 24 January 1987 to the expiration of seven years and three months from 30 June 1980.

15. This second bond was also secured by a mortgage.

"...all monies otherwise payable by the said Hamersley Iron Pty

Limited to Lloyd Stanley Perron or the said L.S.P Pty Limited to The

National Mutual Life Association of Australasia Limited or as it may

for the relevant period.

17. The effect of these transactions was that LSP, in its trustee capacity, received from National Mutual, as consideration for the assignments it made, $3,963,900. It is this amount which the Commissioner submits constitutes net income of the trust estate of the Brennan Family Trust for the year of income ended 30 June 1980, both of the amounts in question being paid in that year of income. As a consequence of the assignments, National Mutual became entitled to receive $400,000 out of the royalty income from Hamersley Iron in the year of income ended 30 June 1980 and $900,00 in each of the years of income ended 30 June 1981 to 30 June 1986 respectively. Any moneys received in excess of these amounts were to be held in trust for LSP in its trustee capacity. Any shortfall in the royalty payments was to be made up by the guarantors under the bonds.

18. Thereafter, Hamersley Iron paid to National Mutual royalties. In some months the royalties paid to National Mutual were less than the amounts to which, under the arrangements, National Mutual was entitled. In some months National Mutual called upon the guarantors under the bonds to make up the shortfall. In other months, the amount paid to National Mutual exceeded the amount which National Mutual was entitled to retain and, in such event, surplus royalties were deposited to the credit of an account entitled "L S Perron Family Account No. 730702".

19. In 1987, the Commissioner issued a notice of amended assessment of income tax in respect of the Brennan Trust, including in the net income of that trust estate the sum of $3,963,900 and imposing additional tax of $7,196 upon the trustee for lodging an incorrect return. The amended assessment was made pursuant to s.99A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). A previous assessment had been made on 3 June 1983. S P Investments Pty Limited, before Lockhart J., submitted that the amended assessment was not within power because there had been a full and true disclosure made by S P Investments. His Honour held that there had been a failure to make a full and true disclosure and that the Commissioner accordingly had power pursuant to s.170 of the Act to make the amended assessment. No appeal was brought against this part of his Honour's decision.

20. Assessments were also issued on 13 May 1987 by the Commissioner against Perron Investments Pty Ltd, being a primary tax assessment for the year of income ended 31 March 1986 and a Division 7 assessment in respect of the year of income ended 31 March 1985. The first included an amount of $900,000 in assessable income as a distribution from the Perron Group Trust and the latter was made on the basis that there was to be added to the assessable income of Perron Investments Pty Ltd an amount of $900,000 as "adjustment distribution from the net income of the Perron Group Trust". The detail of these assessments is not material. All that is relevant for present purposes is that each assumed that amounts of $900,000 were to be included in the assessable income of Perron Investments Pty Ltd in each of those years as a distribution, under s.97 of the Act, ultimately emanating from the Brennan Trust. Perron Investments Pty Limited appealed to the Court against these two assessments and these appeals were upheld and Perron Investments Pty Limited was ordered to pay the Commissioner's costs. Against this order, Perron Investments Pty Limited appealed.

22. It follows that there are two substantive issues. The first is whether the amount received by LSP from National Mutual as consideration for the assignments was assessable income, either under the provisions of s.25(1) of the Act or s.26(a) of the Act. The second issue, which only arises if the first is decided adversely to the Commissioner, is whether the sum of $3,963,900, or some lesser amount, is to be included in the net income of LSP as trustee of the Brennan Trust, having regard to the provisions of s.260 of the Act.

The judgment appealed against

23. Lockhart J. found for the Commissioner in the S.P. Investments matter. His Honour was of the view that the amount received by the trustee of the Brennan Trust from National Mutual was assessable income in the hands of the trustee, both under s.25(1) of the Act and by virtue of the second limb of s.26(a). In so holding, his Honour applied the decision of the High Court in Commissioner of Taxation v. Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199 and the decision of the Full Court of this Court in Henry Jones (IXL) Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 31 FCR 64.

24. Although it is clear that s.260 of the Act could have no application on the facts of the present case if the amounts in question were income in ordinary concepts or made assessable income by s.26(a), his Honour considered the application of s.260, presumably on the hypothesis that his decision, so far as it concerned s.25(1) and s.26(a), was incorrect. His Honour did so because the matter had been the subject of extensive argument and so that an appeal court could have the benefit of his Honour's views as trial judge. On this basis, his Honour concluded that s.260 did have application and that the result of that application was the derivation of assessable income by the trustee, and the inclusion of that assessable income in the net income of the trust estate of the Brennan Trust. In the event, his Honour dismissed, with costs, the appeal of S P Investments but allowed the objections against the assessments against Perron Investments Pty Ltd, although ordering, in each of these last two appeals, that the taxpayer should pay the Commissioner's costs. In these last two matters, the only appeal by Perron Investments Pty Ltd is as to the question of costs. No argument was addressed before us to the issue of costs. Rather, the parties concentrated upon the substantive issues.

The application of s.25(1) to the facts of the case

25. It was conceded, in my view correctly, by senior counsel for the Commissioner that, on the facts of the present case, s.26(a) of the Act could have no operation if the Court were of the view that s.25(1) could not apply. Equally, of course, if s.25(1) applies, it is unnecessary to consider whether s.26(a) might also apply. In these circumstances, I do not propose to consider further the application of s.26(a).

26. In Henry Jones (IXL) I discussed the reasoning of the High Court in the Myer case in some detail. There is little point in my repeating that discussion. Suffice it to say that I identified two separate, although on the facts of that case not unrelated, strands of thought in the judgment of the High Court, each of which led to the conclusion reached by that Court that the amount received by the taxpayer in that case was assessable income. The first strand of thought may be summarised by saying that in a case where a taxpayer makes a profit or gain in circumstances where the property generating the profit or gain was acquired in a business operation or commercial transaction for the purpose of profit-making, then the profit or gain will be income, notwithstanding that the transaction is a one off transaction or an extraordinary transaction, having regard to the ordinary course of a taxpayer's business. The second strand depended upon the proposition that an assignment of a right to receive interest, independent of an assignment of the loan debt itself, for consideration, gave rise, at least in the circumstances of that case, to the derivation of assessable income. The substance of what was received was the present value of income which the recipient would otherwise obtain in the future and this was itself of an income character.

27. The Commissioner submitted, both before us and before the learned judge below, that each of the two strands of thought in Myer was applicable in the present case. That submission was upheld below. With respect to his Honour, I do not agree that the first strand could have any application on the facts of the present case, whatever the applicability of the second.

28. It was critical to the application of the first strand of thought in Myer that the property generating the profit or gain be acquired in a business operation or commercial transaction for the purpose of profit-making by the means giving rise to the profit, namely, by assigning the interest stream. The property which generated the profit or gain in Myer was the loan agreement between the taxpayer and Myer Finance Pty Ltd. It was that loan agreement which contained the covenant to pay interest. That loan agreement, which clearly came into being in the course of a business operation or commercial transaction, was entered into for the specific purpose of the assignment of the interest to Citicorp. So much was, on the facts of Myer, obvious because on the day the loan was made, the taxpayer assigned to Citicorp "absolutely the moneys due or to become due as the interest payment and interest thereon... pursuant to" the loan agreement.

29. In their judgment, the members of the Full High Court emphasised the significance of the fact that the two transactions, the loan agreement and the assignment, were not independent. Their Honours said (at 216):

"The two transactions were interdependent in the

sense that Myer would not have entered into the

loan agreement unless it knew that Citicorp

would shortly thereafter take an assignment of

the moneys due or to become due for a sum

approximating the amount payable in

consideration of the assignment. Indeed, from

the viewpoint of Myer the two transactions were

essential and integral elements in an overall

scheme, that scheme being a profit-making scheme.

If the two transactions, namely the loan

agreement and the assignment, are considered as

separate and independent transactions, Myer's

argument that no relevant profit arose from the

assignment has compelling force. The

consideration payable for the assignment

reflected the true value of the chose in action

which Myer assigned. But once the two

transactions are seen as integral elements in

one profit-making scheme, it is apparent that

Myer made a relevant profit, that profit being

the amount payable on the assignment."

30. In the present case there are not two interdependent actions that together can be referred to as a profit-making scheme. There is only one transaction that is relevant, and that is the transaction of assignment. For this reason, to adopt the words of the Full High Court, the argument that no relevant profit arose from the assignment has compelling force.

31. In the present case, the right of the trustee to participate in the royalty payable by Hamersley Iron arose in October 1964 as a result of the deed made then between Mr Perron and the prospectors and subsequent changes of trustees. Nothing in the facts suggests that that right was acquired for the purpose of selling it or part of it at some later time. So far as appears, this was never suggested to any witness and having regard to the history of the matter, seems very unlikely.

32. The judgment appealed against, while correctly identifying the appropriate tests from Myer, appears to have ignored the requirement, explicit in Myer, that the property generating the profit must have been acquired for the requisite profit-making purpose, a requirement emphasised also by the Full Court of this Court in Westfield Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 28 FCR 333 at 342. That this is so appears from the following passage from the judgment below, in which his Honour explains why the first strand of Myer should apply:

"In the present case, prior to the assignments

made by the two deeds of assignment, the trustee

of the Brennan Trust and its predecessors as

trustees were entitled to receive income of 15%

of the royalties payable by Hamersley Iron to

the prospectors in respect of the Hamersley Iron

Ore venture. The assignment effected by the two

deeds was an isolated transaction and it was not

entered into in the ordinary course of the

trustee's activities (which were primarily

pastoral pursuits). But the amount of

$3,963,900 received by L.S.P. from National

Mutual pursuant to the two deeds of assignment

was a gain made in a business operation for the

purpose of making a profit. The trustee

received as a lump sum an amount representing

the present value of income it would otherwise

have received over the period of the assignment

(the lump sum was not the precise assessment of

present value but certainly substantially so).

In my opinion the 'first strand' of reasoning in

Myer leads to the conclusion that the money

received by the trustee from National Mutual

constituted assessable income in the hands of

the trustee under s. 25(1) and the second limb

of s. 26(a)."

33. In what I have so far said as to the first strand of Myer, I have assumed the existence of a profit. The assignment of the interest for the consideration of $45,370,000 paid by Citicorp gave rise to a profit. The argument that it did not was rejected by their Honours in the following passage (at 216):

"As a result of the two transactions Myer,

having lent $80,000,000 on 6 March 1981

repayable in accordance with the terms of the

loan agreement, had profited by 9 March 1981 to

the extent of the first interest payment

received on 6 March 1981 and the sum of

$45,370,000 paid for the assignment, the

principal on the loan being intact. Of course

the value of the chose in action, the right to

recover the principal sum, was substantially

less than the amount of the principal sum

because there was no obligation to repay until

30 June 1988. But this circumstance cannot

affect the character of the consideration for

the assignment. It exists in every case where

money is lent for a fixed term."

34. With respect to the argument for the Commissioner and to the judgment below, in my view, no profit was derived by the Brennan Trust at all. Profit has to be determined, in a case such as the present, by comparing the position before and after the transaction. Here, before the transaction, the taxpayer had a contractual right to the payment of royalties by Hamersley Iron effectively for the life of the iron ore body. After the transaction, that right had been diminished by an assignment of it for seven years. If one assumes that the purchase price paid represented the present value of the future royalty stream, which at least as his Honour says was substantially the case, the decline in value of the contract to receive the royalty equalled the amount received. The reasoning in Myer, expressed in the passage quoted, depends on the words "the principal (of) the loan being intact." The opposite is the case in the present appeal.

35. The Commissioner submitted that the profit in the year of income represented the difference between the amount of $3,963,900 received by the trustee in the year of income and the amount of $400,000 which, but for the assignment, would have been received in the year of income. This submission resulted, as counsel for the Commissioner conceded, in the peculiar situation that in the subsequent years of income the trustee would have made a loss. Presumably, if the alleged profit made in the 1980 tax year was a revenue profit, so the losses made in the years thereafter during the term of the assignment would be revenue losses. That such a ludicrous result might follow at the least reinforces the view that the submission is erroneous.

36. It is the second strand of the Myer decision upon which the Commissioner principally relies. The Commissioner's case is put on two alternative bases. The preferred basis is that the present case involves an assignment of a right to income without an assignment of the underlying property from which the income is derived. The second is that, even if the present case be one where the income-producing asset has been relevantly disposed of, nevertheless, the amount received represents future income and for that reason is on income rather than capital account.

37. The emphasis in the Commissioner's preferred basis upon there being an assignment of a right to income without an assignment of the underlying property stems from the decision of the High Court in Myer and the subsequent discussion of that decision in Henry Jones (IXL). In Myer, it was quite clear that what was assigned was merely the right to receive interest. There was neither an assignment of the contractual rights under the loan agreement, nor an assignment of the right to repayment of capital.

38. In the course of their judgment, their Honours in the High Court discuss the difference between an assignment of interest, on the one hand, and an assignment of an annuity, on the other. At 218 their Honours say:

"The source of interest is never the mere

covenant to pay. Interest is not like an

annuity. Annuity payments are not derived from

the money paid for the annuity; they are derived

solely from the annuity contract. And so when a

contractual right to be paid an annuity is sold

for a price, the proceeds of sale are ordinarily

capital in the hands of the vendor."

39. For this proposition, their Honours cite the decision of Lord Romer in Commissioners of Inland Revenue v. Paget (1938) 2 KB 25 at 44-45. At those pages, Lord Romer said:

"In these circumstances, the only question to be

decided is whether the proceeds of sale of a

right to receive income in the future can be

treated as income for the purpose of the Income

Tax Acts. The question thus broadly stated

plainly admits of but one answer; and that

answer must be in the negative. The proceeds of

the sale for a lump sum of an annuity, for

instance, are capital in the hands of the vendor

and not income. And this is true even when the

subject of the sale is not the annuity for its

whole duration but the right to be paid the

annuity for a number of years or even for one

year... Nor is it any the less true because in

many cases the net income when paid to the

purchaser is not income in his hands."

40. Counsel for the appellant relied heavily upon this passage and upon what was said to be the approval of it in totality by the Full Court in Myer. It was submitted that the present is a case where the right to receive the fifteen percent of the two and a half percent royalty arose solely out of the contractual arrangements entered into. There was, it was said, no distinguishable difference between the right to an annual payment in the present case and the right to an annuity. Indeed, in the true sense of the word, the present right of the trustee to receive payment is properly to be called an annuity for it represents an annual payment. Further, submitted the appellant, the present was no more than a case where the sale of the annuity was not for its whole duration but the right to be paid it for a number of years and this fell within what Lord Romer said in Paget.

41. It cannot be said that in Myer their Honours endorsed everything that was said by Lord Romer in the passage I have quoted. Indeed, it will be noted that in Myer their Honours carefully confined their remarks to the proceeds of a sale of an annuity (presumably an outright sale), for a lump sum. That their Honours did not accept all that was said by Lord Romer is abundantly clear from the fact that the Court drew a distinction, not drawn in Paget, between the sale of income on the one hand, discussed in Paget, and the sale of a right to interest severed from the debt in Myer. Indeed the Court expressed the view that unless Paget could be distinguished, its authority should not be accepted.

42. In Henry Jones (IXL), in a judgment in which, so far as is presently relevant, Jenkinson and Heerey JJ. agreed, I discussed the application of the second strand of Myer to a different fact situation. In Henry Jones (IXL), the taxpayer assigned to Citicorp for a lump sum consideration the whole of its right, title and interest under a licence agreement which had been entered into and which granted the licensee the right to use certain trademarks in return for a royalty. The issue for decision was whether the amount received for the assignment was income in accordance with the second strand of Myer. Although with some reservations, I expressed my views (at 78) as to the ratio of Myer so far as it related to the second strand of thought. I said that it should be taken:

"as establishing that, except in the case of the

assignment of an annuity where the income arises

from the very contract assigned, an assignment

of income from property without an assignment of

the underlying property right will, no matter

what its form, bring about the result that the

consideration for that assignment will be on

revenue account, as being merely a substitution

for the future income that is to be derived.

Thus, the fact that the future income may be

secured by an agreement, and that the assignment

is of the right title and interest of the

assignor in that agreement, will not affect the result."

43. I pointed, however, to the possibility that this general proposition may need to be qualified by restricting it to receipts in the course of business, using the expression "business", as that expression was used in Carapark Holdings Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1967] HCA 5; (1967) 115 CLR 653 at 664 as meaning the "continuous course of conduct which the appellant was following for the derivation of income". Nothing in what I said in that case could be taken as endorsing everything that was said by Lord Romer in Paget.

44. It may be accepted that an outright assignment of the assignor's right, title and interest in and to an annuity would ordinarily, if for consideration, have the result that the consideration received was capital. I say ordinarily because no doubt cases might be envisaged where the contractual right to the annuity was acquired in the course of a profit-making scheme or where the rights to the annuity assigned were assigned for a consideration which itself was an annuity. These examples are not intended to be exhaustive.

45. However, with the greatest respect, I do not think that it is correct to say that the assignment of the right to be paid an annuity for a number of years, being less than the duration of the annuity, for a lump sum necessarily results in that lump sum being received on capital account. Indeed, the case of the assignment for one year of the right to receive annuity income seems to me to clearly result in the consideration received being received in substitution for the year's income and thereby to have itself the character of income. No doubt there will be a question of degree involved and it would not follow, for example, that the assignment of the right to receive twenty years income payable under an annuity contract would result in any lump sum consideration being received as income.

46. The comments made by Lord Romer in Paget as to the capital character of consideration for an annuity were clearly unnecessary for the decision in that case. The actual issue being addressed by his Lordship was whether the sale of the coupons in that case represented "income arising from securities". As the Master of the Rolls, Sir Wilfrid Greene, observed (at 35), it was impossible to treat the purchase price received by Miss Paget as income arising from the bonds, for whether or not it was income, it arose from contracts of sale and purchase. McKinnon L.J., while relying upon the fact that what Miss Paget sold was but the possibility of making some money abroad upon the acceptance of an offer from the Hungarian or Yugoslavian governments, as the case may be, appears to have decided the matter by reference to other language in the relevant schedule (Case IV Schedule D), rather than upon the broader ground of whether or not the amounts received were income.

47. Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that the relevant assignments should be seen as assignments of the royalty amounts up to the nominated figure, $50,000 per month in the case of the first assignment and $75,000 per month consequent upon the second assignment. There was considerable discussion as to the effect of the words "subject to cl.2" and whether there was, as submitted by the appellant, an absolute assignment of the entire interest in the royalty agreement for the term of the assignment (with the trust referred to in cl.2 thereafter arising), or whether, as the Commissioner submitted, there was no absolute assignment of the underlying property right but only an assignment of certain of those rights, that is to say, amounts of $50,000 per month and $25,000 per month during the periods specified.

48. Counsel for the appellant referred us to a number of decisions in support of his submission that each assignment operated to assign the whole of the vendor's contractual rights for the term of the assignment. We were referred to Burlinson v. Hall (1884) 12 QBD 347; Tancred v. Delagoa Bay and East Africa Railway Co (1889) 23 QBD 239 and Hughes v. Pumphouse Hotel Co Ltd (1902) 2 KB 190. I find these cases unhelpful. Each depends upon its particular statutory context. Thus, in Burlinson v. Hall, the issue was whether the assignment of debts upon trust that the assignee receive them and out of them pay a debt due to him from the assignor, paying the surplus to the assignor, was an absolute assignment (not purporting to be by way of charge only), within the corresponding section to s.12 of the Conveyancing Act (NSW), namely the Judicature Act 1873 s.25(6). The issue in that case was whether the assignment was absolute rather than conditional, ie, by way of charge only, not absolute in the sense of absolute as a sale (see per Day J. at 350).

49. Tancred v. Delagoa Bay and East Africa Railway Co concerned the application of the same provision to an assignment of debts with a proviso for redemption by way of mortgage. Hughes v. Pumphouse Hotel Co Ltd again concerned the same statutory provision in the context of an assignment to a bank by way of continuing security of all moneys due or to become due to the assignor under building contracts. The present is not a case where there is some statutory delineation of assignments which are absolute and effective for tax purposes and those which are not, when the meaning of the word "absolute" would require consideration.

50. Nor is it necessary or indeed profitable to consider whether there is first an assignment of contractual rights following which the trust in cl.2 arises, or whether the trust in cl.2 qualifies that which is assigned by cl.1. The question of whether an amount received is income in ordinary concepts should not be determined by subtleties of drafting, or by the sorts of issues which bedevilled practitioners in the area of death duties in times gone by, such as whether an interest was vested but defeasible on the one hand, or a contingent interest on the other (cf Re Kilpatrick's Policies Trusts (1966) 2 All ER 149 and the pointed comment of Diplock L.J., as his Lordship then was, at 153). This is not to say that the Court will adopt a doctrine of economic equivalence (cf Mullens v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290 at 301), but rather that the Court is required to consider the character of the receipt in the hands of the recipient having regard to all the circumstances of the transaction without a disproportionate emphasis upon the form in which the transaction has been structured (cf Reuter v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1993) 93 ATC 4037 at 4045-4047).

51. This accords with the approach adopted in the United States by the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit in Commissioner for Internal Revenue v. P G Lake [1958] USSC 80; 356 US 260 (1958), a case referred to with apparent approval by the High Court in Myer. In Lake's Case the Court looked at the substance of what was assigned and rejected the approach of form over substance. It cautioned, as I pointed out in Henry Jones (IXL) (at 76), that the essence of arrangements had to be determined "not by subtleties of draftsmanship but by their total effect".

52. In the present case no significance can be placed upon the fact that the assignment is expressed to be of the assignor's right, title, interest and benefit in what the agreement refers to as "the assignor's interest". No different result could follow whether the assignment was so worded or whether it was worded as an assignment of the assignor's right to receive $50,000 or $75,000, as the case may be, for the stipulated period.

53. There is some difficulty concerning the assignment for a limited period of a chose in action constituting the right to receive indefinitely annual royalty payments as being an absolute assignment. Be that as it may, there can be, in my view, no difference in legal analysis between the present assignment, on the one hand, and that involved in Shepherd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 70; (1965) 113 CLR 385, on the other. In Shepherd, the assignment, which was voluntary, was of all the assignor's right, title and interest in and to an amount equal to ninety percent of the income which might accrue during a period of three years under the licence agreement. As Barwick C.J. pointed out, such an assignment was ineffective in law to confer upon the assignee any legal rights but was an effective assignment in equity of part of the chose in action, effective to vest the appropriate part of the right equitably in the assignee. The present assignment is also an equitable assignment, especially as the right assigned was an equitable chose in action.

54. In the present case, the mere fact that the assignment was of part only, in monetary terms, of the total royalty that might become payable to the assignor, can not operate to make that which is received as consideration for the assignment income. There can be no doubt, for example, that the assignment of fifty percent of an annuity right for consideration would no more make that consideration income than would be the case if consideration were given for the assignment of the whole annuity interest. Nor does the fact that the assignment is for a term of years necessarily produce the result that the assignment is income. The assignment here was not an assignment to enure for the whole of the period the subject of the assignor's rights, but merely for a limited term of relatively short duration. It is that fact, coupled with the restriction to a defined amount of income, which in the present case makes it clear that the consideration was received in substitution for the income assigned, or as I described it in Henry Jones (IXL), in a passage erroneously transcribed in the report, was an amount which filled the "hole" (not "whole") of income created by virtue of the assignment. The purpose of the assignment, like the purpose of the insurance in Carapark Holdings Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (at 663) was to fill the place of the revenue receipts which the assignment prevented from arising.

55. What is significant in the present case is that the substance of the agreements entered into with National Mutual is that the assignor is to assign a precise amount of income out of its right to receive the royalty for a period of years in consideration of a sum calculated as the present value of that income stream and in circumstances where the assignor sought to substitute for income receivable in the future the present value of that income. That this was the purpose of the arrangement is made clear (if there were any doubt) by the fact that the assignor entered into the second agreement at the end of the year of income to deal with the next $25,000 of income when it had become apparent that the royalty income would exceed $50,000 per month.

56. So to characterise the receipt is not simply to treat the consideration as income because it was computed or measured by reference to loss of future income: cf Commissioner of Taxes (Vic) v. Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 55 CLR 144 at 156. In any event, as I pointed out in Henry Jones (IXL), what is there said is not expressed as stating an unequivocal principle (see Henry Jones (IXL) at 79).

57. In Henry Jones (IXL), the decisive factor which led to the characterisation of the consideration as income was the retention by the assignor of the underlying property (the trademarks) from which the assigned royalties were derived. Thus, I expressed the principle applicable in that case in the following terms (at 79):

"Where, however, a taxpayer assigns a chose in

action, being a right to receive periodical

sums, which when received would be income

derived from underlying property which is

retained and assigns that right in consideration

of an amount which is calculated as the present

value of that income stream, then I am of the

view that that consideration should be seen as

being as much income as the stream which it

replaces, notwithstanding that it is paid in a

lump sum rather than by periodical payments that

are in substitution for the income stream."

58. In the present case, and to the extent that it is appropriate to refer to the royalty income as deriving from property at all, the property from which the income is derived (ie, the chose in action) cannot, without qualification, be said to continue in the ownership of the assignor. A part only of the rights encompassed by the equitable chose in action, which the appellant had, has been assigned. The assignments are limited both as to quantum and as to period. Subject to the terms of the assignments, the underlying right to take action against the payer of the royalty remains with the assignor. In that sense it can be said that there was an assignment of a right to receive periodical sums which when received would be income, in circumstances where the underlying chose in action is retained subject to the assignment and the right is assigned in consideration of an amount calculated as the present value of the income stream. In my view, in those circumstances, the consideration should be as much seen as being income replacing the income stream assigned as was the consideration for the assignment in Henry Jones (IXL).

59. In so far as analogies are useful, the present case can be seen not so much as an assignment of the tree itself (the underlying agreement which gave rise to the obligation to pay the annuity to the appellant) but as the sale, in advance of the fruit forming, of that fruit for a sum calculated as the present value of the harvest. So seen, the consideration is received as compensation for the future fruit or in substitution for that fruit.

60. There remains one matter which has given me some concern. In Myer and in Henry Jones (IXL), the taxpayers were carrying on a business so that the amount derived as a result of the assignment could be seen in all the circumstances as a business profit.

61. In Myer, the High Court referred to the fact that the profit was one made in the course of carrying on a business as being of "telling significance". Their Honours did not necessarily, by this comment, intend to suggest that a different result would follow if there had been an assignment of future interest other than in the course of business. In Henry Jones (IXL), I left open the question whether the substitution principle was restricted to receipts in the course of business, for in that case too the transaction giving rise to assessable income was in the course of business.

62. In the present case, the trustee could not be said, in any conventional sense, to be carrying on a business activity involving its right to receive a share of the Hamersley royalty, nor could the assignment be said to have taken place in the course of that business. However, I have concluded that the second strand of Myer should not be seen to be so limited, albeit that the fact that the profit occurs in the course of business would be not irrelevant to the outcome of a particular case.

63. It follows that in these circumstances I am of the view that the learned judge below did not err in holding that the amount of the consideration was income in ordinary concepts derived by the appellant.

64. It therefore becomes unnecessary to consider in any detail the submission of the appellant that s.260 can not apply.

65. First, it is obvious that if the amount is income in ordinary concepts, there can have been no relevant avoidance of tax and for that reason s.260 could have no application. Secondly and more importantly, I do not think that s.260 can apply when the only arrangement upon which the Commissioner relies is the actual assignment entered into by the appellant with the assignee. It can not be said of that arrangement, looked at objectively, that it bears upon its face the stamp of tax avoidance.

66. Although the case of Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Purcell [1921] HCA 59; (1921) 29 CLR 464 is not determinative of the issue, it is nevertheless helpful. In that case it was held that a mere transfer of income-producing property to a trust by a declaration of trust fell outside s.53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915, the predecessor of s.260, notwithstanding the particularly onerous provisions of the trust deed which gave considerable control to the trustee. Gavan-Duffy and Starke JJ., commenting on s.53 said (at 473):

"The section, as the Chief Justice says, does

not prohibit the disposition of property. Its

office is to avoid contracts, etc, which place

the incidence of the tax or the burden of tax

upon some person or body other than the person

or body contemplated by the Act. If a person

actually disposed of income-producing property

to another so as to reduce the burden of

taxation, the Act contemplates that the new

owner should pay the tax. The incidence of the

tax and the burden of the tax fall precisely as

the Act intends, namely, upon the new owner.

But any agreement which directly or indirectly

throws the burden of the tax upon a person who

is not liable to pay it, is within the ambit of

s.53... The incidence of the tax as regulated

by the Act is not altered, the respondent is not

relieved from tax which he should pay, and he

does not defeat, evade or avoid duty or

liability imposed upon him by the Act, nor does

he prevent the operation of the Act in any respect."

67. In the present case, a conclusion that the consideration for the assignment was received on capital account, could only be arrived at because the transaction was characterised as the assignment of an income-producing asset, or part of such an asset. The royalty payments as received by the assignee fall within the assessable income of the assignee. It may well be that specific statutory provisions might operate to exempt the assignee from tax. That is of no moment. There will have been no alteration of the incidence of tax, nor can it be said, by viewing the alienation alone, that it must be predicated that the agreement to assign was entered into or carried out so as to avoid tax within the well-known test in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1.

68. There was argument before his Honour as to the admissibility of evidence to the extent that it showed the subjective purpose of Mr Perron and his advisers in entering into the transaction. In the course of his judgment, Lockhart J. expressed the view that the evidence showed that it was the purpose of the trustee of the Brennan Trust to obtain a tax benefit by releasing the trust from liability to pay income tax on the royalty receipts. Reference is made by his Honour to consideration being given as early as February 1979 by Mr Perron and his advisers to an arrangement to avoid tax on the royalty income. His Honour inferred that after taking advice from senior counsel as to the tax consequences of entering into the proposed assignment, the trustee acted on that advice by entering into the deed. His Honour pointed to the fact that Mr Perron's legal adviser was a tax expert. Evidence had, in fact, also been adduced by the trustee that the purpose of the transaction was to raise funds for working capital, which evidence was not accepted by his Honour.

69. The appellant, in its grounds of appeal, claims that his Honour erred in apparently admitting into evidence as relevant, material which went to the subjective motive of the appellant.

70. It is clear beyond doubt that s.260 is not concerned with the subjective motivation of the taxpayer. As I said in Davis v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 86 ALR 195 at 227:

"As a matter of syntax, the purpose of which the section

speaks is the purpose of the contract, agreement or

arrangement and not the purpose of some person."

71. The point was made clear by Gibbs C.J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55 at 65, where his Honour said:

"The section does not refer to the motives of

the taxpayer or other person who entered into

the arrangement which it is sought to impugn;

the purpose or effect of the arrangement must be

ascertained from the terms of the arrangement

itself and from the overt acts by which it was

carried into effect."

72. However, the judgment appealed against makes it clear that his Honour did not at the end of the day have regard to matters of subjective motivation or intention in coming to his conclusion. His Honour said that it was sufficient by merely examining the deeds of assignment and the various documents referred to therein to reach the conclusion that the arrangement was designed to avoid the payment of tax by the trustee. Accordingly, the admission of evidence as to purpose or intention, while involving error, would not have affected the result.

73. Before concluding this judgment, I should say something about s.102B of the Act.

74. It is clear that Mr Perron or his advisers structured each of the assignments to ensure that it would continue for a period in excess of seven years from the date of first receipt of royalty income. That period arises from the terms of s.102A, defining the "prescribed date" for the purposes of s.102B.

75. Division 6A of Part III of the Act, concerned with alienation of income, has no relevance in the present circumstances for it applies only where there has been an assignment of what the Act refers to as the right to receive income from property, defined in s.102A(1) as meaning:

"a right to have income that will or may be

derived from property paid to, or applied or

accumulated for the benefit of, the person

owning the right;".

76. In the relevant sense, the present income was not income derived from property.

77. Division 6A was introduced into the Act as a result of and following recommendations made by the report of the Commonwealth Committee on Taxation, June 1961 (the Lidgertwood Report). The Division is an anti-tax avoidance measure and ensures, in cases to which it applies, that, broadly, assignments of income from property for a period of less than the requisite seven year period are ineffective. In such a case, the assignor continues to be liable to pay tax on the income and conversely any liability of the assignee for tax on the income will be eliminated because the effect of the Division is to treat the transfer as not having taken place.

78. As originally enacted, Division 6A operated indifferently whether the assignment was or was not for consideration. It did not ensure that a long term assignment would be effective. It merely ensured that a short term assignment would not.

79. Following the decision of the Full Federal Court in Myer, the Act was amended by the insertion of s.102CA, in relation to assignment of income after 9 October 1985, to ensure that where there had been an assignment of a right to receive income from property in a case to which s.102B(1) did not apply and that assignment was for consideration, the assessable income of the transferor was to include the amount of the consideration. Section 102B(4A), inserted by Act No. 46 of 1986, was then inserted to ensure that, in a case where there was consideration and where s.102B(1) applied, the consideration should not be included in assessable income.

80. Although the obvious purpose of Division 6A was to negate favourable tax consequences of short term assignments and thus to treat those assignments as not having taken place, it can not be said that there is any policy to be found in Division 6A to confer upon assignments longer than the seven year period, protection from attack. In particular, no implication can be drawn from the provisions of Division 6A that any consideration received for the assignment of income would be on capital account. All that can be said is that the legislature, following the decision in Myer, realised that without legislative intervention Division 6A could operate to render the assignment ineffective and so tax the assignor but in cases where the receipt of the consideration also represented income derived by the assignor. Likewise, the legislature was concerned to ensure that, in the event of an assignment for consideration where the assignment was for a period in excess of the seven year period so that the assignment was not negated by Division 6A, nevertheless the consideration was to be included in assessable income.

81. I would accordingly dismiss the appeal brought by S.P. Investments Pty Limited with costs. In respect of the remaining two appeals brought by Perron Investments Pty Limited, I would propose that the parties bring in short minutes of order on a date to be fixed with counsel.

O'LOUGHLIN J. I have had the advantage of reading the judgment of Hill J. I agree, for the reasons stated by his Honour, that this appeal should be dismissed with costs.
