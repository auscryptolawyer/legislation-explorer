AT93/29 and Commissioner of Taxation [1994] AATA 618; (1994) 29 ATR 1102; 94 ATC 412 (8 September 1994)

Last Updated: 18 May 2009



INCOME TAX : Exemption from tax - classification as public educational institution - educational activities carried out by affiliated State bodies - Federal association



INCOME TAX : Exemption from tax - purpose of promoting industrial resources of Australia - must be primary purpose - profession cannot be said to be industrial resource



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : S.23

Taxation Administration Act 1953 : S.14ZZJ



Australian Insurance Association and FC of T 79 ATC 4569

Case U118 87 ATC 710



AAT Decision No 9723



DECISION AND REASONS



Re:

APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT



No : AT93/29

Date : 8 September 1994

Place : Canberra



Tribunal : Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



DECISION



The Tribunal decides that the decision under review be affirmed.



(Sgd) K L Beddoe

(SENIOR MEMBER)



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) AT93/29

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:

APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT



REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 8 September 1994

Place: Canberra



The applicant seeks review of an objection decision whereby the respondent denied the applicant exemption under section 23 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”).



2. The hearing of this matter was not conducted in public and the Tribunal is therefore bound by section 14ZZJ of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. The effect of that section is, so far as is relevant here, that the Tribunal must ensure, as far as practicable, that its reasons for decision are so framed so as not to be likely to enable the identification of the person who applied for the review. I confess to having found this task difficult because the essential nature of this case will lead an informed observer to have little doubt as to the identity of the applicant in these proceedings.



3. The applicant relies upon two paragraphs in section 23, namely paragraphs 23(e) and 23(h). Paragraph 23(e) provides that the income of a religious, scientific, charitable or public educational institution shall be exempt from income tax.



4. Paragraph 23(h) provides that the income of the society or association not carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to the individual members thereof, established for the purpose of promoting the development of aviation or of the agricultural, pastoral, horticultural, viticultural, manufacturing or industrial resources of Australia shall be exempt from income tax.



5. At the hearing of this matter Mr Bartlett of Price Waterhouse represented the applicant and an officer of the Australian Taxation Office represented the respondent Commissioner. The documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the T documents”) were placed before the Tribunal as the T documents and a further document was marked as Exhibit A. The applicant called two witnesses to give oral evidence and the respondent called one witness to give evidence by telephone.



6. The applicant Association was incorporated on 23 June 1983 in the Australian Capital Territory and has established an office in Canberra from before that time. It evolved out of a private practice panel of the Institution of Surveyors. Apparently there was a perception amongst some members of the Institution of Surveyors that private practice surveyors were being neglected, or overlooked, by comparison with the majority of members who were employed by governments.



7. The objects of the Association include the promotion for the benefit of the public in general, of the art, science, technology, profession and practice of surveying and/or its associated callings. The Association has three grades of members, being practitioner members, honorary life members, and retired members. The rules of the Association provide for qualifications for admission. Those rules for admission which are set out in Exhibit A emphasise that the Association is essentially for private practitioners. There is a further qualification to the effect that the practitioners must be principals of the business. While membership of the Association is confined to principals, the services of the Association are made available to the employees of those principals.



8. Exhibit A includes a summary of the history of the Association. It is not necessary that I set that summary out here, but I note, in particular, that in 1985 the annual report of the Association includes a statement to the effect that one of the major benefits of the Association is the contribution it can make to the profile and viability of the private sector, and promotion of private practice at a federal level. Clearly that refers to private practice as a surveyor. In particular, activities noted during 1985 included the introduction of a group professional indemnity insurance scheme, the production of industry statistics surveys, the representation of the private sector on various national organisations and committees, including the National Surveying and Mapping Industry Council, the investigation of potential roles of surveyors in the delineation of land rights areas and sacred sites; and the monitoring of industrial relations matters of importance to surveyors in practice. The activities of the Association also include the introduction of a quality assurance program and the conduct of seminars on quality management throughout Australia. That developed into a quality certifying body being established for the purposes of certifying the surveying profession. The Association has also established a joint education fund for the surveying profession in conjunction with an associated insurance society.



9. The Association was responsible for the incorporation of the Consulting Surveyors Quality Corporation which, in effect, accredits surveyors both in Australia and New Zealand, but accreditation is not restricted to members of the Association.



10. The Association is a non profit organisation which is prevented from making any distribution of funds to its members. Upon a winding up or dissolution of the Association the net assets of the Association are to be transferred to another association with objects similar to those of the Association. There is a prohibition on distribution of assets to members on winding up.



11. After apparently taking much of the information I have set out above into account, the respondent decided to accept that the Association was exempt from income tax under paragraph 23(e) of the Act. A letter to that effect was sent to the Association on 9 July 1991 by a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation. However, on 15 January 1992 that approval was, in effect, withdrawn by the same Deputy Commissioner of Taxation.



12. The Memorandum and Rules of the Association are set out in the T documents at folios 18 to 30. Clause 3 sets out the Objects of the Association and includes the following:



“3. (i) the promotion for the benefit of the public in general, of the art, science, technology, profession and practise of Surveying and/or its associated callings and



(ii) the advancement of education in the said art, science, technology, profession and practise; and for those purposes to do any of the following things:



(a) to hold meetings and provide facilities for the reading of papers, the delivery of lectures and the holding of discussions and debate upon subject relating to Surveying and associated callings

(b) to facilitate the interchange of ideas respecting Surveying and its associated callings and to provide for the publication, dissemination and acquisition of information connected with such professions



(c) to hold classes, conduct examinations, establish scholarships, grant premiums and prizes for papers, essays or theses and by any similar means to assist persons studying to become proficient in the skills of Surveying and/or associated callings and/or carrying out research into and/or to enlarge the knowledge of such professions



(d) to undertake, superintend, administer, and contribute to any charitable or benevolent fund from which may be made donations or advances



(i) to persons at any time employed by the Association and who are not Members of the Association



(ii) the necessitous Members of the profession and widows and dependants of such Members, or



(iii) other deserving persons who may have been connected with the profession



(e) to establish, form and maintain a library and collection of models, designs, drawings and other articles of interest in connection with the development and improvement of the health of the community by means of Surveying and its associated callings



(f) to conduct research, promote, develop and advance the scientific and technical aspects of any matters appertaining to the profession and associated callings



. . .



(vii) to protect and improve the professional standards of proficiency and training in Surveying and associated callings to lay down Rules of discipline for the promotion of honourable practise by Members of the profession in general and, in particular, to oblige every Member of the Association to order his conduct so as to uphold the dignity, standing and reputation of the profession and practise of Surveying and/or associated callings and in his responsibilities to his employer and to the profession to have full regard to the public interest and to discharge his duties to his employer with integrity and not to injure or attempt to injure, maliciously or indirectly the professional reputation of another Member of the profession.”



13. The applicant called two witnesses. It is not necessary that I detail the evidence of those witnesses in full, but it is necessary that I make certain findings of fact. It is established from the evidence of those witnesses that the applicant evolved out of the Institution of Surveyors. That evolution seems to have been at least a two stage process which commenced with the establishment of panels of private practising surveyors within the Institution of Surveyors. From there, those private panels became autonomous groups within their own State and concerned themselves with the practice of surveying by private practitioners as compared with the practice of surveying of the majority of the members of the Institution of Surveyors employed by governments and related authorities.



14. The evidence establishes that there are approximately 560 firms of consulting surveyors in Australia, who are members of the applicant Association. The Association concerns itself with looking after the requirements of those members as professional surveyors in such matters as public liability insurance, professional development training, the training of the employees of the members of the Association and the standards of the members in their dealings with their clients and the delivery of their services to those clients. In seeking to achieve these ends the applicant Association runs the quality assurance program throughout Australia. That program seeks as its objective to ensure that members of the Association provide a service to their client which is a full professional standard. The program itself requires that members be accredited within the terms of the program.



15. The applicant Association has a number of publications including a business practice manual and various technical news letters and booklets which are issued to both members and non members of the Association. The Association places particular emphasis on education of its members and their employees, with a view to professional development of those members and employees.



16. The evidence also establishes that the Association acts as a lobbyist for private consulting surveyors. In this regard the Association seeks to open up avenues for additional work for its members and, in particular, seems to have focussed on access to government work.



17. In relation to educational activities which are outside what might be described as professional development of members and employees, the Association does very little, on the evidence before me, other than to provide prizes to various academic institutions. It is not the case that the applicant Association conducts examinations or conducts classes in surveying.



18. The respondent called evidence from a Mr J B Medbury, who gave evidence by telephone. Mr Medbury is a consulting surveyor and also the President of the Institution of Surveyors of Australia. He said in the course of his evidence that the Institution was established in 1952 as a federal body, with seven grades of membership and the objects of the Institution include the promotion of the science and practice of surveying in all its branches and the usefulness and efficiency of persons who are engaged therein to raise the character and status of the profession and its members. Furthermore, the Institution encourages the study of surveying and approves and elevates the general technical knowledge of the surveyor. These objects were achieved by the publication of journals devoted to research in the broad field of surveying and by meetings for its members. The evidence in relation to the Institution of Surveyors was really of little assistance to the Tribunal because there is no evidence about the standing of that Institution for income tax purposes and furthermore, I am required to decide this matter on the facts as they relate to the applicant, not by making a comparative analysis with another institution.



19. In summary, then, it can be said that the applicant Association is concerned with the objects as set out in Clause 3 of the Memorandum, and I am satisfied on the material before me, and so find, that it does in fact give effect to each of those objects and is carried on for that purpose.



20. I turn to consider whether the applicant is a public educational institution within the terms of paragraph 23(e) of the Act. There seems to be no doubt, and I so find, that the applicant is an institution. That aspect of the matter was not really argued before me. However, the claim for exemption under paragraph 23(e) fails on the very straightforward factual ground that the applicant itself is a federal body and the educational activities which it includes in its objects and in its day to day activities are, in fact, conducted by State bodies, which are affiliated with, but not part of, the applicant. In a sense the applicant provides the coordinating role, but does not perform the educational role. While the educational activities carried out by those State bodies are open to members and non members of the Association, I cannot be satisfied that the activities of the State bodies thereby confer upon the applicant the status of a public educational institution, whatever the arguments may be about the educational activities carried on by those State bodies. I am therefore satisfied that the applicant cannot come within the terms of paragraph 23(e) of the Act on that very fundamental point.



21. More difficult is the claim in respect of paragraph 23(h) of the Act. The material before me satisfies me, and I so find, that the applicant Association is not carried on for the profit or gain of its individual members. That is not to say that the members do not benefit from their membership of the applicant Association. That benefit is the usual benefit that arises with all professionals who belong to a body which looks after the interests of their profession. That, however, is not the sense in which the words “profit or gain” appear in paragraph 23(h), and I think it is now well beyond argument that a non profit association carried on for the professional benefit of its members will come within the terms of paragraph 23(h) if other criteria are satisfied.



22. In Case U118 87 ATC 710, the Tribunal discussed the meaning of “gain or profit” in paragraphs 41 and 42 of the reasons for decision. In paragraph 43 the Tribunal came to the conclusion that the applicant Association was carried on for the purposes of gain to its members. As a consequence that association was outside the terms of paragraph 23(h). The present case is different. It differs from the facts in Case U118 quite substantially. In that case the whole purpose of the formation of the association was for growers to get access to additional water storage for their commercial operations and also to get water at a discounted price. The benefits derived from the arrangement were entirely to do with the commercial operations of the growers who belong to the association and the arrangements made by the association for additional water storage facilities were for the purposes of gain to its members and that was a quantifiable gain in commercial terms. As I have already said the gain in the present case to the members of the Association is that of co-ordinating professional education and other professional activities, including promoting the profession and lobbying for access for the profession to government contracts etc. Those gains, such as they are to the members of the Association, are not quantifiable in any commercial sense, although, no doubt, to pursue those ends, is to improve the professional standing and the business prospects of the members of the association.



23. It seems to me the hurdle which the applicant cannot clear in this case is the decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Australian Insurance Association and FC of T 79 ATC 4569. That decision was concerned with the status of the Australian Insurance Association within the terms of paragraph 23(h) of the Act. The taxpayer in that case submitted that the relevant resource was the Australian insurance industry or, in the alternative, that the insurance know-how and expertise which the association fostered and which was made available to Australian industry generally (not just the insurance industry) could properly be said to be established to promote an industrial resource for Australia. Sheppard J found that the taxpayer in that case was concerned with the protection and furtherance directly and indirectly of the interests and businesses of its members in the sense of protecting or furthering the interests of Australian insurance companies generally and as a whole. He also found that the taxpayer’s aim was to create, in Australia, a pool of underwriting skill and knowledge so that insurance companies carrying on business in Australia have access to expert personnel, thereby avoiding the need to rely on persons from overseas. His Honour was satisfied that the undertakings of Australian insurance companies, geared as a whole, could be described as a resource of Australia and that the association therefore was promoting a resource of Australia. He was further satisfied that the dominant purpose of the taxpayer in that case was to promote the Australian resources. However, his Honour eventually upheld a submission of counsel for the Commissioner of Taxation that the words “industrial resources” when inserted in the paragraph to cover such resources as those of the building industry, the mining industry, or the quarrying industry. His Honour said that the word “industrial” was thus said to have been used in its traditional or conventional sense (at page 4574) and at page 4575 his Honour said that the building, mining and quarrying industries and the resources which these industries have, are not aptly described as manufacturing industries, but they are industries in the accepted sense of the word. Industries in a similar category are the shipping and transport industries. His Honour went on to say that the reference to the resources of those industries, as the industrial resources of Australia, does not impose upon the language any strain and enables the two expressions, “manufacturing resources” and “industrial resources”, to stand alongside each other and each to be given meaning and effect. His Honour therefore found that the Australian Insurance Association was not carried on for the purpose of promoting industrial resources of Australia.

24. The second leg of his Honour’s decision in the Australian Insurance Association case was to uphold the submission by the Commissioner of Taxation that the purpose referred to in paragraph 23(h) must be the principal or dominant purpose for which the association was established.



25. I am not satisfied, in the light of what was said by Sheppard J, that surveying comes within the meaning of “industrial resources” of Australia because the association promotes what can only be described as a service industry. It is undoubtedly true that all forms of industry in Australia will have a need for the services of a surveyor from time to time, although certain industries will clearly have a much greater demand for the work of surveyors than others. It is also equally clear, on the material before me, that persons who are not in business will also have a demand for the use of surveyors, but the ordinary citizen probably would only have such a requirement once in their lifetime, at the time of purchasing the family home, and it can be taken as a matter of notoriety that many purchasers of homes, in fact, do not get the property surveyed. I cannot see, in the light of what his Honour said in the Australian Insurance Association case how it can be said that the surveying industry itself or, perhaps more correctly describing it the surveying profession, can be said to be an industrial resource of Australia and I think, therefore, that the applicant must fail in its submission that it comes within the terms of paragraph 23(h).



26. Alternatively, I am also satisfied, and so find, that the Association is conducted primarily for the purpose of promoting the private surveying profession. In doing so, many activities are conducted. Some of those activities are directly related to the interests or the professional development of the members, while others will have less obvious connections, but taken as a whole it must be accepted, in my view, that the applicant Association is carried on for the purposes set out in the objects of the Association. It follows that I cannot be satisfied that the principal or dominant purpose for which the Association was established, is for the purpose of promoting the industrial resources of Australia. It is clear, in my view, and I so find, that the principal or dominant purpose for which the Association was established, was to promote the interests of its members. Therefore, if I be wrong in relation to whether the surveying profession is an industrial resource of Australia, I would still affirm the decision under review on the grounds that I cannot be satisfied that the principal or dominant purpose for which the Association was established and is carried on, comes within the terms of paragraph 23(h).



27. For these reasons the decision under review will be affirmed.





I hereby certify that the preceding nine (9) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matter of AT93/29 and COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION
