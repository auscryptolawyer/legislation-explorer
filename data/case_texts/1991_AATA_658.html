NT91/33 and Commissioner of Taxation [1991] AATA 658; (1991) 22 ATR 3356; 92 ATC 407 (6 August 1991)

Last Updated: 27 May 2009



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - professional footballer - legal costs incurred in obtaining a release from NZ Rugby League enabling applicant to play rugby league in Australia - whether costs incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income - whether costs deductible - whether of a capital nature - Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) s 51(1)



Income Tax Assessment Act- s 51(1)



Buckley v Tutty [1971] HCA 71; 125 CLR 353



Case X42 90 ATC 352

REASONS FOR DECISION



6 August 1991 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



1. The applicant is a professional footballer, currently employed by a club ("the club") in a city of New South Wales. He incurred legal costs of approximately $49,686 in the course of litigation in New Zealand against the New Zealand Rugby League in an endeavour to obtain a release from that organisation to enable him to pay rugby league in Australia. His claim for a deduction of those costs and associated interest, under s 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act was rejected after due objection. This application is brought to review that objection decision.



2. The applicant was born in New Zealand and played rugby league as a school boy. He participated in an Australian tour with the New Zealand School Boys at the age of 14. He later played semi-professionally in New Zealand up until 1987. In between New Zealand football seasons, he played in the United Kingdom for the Junior Kiwis.



3. At the end of 1987, he signed a contract to go to Australia and played with the club during the 1988 season. He obtained a release from the New Zealand Rugby League under a scheme where outstanding "rookies" were allowed to play in Australia for one season's experience. At that time, New Zealand rugby league players were limited in their ability to play outside their own country. It was the policy of the New Zealand Rugby League that a clearance would not be given to any player unless he had played either 6 tests or had gone on 2 full overseas tours for the New Zealand Rugby League. As these conditions obviously could not apply to young players, a special scheme for their development had been arranged.



4. Although he was aware of this, the applicant said that his intention was to stay in Australia indefinitely and to play in the Winfield Cup. He discussed this intention with the club's solicitor with whom he went to live during his stay in Australia.

5. As the 1988 season progressed, it became apparent that the club was pleased with his performance. Discussions took place concerning a further contract for the following season. The applicant said in evidence that he knew that he had a clearance only for the 1988 season, but he believed on advice from the solicitor that the New Zealand rules were not enforceable and that there would be no legal impediment to his obtaining the necessary clearance. Evidence was given that professional rugby league can not be played in New South Wales, unless the player

is registered with the NSW Rugby League. That registration is denied to a foreign player unless a clearance is obtained from the player's own league.



6. By the end of August 1988, the applicant said that he had concluded negotiations with the club for a contract for the 1989 season. The financial terms were much better than he would have been able to obtain in New Zealand. The contract contained an option on the part of the club to require his services for a further season. I was asked to reject evidence that this August 1988 contract had ever been signed for 2 reasons. It was said, firstly, that a copy of the agreement had never been made available to the respondent prior to the hearing nor had its existence been made known to the respondent; secondly, in the judgment of the New Zealand High Court it was said that the applicant "has been offered and is desirous of entering into a playing contract with [the club]".



7. I am unable to say why the New Zealand judge took this view of the facts. No doubt he proceeded only on the evidence put before him. No explanation was offered as to why the case was presented on this basis. Nevertheless, I am prepared to accept the applicant's evidence that there was a second contract in writing, even though he was unable to produce it. The applicant is clearly a truthful person. He was not particularly concerned about retaining a copy of his contract. He left that to others. Evidence was given by the club's solicitor in a careful and measured account of the circumstances. I accept his evidence that he made diligent searches amongst his own records at home where the applicant lived, at his office and at the office of the club. The club had been under different managers from time to time and the records may not have been in perfect order. The existence of the contract was also corroborated by a letter of 15 March 1989 from the president of the club to the club's solicitors in the following terms -



"As President of [the club] I confirm that [the club] and [the applicant] have signed a contract guaranteeing [the applicant] a sign-on fee plus weekly match payments. The contract is signed subject to the granting of a clearance to [the applicant] by [the club] Board and the registration of [the applicant] as a player by the New South Wales Rugby League.



I advise that [the applicant] directed [the club] to pay portion of the sign-on fee to meet his legal costs due and owing to his New Zealand lawyers which he estimates amount to about $30,000 at this stage.



Any effort that you could make to have the New Zealand case finalised so that we [the club] can press the New South Wales Rugby League to register [the applicant] as a player would be greatly appreciated".



8. I also accept the evidence of the applicant and the solicitor that the basic terms of the contract were the same as those for the 1988 season. This is a form of printed contract upon which the approval of the NSW Rugby League Limited is endorsed. It is a contract made between the player, the club and the NSW Rugby League under which the player contracts to play the game of rugby league for the club in such team and grade as the club shall specify. A number of ancillary obligations on the part of the player are also set out in the standard terms. Provision is made for negotiating an extension of the contract by the giving of certain formal notices prior to negotiations. Special conditions (or inserts as the solicitor called them) are added to cover matters personal to the particular player. These include the extent of the remuneration, the provision of a motor vehicle, the provision of outside employment or payment of a weekly wage in lieu thereof, the payment of match fees, the payment of air fares and certain other conditions dealing with medical treatment and insurance.



9. Prior to this contract being signed, the solicitor had written to the chairman of the New Zealand Rugby Football League requesting a release "which will enable the applicant to remain in Australia on a long term basis". According to the judgment in the subsequent New Zealand case, this letter remained unanswered. The judgment goes on to deal with subsequent correspondence. Copies of these letters were not placed in evidence before me but I have no reason to doubt their existence. Their terms were summarised by the judge in these words -



".....By letter dated 9 August 1988 the plaintiff's solicitors in [club's city] wrote to the League seeking clearance for the 1989 season, setting out reasons in support. At the same time the [NZ] League was sent a letter written by the plaintiff in which he stated that he had decided to stay in Australia on a permanent basis regardless of the outcome of the application. On 24 August the League's solicitors replied advising that the clearance would not be given, setting out what are some of the reasons which led to the refusal and also inviting information as to the financial arrangements available to the plaintiff in Australia were he able to take up the [club's] contract. Those details were supplied by letter of 30 August and on 8 September the League's solicitors wrote to the plaintiff's solicitors advising that after considering the letters of 9 August and 30 August and all other relevant matters the executive committee on 7 September had decided to confirm the earlier decision not to grant the clearance. These proceedings were then issued on 3 November."



10. The applicant gave evidence that he returned to New Zealand in September 1988 after the conclusion of the football season. He agreed that he had been pressing for a clearance for some time prior to that, and that he was still trying to obtain it. Just before Christmas he became aware that the New Zealand Rugby League did not intend to give him the clearance he sought and he decided to commence legal proceedings. The Court case was heard on 7, 8 and 15 February 1989. It concerned an allegation of unreasonable restraint of trade and resulted in a declaration that a certain rule and by-law of the New Zealand Rugby League constituted unreasonable restraint of trade and were void as between the applicant and the New Zealand Rugby League. Further leave to apply was reserved.



11. By that time the applicant was back in Australia to commence training for the 1989 season. He was ready to play on one particular day when he was told that, contrary to his anticipations, he was not to get the clearance he desired. Notwithstanding the declaration by the New Zealand High Court, the New Zealand Rugby League had again declined to give the necessary clearance. Further negotiations followed and did not conclude until April 1989 when agreement was reached between the parties. Under the terms of settlement, the club paid a sum of money by way of transfer fee, the New Zealand Rugby League gave the necessary release and each party agreed to pay its own costs.



12. The applicant had received an estimate of the amount of these costs. He said that it had been in his mind when he signed the August 1988 contract. By the time all the litigation and subsequent negotiations had been concluded the legal costs were much higher than the estimate and it was clear that the second contract would not be sufficient to enable the applicant to pay this account comfortably. At the request of the solicitor (who was not only an official of the club but also acted as the applicant's solicitor), the club agreed to enter into a third contract which was signed on 14 April 1989. The general terms of the contract remained the same. The financial rewards however were considerably greater and the length of the contract was extended to 3 years with 2 subsequent one year options. The applicant has been employed under the terms of that contract by the club since that time.



13. The solicitor gave evidence that he had been involved as an official and as a legal adviser in rugby league affairs for many years. He agreed that the NSW Rugby League and other leagues had always had restrictions on transfers. These however were varied regularly. He agreed that it was the responsibility of a player to obtain the necessary clearance before transferring to another club or to another league. From the club's point of view he said the practice was to enter into a contract first to secure the services of the player before negotiations were entered into to obtain the release. He said that the club was aware there had been many cases concerning the unreasonable restraints placed upon professional footballers (the leading Australian case is perhaps Buckley v Tutty [1971] HCA 71; 125 CLR 353). Having regard to the way the relevant law had been unfolded, he considered that there was a general expectation of little difficulty in obtaining a clearance. Nevertheless he agreed that the NSW Rugby League and his club, in particular, had had difficulty with the New Zealand Rugby League in obtaining clearances in the past. The club had been formed shortly before the applicant was signed on. It was necessary to build up a complete new team. In the course of doing this, the club had recruited a number of New Zealand players. Some were entitled to release, having had the necessary overseas experience, others prima facie were not.



14. The solicitor gave evidence that his understanding was the applicant had a contract with the club before a decision was made to embark on the litigation. He said that his firm had given instructions to a firm of New Zealand solicitors to represent the applicant. This put his firm in a position of responsibility to the New Zealand firm for their costs. He said he would not have undertaken that responsibility if he had not thought that the applicant had a secure source of funds from which he could be repaid. He denied that the contract was worthless unless a release was obtained. The purpose of the contract, he said, was as much to stop competing clubs from poaching as to secure the services of the desired player. Although the player could not play without a release, he could not transfer or deal with other clubs without the payment of a fee to his club. he agreed that although the second contract (and indeed the third contract) were not expressed to be subject to the obtaining of a clearance, they were in fact subject to successful conclusion of negotiations with the New Zealand Rugby League. That condition did not bother the club as "there was a strong feeling we would have been successful".



15. It is clear that the applicant at all material times was an employee notwithstanding the existence of the various contracts of service. The applicant's status is similar to that of the football players whose affairs were considered in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Maddalena 71 ATC 4161 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper [1991] FCA 164; 99 ALR 703. The applicant himself agreed that he did not consider he was carrying on a business. Accordingly, the only basis upon which he could be entitled to the deduction would be the first limb of s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. He thus must show that the amount in question is an outgoing "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" except to the extent to which it is an outgoing of capital.



16. It is well settled that to be deductible, such an outgoing must be "incidental and relevant" to the gaining of assessable income (John v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1989] HCA 5; 166 CLR 417 at 427). Merely because it is a pre-requisite to the earning of the taxpayer's income does not make it incidental or relevant. This was expressly re-stated in Lodge v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1972] HCA 49; 128 CLR 171. Lockhart J expressed the principle in Cooper at page 710 -



"The deductibility of the expenditure depends upon determining the essential character of the expenditure itself and not upon the fact that, unless it is incurred, the taxpayer will not be able to engage in the activity from which his income is derived."



17. It is necessary in determining the essential character of the expenditure to have regard to the time when it was incurred in relation to the earning of the assessable income. The outgoing is deductible only if it has the requisite temporal connection. If it is incurred before the income earning activity is commenced, then the necessary temporal connection is not established and any loss or outgoing is not deductible. In the well known passage in Maddalena Menzies J points out such expenditure would come "at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income. Expenditure incurred before the income earning activity is commenced, "would have been incurred in getting, not in doing, work as an employee".



18. The applicant contended that the present expenditure could be distinguished from that considered in Maddalena. Here, it was said, the money was not spent to gain new employment but was spent in an attempt to preserve and secure the then current employment. This was a view taken of the particular facts in Case X42 90 ATC 352 at 353. In my view, this is not an argument capable of application to the present facts.



19. I have found that there was a formal contract between the applicant and the club at the time the outgoing was incurred. Nevertheless there had not been, nor could there have been, any income earning activity as a result of that contract until such time as the New Zealand clearance had been obtained. This is clear from the evidence of the parties who went to such considerable lengths to obtain the necessary clearance. The contract may have been of some value to the club without the clearance in that it kept the applicant away from the club's rivals. This however is not the function of the contract with which I am concerned. I must look not to employment as such, or to contractual obligations as such, but to the applicant's income earning activity. This did not start until after the expenditure had been incurred. Indeed it was crucial that the expenditure be incurred before the income earning activity could be put in place.



20. This being so, I see no real distinction between the facts in this case and those considered in Maddalena. The principles discussed by the High Court in the case are equally applicable in the present circumstances. In Cooper at page 722 Hill J noted that "What is important is that the concept enshrined in the first limb of the sub-section is one of deductibility of working expenses". The subject sum can not be so described as it was not incidental and relevant as a working expense, not only because of the nature of the expense itself but also because it lacked the requisite temporal connection with the derivation of income.



21. Furthermore, the expense also has all the hallmarks of an outgoing of capital. The well known tests set out in Sun Newspapers Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; 61 CLR 337 at 363 inexorably point to this characteristic. It was a "once only" sum paid to secure a lasting advantage, a structural asset, namely the right to play remunerative football in New South Wales. It therefore falls, in any event, within the exception contained in s 51(1).



22. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
