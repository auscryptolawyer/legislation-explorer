DECISION

Date 18 December 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



........(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe............

CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - death benefit ‘eligible termination’ payment - whether dependant of deceased - whether spouse - whether living on a bona fide domestic basis as husband and wife

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 6(1), 27A, 27AAA

Roy v Sturgeon (1986) 11 NSWLR 454

Report of New South Wales Law Reform Commission on De Facto Relationships - June 1983



REASONS FOR DECISION

18 December 1997 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection lodged by the applicant against an amended assessment of income tax for the year ended 30 June 1995. The amendment was issued to include $79,270 as an eligible termination payment (“ETP”).
  2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by her accountant and tax agent, and the respondent by an officer of the respondent. Evidence was given by the applicant in person and by a friend of the applicant, Ms L, by telephone and by way of a signed statement. Pursuant to section 14ZZE of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, the applicant requested that the hearing be in private.
  3. In the return of income lodged on 6 November 1995 by the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1995, there was no reference to receipt of an ETP. By letter of 8 November 1995, the tax agent requested an amendment to include an ETP resulting from the death of a Mr B consisting of:
    • Pre July 1983 component $75,292
    • Post July 1983 component $75,506
    • Tax Instalment Deduction $23,709

The respondent issued an original assessment on 12 January 1996 without the inclusion of the ETP. On 24 April 1996 an amended assessment was issued to include $79,270 as income being 5% of $75,292 plus $75,506. An incorrect return penalty of $1,886 was imposed. The applicant’s tax agent lodged an objection on the grounds that the ETP should not have been included in assessable income having been received as a dependant of the deceased person. The objection was disallowed other than the remission of the incorrect return penalty.

  1. There was no dispute that the applicant received the payment from a superannuation fund of which Mr B was a member as the nominated beneficiary of that deceased member. It was accepted that the amount was an ETP as defined in paragraph (ba) of the definition of an ETP in section 27A(i) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”), and was covered by Item 2 in Table 1 of section 27AAA(2). The effect of these provisions is that the amount would be taxed concessionally as a death benefit ETP if the applicant was a dependant of the deceased person either at the time of his death or at the time of payment of the death benefit. The definition of dependant in section 27A(i) includes a person who is or was a spouse of the person. The definition of spouse is contained in section 6(1) of the Act and states:
“ ‘spouse’ in relation to a person includes another person who, although not legally married to the person, lives with the person on a bona fide domestic basis as the husband or wife of the person.”
  1. The issue before the Tribunal was whether the applicant satisfied the definition of a dependant of the late Mr B as being a spouse. There was no argument that she was not otherwise a dependant. It was argued solely that the applicant, whilst not legally married to Mr B, was a de facto spouse.
  2. The applicant gave evidence that she was introduced to Mr B by her brother in late 1989 and they became friends, although a more romantic, sexual relationship did not develop until the latter part of 1991. In a written statement submitted to the Tribunal the applicant said:
“... towards the end of 1991, a more serious dating relationship began, whereby we saw each other regularly, about three times a month. We stayed at each other’s place, were intimate with each other, and both only wanted a ‘one-on-one’ arrangement. We left a few of our personal things at each other’s place. We enjoyed going to movies, live theatre, wining/dining, staying home, watching videos, picnics and taking walks. Throughout the relationship we also took breaks from the city during holidays times, staying in my caravan. As time progressed, and up until 1994, we saw each other more often (at least once a week), depending on our commitments.

The relationship did not evolve into the conventional de facto domestic arrangement because of the practicalities of our work situation as B was working in Frankston where as I work in Collingwood. As well as this, I did not want to complicate my relationship with my children by exposing them to different influences and issues, than what they were used to. I also did not want my relationship with B to affect the special relationship they had with their father.

Our financial arrangements were separate because we both maintained separate jobs and income. I did not want to complicate this aspect of our relationship and we both felt it important to maintain our financial independence. However, we always helped each other financially when necessary. We both agreed that this arrangement would prevent any future financial disputes from being a source of contention.

The duration we spent together as partners effectively began at the end of 1991 until 1994. During this time, we saw each other on a weekly basis, mainly on the weekends and often stayed at each other’s place. It was impractical to spend greater periods of time together due to our work commitments, the distance between localities and the demands of my children, who at that time were aged eight and ten years old.”

In her oral evidence, the applicant said that she had hoped for a future permanent relationship. She said that Mr B did ask her to marry him in 1993 but she was not prepared to accept the proposal because of the concern for her children. She said that when Mr B stayed at her home the children generally were not present as she wanted to be discrete and careful because of a problem in a previous relationship where the other person wanted to influence the children.

  1. The statement of Ms L stated:
“I met the applicant in 1990 and knew of her relationship with Mr B from 1992 until 1994. I was aware of her involvement and relationship with B and considered them to be ‘an item’.

I saw them at a variety of social functions, including dining-out at restaurants, attending parties together, and different functions such as the annual ball.

They did not live together because of the applicant’s commitment to her children and the impracticality of the distance between localities.

I was also aware of their strong commitment to each other, and the fact that the relationship was an exclusive one.”

In her oral evidence by telephone, Ms L said that she considered the relationship of the applicant with Mr B as similar to that with her own partner for the seven years prior to their marriage, that is, an exclusive relationship. She accepted the proposition that a “de facto marriage” was generally seen as involving the parties living together but considered that this should not be an essential characteristic.

  1. It was submitted for the applicant that the question of whether the applicant was in a de facto relationship should be judged according to changing community standards and not on legal precedents established many years ago. It was said that while the applicant and Mr B did not share a common residence there was mutual commitment and support over some two and a half years, ended only by the illness and subsequent death of Mr B. They were said to have been perceived by friends as a couple with an exclusive sexual relationship. It was submitted that current attitudes favour more personal and financial independence.
  2. For the respondent it was submitted that the applicant did not satisfy the definition of spouse as she did not live with Mr B, there was no bona fide domestic basis of the relationship nor was the relationship equivalent to that of husband and wife. The respondent’s representative referred to several decisions of the Court and this Tribunal primarily in relation to Social Security and Family Law in support of the submission.
  3. It is with no disrespect to the comprehensive submissions of the representatives of both parties that I do not consider in detail the cases referred to. For the applicant, who relied on what was said to be the contemporary community view of what constituted a de facto relationship, it was not surprising that no precedents could be referred to which supported the view that the relationship of the applicant and Mr B was a de facto marriage. The case of Roy v Sturgeon (1986) 11 NSWLR 454 was said to provide an indication that it was not necessary to live in one home in order to satisfy the usual definition. It was suggested that a Report of the New South Wales Law Reform Commission (“NSWLRC”) indicated a possible change in standards in de facto relationships. It is assumed that the NSWLRC Report on De Facto Relationships of June 1983 is the report referred to. This report, when using the expression “de facto relationship” adapted the definition used in the Commonwealth Social Security Act 1947 as “the relationship between a man and a woman who, although not legally married to each other, live together as husband and wife on a bona fide domestic basis”. The Commission considered that the parties may have to satisfy additional criteria such as to have lived together for a specified minimum period before the law should confer benefits or impose obligations upon them. I am unable to see that anything in this report assists the applicant.
  4. The NSWLRC and the various decisions of the Tribunal and Courts have identified the following factors as relevant in deciding whether or not a relationship amounts to a de facto relationship:
    • Nature and extent of a common residence
    • Duration of the relationship
    • Degree of financial inter-dependence between the parties
    • Ownership, use and acquisition of property
    • Whether or not the couple have children
    • Organisation of the household
    • Degree of mutual commitment and support
    • Public perception of the relationship.

In this case it is difficult to see that the relationship of the applicant and Mr B had many of these factors. The predominant factor which, in my view, results in the applicant failing to satisfy the relevant definition is that they did not live together in any sense of that phrase. In the words of the applicant, they “often stayed at each other’s places”. It would appear that, at best, this was once per week, although the evidence seems to suggest less frequent intervals. There was no financial interdependence, no regular sharing of household duties, no joint ownership or use of property, and no evidence that they were seen in the eyes of friends or the public as being in a marriage-like relationship. The best that Ms L could do was to describe them as “an item”. My understanding of this colloquial term is that the applicant and Mr B were accepted as a couple who went to social occasions together, neither were in a relationship with another person, and there was a possibility of a future commitment. I have difficulty in seeing this relationship as being different to many boyfriend/girlfriend relationships or the relationship between a couple who are engaged to be married, the majority of which would be concerned if they were described as being in a de facto relationship.

  1. I am satisfied on the evidence of the applicant that she did not at any time live with Mr B on a bona fide domestic basis as the wife of Mr B. I accept that there was a loving relationship, a commitment to each other and a possibility that, if the impediments to marriage or a marriage-like relationship could be removed, a legal or de facto marriage might eventuate. There were employment, distance and children factors which led the two persons to build the relationship to that described by the applicant. However, the relationship does not satisfy the definition to enable the applicant to be regarded as a dependant of Mr B. Consequently, the decision under review must be affirmed.
