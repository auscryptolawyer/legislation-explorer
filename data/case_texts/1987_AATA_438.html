WT85/508-510 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 438; (1987) 19 ATR 3215; 88 ATC 288 (9 December 1987)

Last Updated: 3 August 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income tax - Assessable income - Alienation of income - Have trust, will travel - Taxpayer's services provided by the corporate trustee of his family trust as a "consultant" to various real estate agents - whether taxpayer's employment arrangements were shams - Whether sec. 260 applied - Illegality -Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1979, secs 19, 25, 260.

Cridland v FC of T 77 ATC 4538

FC of T v Barrett [1973] HCA 49; (1973) 129 CLR 395

Feate v FC of T (1964) 111 CLR 443

Case T4 86 ATC 123

FC of T v Gulland 85 ATC

Watson v FC of T ATC 4765

Pincus v FC of T 85 ATC 4765

Pursuant to subsection 43(1) (a) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal affirms the objection decision in relation to the assessments for the year ended 30 June 1979, 1980 and 1981.

(Sgd P Gerber)

SENIOR MEMBER

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. WT85/508, WT85/509, WT85/510

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And:

RESPONDENT

Before: Dr P Gerber

Date: 8 September 1987 and 9 December 1987

Place: Perth

The taxpayer in this application comes from a farming background. Together with his wife and other members of the family, he had been a member of a farming partnership in Western Australia. In 1976, for eminently respectable reasons of estate planning, a family trust - Craven A Pty Ltd - was established, the beneficiaries of which were the immediate members of the applicant's family (including the applicant). I shall henceforth refer to the trust as "Craven A". By 1977 the applicant, who suffers from an orthopaedic disability, had concluded that his condition would prevent him from making further useful contributions to the farming partnership. He therefore proceeded to look around for other opportunities of working within his physical reserve.

2. The family had been friendly with a Mr Benson, of Benson & Hodges, a firm of real estate agents, and after some negotiations, a new business relationship emerged involving the applicant - or rather Craven A - and the aforementioned Mr Benson. The applicant himself put this new relationship succinctly:

"The situation that we eventually developed and undertook was that I would operate one of the investment divisions, totally manage it, so therefore - because I was pretty concerned at the time, having been involved in a farming partnership where we had a situation that my wife was involved to a degree in the management and total package, that we structured something along the same lines. So I discussed this prospect with our accountant and he suggested that in view of the fact we already had a family trust in place and a trustee company in place, there was really no need to start a new partnership with just my wife and myself involved: that we were far better to simply use the existing vehicle that was in place, being our family trust, to be the operating business side of our operation."

3. In the result, Craven A proceeded to operate as a real estate agent, managing Benson & Hodges' rural division from an office within the firm's complex, albeit as a self-contained operation and following

"along similar lines to the partnership arrangement whereby the trustee company paid for all the costs associated with that particular Division, so in effect I was really, I guess, running another little business within my own entity under the umbrella of a larger organisation; so that the power costs, office costs, rental costs, stationery costs, advertising costs - every cost that was attributable to that section of business, Craven A paid."

This same trust account, incidentally, would, at times, be used to pay the dentist and the family doctor. This fact assumes some significance because the respondent, in one of his arguments, submits that the arrangement pursuant to which this trust purported to operate as a real estate agent was a sham. I shall return to this later.

4. Pausing here, what was sought to be achieved by the interposition of the trust is this: the trust employs the applicant (and his wife) in an employer/employee relationship and offers his services for hire to any real estate agent prepared to accept this arrangement. The estate agent agrees to pay the trust a "consultancy fee" for the hire of its employee(s), whilst the trust would make an agreed contribution for the use of the agent's office, rent, cost of telephone, etc. The profits of this combined operation will - after certain deductions, which include, inter alia, the wages paid by the trust to the applicant - be divided evenly between the firm and the trust, thus enabling the trust to distribute its share of the income in a way which produces a better fiscal result than if the income, generated by the trust's employee from commissions, were treated as having been derived by him personally.

5. The above brief summary encapsulates the arrangement entered into between the trust and three different real estate firms between the years 1979 and 1981. The arrangements were quite informal and were only formalised by professionally drawn up service agreements in 1982, a period outside the years now under review. That this arrangement was "unusual" was happily conceded by a Mr Rothman, of Rothman Bros Real Estate (the last of the three real estate agents to "employ" the trust in the years now under review). This gentleman had been in the real estate industry for over 20 years and held executive positions with the West Australian Institute Council and the Real Estate Institute (WA). He was asked by counsel for the applicant:

"During the course of that career have you seen other people in the same situation as the applicant? - No, I have not. I have seen a lot of people registered as sales persons, but not operating independently like the applicant, if that is what you want me to say, if that answers the question. I believe it is the first time in the whole of my career that I have seen or had an approach or been involved with somebody under those circumstances. It is abnormal to the usual employment of sales persons or registered sales people."

6. How the arrangement worked in practice was demonstrated by the 1981 Revenue and Expenditure Account of "Rothman Bros Real Estate Rural Division", the Division "operated" by the trust. This document disclosed that the entire income was received from commissions, that is, commissions earned on sales. The gross amount was $58,626. After deducting expenses - including "salaries 12,000" and "management fees 6,000" - a net profit of $26,204 was shown. In other words, the arrangement between the parties provides for a payment by the Rural Division of $6,000 to Rothman described as "management fees", as well as 50% of the net profit after certain deductions, including a salary of $12,000 paid to the applicant (although he preferred to call it a "consultancy fee" at the hearing).

  1. The dispute in the instant case involves amounts of $7,000, $53,330 and $31,881 received by Craven A from its real estate activities in the years 1979, 1980 and 1981 respectively. The Commissioner asserts that these amounts did not constitute "management fees" as alleged, but form part of the assessable income of the applicant. Alternatively, the arrangements constitute shams and/or are void as against the Commissioner by operation of sec 260 of the Tax Act.
  2. Reliance was placed on the provisions of the Real Estate and Business Agents Act (WA) 1978, which defines "Real Estate Agent" and "Real Estate Sales Representative" and provides that a person shall not carry on business, or by any means hold himself or itself out, as a real estate agent unless he or it is licensed as such under the Act and holds a current triennial certificate in respect of the licence. Section 42 provides that "a certificate of registration as a real estate representative shall not be granted otherwise than to an individual natural person". Section 44 (1) states:

"On and after 1 December 1979 a person not being a licensee who is the holder of a current triennial certificate shall not act as, or carry out any of the functions of, a real estate sales representative unless he is the holder of a current certificate of registration and he acts, or carries out those functions, for and on behalf of a licensee who is the holder of a current triennial certificate, or for and on behalf of a developer. Penalty: $300"

The effect of this legislation is that the agreement pursuant to which the applicant purported to work for Craven A (which was not at the relevant time a licensed real estate agent) as a real estate sales representative is void for illegality. It was urged upon me that I should therefore ignore the agreement, if only on the grounds of public policy. Mr Birmingham, of learned counsel for the Commissioner, put the argument persuasively. Nevertheless, I have concluded that the better view is that illegality simpliciter cannot, of its own force, give aid and comfort to the Commissioner. In this case, neither party to the agreement is seeking to enforce it, and, assuming the agreement to be unenforceable for illegality, the income derived by the trust from its (illegal) practice as a real estate agent is nonetheless taxable in its hands unless the agreement is annihilated by recourse to sec 260. A similar problem vexed Menzies J at first instance in Peate v FC of T ((1962) 111 CLR 443, 455-457). His Honour examined the alleged illegality in that case, but added that "ultimately I do not think that my conclusion upon the questions that were argued are vital to my decision upon Dr Peate's objection." I have reached a similar conclusion in this case, subject only to the rider that the illegality of the arrangement does indeed, at first blush, lend some support to the Commissioner's contention that the arrangements were shams and/or are explicable only in terms of tax avoidance.

9. In the end, I am satisfied that the resolution of this case does not depend on a sophisticated analysis of the decisions of the High Court in FC of T v Gulland, Watson v FC of T and Pincus v FC of T (85 ATC 4765), in that what occurred here is only explicable in terms of tax avoidance. Here we have a real estate sales representative solemnly engaged in selling land, ostensibly on behalf of a firm of real estate agents, in circumstances so unique that one of the agents deposed that he had never, in his entire career, "seen or had an approach or been involved with somebody under those circumstances.” He might have added that the arrangement was also illegal. It is sufficient if I merely cite the statement from Dawson J in Gulland where his Honour held that the "very complexity of the arrangement pointed to its contrived nature and took it beyond the range of transactions ordinarily encountered in the organization of affairs for business or personal reasons." (at p 4795). I am satisfied that in the instant case, the trust's employment of the taxpayer as a salesman is a facade for the reality, viz. that the income, made up of commissions on sales, is the sole product of the applicant's personal exertion as an employee of the various real estate agents, and the trust a mere repository for his earnings, no matter how hard the applicant and the various agents went out of their way to deny a relationship of master and servant inter se. It is true that some of the customary incidence between master and servant appear to be lacking in the arrangements in this case, such as superannuation, annual leave, long service leave, etc. However, no evidence was adduced on the usual practice of the trade relating to the engagement of land salesmen employed on a "commission only" basis, although counsel for the Commissioner referred me to FC of T v Barrett [1973] HCA 49; (1973) 129 CLR 395, which dealt with salesmen employed pursuant to the Land Agents Act of South Australia who were engaged in selling land on commission. It was held that such salesmen were "employees" for purposes of the Pay-roll Tax Assessment Act. In that case, such salesmen were paid by commission on sales and the evidence disclosed that most salesmen elected to be paid on a "commission only" basis because it gave them greater earnings. As in this case, the salesmen in Barrett employed on "commjssion only" did not receive annual leave or sick leave. In short, the conditions of employment as disclosed in that case appear very similar to those which regulated the relationship between the applicant and the various real estate agents in this case and which were relied on to emphasise the absence of an employer/employee relationship. I am satisfied that such differences as there are here do not negative a master and servant relationship.

  1. In the result, the amounts in issue in these applications constitutes income from personal exertion and were derived in each of the years now under review by the salesman who performed the exertion - the applicant/taxpayer.
  2. Counsel for the taxpayer placed heavy reliance on Case T4 86 ATC 123, the facts of which bear some resemblance to those in this case. They are fully summarised in the headnote:

"The taxpayer was a qualified draughtsman, although he had not practised as such between 1962 and 1978. In 1978 he found it difficult to secure permanent employment and his search for work led him back to drafting. During 1978-1979 he obtained employment in various fields and was an employee draughtsman with several firms for short periods. In the latter part of 1978 he operated a franchise through a family trust to market and install solar water heaters. Unfortunately, that business failed and the taxpayer took what jobs he could obtain for the remainder of 1979.

In December 1979 the taxpayer obtained drafting work through a personnel agency and was employed by that agency for four weeks. The chief draughtsman of the company at whose premises the taxpayer performed his work suggested that the taxpayer should operate through his own company structure as everybody else did. The taxpayer decided to operate through his existing family trust and future invoices were submitted to the personnel agency purporting to be on behalf of the trust's corporate trustee. The amounts received were entered in the trustee's books and deposited in its bank account. The taxpayer was not paid a salary by the trust until May 1981. During the 1982 year of income drafting services were supplied to seven firms. The taxpayer returned as income for that year the salary he was paid by the trustee of his family trust together with a distribution of trust income. The trustee returned the gross fees from the taxpayer's drafting activities and claimed deductions for expenses, salaries and superannuation contributions in respect of the taxpayer. The trust's income was shown as having been distributed to the taxpayer, his wife and two of his children.

The Commissioner assessed the taxpayer on the gross fees from the provision of drafting services on the basis that the income was derived by him and not the trust. The taxpayer objected and, on his objection being disallowed, he requested it be referred to a Board for review.

Held: objection allowed.

  1. The gross income, which was received by the trustee of the taxpayer's family trust for drafting services physically provided by the taxpayer, was derived by the trustee and not the taxpayer. The taxpayer was, from at least February 1980, an employee of the trustee. Each firm which benefited from and paid for drafting services performed by the taxpayer, did so pursuant to a bargain between the firm and the trustee.
  2. Section 19 of the Assessment Act did not operate to make the taxpayer assessable on the gross fees received by the trustee. The taxpayer had no contractual entitlement to receive those fees. The fees were derived by the trustee and not the taxpayer.
  3. The evidence did not disclose that the taxpayer had attempted to assign to the trustee earnings which he had derived from the firms to which his drafting services were provided. The trustee was contractually entitled to the fees, not the taxpayer.
  4. The arrangements between the taxpayer and the trustee relating to his employment, and between the trustee and the firms in relation to drafting services, were not shams.

5. The arrangements in this case were not ones which, objectively considered, were capable of explanation only as an attempt by the taxpayer to avoid tax. Section 260 had no application to this case.

The first thing to observe is that the decision in that case was penned before the three "doctor cases" were handed down, although Messrs McCarthy and Roach (Members) noted the High Court decisions in a postscript. They nevertheless concluded that these cases were of little relevance to the issue they had to decide. Critical to the Board's decision in Case T4 case was the finding that each firm which had the benefit of the drafting services performed by the applicant in that case did so pursuant to an agreement between that firm and the trustee. That is a far cry from the facts now before me, where it clearly emerged that anyone dealing with this taxpayer/salesman dealt with him as part of the employing real estate organisation. There was certainly no evidence that any of the clients of Bensen & Hodges had ever heard of Craven A or were aware that they were dealing with that entity. The inference is clear: the applicant was seen by the world at large as a real estate salesman employed by the agent under whose auspices he purported to act. No good reason was advanced why, as against the Commissioner, he should be seen as something else.

12. In the result, applying section 260, 1 find that the arrangement entered into between the trust and the various estate agents is void as against the Commissioner, it having as its main or dominant purpose the avoidance of tax. I find furthermore that the establishment of the family trust at an earlier point in time does not constitute an "antecedent transaction" as that concept has been applied in such cases as Cridland v FC of T 77 ATC 4538) so as to take it out of the "classic" interpretation of sec. 260 as enunciated in Peate v FC of T (supra). When the facade in this case is stripped away, the facts which are revealed support an actual liability to tax without the need for any so-called "reconstruction". The arrangement is no more and no less than an attempted assignment of income from personal exertion to a trust and void as against the Commissioner. The Crown's alternative argument on "sham" does not, in the circumstances, require further consideration.

13. The decisions on the objections in each of the three years now under review must be upheld.

This is to certify that this and the preceding 10 pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons for Decision of Dr P. Gerber, Senior Member in matter Nos. WT85/510, WT85/509 and WT85/50B.
