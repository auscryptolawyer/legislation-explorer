CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - Australian resident in receipt of pension from Malaysian Government - whether pension taxable in Australia in light of the Agreement Between The Government of Australia and The Government of Malaysia For The Avoidance of Double Taxation and The Prevention of Fiscal Evasion With Respect to Taxes on Income - decision affirmed

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss.25(1), 161

Income and Fringe Benefits Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953 (formerly the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953) - ss.4, 11F(1), Schedule 16

REASONS FOR DECISION

3 April 1998 Deputy President B.H. Burns



  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent on 25 February 1997 to disallow the applicant's objection to his tax assessment dated 30 October 1996.
  2. The background to the application is that the applicant lodged an annual tax return dated 9 August 1996 for the year of income ended 30 June 1996 with the respondent pursuant to s.161 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the ITAA”) (T3). The respondent’s assessment of the applicant's taxable income and the amount of tax payable for the relevant year of income led to the issuing of a Notice of Assessment dated 11 September 1996 (T5). The applicant lodged an objection against the Notice of Assessment dated 30 October 1996 contending that the amount of tax payable had been erroneously assessed by the respondent (T6). On 25 February 1997, the respondent made the decision to disallow the applicant's objection which is now the subject of review (T8).
  3. The applicant was represented by Mr McWilliams, a chartered accountant, and the respondent was represented by Ms Hurley. This matter proceeded without the need to call any oral evidence. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the “T documents”) together with the exhibits tendered by each party and their written submissions.
  4. The parties tendered a statement of agreed facts (Exhibit R2), and the Tribunal finds as fact the following contained therein:

‘1. The matter in dispute for the consideration of the Tribunal is the

interpretation of Paragraph 2 of Article 18 of Schedule 16

[“Malaysian Agreement”] of the International Tax Agreements Act

1953 (sic) [“TAA”]. (See paragraph 5 for clarification with respect to

the correct names of the relevant legislation and their abbreviations

for the purposes of these Reasons for Decision.)

  1. The applicant is a resident of Australia for the purposes of

    Australian tax.
  2. The applicant is not a resident of Malaysia for the purposes of

    Malaysian tax.
  3. The applicant receives a Malaysian Civil Service Pension of

    MR$1,212.50 per month. In the 1996 year, he received the

    equivalent of seven such monthly payments paid in a lump sum

    plus a further six monthly payments, totalling MR$15,762.50.
  4. The applicant’s entitlement to the pension arose from his previous

    employment with the Malaysian Inland Revenue Department.
  5. The services rendered by the applicant, in his capacity as an

    employee of the Inland Revenue Department of the Government of

    Malaysia were not services rendered in connection with a trade or

    business carried on by the Government of Malaysia.
  6. The pension is a pension of the kind referred to in Article 18 of the

    Malaysian Agreement.
  7. On 13 August 1996, the applicant lodged an Income Tax Return for

    the year ended 30 June 1996. In that return he included Malaysian

    pension income of A$8,169.00 being the Australian dollar

    equivalent of the MR$15,762.50 received during the year. He

    claimed a foreign tax credit of A$260.98.
  8. By notice of assessment issued to the applicant on 11 September

    1996, the respondent assessed the applicant for the year ended

    30 June 1996 as liable to tax of $991.45 on the Malaysian pension

    income of A$8,169.00. In arriving at the liability to tax on the

    income, the respondent allowed a foreign tax credit of $260.98.
  9. The foreign tax credit of $260.98 was allowed in accordance with

    the double taxation relief provision contained in Paragraph 3 of

    Article 23 of the Malaysian Agreement.
  10. By Notice of Objection dated 30 October 1996, the applicant

    objected to the assessment on the grounds that Paragraph 2 of

    Article 18 of the Malaysian Agreement does not allow for his

    Malaysian Civil Service Pension to be taxed in Australia.
  11. By Notice of Objection Decision dated 25 February 1997, the

    respondent disallowed the objection.’
  12. As is apparent, it was agreed by the parties that the applicant's pension was to be considered and assessed for taxation purposes under the Agreement Between The Government of Australia and The Government of Malaysia For The Avoidance of Double Taxation and The Prevention of Fiscal Evasion With Respect to Taxes on Income (the “Malaysian Agreement”). As stated in the preamble, the Malaysian Agreement is an agreement made by the respective governments of each country for “the avoidance of double taxation and the prevention of fiscal evasion with respect to taxes on income”. The Malaysian Agreement is incorporated into Australia’s domestic law by virtue of s.11F(1) of the Income and Fringe Benefits Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953 (the “Tax Agreements Act” or “TAA”), (formerly the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953), which relevantly provides:
‘Agreement with Malaysia

11F. (1) Subject to this Act, on and after the date of entry into force of the Malaysian agreement, the provisions of the agreement, so far as those provisions affect Australian Tax, have, and shall be deemed to have had, the force of law⊗

...

(b) in relation to tax other than withholding tax⊗ in respect of income of any year of income that commenced on or after 1 July 1979 and in relation to which the agreement remains effective.

...’

The terms of the Malaysian Agreement are set out in Schedule 16 of the TAA.

  1. Section 4 of the TAA provides that the ITAA is to be incorporated into and read as one with the TAA. It relevantly provides:
‘Incorporation

4. (1) Subject to sub-section (2), the Assessment Act is incorporated and shall be read as one with this Act.

(2) The provisions of this Act have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent with those provisions contained in the Assessment Act (other than section 160AO or Part IVA of that Act) or in an Act imposing Australian tax.

...’

By virtue of this section, Australia has agreed to limit its general taxation power with respect to non-exempt foreign incomes (of which it was agreed the applicant's pension was one) which ordinarily arises under s.25(1) of the ITAA, and in the manner prescribed by the terms of international tax treaties. The Malaysian Agreement is but one of many agreements entered into by Australia and other countries.

  1. It was agreed that the applicant’s pension was a government service pension that fell for consideration under Article 18 of the Malaysian Agreement. The primary dispute between the parties relates to the interpretation to be given to Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement, and whether Article 18(2) limits Australia’s domestic taxing powers under the ITAA, if at all. In its entirety, Article 18 reads as follows.
‘Government Service
  1. Remuneration (other than a pension or annuity) paid by a Contracting State or a political sub-division or a local authority thereof to any individual in respect of services rendered in the discharge of governmental functions shall be taxable only in that State. However, such remuneration shall be taxable only in the other Contracting State if the services are rendered in that other State and the recipient is a resident of that other state who:
(a) is a citizen or national of that State; or

(b) did not become a resident of that State solely for the purpose of

performing the services.
  1. Any pension paid by, or out of funds created by, a Contracting State or a political sub-division or a local authority thereof to any individual in respect of services rendered to that State or sub-division or local authority thereof shall be taxable in that State.
  2. The provisions of paragraphs 1 and 2 shall not apply to remuneration or pensions in respect of services rendered in connection with any trade or business carried on by one of the Contracting States or a political sub-division or a local authority thereof. In such a case, the provisions of Articles 14, 15 and 17 shall apply.’

In the context of the applicant’s pension, Article 18(2) would read that ‘Any pension paid by, or out of funds created by, Malaysia to any individual in respect of services rendered to Malaysia shall be taxable in Malaysia’.

After some dispute as to the interpretation of the Malay language version of Article 18(2), a translation was made by an accredited translator, and the parties agreed the following to be an accurate English translation.

‘Whatever pension which was paid by, or of moneys produced by, a Contracting State or a political small section or a local authority to any individual persons relating to services given to that Contracting State or the political small section or local authority may be taxed in the Contracting State.’
  1. The Tribunal would indicate at the outset, that the parties have made their submissions on the basis that Article 18(2) reads as first outlined above and the Tribunal is satisfied that that is the correct portrayal of Article 18(2) for present purposes and the one utilised by the Tribunal in its deliberations.
  2. In short, the applicant’s case was that the preferred interpretation of the words of Article 18(2) is one which confers on Malaysia the sole right to tax a government service pension paid by the Malaysian Government. The applicant contended that its preferred interpretation had the effect of precluding the respondent from also taxing the same pension. In this way, the applicant submitted that by virtue of s.4(1) of the TAA, Article 18(2) operates to remove any right which the respondent might have otherwise had under the ITAA to tax government service pensions emanating from Malaysia such as that received by the applicant.
  3. The respondent’s case was that it could not be said, having regard to the words of Article 18(2), the context, aims and objects of the Malaysian Agreement as a whole and to the principles of interpretation of international treaties, that Article 18(2) in any way limited or removed the respondent’s power under domestic law to tax pensions of the kind described in Article 18(2). The respondent’s preferred interpretation is one which doesn’t confer on Malaysia the sole power to tax the applicant’s pension, but permits both Contracting States to tax, subject to any foreign tax credits that might be available under Article 23, the double taxation relief provision.
  4. Each party provided submissions from time to time, and the Tribunal is grateful for the assistance these have provided to it in its deliberations. The Tribunal finds it unnecessary to detail each and every submission made, but indicates that at this point, it would be useful to summarise the main submissions of each party.
  5. Both parties submitted, and the Tribunal agrees, that the rules of interpretation contained in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (“the Vienna Convention”) apply to the interpretation of the Malaysian Agreement. Article 31 of the Vienna Convention relevantly provides that:
‘1. A treaty shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose.
  1. The context for the purpose of the interpretation of a treaty shall comprise, in addition to the text, including its preamble and annexes:
(a) any agreement relating to the treaty which was made between all

the parties in connexion with the conclusion of the treaty:

(b) any instrument which was made by one or more parties in

connexion with the conclusion of the treaty and accepted by the

other parties as an instrument related to the treaty.
  1. There shall be taken into account, together with the context:
(a) any subsequent agreement between the parties regarding the

interpretation of the treaty or the application of its provisions;

(b) any subsequent practice in the application of the treaty which

establishes the agreement of the parties regarding its

interpretation;

(c) any relevant rules of international law applicable in the relations

between the parties.
  1. A special meaning shall be given to a term if it is established that the parties so intended.’

  2. Article 32 of the Vienna Convention provides that:

‘Recourse may be had to supplementary means of interpretation, including the preparatory work of the treaty and the circumstances of its conclusion, in order to confirm the meaning resulting from the application of article 31, or to determine the meaning when the interpretation according to article 31:

(a) leaves the meaning ambiguous or obscene; or

(b) leads to a result which is manifestly absurd or unreasonable.’

  1. The Tribunal now turns to a consideration of the parties submissions.
  2. The respondent submitted that the words of a treaty are paramount, and that to use alternative words to those in the treaty is not to interpret the agreement in accordance with the Vienna Convention. The respondent submitted that the ordinary meaning to be ascribed to the words “shall be taxable in that State” is that Malaysia has reserved its right to tax a government service pension paid by it to a resident of Australia. The respondent submits that by the words alone, Article 18(2) is silent on Australia’s ability to also tax a Malaysian Government pension.
  3. The respondent then submitted that when interpreting international tax treaties, there is a presumption in favour of domestic tax law being operative, except where it is expressly suspended or limited by the provisions of a treaty. The respondent submitted that the terms of a treaty operate to alter domestic tax law either by expressly excluding certain incomes from taxation, or expressly allowing a foreign tax credit against domestic tax payable for the tax already paid in the other State. The respondent said that where a taxation treaty is silent on a particular right, it is to be implied that the treaty does not limit domestic taxing law. Thus, the respondent contends, as Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement does not expressly limit Australia’s domestic taxing powers with respect to overseas sourced incomes such as the applicant's government service pension, no such limitation can be read into the words of the treaty, nor should it be read in having regard to the rules of interpretation set down in the Vienna Convention.
  4. The respondent provided further submissions in support of its interpretation of Article 18(2). The respondent submitted that absence of the word “only” in Article 18(2) is significant in the interpretation of this provision. The respondent compared the wording of Articles 18(1) and (2) and submitted that had the negotiators wanted one State or other to reserve a sole taxing right with respect to government service pensions, the word “only” would have been included in the phrase “shall be taxable in that State”, as had been done in Article 18(1) with respect to remuneration other than pensions or annuities. The respondent said that the distinction between 18(1) and (2) and specifically the omission of the word “only” in the latter provision, was purposeful on the part of the negotiators of the treaty and such distinction implies a clear intention that Australia was also reserving a right to tax government service pensions emanating from Malaysia.
  5. In its submissions, the respondent also took the Tribunal to Article 22 of the Malaysian Agreement which reads as follows:
‘Sources of Income

Income derived by a resident of one of the Contracting States which, under any one or more of Articles 6 to 8, 10 to 16, and 18 may be taxed in the other Contracting State, shall for the purpose of Article 23, and of the income tax law of that State, be deemed to be income from sources in that other State.’ (emphasis added)
  1. The respondent points out that Article 22 is a deeming provision which facilitates the operation of Article 23, the provision which allows for relief from double taxation in the form of foreign tax credits. The respondent says that since Articles 18(1) and (3) both refer to income which is solely taxable in only one State, Article 22 must necessarily contemplate that Article 18(2) permits pensions to be taxed in both States. The respondent contends that were this not the case, then Articles 22 and 23 would be made a nonsense, since the wording of Article 22 contemplates that somewhere in Article 18 will be income which may be taxed in both Contracting States, to which relief in the form of foreign tax credits will then be available under Article 23. This, says the respondent, precludes any interpretation of Article 18(2) which limits taxation solely to Malaysia.
  2. The Tribunal now turns to the submissions on behalf of the applicant.
  3. The applicant contended that the ordinary meaning of Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement, and specifically the phrase “shall be taxable in that State”, is that Malaysia is compelled to tax any government service pension which it pays to a resident of Australia.
  4. The applicant submitted that the Malaysian Agreement does not have the mandatory imposition of taxation as one of its objectives. In agreement with the respondent, the applicant contended that the terms of the treaty operate to restrict domestic tax law by either excluding certain incomes from taxation, or by allowing a foreign tax credit against the tax already paid in the other Contracting State, but does not permit the mandatory imposition of taxation. Hence, the applicant contended that an alternative meaning must be found which better reflects the aims and objects of the treaty.
  5. The applicant then submitted that there are two possible interpretations of the words “shall be taxable in that State” which avoid the interpretation problem outlined above. The first is that the phrase means “shall be taxable only in that State”, meaning that Malaysia has reserved the sole taxing right in respect of government service pensions paid by it. The second is that the phrase means “may be taxed in that State”, being a standard form whereby both Contracting States agree to reserve a taxing right over particular income.
  6. The applicant submitted that the former of these two interpretations is the preferable one. In this regard, the applicant examined the Malaysian Agreement in its entirety and concluded that as a rule, use of the word “shall” denoted an intention that one State reserves the sole taxing right over a particular form of income, whereas use of the word “may” denoted an intention for a taxing right to be shared between two States. Refuting the submission of the respondent, the applicant contended that absence of the word “only” following the words “shall be taxable” in Article 18(2) did not derogate from a clear intention to give Malaysia a sole taxing right. To this effect, the applicant maintained that had the treaty negotiators intended the taxing rights over government service pensions be shared, the word “may” would have been substituted for “shall”. This, the applicant said, would be consistent with other Articles in the Malaysian Agreement including Articles 6 (income from land), 7 (business income not derived from a permanent establishment in the other State), 8(2) (shipping and air transport), 10 (dividends), 11 (interest), and 12 (royalties).
  7. A further submission of the applicant relied upon the Vienna Convention. At the outset the applicant contended that the meaning of Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement is ambiguous. Further, the applicant submitted that the respondent’s interpretation would produce a manifestly absurd and unreasonable result, since the object of the Malaysian Agreement is to avoid double taxation, not impose it as the respondent would have. This ambiguity and absurdity, the applicant says, allows recourse to be had to supplementary means of interpretation pursuant to Article 32 of the Vienna Convention.
  8. In terms of supplementary material, the applicant suggested firstly that the Tribunal refer to other international double tax agreements similar to the Malaysian Agreement. The applicant submitted that throughout these agreements there is a presumption that the State paying a government service pension will have the sole taxing right over that pension which reverses only where there are express provisions to this effect. Article 19(2) of the Sri Lankan Agreement (Schedule 31 of the TAA) was proffered as an example of the type of express condition upon which the tax onus with respect to government service pensions usually shifts to the non-paying State. Here, the sole taxing right will shift to the State which doesn’t pay the pension only when the recipient of the income is a resident, and a citizen or national of the other State. By analogy, the applicant says that a similar express condition absent from Article 18(2) would be required for the presumption of taxation to be shifted away from Malaysia in respect of government service pensions paid to residents of Australia
  9. By way of further supplementary material, the applicant looked to the 1977 OECD Model Convention and Commentary, on which the Malaysian Agreement was based. In that document, Article 19(2) is virtually identical to Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement but for the word “only” used in conjunction with the phrase “shall be taxable”. The applicant's submission was that this Article and adjoining commentary provided weight to the proposition that it is internationally accepted that government service pensions be taxed only in the State which pays them, and that the onus of taxation is only altered when certain express conditions are met. Like Article 19 of the Sri Lankan Agreement, Article 19(2) of the OECD Model Convention provides an express condition reversing the taxation onus, ie that “such pension shall be taxable only in the other Contracting State if the individual is a resident of, and a national of, that State”. In summary, the applicant contended that the Malaysian Agreement should be interpreted in conjunction with the terms of the 1977 OECD Model Convention and other international taxation agreements in which it is usually presumed that an exclusive taxing right is given to the paying State in respect of government service pensions.
  10. The respondent provided brief submissions in answer to the applicant’s submissions. Firstly, the respondent submitted that none of the supplementary material relied on by the applicant was relevant, as each treaty is negotiated under different political, economic and cultural climates. The respondent submitted that in a case such as this where there is a sensible interpretation available, having regard to the words of a treaty alone, then no recourse to supplementary material is permitted.
  11. Also, the respondent took issue with the applicant's initial argument to the effect that the ordinary meaning of the words “shall be taxable” was to impose a mandatory taxation obligation on Malaysia. The respondent said that this was only the case if one has regard to the words of Article 18(2) in isolation. Rather, having regard to the words in the context of the entire Agreement, a sensible interpretation is ascertained without recourse to supplementary material. The applicant submitted that it was preferable to interpret the words of Article 18(2) in a manner consistent with the words of the treaty as a whole, rather than take the words out of their context, interpret them in isolation, and then resort to extrinsic and supplementary material to clear up any ambiguities as the applicant had done. This, the respondent said, was in contravention of the principles in the Vienna Convention.
  12. The respondent also took issue with the applicant’s claim that the respondent’s preferred interpretation of Article 18(2) would lead to a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result, given, in the applicant’s submissions, that the treaty’s aim is to avoid double taxation rather than impose it. The respondent submitted that the objects of the Malaysian Agreement are equally served by the operation of Article 23 which provides for the elimination of double taxation via foreign tax credits, as they might be by provisions which prohibit double taxation. The respondent submitted that double taxation is contemplated by the Malaysian Agreement to be valid in some situations, hence the provision for foreign tax credits, and hence the reasonableness of its interpretation. The respondent contended that since its preferred interpretation is entirely consistent with the aims and objectives of the Malaysian Agreement, there can be no ambiguity such that recourse be had to any supplementary means of interpretation.
  13. Notwithstanding its submission that extrinsic materials are irrelevant, the respondent contended that Australia’s position has always been that all pensions are taxable in the country of residence, ie. in Australia, despite being taxed in their country of origin as well. This is evidenced by the reservation lodged by Australia to Article 18 of the 1977 OECD Model Convention, a provision stating that pensions should be only taxed in the country or origin. This, the respondent said, gives weight to the assumption that the Australian intention was clearly to reserve its right to tax foreign sourced pensions during the negotiation of the Malaysian Agreement, hence the intended omission of the word “only” in the phrase “shall be taxable in that State”.
  14. The Tribunal now turns to a consideration of the matters at hand and, in doing so, would indicate that it has given careful consideration to the submissions of the parties in light of the material before it. It is clear, and the Tribunal so finds, that the applicant, being a resident of Australia, is subject to the taxation laws of this country. The assessable income of the applicant includes the gross income derived from all sources whether in or out of Australia which is not exempt income ⊗ s.25(1) of the ITAA.
  15. It is clear, and the Tribunal so finds, that unless the Malaysian Agreement and, in particular, Article 18(2) of that Agreement have the effect of limiting or exempting the domestic taxing powers of the ITAA, which powers have been incorporated into the TAA, then the decision under review must be affirmed.
  16. The Tribunal’s considered view is that as the Malaysian Agreement has been drawn into Australian domestic law by virtue of s.11F(1) of the TAA, then its interpretation is governed by Articles 31 and 32 of the Vienna Convention. Article 31(1) requires the Malaysian Agreement and, in particular, Article 18(2) of that Agreement, for present purposes, to be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the Agreement in their content and in light of its object and purpose. In so doing, the Tribunal must have regard to paragraphs 2, 3 and 4 of Article 31.
  17. The object of the subject Agreement is clear from the preamble to the Agreement set out in Schedule 16 of the ITAA, namely “... to conclude an Agreement for the avoidance of double taxation and the prevention of fiscal evasion with respect to taxes on income” as is the purpose which is plainly set out in the explanatory Memorandum that accompanied the ITAA, namely “The agreement(s) set out the basis on which, and the extent to which, income derived in one country by residents of the other is to be taxed in each country and the basis on which relief from double taxation is to be effected where income may be taxed in both countries”.
  18. Regard must also be had to Article 18(2) in the context of the terms of the Malaysian Agreement. It is clear that for the purpose of allowing for the elimination of taxation by the credit method which is only applicable where a payment may be taxable in both Contracting States, that Article 22 of the Malaysian Agreement deems income derived by a resident of one of the Contracting States, which under any one or more of Articles 6 to 8, 10 to 16 and 18 may be taxed in the other Contracting State shall, for the purposes of Article 23 and of the income tax law of that other State, be deemed to be income from sources in that other State. Article 18 is specifically referred to in Article 22 and the only income that might be said to allow for each Contracting State to tax are the pensions referred to in Article 18(2).
  19. It must also be borne in mind that Article 23 of the Malaysian Agreement plainly says that “The laws in force in each of the Contracting States shall continue to govern the taxation of income in that Contracting State except where provision to the contrary is made in this Agreement. Where income is subject to tax in both Contracting States relief from double taxation shall be given in accordance with the following ...” (emphasis added). And so, it is clear that the Malaysian Agreement explicitly recognises the continuum of the tax laws of each of the Contracting States. It is also clear that for any exemption to that state of affairs, there needs to be a provision to the contrary.
  20. The Tribunal’s considered view is that Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement is not such a provision. If it were the intention of the Contracting States to make the government pensions referred to in Article 18(2) only taxable in the State which paid the pension in the first instance, then it would have said so. All that was needed was to relevantly use the word “only”. This latter word has been used from time to time throughout the Malaysian Agreement to indicate the Contracting States’ intention to limit the taxing of certain income to only one of the Contracting States to the exclusion of the other - see Articles 7, 8, 14, 17 and 18(1). The absence of the word “only” is clearly significant, particularly bearing in mind its use elsewhere in the Malaysian Agreement.
  21. It is also to be noted that a number of Articles make use of the word “may” in terms of a Contracting State’s power to tax in certain situations ⊗ see for example Articles 12, 13 and 15. It is clear by its use that the Contracting States wished to clearly set out where and in what circumstances a Contracting State’s taxing power may be exercised.
  22. It is the Tribunal’s view that the use of the word “shall” in Article 18(2) indicates a contrary intention on the Contracting States’ behalf, namely to clearly indicate with respect to the subject government pensions that they are taxable as distinct from the permissive sense which would have been conveyed by the use of the word “may.”
  23. Quite clearly, the Contracting States have indicated by the terms of Article 18(2) that where the relevant pension is paid for services rendered to that State then it is taxable in that State. In short, Article 18(2) means what it says and it does not say that the relevant pension is taxable only in the State which paid it. One must also keep in mind that one of the purposes of the Malaysian Agreement is to provide relief from double taxation. Article 23 specifically deals with the provision of this relief.
  24. The Malaysian Agreement, read as a whole, clearly delineates between, on the one hand, situations where the taxing power can only be exercised by one Contracting State to the exclusion of the other and, on the other hand, situations where both Contracting States exercise their taxing powers in relation to the same subject matter.
  25. The Tribunal’s considered view is that the subject matter before this Tribunal clearly constitutes one of those latter situations.
  26. The ordinary meaning to be given to Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement is that it means what it says, no more and no less. Its meaning is plain and not such that the Tribunal should have recourse to Article 32 of the Vienna Convention for further assistance in the interpretation of Article 18(2) of the Malaysian Agreement.
  27. In conclusion, the Tribunal would indicate that it is in agreement with the submissions made by the respondent and rejects the submissions made by the applicant for the reasons outlined above and for those advanced by the respondent.
  28. Accordingly, the decision of the Tribunal is that the decision under review is affirmed.
