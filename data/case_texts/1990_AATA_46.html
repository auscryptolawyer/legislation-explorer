Re Catherine Mabel Ray and Robert Charles Ray As Trustees of the Estate of Douglas Robert Ray (Deceased) and Repatriation Commission [1990] AATA 46 (1 March 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CATHERINE MABEL RAY AND ROBERT CHARLES RAY as

trustees of the estate of DOUGLAS ROBERT RAY (deceased)

And: REPATRIATION COMMISSION

No. V87/691

AAT No. 5740

Veterans' Affairs

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

VETERANS' APPEALS DIVISION

Mrs R.A. Balmford(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Veterans' Affairs - application for special rate of pension - whether war-caused condition alone prevented veteran from continuing to undertake remunerative work - share trader

Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 s.24, 24A

Re Cavell and Repatriation Commission (1986) 10 ALN N233

Re Lambert and Repatriation Commission (Decision No 5353: 31 August 1989)

HEARING

MELBOURNE

1:3:1990

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the late Douglas Robert Ray was entitled, with effect from 31 December 1986, to pension at the special rate provided for in section 24 of the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986.

DECISION

This is an application lodged on 12 November 1987 by Douglas Robert Ray ("the veteran") for review of a decision of the Repatriation Commission made on 20 May 1986 assessing his incapacity from war-caused injury or disease at 100 per cent of the general rate with effect from 24 February 1986. On 18 August 1987 that decision was affirmed by the Veterans' Review Board.

2. Mr Ray died on 17 June 1989, and by a Direction of the Tribunal made on 22 November 1989 pursuant to section 126 of the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 ("the Act"), the present applicants, as trustees of his estate, were substituted as the applicants in these proceedings.

3. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and numbered T1 to T5 ("the T documents"), together with other material, including taxation returns, which was lodged at the hearing.

4. Evidence was given at the hearing by Mr Ray's widow, Mrs Joan Ray. Her evidence was not challenged by the respondent, and I accept it in full. A statement by a stockbroker who assisted Mr Ray for many years was, with the consent of Mr Parrott, conveyed to me verbally by Mr Spence, and I accept that statement also. The applicants were represented by Mr Spence of counsel and the respondent initially by Mr Parrott, and later by Mr Smith, both of the Advocacy Section of the Department of Veterans Affairs.

5. Section 24 of the Act is the present statutory provision establishing the special rate of pension (also known as the TPI rate). The submission of the applicants is that Mr Ray was entitled to be paid pension at that rate. By virtue of sections 14 and 15 of the Veterans' Affairs Legislation Amendment Act 1988 ("the amending sections") section 24 has since 22 December 1988 read, so far as relevant, with effect from 22 May 1986:

"24.(1) This section applies to a veteran,

other than a veteran to whom section 25

applies, if -

(a) either

(i) the degree of incapacity of the

veteran from war-caused injury or

war-caused disease, or both, is

determined under section 21A to

be 100% or has been so determined

by a determination that is in

force; or

(ii) ...

(b) the veteran is totally and permanently

incapacitated, that is to say, the

veteran's incapacity from war-caused

injury or war-caused disease, or both,

is of such a nature as, of itself alone,

to render the veteran incapable of

undertaking remunerative work for

periods aggregating more than 8 hours

per week; and

(c) the veteran is, by reason of incapacity

from that war-caused injury or

war-caused disease, or both, alone,

prevented from continuing to undertake

remunerative work that the veteran was

undertaking and is, by reason thereof,

suffering a loss of salary or wages, or

of earnings on his or her own account,

that the veteran would not be suffering

if the veteran were free of that incapacity."

Also relevant is section 24A, which reads, so far as relevant:

"24A. Where the Commonwealth is or

becomes liable to pay a pension to a veteran

at the rate applicable under section 23 or

24, that rate continues, while a pension

continues to be payable to the veteran, to

apply to the veteran unless:

...

(c) in the case of a veteran to whom section

24 applies - the veteran is undertaking

or is capable of undertaking

remunerative work for periods

aggregating more than 8 hours per week."

6. The amending sections also substituted "70%" for "100%" in sub-paragraph 24(1)(a)(i) with effect from 22 December 1988. As Mr Ray's degree of incapacity was determined to be 100 per cent with effect from 24 February 1986, that amendment is not relevant for present purposes. As at that date, Mr Ray clearly satisfied the requirements of paragraph 24(1)(a) and I so find.

7. The informal application for an increase in pension, from which the present proceedings derive, was lodged with the Department of Veterans' Affairs on 24 February 1986. That being so, I am empowered by section 19 of the Act as it now stands to consider whether Mr Ray met the requirements of section 24 as at any time from that date up until the date of his death. Mr Smith, in my view rightly, made no submission that on the evidence Mr Ray did not on 24 February 1986 satisfy the requirements of paragraph 24(1)(b), and I find accordingly. That being so, the only question before me is whether at any time from 24 February 1986 Mr Ray met the requirements of paragraph 24(1)(c) so as to have been entitled to pension at the special rate.

8. Mr Ray's disabilities accepted as being war-caused, with the dates of their acceptance, were chronic sinusitis, 6 March 1946; Dupuytren's contracture (bilateral), 6 March 1946; bronchitis with asthma, 7 August 1954; anxiety neurosis with depression, 21 July 1987; ischaemic heart disease, 21 July 1987; and essential hypertension, 21 July 1987. The disability of psychopathic personality was rejected on 11 February 1959 as not being war-caused. However, Mr Smith indicated that the view of the respondent was that that condition was subsequently subsumed in the accepted disability of anxiety neurosis with depression. On discharge in 1946 Mr Ray's disability pension was assessed at 10 per cent of the general rate. It was increased to 20 per cent in 1954, 25 per cent in 1957, 40 per cent in 1960 and 100 per cent in 1986, by virtue of the decision under review.

9. Having considered the evidence, I find the facts set out in this and the following seven paragraphs. Mr Ray, who was born in 1913, left school at the age of 12 and subsequently qualified as a master plasterer. Before the war he worked in his trade and in other jobs. He enlisted in the Royal Australian Air Force in 1943 and saw service in Borneo as a tail gunner. He was grounded because of sinus trouble and re-enlisted as a commando. He was discharged in 1946, and was unable to return to his trade because of his accepted disabilities.

10. In 1947 he undertook adult matriculation and met his future wife, whom he married in 1953. He obtained the degree of Bachelor of Arts at The University of Melbourne in 1951, and commenced a Diploma of Education, which he completed in 1957. Commencing in 1951 he taught in various schools with the Victorian Education Department. He had problems with asthma and bronchitis, especially in winter. He had a series of breakdowns, and in 1959 was superannuated out of the Department.

11. To supplement his superannuation, he began buying old violins and restoring them for resale, and he and his wife gradually established a business, at first at home and later from a shop, of buying goods at auction and reselling them. They also had a drycleaning agency.

12. In 1969 Mr Ray returned to the Education Department. Mrs Ray, who was also a teacher, found it was not practicable to operate the business on her own, and she also returned to teaching later in the same year. Mr Ray finally retired from teaching in 1974. He tried two jobs, both of which caused problems with his chest conditions, and had to be abandoned. From 1975 he concentrated on his and his family's investments.

13. Mr Ray had been interested in the share market for many years, but had had to sell his shares to pay for medical treatment. After his first retirement he again began putting any spare money into shares, and when he returned to teaching in 1969 had a portfolio worth about $8000. Mrs Ray inherited some $30,000 worth of shares from her father in 1972 and he administered those and other holdings for her. He also managed her mother's shares, which were worth somewhat more than hers, in respect of which he was paid a commission on the increased value of the portfolio each quarter. By the middle of 1986, Mr Ray's shares were worth about $180,000, and Mrs Ray's about $200,000. He administered his own as a trading portfolio, and his wife's as an investment portfolio. In his taxation returns for the taxation years 1984 to 1989, which were before the Tribunal, he gave his occupation as "share trader".

14. Each morning Mrs Ray would buy the Age newspaper and drop it back to Mr Ray before going to school. When she left home at 8 o'clock he would already have started checking the state of their investments. Later in the day he would walk to the newsagent and buy the Australian Financial Review and appropriate weekly journals, and would study them carefully. He had a system for recording prices and volume of sales. He principally used one broker, and would speak with him almost daily, although he made some use of other brokers. He received a lot of relevant mail and subscribed to various relevant journals. He would spend a minimum of four hours daily on the care of their investments.

15. In 1985 Mr Ray's accepted disabilities were seriously affecting his ability to manage the shares. His record-keeping was not good, and Mrs Ray was concerned that, for this reason, he might inadvertently purchase something which he could not afford. She took long service leave in September of that year to get the records up to date, and finally resigned at the end of the year. She could keep the records, but could not make the decisions. The introduction of capital gains tax with effect from 20 September 1985 came at a time when Mr Ray was not well enough to cope with the necessary record-keeping, and this created problems.

16. His health steadily deteriorated, as a result of his accepted disabilities, from late 1985 into 1986, and continued to deteriorate until his death in June 1989. He was sleeping badly, and was unable to sit for long periods. He was shopping around for someone to take over the management of the shares. He tried out different stockbrokers, none of whom he found entirely satisfactory. The trading continued, but more of the decisions were being made by other people, although he would always be asked to confirm them. He tried to cut down the work by reducing the number of companies in which shares were held. He never felt able to hand over the whole portfolio to one person who he would trust to make the decisions for him. In about 1986 or 1987 he ceased trading through his previous stockbroker and turned to the larger firms. When the stock market crash came in October 1987, he felt that if he had been handling the shares himself he might have sold more than he did before the crash. Finally, he said that he was just getting things sorted out for his probate.

17. It is not in issue, and I find, that Mr Ray was conducting a business as a share trader, and that the conduct of that business constituted the undertaking of "remunerative work", in terms of the definition in sub-section 5(1) of the Act. Mr Smith conceded, and I find, that his age did not affect his ability to conduct that business. The management of investments is an activity commonly performed by people past the standard age of retirement from the workforce. It is not in issue that any incapacity from which he suffered, and which may have affected his ability to conduct the business, was the result of his war-caused disabilities alone, and I so find.

18. The two remaining matters which I have to consider are first, whether Mr Ray in fact was "prevented from continuing to undertake" that remunerative work, given that if he was so prevented, it was by reason of that incapacity: and second, whether, if so prevented, he was "by reason thereof, suffering a loss ... of earnings" that he would not have been suffering had he been free of the incapacity.

19. Mr Smith submitted that, on the evidence, Mr Ray had in fact continued to conduct his business as a share trader, and had not been prevented from doing so. Although he was looking to find a broker to whom he would entrust his whole portfolio, he had not in fact done so.

20. The question is clearly one of degree, and it is not easy to reach a decision on the evidence before me. However, on the basis of Mrs Ray's evidence, and the statement by the stockbroker to which I have referred, I am inclined to the view that, on balance, by 1 July 1986, an admittedly arbitrarily selected date, Mr Ray's control of his business had lessened to the extent that he could be said to have been "prevented from continuing to undertake" it, and I so find.

21. On that basis, the remaining question is whether, in any of the financial years ending on 30 June 1987, 1988 and 1989, Mr Ray was, by reason of being so prevented, suffering a loss of earnings which he would not have been suffering if he had been free of the incapacity.

22. In the material before the Tribunal were copies of Mr Ray's taxation returns for the financial years 1984 to 1989. As he died on 17 June 1989, the 1989 return covers almost a full 12 months and is accordingly comparable with the returns from previous years. Tables I and II below have been prepared from information contained in those returns. The only sources of income appearing from the returns are the share trading business and superannuation. Accordingly, the figure in column F does not include Mr Ray's non-taxable disability pension. The only change in the rate of that pension in the period with which I am concerned was the increase from 40 per cent of the general rate to 100 per cent with effect from 24 February 1986 by virtue of the decision under review. It should also be noted that the figures in column F for 1988 and 1989 do not accord with the figures appearing in the returns for those years as total assessable income, for reasons to do with the inclusion of imputation credits and the treatment of losses in the returns.

23. Both counsel relied on dicta of Deputy President McMahon in Re Cavell and Repatriation Commission (1986) 10 ALN N233. I would, with respect, adopt the passage (at page N234) in which Mr McMahon said, referring to the word "loss" in paragraph 24(1)(c):

"The Act does not talk about a substantial or

significant loss. It talks about 'a loss'.

As with the word 'alone', it seems to me that

it should be given its plain grammatical

meaning. A loss must mean a diminution of

what the veteran might reasonably be expected

to earn had he not been incapacitated. I

see no reason to put an unnecessary gloss

upon the Act by requiring the veteran to

quantify that loss beyond a certain minimum

amount. If it is possible to assess what he

would have been earning, then any actual

income below that possible level represents a

diminution and the difference represents a

loss. Accordingly, on the Commission's own

figures, a loss has been demonstrated."

24. Mr Smith referred to a further passage, appearing at page N235, in the following terms:

"(T)he word 'earnings' must, in my view, mean

the totality of the periodic payments which

the applicant receives, which under normal

accounting concepts, would be regarded as

income."

He submitted that the effect of that passage is that, if the veteran's total income from all sources for the relevant period is not reduced, the veteran cannot be said to be "suffering a loss of earnings" despite a reduction in earnings from a particular source constituted by remunerative work which he is prevented from continuing to undertake by reason of accepted disabilities alone. In support of that submission he relied on Re Lambert and Repatriation Commission (Decision No 5353: 31 August 1989) but conceded that that decision was not directly in point.

25. If the passage cited in the preceding paragraph is intended to bear the meaning submitted by Mr Smith, then, with respect, I must express my disagreement with it. Assume a veteran with income from sources X, Y and Z. If his earnings from source X cease, in circumstances which otherwise attract the operation of paragraph 24(1)(c), he is suffering an appropriate "loss of earnings". If his total income from sources Y and Z increases over the relevant period, it must be assumed that, by definition, his total income would have increased still more had there been a contribution from source X. Whether the whole or the part is considered, there is still a "loss of earnings". Thus I am concerned simply with Mr Ray's earnings from share trading, and not with his total income.

26. At the hearing, Mr Spence relied on the significant changes in the results of Mr Ray's purchases and sales, appearing in column C of Table I, as indicating a loss of earnings after the year ending 30 June 1986. However, I am satisfied that it is necessary to take into account the totality of his earnings from shares (and analogous investments), and not merely the profit or loss on sales. The knowledge that a dividend is to be, or recently has been, declared, is a significant factor in the making of a decision to buy or sell shares. Not only is the dividend income in the hands of the owner of the shares on the date of its declaration: the declaration of the dividend affects the value of the shares immediately after it is paid. The receipt of dividends is not a mere accidental incident in the business of a share trader who, like Mr Ray, manages his portfolio as a trading, rather than an investment portfolio. Further, the fact that some of Mr Ray's investments were of a kind which produced income by way of interest rather than dividends, and hence led to their inclusion in a different section of his taxation returns, is not a reason to disregard that income in the present context (as was done at the hearing). Accordingly, I find that Mr Ray's "earnings" in the years 1984 to 1989 from the "remunerative work" constituted by his share trading business are the amounts shown in column D of Table II.

27. I now turn to determine whether Mr Ray suffered, in any of the years ended 30 June 1987, 1988 and 1989, that is after the date on which I have found that he was prevented from continuing his share trading business, a loss of earnings from that business that he would not have been suffering had he been free of his incapacity. In order to do so, it is necessary to form a view as to what his earnings from that business would have been in those years had he not been so prevented. For this purpose, it is not sufficient merely to compare the amounts appearing for those years in column D with the amounts for the previous years.

28. A number of matters are relevant to the attempt to ascertain what would have been Mr Ray's earnings after 30 June 1986 had he not been suffering his incapacity, and to compare those hypothetical earnings with his actual earnings in those years and in previous years. The effect of inflation must be borne in mind in comparing figures from year to year. The introduction of dividend imputation with effect from 1 July 1987 has in many cases had the effect that corporations have paid out higher amounts than before by way of dividends. For that reason, Mr Ray may well have received more by way of dividends after that date than he would have done had dividend imputation not been introduced. It is not possible to ignore the stock market crash of October 1987, from which few share traders would have emerged unscathed. The business of share trading is, in any event, an uncertain one, and the income of a share trader must inevitably fluctuate.

29. When those matters are severally taken into account, and allowance made for each of them, all of the amounts shown in column D should be appropriately reduced year by year to allow for inflation; the amounts for 1988 and 1989 possibly reduced, to an unknown extent, to allow for changed dividend policies in contrast with previous years; and the amount for 1988 increased to negate the effect of the probability of a reduction in earnings as a result of the October crash, and render 1988 comparable with other years. None of these adjustments (save for inflation) is quantifiable, and save for inflation, they are speculative. Nevertheless, I consider that, looking at the amounts in column D in the light of those considerations, it can be seen that the amounts for each of 1987 and 1989 should be, to some unknown extent, reduced and that for 1988 probably, on balance, increased, in order to reach some sort of estimate, which is comparable with his earnings in previous years, of what Mr Ray's earnings would have been had he not been prevented by his incapacity from continuing to undertake his business.

30. If adjustments of that kind are notionally made, they have an intensifying effect on the changes which appear in column D. The apparent reduction in income in each of 1987 and 1989 becomes greater, as does the apparent increase in 1988. No more specific conclusion than this can be drawn from the material before me. It then becomes appropriate to compare the amounts shown in column D, that is, Mr Ray's income from his share trading business, for 1987 to 1989, with the amounts shown in that column for 1984 to 1986.

31. On that basis, I am satisfied that in the year ending 30 June 1987, Mr Ray suffered a loss of earnings which he would not have suffered had he been free of his incapacity. An arbitrary date for the commencement of that circumstance is 31 December 1986, the mid-point of the year. Accordingly, I find that on that date Mr Ray satisfied all the requirements of paragraph 24(1)(c), so as to be entitled to payment at the special rate.

32. Looking at the year ended 30 June 1988, I am unable to find that in that year he suffered such a loss of earnings. However, the effect of section 24A of the Act is that, in all the circumstances of this case, the Commonwealth having become liable on 31 December 1986 to pay a pension to Mr Ray at the special rate, that rate continued to be payable to him in subsequent years.

33. The decision under review will be set aside and an appropriate decision substituted therefor.
