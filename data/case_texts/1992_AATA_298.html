Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 298; (1992) 92 ATC 350 (1992) 24 ATR 1104 (5 October 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT92/51-54

AAT No. 8292

Number of pages - 12

Income Tax

(1992) 92 ATC 350

(1992) 24 ATR 1104

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President), Mrs R.A. Balmford(1) (Senior Member) and Mr B.H. Pascoe(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - payments for insurance bonds by employer company - liability for future lump sum payment to employee - indemnity agreement with associated company - whether payments deductible - whether a scheme to which Part IVA applies

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.51, Part IVA

Ransburg Australia Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4104; 80 ATC 4114

Telecasters North Queensland Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4494

Case W62 89 ATC 570

HEARING

MELBOURNE

5:10:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decisions under review.

DECISION

These are applications for review of four decisions to disallow objections against amended assessments of income tax in respect of income of the years ended 30 June 1987, 1988 and 1990 and an assessment of income tax in respect of income of the year ended 30 June 1989.

2. This issue in this case is whether an amount of $200,000 expended by the taxpayer company in each of the years in question and described in the returns lodged as 'indemnity insurance' is an allowable deduction.

3. During each of the relevant years the taxpayer company carried on the business of the production, processing and sale of abalone. The business was originally established in 1970 and has been very profitable since. H was the Managing Director of the taxpayer company and was the beneficial owner of the shares in the company. His wife ('C') was a director and an employee of the company until their divorce in March 1990. By agreements dated 29 May 1987, H and C entered into contracts of employment with the taxpayer company. Under these agreements H was to be employed at a monthly salary of $2500 or as the parties should mutually agree, and C at a monthly salary of $1666.00 or as the parties should mutually agree.

4. Under each agreement the taxpayer company also agreed to pay a further lump sum amount at a future specified date or on any earlier retirement of the employee, such amount to be payable to such person or persons as the employee nominated in writing. The amounts payable were, in the case of H, $2,500,000 or the amount standing to the credit of the policy of the insurance effected in accordance with the agreement whichever was the less and the amount to be payable on 1 June 2001 or earlier retirement. Under the agreement with C, the amount payable was $3,000,000 or the amount standing to the credit of the policy of the insurance effected in accordance with the agreement, whichever was the less, and payable on 1 June 2002 or such earlier date as the employee ceased employment. In both cases the policy of insurance was to be in the form of an insurance bond issued by APA Life Insurance Limited with the total sum payable on the specified dates. The agreement required the taxpayer company to pay on or before June 1987, the sum of $100,000 in respect of each bond, and thereafter $100,000 per annum for additional units in each bond in each year ending 30 June so long as the employee remained in the employment of the employer.

5. By deed of indemnity dated 3 June 1987, another company, S Pty Ltd, undertook to pay to the persons nominated under the employment agreements the lump sum amounts. In consideration the taxpayer company assigned its interest in the insurance bonds and undertook to make the additional payments to the insurance company in each of the subsequent years in accordance with the employment agreements. Although the indemnity agreements recited that the taxpayer company had taken out a policy of insurance by way of an insurance bond, the evidence showed that the cheques in payment of the first $100,000 in each case were dated 3 June 1987 and that the bonds were issued on 11 June 1987. The actual assignment of bonds was dated 15 June 1987 and registered by the insurance company on 22 June 1987. The insurance policies were originally issued in the name of the taxpayer company.

6. By letter of 26 June 1987, S Pty Ltd requested redemption of units in each of the bonds to the value of $75,000 and a cheque for $150,000 was forwarded to that company by the insurance company on 4 August 1987. In the letter enclosing the cheque, the insurance company referred to the letter of request for redemption as having been received on 27 July 1987. The proceeds of this partial redemption were paid to H by way of loan from S Pty Ltd and immediately on-lent by H to the taxpayer company. A further partial redemption of bonds amounting to $390,000 was received by S Pty Ltd on 8 May 1990 and again lent to H who on-lent the amount to the taxpayer company. H gave evidence that the first redemption came about when the taxpayer company needed funds for the purchase of an equity in an abalone licence and the second was to provide funds to meet a payment of $250,000 by the taxpayer company on behalf of H as a settlement resulting from his divorce from C.

7. The basic arguments brought on behalf of the respondent Commissioner of Taxation were that the amounts of $200,000 expended in each year by the taxpayer company were neither incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, nor necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for such a purpose; alternatively, that the expenditure was capital or of a capital nature and not deductible under section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ('the Act'). Alternatively, the Commissioner argued that the arrangement attracted Part IVA of the Act. The taxpayer company, on the other hand, submitted that the obligations for the taxpayer company to make the payments arose from the terms of the deed of indemnity and were, accordingly, outgoings incurred by the taxpayer company in gaining and producing assessable income and necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for such a purpose. A vigorous argument was put against the possible application of Part IVA of the Act.

8. Oral evidence and a signed statement were provided at the hearing by H. The only other witness was Mr Coppard, who had provided the advice and documentation and arranged the purchase of the insurance bonds.

9. H gave evidence that, since 1974, the taxpayer company had been involved in acquiring equity in abalone diving licences and making loans and providing guarantees to divers for the purpose of enabling them to acquire equity in licences. These investments were better to secure the purchase of abalone. To finance these investments, the company, by August 1987, had obtained approximately $1,500,000 of loans from the ANZ Bank and $3,500,000 from overseas sources. In his written statements, H stated that the ANZ Bank manager had made it clear that unless insurance was effected on H's life to the extent of $3,000,000, the bank would not provide further loans and could be expected to seek a reduction in existing loans. However, in his oral evidence H maintained that the insurance was a suggestion only and not a demand. The bank manager recommended a meeting with a Mr England, a representative with ANZ Life Company. In turn, Mr England advised H to see Mr Coppard, a business consultant and a life insurance representative, who would be able to make some constructive proposals relating to the debt and equity structure of the company.

10. H stated that, prior to this, he had considered the possibility of outside parties subscribing for equity in his company, but was reluctant to take any step which would deprive him of ownership and control. He stated that Mr Coppard had recommended that H and his wife should enter into an employment agreement to secure their right to employment by the company before contemplating any sale in whole or in part of the equity of the company. Mr Coppard also proposed that such employment agreement should provide for the payment of a lump sum on the termination of employment. He suggested that, to give flexibility to the proposal, the employment agreement should provide that this lump sum be payable to a person nominated by the employee. In each case he proposed that the lump sum payable should be secured by an insurance bond. A further element proposed by Mr Coppard was the formation of a separate entity to take up and hold the insurance bonds, with the separate entity undertaking to indemnify the taxpayer company against its liability under the employment agreement. This was said to be for protection in the event of losing control of the taxpayer company either by sale of a substantial proportion of the equity or if the business was not as successful as anticipated.

11. Although not produced in evidence, it was stated that a separate 'key man' insurance policy had been taken out and the proposal for investment in the insurance bonds was unrelated to the maintenance of the insurance policy on the life of H.

12. Mr Coppard stated in his evidence that he had been in the insurance industry for over 40 years in the United Kingdom, South Africa and, since 1964, Australia. He had been State Manager for two insurance companies, the latter being the ANZ Life Company. Mr Coppard confirmed his involvement in arranging the employment agreement, deed of indemnity and the purchase of the insurance bond. He maintained that the purpose of the arrangements was to provide continuity of employment for H and C and to provide for security of the family by way of a lump sum. He argued the deed of indemnity was essential to provide the security by holding the investment in a separate entity to protect against any possible insolvency of the employer company.

13. Both witnesses maintained that there had been no discussion, prior to making these arrangements, in relation to the taxation consequences of the purchase of the insurance bonds and the indemnity agreement, nor was there any discussion about the ability to borrow against or obtain redemption of part of the insurance bond money. Notwithstanding the sworn evidence of both witnesses, we cannot accept that an experienced life insurance representative and a successful business man would not have discussed and considered the taxation implications of the proposed arrangements.

14. From cross-examination of both witnesses, we are satisfied that both the employment agreements and the deeds of indemnity were, in fact, executed at the same time, notwithstanding the five days difference between the two dates shown on the agreements and the assertion of the witnesses that the agreements were executed on the dates stated in the agreements.

15. Although both Mr Nettle of counsel for the Commissioner and Mr Komesaroff for the taxpayer company spent considerable time in their addresses directed towards the possible application of Part IVA of the Act, it is clearly appropriate to first consider whether or not the amounts in dispute were deductible pursuant to section 51 of the Act. Only if the Tribunal considers that the amounts are deductible does the question of Part IVA arise.

16. Both parties sought comfort from the decision Ransburg Australia Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4114. In that case, the taxpayer company claimed deductions for payments made to a related company pursuant to what was referred to as a 'contract of indemnity', designed substantially to indemnify the company against future liability for employees' holiday pay and long service leave. At first instance, Waddell J (79 ATC 4104) held that the deductions were not allowable under section 51 of the Act because the applicant had failed to establish the necessary nexus between the payment and the income earning activities. On appeal the matter was dismissed, albeit on different grounds.

17. Mr Nettle submitted that the comments of Deane J at page 4116 applied to this matter where he said:

'The outgoings in the present case which were made to

provide future funds which were intended to, but which need not

necessarily, be applied in relation to such future payments to

employees cannot, however, any more be properly so characterised than

could a payment by a taxpayer to the credit of a savings bank account

properly be characterised, for the purposes of Section 51(1) of the

Act, by reference to the object or objects to which the taxpayer

proposed, at some future time, to apply the proceeds of the account.'

On the other hand, Mr Komesaroff sought to distinguish this case from

the facts in Ransburg (supra) and to rely on an earlier Tribunal

decision reported as Case W62, 89 ATC 570.

18. In considering the deductibility under section 51, it is appropriate first to consider the position of the taxpayer company under the initial employment contracts. Although the contracts provided for specific sums ($2,500,000 for H and $3,000,000 for C) to be paid at a specific future time, the effects of clauses 4 and 5 of the contracts were to limit the lump sum to the amount payable under the insurance bonds. In the contract entered into by H, these clauses read as follows:

'4. The payment to be made as aforesaid shall be the sum of

$2,500,000 or the amount standing to the credit of the policy of

insurance effected in accordance with clause 5 hereof (whichever is

the lesser of the two amounts) on the 1st day of June 2001 or on such

earlier date as the employee ceases to be employed by the employer (as

the case may be).

5. In order to better secure the aforesaid payment by the

employer as provided by clauses 4 and 5 hereof the employer shall

forthwith effect an insurance policy in the form of an insurance bond

under which the sum of $2,500,000 shall become payable on 1 June 2001,

issued by A.P.A. Life Insurance Limited to which the employer shall

pay on or before 30 June 1987 the sum of $100,000 to such bond and

thereafter $100,000 per annum for additional units in such bond in

each year ending on 30 June, so long as the employee remains in the

employment of the employer.'

The wording of the contract entered into by C was identical other than the amount - $3,000,000 - and the due date, 1 June 2002.

19. It should be noted that, although Clause 4 of the agreement referred to a policy of insurance, the insurance bonds taken out had no element of indemnity or death cover. They solely provided for the repayment of the amounts invested plus accumulated income at the expiration of 10 years or the earlier death of the nominated person. It is also noted that the bonds matured in 1997 not 2001 or 2002 as required by Clause 5. Mr Coppard, in his evidence, sought to explain this difference and stated:

'The bonds normally have what is known as a 10 year life.

Their issue is for 10 years unless the person upon whose life they

stand unfortunately dies earlier. However, they are also known in the

industry as open ended contracts in as much as at the end of the 10

year term the companies are quite prepared to leave the money there

and let it accrue for a further period of time.'

20. Although no provision was made for the possibility of any subsequent redemption, very soon after the first contribution of $100,000 for each bond, the sum of $75,000 was redeemed, leaving bonds with a then value of $22,294.38 each. A further redemption of $390,000 was made in May 1990 and another in a period subsequent to the last year with which we are concerned. Equally, no provision was made for dealing with any surplus on the bonds in excess of the specific maximum sums due under the contracts. Evidence was given by Mr Coppard that the calculations were based on an assumed rate of return on the bonds of eight per cent per annum.

21. The result of this is that the company, at best, committed itself to a payment at some future date to a nominee of the employee of an amount equal to a product of an annual investment, less any redemption, to a maximum of specified sums.

22. Both sides addressed themselves as to whether or not the ultimate lump sum would be deductible under section 51 when paid. There must be considerable doubts on that future deductibility in the light of decisions such as Telecasters North Queensland Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4494, and the evidence provided by H and Coppard that the primary motivation in including a lump sum in the employment contracts, was for the protection of those employees in the case of a take-over and to provide future security for these two employees and their family. However, it is not necessary to determine that issue at this point, as the expenses in the relevant years in this case are for the investments in insurance bonds made in those years, the product of which may be paid in a future year. In our view such expenditure would clearly be of a capital nature and not deductible under section 51.

23. What, then, is the effect of the indemnity agreement? In essence, this agreement provides for S Pty Ltd to hold the insurance bonds as assignee and to pay the proceeds to a nominee of the employee at a future date and for the taxpayer company to continue paying to APA Life Insurance Limited the $100,000 instalments on each contract in each year. Under Clause 4(b) of the agreement, S Pty Ltd had the right, if payment was not made by the taxpayer company within one month of a demand in writing, to determine the agreement, surrender the insurance bonds and pay the proceeds to any relative or dependant of the employees.

24. The employees were not a party to this agreement and the taxpayer company remained liable to the employees under the employment agreements including, it would appear, the requirement to secure the lump sum payment obligation by paying $100,000 per annum as an investment in an insurance bond.

25. From the evidence, it would appear that S Pty Ltd had no other role in life than, as a trustee of a family trust, to accept ownership of the insurance bonds and to enter into the indemnity agreement. Although described as an 'indemnity agreement', its effect was solely to provide for S Pty Ltd to pay out the product of insurance bonds, units which were to be subscribed for by the taxpayer company. S Pty Ltd had no other assets or income other than the loan account asset to H when it redeemed part of the insurance bonds and lent the proceeds to H. As such, we are of the view that S Pty Ltd was nothing more than a trustee or agent for the taxpayer company and its interposition in the arrangement did nothing to remove a categorisation of the relevant annual expenditure as being one of capital. In any event we are of the view that the decision in the case of Ransburg (supra) must apply to the expenditure in each of the relevant four years and that the decision in Case W62 (supra) is readily distinguishable on its facts.

26. In the event that the Tribunal is incorrect in this view and in deference to Mr Nettle and Mr Komesaroff who occupied much of the time of the hearing and in their addresses on the alternative approach by the Commissioner that Part IVA applies in any event, it is appropriate to consider this approach.

27. In the view of the Tribunal, Part IVA would apply to render the deductions claimed liable to disallowance if, contrary to our view, they are otherwise allowable under section 51. We come to this view for the following reasons:

1. There was a scheme as required by Section 177D which comprised the

entering into employment agreements, formation of S Pty Ltd , the

entering into indemnity agreements, investment in insurance bonds, the

assignment of those bonds and the part redemption of the bonds.

2. Notwithstanding the differing dates shown on the two agreements, it

is clear that all aspects of the scheme were prepared and executed at

the same time as an integrated scheme.

3. The documentation appeared somewhat inadequate in setting out the

full rights and obligations which would otherwise be expected in an

arm's length employment agreement involving the sums contemplated here

and a comprehensive 'indemnity' agreement.

4. The evidence of both H and Mr Coppard of the lack of any discussion

of tax consequences or an ability to redeem part or all of the

insurance bonds cannot be accepted when it is considered that H was a

successful business man and Coppard a very experienced insurance

executive discussing the investment of a significant sum of money. We

are satisfied that both aspects of the scheme were discussed and were

significant factors in the decision to proceed with the proposal put

to H by Coppard.

5. Notwithstanding the evidence of H that the redemptions were made as

a result of subsequent discussions with Coppard and for specific

purposes of the taxpayer company, we are satisfied that the elements

of the scheme which motivated the taxpayer company to enter into it

were the expected tax deductibility of the amounts invested in

insurance bonds and the recovery of a major part of the amount

expended as non-assessable redemptions. The timing of the agreements,

the first subscription for the insurance bonds and the first request

for redemption lead to a conclusion that each were components of a

predetermined arrangement.

6. Contrary to the expressed purposes of establishing a separate entity

so as to provide security for the ultimate lump sum payments, the

regular redemptions left S Pty Ltd with very little in the way of

secure assets to provide any significant lump sum in the future.

7. Notwithstanding the retirement of C as a director and employee of

the taxpayer company, the full $200,000 continued to be invested

annually, although H was the only employee remaining with an

employment agreement requiring $100,000 to be paid. Additionally, no

payment pursuant to the indemnity agreement was made to C by S Pty Ltd

on her retirement from employment and the amount paid to her by the

taxpayer company was $250,000, the amount which H was ordered to pay

by the Family Court by way of the settlement following the dissolution

of the marriage. This amount had no relationship to the amount

standing to the credit of the insurance bonds at that date as required

under the employment agreement and consequently, the indemnity

agreement.

8. S Pty Ltd was a company in which H and C were the sole shareholders

and directors until 25 July 1989 when C was replaced as a Director and

secretary by Mr C.E. Smith of the accounting firm which acted for the

taxpayer company. Clearly, there was no arm's length relationship

between the two companies and no other apparent purposes for its

establishment other than to provide a second entity for the purposes

of the scheme.

9. The manner in which the scheme was established and carried out is

not explicable on the basis of normal commercial transactions but we

find was established for the dominant purpose of providing a tax

benefit for the taxpayer company.

28. We are of the view, therefore, that, to the extent that any deduction was allowable for the amounts contributed to insurance bonds by the taxpayer company, the taxpayer company has obtained a tax benefit pursuant to section 177C of the Act which is liable to cancellation under section 177F. 29. For the above reasons, the Tribunal affirms the decisions under review.
