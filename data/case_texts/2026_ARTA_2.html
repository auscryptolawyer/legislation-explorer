Statement of Reasons

  1. This statement sets out the Tribunal’s reasons for refusing the Applicants’ application for a stay of the operation of related decisions of the Respondent terminating their registration as registered tax agents and imposing a non-registration period of 4 years (collectively, the review decisions).
  2. The Applicants (Mr Mulcahy and the Company, Mulcahy & Co Accounting Services Pty Ltd) are part of an accountancy, financial planning and tax practice primarily based in Ballarat, Victoria.[1] They have each brought applications in the Tribunal seeking to overturn the review decisions.
  3. The Respondent investigated the Applicants following findings by the Supreme Court of Victoria[2] that they had:
    • - breached their fiduciary duties to their former clients (Mr Porter and Conheady);
    • - wrongfully used confidential information; and
    • - that Mr Mulcahy engaged in dishonest and fraudulent conduct (the fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground).
  4. While the Respondent’s investigation was underway, the Applicants unsuccessfully sought leave to appeal to the Victorian Court of Appeal[3] arguing Mr Mulcahy was not afforded procedural fairness on the fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground. They have recently filed an application for special leave to appeal to the High Court of Australia (the SLA) contesting that same ground.[4]
  5. The operation of the review decisions was temporarily stayed by consent of the parties pending the hearing of the stay application.[5] The Applicants estimate the SLA will be determined by around March 2026 and have proposed stay orders with stringent conditions until 28 days after the determination of the SLA. The Respondent opposes the stay application (including the proposed conditions).
  6. The stay application was heard on 17 December 2025. The Applicants relied on two affidavits of Mr Mulcahy and one affidavit of Vincent Licciardi (the Applicants’ solicitor).[6] Neither of the witnesses were cross-examined at the stay hearing.
  7. The Respondent did not file evidence but the s 23 Tribunal Act documents were exhibited for the purposes of the stay hearing.

BACKGROUND

The commercial dispute with Mr Porter and Mr Conheady and the court proceedings

  1. Mr Mulcahy is a qualified accountant and financial planner and is a shareholder and director[7] of the Company which was incorporated in 2003. Mr Mulcahy has been a registered tax agent since 1 March 2010, while the Company has been registered since 1 October 2005.[8]
  2. The full details of the commercial dispute between the Applicants and their former clients are a matter of public record[9] but can be summarised as follows.[10] In 2017, Mr Porter and Mr Conheady sought advice from Mr Mulcahy about a potential commercial opportunity. They allege the Applicants then breached their fiduciary obligations, as well as obligations of confidentiality owed by retainer to each of them to take advantage of the commercial opportunity for themselves. Mr Porter and Mr Conheady subsequently commenced legal proceedings against the Applicants and sought orders for damages and costs (the primary proceeding).
  3. The primary judge (Delany J) delivered multiple judgments (the primary judgments)[11] in determination of Mr Porter and Mr Conheady’s claims.[12] He relevantly found that:
    • - the Applicants had breached 2 client retainers with adverse impact on Mr Conheady;
    • - the Applicants had breached their fiduciary duties to Mr Conheady;
    • - there were also breaches of confidentiality obligations to both Mr Porter and Mr Conheady;
    • - the Applicants were liable for damages to both Mr Porter and Mr Conheady;
    • - Mr Mulcahy had engaged in dishonest and fraudulent design and that his conduct was dishonest (the dishonest and fraudulent conduct ground);[13] and
    • - the Applicants were liable for damages ($21,527,584) and interest ($4,721,807.02).[14] Awards were made in respect of both Mr Conheady and Mr Porter.
  4. The Applicants then sought leave to appeal the Victorian Court of Appeal on the question of liability to Mr Porter and the dishonest and fraudulent conduct ground.
  5. On 29 October 2025, the Court of Appeal delivered judgment (the appeal judgment).[15] It reversed the liability finding in respect of Mr Porter (the Porter liability ground) but found the Applicants’ fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground of appeal to be incompetent. The Court of Appeal then proceeded to observe that even if the fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground had been competent, the primary judge had not failed to afford procedural fairness to Mr Mulcahy in making the finding.
  6. The SLA challenges the validity of the Court of Appeal’s findings on the fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground. The Applicants contend Mr Mulcahy has been denied procedural fairness given the question of whether he engaged in fraudulent and dishonest conduct was not pleaded or addressed in submissions before the primary judge.
  7. The Applicants have otherwise abided by the primary and appeal judgments, a fact which has been observed by both Mr Porter and Mr Conheady in their Response to the SLA[16] and the Respondent, in its submissions opposing the stay application.

The Respondent’s investigation and decision

  1. The Respondent’s investigation (the investigation) commenced on 19 July 2024 upon the Respondent becoming aware of the primary judgments.[17] The Applicants unsuccessfully sought to have the Respondent’s investigation ‘paused’ pending hearing and determination of the appeal.[18] Mr Mulcahy also unsuccessfully sought to terminate his registration by surrender ahead of completion of the Respondent’s investigation.[19]
  2. The Respondent issued show cause notices to the Applicants on 9 April 2025.[20]
  3. On 18 July 2025 (prior to the hearing and determination of the appeal), the Respondent determined:[21]
    • - it was satisfied the Applicants had each failed to comply with ss 30-10(1), (4), (5) and (6) of the Code of Professional Conduct in the TASA;[22] and consequent upon these findings
    • - it was satisfied the Applicants had each ceased to meet the registration requirement that they be a fit and proper person;[23]
    • - to terminate the Applicants’ registrations;[24] and further
    • - the Applicants should be prohibited from applying for registration for 4 years from the date on which their terminations took effect.[25]
  4. The Respondent’s reasons record its perception of the seriousness attaching to Mr Mulcahy’s conduct:[26]

His conduct undermines time and resources to be utilised by the ATO in reviewing and auditing of tax practitioners. The seriousness of the conduct is such that the TPB, the Commissioner, clients and the public could not have confidence that Mr. James Mulcahy would perform his functions as a registered tax agent with integrity. The Committee also noted the need for the community to have confidence that they are able to obtain suitable advice from their tax agents without concern for how that information will be used by their tax agent.

  1. The review decisions were to take effect from 15 August 2025.[27] The Applicants applied to the Tribunal for review of the review decisions on 11 August 2025[28] and also sought a stay of the operation of the review decisions.[29] On 14 August 2025, the parties agreed to temporary stay orders pending determination of the stay application. Those orders were amended by consent on 17 September 2025 and 7 November 2025.
  2. The SLA was filed in the High Court of Australia on 24 November 2025.[30] On 15 December 2025, the Respondents to the SLA filed their Response, opposing the SLA.[31]

THE LEGISLATION

  1. An applicant seeking review by this Tribunal does not have an automatic right to a stay of the review decision. Section 32 Tribunal Act relevantly provides:

32 Reviewable decision continues to operate unless Tribunal orders otherwise

General rule

(1) The making of an application to the Tribunal for review of a reviewable decision does not affect the operation of the decision or prevent the taking of action to implement the decision.

Exception—Tribunal may stay operation or implementation

(2) However, on application by a party to a proceeding for review of a reviewable decision, the Tribunal may make an order staying or otherwise affecting the operation or implementation of the decision if the Tribunal considers that it is desirable to do so for the purpose of ensuring the effectiveness of the review.

(3) The order is subject to any conditions specified in the order.

(4) The order has effect until the decision of the Tribunal on the application for review comes into operation.

  1. In Zartaloudis v Tax Practitioners Board,[32] I made a number of observations about s 32 Tribunal Act which I have summarised below:
(a) the test in s 32 essentially preserves, the same test as its predecessor, s 41 Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act (1975) (Cth);

(b) the primary position under s 32 is that an application to this Tribunal will not affect the operation of a reviewable decision while review of the decision is undertaken. Section 32(2) creates an exception to the primary position and permits the Tribunal to make an order staying the operation of the reviewable decision if the Tribunal considers that it is desirable to do so for the purposes of ensuring the effectiveness of the review;

(c) in order to grant a stay under s 32(2), the Tribunal must be satisfied on the evidence before it that in all the relevant circumstances, it should exercise its discretion in favour of the Applicant;[33]

(d) the appropriate test is set out by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Australian Securities and Investments Commission v Administrative Appeals Tribunal[34] where the Court found that any order made by the Tribunal which stays the operation or implementation of the decision under review must be one which secures the effectiveness of the hearing and determination of the application[35] but permits the Tribunal to make orders bearing in mind the relevant statutory scheme – to prevent or modify consequences flowing from the reviewable decision in circumstances where it is possible the decision under review may be set aside;[36] and

(e) In Re Scott v Australian Securities and Investments Commission,[37] the Tribunal set down a list of factors (non-exhaustive) which may provide guidance for the Tribunal in applying the statutory test:

  • the prospects of success for the substantive application;
  • the consequence for the applicant if the stay application were refused, including consideration of whether the proceeding would be rendered nugatory;[38]
  • the public interest;
  • the consequences for the respondent if the stay application were granted/refused; and
  • any other relevant matters.
  1. The Applicants in this case have emphasised their willingness to comply with any/all reasonable conditions in order to secure a stay and have attempted to negotiate with the Respondent to achieve consent conditions for a stay. While I acknowledge their flexible and pragmatic approach, the statutory test in s 32 obliges the Tribunal to satisfy itself in all the circumstances that the application is one where the grant of a stay is desirable to secure the effectiveness of the hearing and determination of the application. Its task is not to preside over or endorse a negotiated proposal. Nor is it to consider where the balance of convenience lies. Instead, it is required to act thoughtfully in the exercise of its discretion. That exception to the primary position should be exercised in appropriate cases to prevent or modify significant and adverse consequences flowing from the reviewable decision where it is possible the decision under review may be set aside.[39]

EVALUATION OF THE APPLICATION

The SLA

  1. The Applicants sought orders staying the Respondent’s decision until 28 days after the SLA is heard and determined. They contended the Respondent’s decision wholly relies on the primary judgments[40] which have been overtaken by the appeal judgment and the SLA. They acknowledge they abided by aspects of the primary judgments and the appeal judgment but do not concede the allegations made against them in the Respondent’s decision. They submit they have real prospects if they succeed on the SLA because the dishonest and fraudulent conduct finding is material to the Respondent’s finding in the review decisions that they are not fit and proper to hold registration.
  2. In these circumstances, the Applicants submit the effectiveness of the review will likely be adversely affected if a stay is not granted. However, aside from making submissions about the likely loss of reputation for the Applicants (which is addressed below), Mr Mulcahy did not explain why a stay was required to secure the SLA or precisely why it was necessary for the effective operation of the proceeding that the operation of the review decision to be stayed until the SLA was determined.
  3. I accept the dishonest and fraudulent conduct ground is relevant to the matters the Tribunal must determine in this case. By this, I mean that I am persuaded that the matters raised in the SLA have some bearing on the issues which are before the Tribunal and I cannot presently exclude the possibility that they might lead to a different result.[41] I have addressed the question of prospects below, but note the Respondent’s reasons for decision refer to the fact that the Company breached its fiduciary duty and confidentiality obligations owed to Mr Porter and Mr Conheady and provided confidential information to associates of Mr Mulcahy.[42] The minutes of the decision also record that the Respondent considered:[43]
    • - the seriousness of James Mulcahy’s conduct, and that it involved dishonest or fraudulent conduct; and
    • - that a termination was appropriate in this instance, given the nature of the conduct and the Board’s objectives to ensure that tax agent services are provided to the community in accordance with appropriate standards of professional conduct.
  4. I also accept the Tribunal should carefully consider the timing of the lodgement of evidence and the final hearing of the substantive applications bearing in mind the anticipated timing for determination of the SLA and any subsequent appeal (if leave is granted).
  5. However, I am not persuaded from the evidence or the Applicants’ submissions, that a stay of the operation of the review decisions is necessary to secure the SLA or any subsequent appeal. Nor am I persuaded there would be some form of irreversible prejudice to the SLA if the stay were not granted before it was determined.
  6. It follows that I am not satisfied that the existence of the SLA without more, weighs in favour of a stay being granted.

The question of proportionality

  1. The Applicants submitted that in considering whether to grant the stay application, the Tribunal should carefully consider the relatively short period for which a stay is proposed against the chronology of events concerning the commercial dispute – which date back as far as 2017.
  2. I accept the commercial dispute between the Applicants and Mr Porter and Mr Conheady has a long history. The primary judgments were delivered in 2021 and 2024 respectively. The Respondent commenced its investigation in 2024 and took regulatory action in 2025.
  3. The Applicants have only sought a stay until 28 days after the SLA is determined. They have shown pragmatism and constraint in presently proposing the terms for the stay, including each of the proposed conditions. That said, it was apparent from their oral submissions at the stay hearing that they reserve the right to apply to revise/extend any such orders should they be granted special leave to appeal to the High Court.
  4. Section 32(3) Tribunal Act permits the Tribunal to exercise its discretion to order a stay subject to conditions. However, in considering whether to grant a stay (with or without conditions), the Tribunal must still have in mind the statutory test which requires it to consider what is desirable to ensure the effectiveness of the review.[44]
  5. Ultimately, I am not satisfied that the fact that the Applicants are currently proposing the stay be of temporary duration is a matter on its own which weighs in favour of the grant of a stay.

The prospects of success

  1. Consideration of the Applicants’ prospects for success on a stay application requires a preliminary evaluation of the prospects of the substantive application but not a preliminary hearing of the evidence.[45]
  2. In making its preliminary evaluation, the Tribunal is only required to be satisfied that there are some prospects for success in the application.[46] As I observed in Zartaloudis:[47]
    • - the terms ‘reasonable prospect of success’ and ‘prospect for success’ in a litigation context, can refer to a range of possible outcomes for a party. A dispute may have some prospects on a particular aspect but the question of the range of likely outcomes if it proceeds to hearing will often depend upon the hearing and evaluation of evidence, and in some cases, can turn on the question of witness credibility;[48] and
    • - there is a difference between assessing a case as having poor prospects or as unlikely to be successful and saying it is wholly unarguable.
  3. The Tribunal has previously observed that in regulatory cases of this kind, prospects for reduction in the timing of a banning order are not necessarily determinative of whether a stay should be granted, compared to the prospects of overturning a decision to terminate registration.[49]
  4. The Applicants have challenged the fraudulent or dishonest conduct ground via the SLA. They contend that the review decisions ‘involved the wholesale adoption of findings made by the primary judge in the liability judgment’ and that the Board’s decision refers to ‘dishonest or fraudulent conduct by Mr Mulcahy[50] as relevant to its decision that it was appropriate to terminate his registration. As such, they contend the fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground is material to their prospects on the review decisions and the outcome on the SLA (as well as other evidence) will be relevant.[51] They dispute the Respondent’s contentions that:
    • - nothing turns on the SLA because the review decisions also point to the Applicants’ breaches or their fiduciary and contractual duties to their clients and the liability finding in respect of Mr Conheady which have not been contested and that these are the relevant factors the Tribunal should consider in assessing the Applicants prospects of challenging the decision to terminate their registration;
    • - the Applicants have abided by these findings which are serious and they underpin the Respondent’s determination that they are not fit and proper; and
    • - on the facts as presented, and given these findings the Applicants inevitably cannot avoid termination of their registration, regardless of the outcome on the SLA.
  5. Counsel for the Applicants acknowledged that they do not enjoy an automatic right to appeal to the High Court of Australia on the fraudulent and dishonest conduct ground. They must demonstrate that their proposed questions for appeal concern a question/s of law of public importance or which reveals differences of opinion between different courts or within a single court and that it is in the interests of the administration of justice that the High Court consider the questions.[52] They acknowledge that the High Court of Australia does not often grant leave to appeal. Further, the SLA is opposed by Mr Porter and Mr Conheady.[53] These matters collectively mean their statistical chances of obtaining a grant of leave are not strong.
  6. Both parties otherwise submit and I accept it is otherwise not appropriate for the Tribunal to attempt to evaluate or assess prospects for the SLA for the purposes of the stay.
  7. I accept for the following reasons that the Applicants face a significant hurdle in challenging the termination findings in the review decisions. They have had mixed success on the liability grounds. They also decided to abide by the primary judge’s findings that they breached their fiduciary and contractual duties to Mr Conheady and that they disclosed confidential information in breach of retainers for both Mr Porter and Mr Conheady. As the Tribunal has previously observed,[54] unchallenged critical facts can be crucial to the issue of an Applicant’s prospects.
  8. Despite this, I cannot accept at this early stage that the Tribunal can or should assess the ultimate outcome of these proceedings as ‘inevitable’ for the following reasons:
    • - I accept the SLA is relevant to the matters the Tribunal must decide and it is not yet determined;
    • - the parties both submit/acknowledge that the Applicants’ prospects for the SLA are objectively not favourable for statistical reasons. However, the stay application has been made at a very early stage in the proceeding and prior to determination of the SLA and any evidence being lodged. In such circumstances, I am persuaded a cautious assessment of the Applicants’ prospects is warranted;
    • - I do not accept the Respondent’s submission that the dishonest and fraudulent conduct ground is not material to the review decisions merely because it can point to other grounds it relied upon to find a basis for the review decision. In any event, the Tribunal has been tasked with independently reviewing the review decisions. It is not bound by the precise reasoning and emphasis in the Respondent’s decisions.[55] I do accept that ultimately the Tribunal will have to weigh all relevant factors in reaching its decision but it is not appropriate or correct for it to presently attempt to do so;
    • - the Applicants’ prospects on the banning periods could be different to their prospects on the termination decisions. While I accept banning periods are often less compelling in context of the stay application, in this case, the prejudice which is identified is wholly reputational and so I am not convinced they are wholly irrelevant to my consideration of the Applicants’ prospects; and
    • - while I do not presently assess the Applicants’ prospects as strong, they have at least provided some plausible explanation for how they intend to challenge the review decisions.[56] It will be for the Applicants to develop their case but I apprehend from their submissions that they intend to approach their task surgically, starting with the SLA. I cannot say their prospect for seeking a ‘different result’ is wholly unarguable.
  9. Overall, my preliminary assessment is that the Applicants’ prospects are not strong but aspects of the applications are at least arguable.[57] I expect the Tribunal would be better placed to assess the strength of their case following determination of the SLA and lodgement of their evidence.
  10. Given my assessment of the Applicants’ prospects, I am not persuaded they weigh heavily in favour of a stay but they do not weigh against it being granted.

The consequence/s for the Applicants if the stay application were refused

  1. By the time the stay application was heard, the Tribunal’s temporary stay orders had been in place (in one form or another) for 4 months. These orders were conditional upon the Applicants not engaging new clients and Mr Mulcahy not providing registered tax services in his own name or the name of the Company.
  2. The Applicants did not suggest these conditions were causing them particular hardship. In fact, they proposed the conditions continue together with an additional condition that Mr Mulcahy not provide registered tax services in the name of any other entity. I infer this was because the tax practice has been transferred to a related entity[58] so the ‘business’ can continue without interruption while either the SLA or the applications are heard and determined.
  3. This restraint on Mr Mulcahy’s ability to practice is no imposition because he told the Tribunal his role in running the Company already means he does not personally provide registered tax services.[59]
  4. The Applicants did not identify particular hardship or potential threats of hardship as a result of operation of the review decision (such as staffing problems or vulnerability, or financial distress or constraints, or clients abandoning the business).
  5. Instead, their submissions focused on the following adverse consequences which they contended will arise if the stay is not granted. First, they argued the Respondent will publish details of the terminations and will notify the Commissioner of Taxation and other professional associations such as CPA[60] Australia if the stay is not granted. They submitted this coupled with the existing media attention[61] about the dispute with Mr Porter and Mr Conheady will damage the Applicants’ reputations and that of the broader Mulcahy and Co group.
  6. The Respondent confirmed it had not notified the CPA and further, it undertook not to do so while these proceedings are being heard and determined. It also confirmed it was required by law to inform the Commissioner of Taxation of the decision and had already done so.[62]
  7. Second, the Applicants submit they will be required to notify current or prospective clients of the termination decisions if the temporary stay is lifted and these are likely to be widely publicised and adverse to them. The Respondent disagrees and submits that this obligation does not immediately arise for the Applicants.
  8. Third, the Applicants contended there will be harm to their professional reputations within the industry and with clients if news of the terminations circulates. This reputational damage, they say, will be irreversible. Once again, no evidence was provided to assist the Tribunal to understand whether and to what extent the review decision was already known or what steps would/could be taken to ensure it remained confidential if a stay were granted.
  9. The Respondent submitted:
    • - any adverse consequences the Applicants may experience are those any person in their situation would experience. It contended the privileged commercial position the Applicants have enjoyed by operating in a registered environment brings with it the risk of regulation and the consequences which can follow; and
    • - the Applicants’ dispute with Mr Porter and Mr Conheady has already attracted substantial and adverse media attention.
  10. I am not persuaded the matters the Applicants have identified weigh in favour of a stay for the following reasons:
    • - it was not clear from the Applicants’ evidence whether their clients have or have not been informed of the review decision, or whether and to what extent it was reasonable to assume it was publicly known (or not) within the Ballarat community or more broadly. Further, there was no evidence to suggest steps had been taken or could be taken to keep the information private;
    • - I accept that the Applicants have a general obligation to keep their clients informed of matters relating to their registration,[63] but was not in a position to assess whether and to what extent clients of the business had/had not been informed of the review decisions in the many months which had passed since the review decisions were made;
    • - I am satisfied from the evidence that there has already been detailed and adverse scrutiny of the Applicants arising from the commercial dispute[64] including publication in the media of the primary judge’s finding that Mr Mulcahy engaged in dishonest and fraudulent design as well as the finding that he had breached his fiduciary duty and taken advantage of confidential information. The Tribunal was referred to no less than 5 articles published by the Ballarat Courier as well as articles by ABC News, and the Accounting Times. I infer that such extensive and adverse media attention is likely to have already damaged the Applicants’ reputations.[65] I am not persuaded that they have demonstrated that there will be measurable additional damage to their reputations flowing from the operation of the review decisions;
    • - further, the Applicants’ centre of operations is in a regional town. The review decisions were made many months ago. In the circumstances, I find it difficult to accept that members of the Ballarat community (particularly those in the profession and clients) would not have already learned of the review decisions and formed any adverse view they were likely to make from these stories. The business also has other offices (in Ararat, Mildura, Geelong, Birtinya and Toowoomba), but I note that some of the media reports are nationally distributed and so infer (in the absence of evidence to the contrary) the same may apply in those locations;
    • - additionally, these proceedings are being held in public and have already been on foot for 4 months. The Applicants have not made any application for them to be heard in private. In such circumstances, the likelihood of the review decision remaining confidential seems even more remote; and
    • - additional scrutiny may well arise because of the SLA. The High Court’s decision will be published and the outcome may independently generate further adverse publicity for the Applicants, regardless of the outcome.
  11. The Applicants did not submit that their applications would be rendered nugatory if a stay were not granted. On the contrary, I infer the arrangements the Applicants have put in place for the Company and Mr Mulcahy’s pre-existing role in the business will allow these proceedings to run without much additional adverse financial impact on them.
  12. Instead the Applicants focussed on the likely ‘irreversible adverse impact’ arising for their reputations. I am not persuaded in the circumstances that this would rise any higher than embarrassment or discomfort but note for completeness that they did not explain how it would flow from the review decisions as opposed to the prior media articles and/or the primary or appeal judgments.
  13. Further, there was no suggestion from the Applicants of any adverse health or other impacts for Mr Mulcahy should, the stay be refused.
  14. While I appreciate the Applicant will not find these proceedings positive or pleasant, they have adopted a position which is resolute in opposition to the review decisions and further, have taken prompt action to advance their interests via these proceedings and the SLA. Further, the Applicants appear to have made arrangements and are currently well placed to manage the Tribunal’s process without further and significant detriment to the business.
  15. In all these circumstances, I am not satisfied there is sufficient evidence of prejudice to either Applicant to weigh in favour of a stay. Given the nature of the prior adverse publicity and the court proceedings, the Applicants have inevitably already suffered loss of reputation. I am not persuaded a stay of the operation of the review decision can reverse this outcome or guard against further adverse comment.

The consequences for the Respondent if the stay application is granted/refused and the public interest

  1. As I observed in Zartaloudis, there is a public interest in the Respondent being permitted to discharge its statutory obligations without impediment.[66] Without suggesting it is an absolute imperative, I accept a stay of the operation of the Respondent’s decisions would impede the Respondent in discharging these functions.
  2. As I also observed in Zartaloudis, there is a general public interest in the general deterrence effect that flows within the profession from operation of the Respondent’s decisions.[67] This can extend beyond those involved in the case in question to the industry and/or the public as a whole.
  3. In some cases, the mere notification of the Respondent’s decisions may go some way toward establishing the deterrence effect I have just described.[68] However, in a case where there is conduct described as serious and where it could be viewed as adversely impacting clients and/or potential of registered tax agent services (including the perception that clients may be at risk), there is an additional public interest in the public’s confidence that the Respondent is able to and does act promptly to protect members of the public receiving registered tax services.[69]
  4. These public interest factors are not absolute, but I am persuaded on the uncontested facts in the present case that the perceived risks weigh heavily against the grant of a stay of the operation of the review decisions. The Applicants submit and I accept that the extent of the adverse media attention to date means the public already likely apprehends in general terms the commercial dispute which has given rise to the review decisions. However, in this case, there is an additional public interest in the Respondent, as the industry regulator being seen to act promptly and pre-emptively on its decisions to reassure the public any risk will be managed while the review of its decisions is heard and determined.
  5. The Applicants submit that any stay would be of short duration. In the circumstances, I am not persuaded that would outweigh the public interest factors I have identified given the uncontested findings but would also observe the Applicants cannot presently guarantee that any stay would not need to be revisited if they were to be granted special leave to appeal to the High Court. While any such appeal might be expected to be heard in a matter of months, that timeline does not account for delivery of judgment.
  6. The Applicants have been proactive suggesting possible conditions which may attach to a stay. They argue these conditions could operate to protect the public from any possible harm. However, the fact remains that if the operation of the decisions is stayed, the Applicants will remain registered. Given the adverse publicity, the extent of the uncontested findings of the Courts to date and the fact that the allegations involve client facing conduct, I am not persuaded that the appropriate balance is to maintain the status quo while their applications are heard and determined.
  7. The Respondent has given a clear indication in its reasons for decision that it regards Mr Mulcahy’s conduct as serious and the public has been given to understand similarly from media reports and the preliminary and appeal judgments. The conduct to which the review decisions relate is notorious given the media attention.
  8. In such circumstances, I am not persuaded the conditions the Applicants are proposing adequately address the public interest factors arising in this case and the adverse consequences for the Respondent as the industry regulator if its decision is not operable.

Other relevant matters

  1. The parties did not identify any other material matters.

CONCLUSION

  1. Having weighed all these factors and having considered the Applicants’ evidence, I am not satisfied in all the circumstances that there are compelling reasons why the Tribunal should exercise its discretion to grant a stay of the operation of the decisions under review. I am persuaded the adverse consequences for the Respondent and the public interest weigh strongly against the grant of a stay.
  2. In the circumstances, I am not persuaded there is a basis to exercise my discretion to grant the stay sought by the Applicants. The application is therefore dismissed.
  3. The Applicant sought 28 days in which to consider appropriate action if my decision were to dismiss the stay application. That request was opposed by the Respondent. In the circumstances, I will order that the temporary stay orders will cease to have effect 28 days after the parties receive notice of this decision. That should afford the Applicants a reasonable opportunity to consider their positions.
