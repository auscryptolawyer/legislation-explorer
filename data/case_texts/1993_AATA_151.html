ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decisions in respect of the 1987 and 1988 tax years and varies the objection decision in respect of the 1989 tax year by reducing the taxable income for that year by $2,000.

DECISION

P. GERBER This case was originally set down for two days. There have, for various reasons, been three applications to adjourn the hearing as well as a change of counsel. The last application for an adjournment was made on 27 October 1992, when Mr Nettle of learned counsel for the respondent, opposed the application. After hearing Mr Searle - the most recent choice of counsel for the applicant - I refused the application and the hearing began on Monday, November, 2 1992 and finished at the end of Friday, November 6. The taxpayer gave evidence. In addition, Mr Searle called several witnesses, including the taxpayer's wife and Mr Alex Lewenberg, a solicitor. (One ground for the adjournment, albeit made by another counsel, was that the applicant intended to call between 13 and 18 witnesses, including a witness claimed to be vital to the applicant's case. This gentleman will be referred to as "Joseph K").

2. In the 1987 tax year, the taxpayer ("T"), describing himself as a fitter and turner, returned his taxable income as $36,046. This was accepted by the Commissioner as returned and a notice of assessment issued on 22 December 1987. In the 1988 tax year, T, again describing himself as a fitter and turner returned his taxable income as $34,684. This was also accepted by the respondent as returned and a notice of assessment issued on 18 January 1989. No income tax return was lodged in 1989 for reasons which will become apparent.

3. In early 1989, T was arrested and charged with criminal offences going back to 1985 and placed in a remand centre. For good measure, T's assets were confiscated pursuant to the Proceeds of Crime Act 1987 and, after an audit of T's affairs, the Commissioner issued Asset Betterment Statements in respect of the income years 1987, 1988 and 1989 (the years now before the Tribunal). On 15 February, 1990, a notice of amended assessment issued in respect of T's 1987 income year. Pursuant to that notice, an additional $402,062 was included in T's assessable income together with sec 223(1) penalties (partially remitted pursuant to sec 227(3)) amounting to $158,241. On the same date, a notice of amended assessment issued in respect of the 1988 income year which included an additional $313,775 to T's assessable income for that year, together with penalties of $85,357. Also on the same date, the respondent issued a default assessment in respect of his 1989 income year, assessing T's taxable income in the amount of $272,205. The taxpayer did not deny that there was a substantial increase in his net assets over those years, but asserted that the bulk of these assets were the result of successful gambling on horses ("which can be verified by TAB accounts and from some bookmakers"; per Mr Searle in his opening), Tattslotto wins, and the proceeds of sale of a substantial inheritance from T's mother, a lady said to have been very wealthy, who arrived in this country as a Polish migrant with most of her wealth intact, albeit in receipt of the old age pension at the time of her death in Melbourne in February 1983. T, who has a brother and sister in Poland, was said for all practical purposes to have been the sole beneficiary under his mother's "will". The mother's wealth was said to include a blue and white diamond ring weighing 5 1/2 carats as well as other jewellery, some 150 rings allegedly acquired in Australia, several furs, 17 paintings, including a "painting" by Albrecht DHrer, some stamps and coins, an old bible, an antique torah and valuable tapestries. These items were allegedly sold after the lady's death (some were said to be still in Poland at the time of her death and gradually taken out of the country by T) and the proceeds from their sale either banked in various bank accounts - some in names of members of his family - or else invested in real estate. The alleged sale of this lady's estate will be dealt with in detail.

4. I will state at the outset that I consider T to be a wholly unreliable witness to whose evidence I can attach little weight; indeed I am satisfied that at times he would lie without scruple if he thought his response would assist his case; at other times he would be recklessly indifferent whether his answers were truthful or not. I have included much of his evidence-in-chief and cross-examination to illustrate that he was a witness prepared to say anything on oath that came into his head. In the result, I have rejected much of his testimony which was not independently corroborated.

5. In a letter to T, dated 27 November, 1989, the Commissioner sets out the various assets and liabilities (amended in February 1990) which were revealed after an intensive investigation. Briefly, the Commissioner discovered numerous bank accounts in the names of various members of T's family, as well as a number of real properties. He arrived at the increase in T's taxable income by preparing a statement of assets and liabilities, after which he proceeded to calculate T's taxable income by a method known as the "Asset Betterment Method". (In his opening, Mr Searle alleged that the Betterment Statements contained some "obvious errors", such as the omission in 1988 of T's salary and wages, a receipt of workers' compensation and a lump sum of holiday pay in 1988. I will return to this aspect of the case later when dealing with the evidence). The Commissioner's statement and calculations are set out below:

9. Since it was put to the Tribunal that T arrived in this country as a man of wealth, one must wonder why, given his habit of acquiring real estate whenever funds were available, or else banking surplus funds, he waited four years before buying his first home, financed by loans of $9,000 and savings of $5,000. For good measure, Mr Odachowski's summary of "family income available for investment" from 1964 to 1970 shows a mere surplus of some $1,000 p.a. even though T's wife was then working. If T arrived in Australia with wealth, he must have put it under his mattress, since it does not show up anywhere as either having been banked or invested. Having observed him in the witness box, I am satisfied that if he did indeed arrive here in 1963 with the wealth claimed for him by his counsel, his subsequent habits show that he would have put it to use. In short, I completely reject Mr Searle's submission, constantly re-iterated, that T had "significant assets" from an early age. Some photographs were made an exhibit, said to support this alleged "wealth". One photograph showed T's father operating a taxi in Poland, another shows the frontage of a general store in Vienna, said to be operated by "relatives of T". The family photo album contains pictures of a family car, the family gravestone in Poland, a lady astride a BMW car, said to be identical to the car claimed to have belonged to T in Poland prior to his migration to this country, and the family in their sitting room in their home in Poland. In the background of this photo is a tapestry said to have been sold in Australia. I find that these numerous family snapshots to have more sentimental than evidentiary value; they certainly do not support the assertion that the family disposed over the kind of wealth now claimed for it. There is no hunting lodge, no Rolls Royce, no Louis quinze furniture, no maid in frilly apron or liveried chauffeur; just a cosy domestic scene of an average family against average domestic furniture. Nor can this great wealth be explained by a lack of ostentation, since the mother was said to be in the habit of wearing a large diamond ring so valuable that it looms large in the purported asset accretion, having been sold by T after mother's death. A written statement from the sister of T's mother, resident in Poland, was tendered over Mr Nettle's objection, stating that T's parents were the owners of a "Footwear and Leatherwear Shop, a Transport Company and subsequently a taxi in Krakow." It went on:-

I also declare that while leaving for Melbourne in 1964, my

sister has left in my keeping part of her assets which she could not

take out of Poland due to the existing regulations. These included,

among other things

- valuable paintings by the Kossak brothers, icons etc.

- items made of gold and silver (cutlery, jewellery, cigarette cases)

- carpets and Persian kilims

- old grandfather clocks and watches

- old valuable religious books (Orthodox Bible and Torah)

- a collection of coins and stamps

During her numerous trips to Poland over the period of 17 years, my

sister would successively take all these objects to Australia. I also

learned from the conversation with my sister that in her Will, she

has conveyed the legal deeds of her estate in the eastern territories

of the Second Republic of Poland to her sons (T) and Ignaz.

Sworn at Wroclaw, 14/10/1991

10. T was called as the principal witness and taken through his various financial transactions since his arrival in Australia in 1963. The evidence of his early dealings is largely irrelevant - other than revealing a habit of investing surplus funds - since it is common ground that by 1982, T disposed over assets valued at $270,000 (see item 16 of "chronology of major transactions" above).

11. In his evidence, T was carefully taken through the contents of his mother's estate. As already mentioned, his mother died in February 1983, leaving a holograph will in the Polish language executed in this country. The will fails to appoint an executor, the attestation clause does not comply with Victorian law, some provisions, such as "$500 to the 'Old Age Home' and $500 to the poor and hungry children", and her books "to the library" appear to me to be void for uncertainty. For good measure, no attempt was made to obtain probate, take out Letters of Administration, or lodge the will with the Supreme Court of Victoria. Be that as it may, the "will" is claimed to bequeath to T all monies remaining after the specific bequests. (The alleged conversation with the testatrix's sister in Poland re her will, in which the sister claimed that her sister willed her Polish estates to T and his brother is not borne out by the holograph will. Indeed, the evidence suggests that T did not benefit from any estate that may still be in Poland. Since the will was executed at Caulfield Hospital and the lady died a year later, one must wonder when the conversation with the sister in Poland about her testamentary disposition took place). For good measure, there are two official translations of the will, each provided by the Department of Immigration, Local Government and Ethnic Affairs in Melbourne (exh "D"), one dated 2 December, 1991 and the other 6, October 1992. The two "translations" vary in major respects, albeit both quote an identical registration number. and he was - it was a sentimental value for him because some things

which brought from Poland, there was something which in Poland was

rotting and I believe - this what he explained - that for Jewish

people that it was heartbreaking, this stuff is just rotting there

... For people who are not Jewish or they don't put value to the

painter or the person who done it, is of no value. ... For some

people of - no value for me because there is nothing much, to be

honest. But for other person will be valuable, so is - This how I

look, I don't know."

62. I am sure that Mrs T, steering a narrow course between Scylla and Charybdis - between the truth as she knew it and loyalty to her husband - did her best to be honest. However, on critical issues her evidence was not able to shed much light on the darker aspects of her husband's testimony. She was Mr Searles' last major witness. It was then sought to tender a signed statement of Joseph K, dated 4 March 1992. As noted before, Mr Nettle objected to its tender and I overruled his objection and accepted the statement (exh "AK").

63. Before dealing with the contents of this signed statement, I propose to recapitulate briefly the role Joseph K played in this drama. He might well be compared to Hamlet's ghost, i.e. although he did not appear in this play, his shadow hung gloomily over the whole performance. Thus, several applications were made for an adjournment before the proceedings on the grounds that Joseph K was a vital witness, that he was currently on trial on criminal charges and that he would be unwilling to give evidence until the conclusion of his trial. Mr Murley, of counsel, who appeared on one such application, finally decided - when faced with an unsympathetic Tribunal - that, provided he was still briefed, he would attempt to proceed without this witness. Subsequently, after Mr Searle was retained, a further application for an adjournment was made by him and refused by me, with the indication that he, Mr Searle, could renew his application at the end of the applicant's case if he felt that he was prejudiced by the absence of this witness. During the hearing, I made it clear to Mr Searle on at least two occasions that I would view an application for an adjournment at the end of his case with sympathy, but was assured each time that the case would be conducted without Joseph K. At the end of the evidence, the following took place:

The D President (to Mr Searle): Before accepting the tender -

and I note your objection Mr Nettle - I just want to make it quite

clear, so that we are ad idem on this. You foreshadowed last week

that you would be seeking an adjournment, and I indicated to you in

chambers that I would refuse it.

Mr Searle: Yes, Sir.

The D President: I did not indicate to you that you would not be in a

position to renew that application at the end of your case.

Mr Searle: Yes, Sir.

The D President: Now, I take it you do not wish to adjourn the

proceedings awaiting the availability of Joseph K?

Mr Searle: Well, no Sir.

The D President: Well, in those circumstances I propose to accept the

tender.

64. During the heat of battle, I decided to accept the tender, mindful that I am not strictly bound by the rules of evidence and that "Hamlet's ghost" may shed some light on the events. On more mature reflection, I believe I should have rejected it. Right or wrong, having now looked at this exhibit, I am satisfied that, in any event, I can give no weight to its contents, if only because it contains matters which, as a matter of procedural fairness, should be subjected to cross-examination. For good measure, the statement contains critical allegations which were not raised in evidence. Thus, it is stated that the proceeds from the sale of mother's estate, estimated at $300,000, was allegedly paid in cash "over a period of 2 years" and that the racing partnership between Joseph K and T resulted in one win of some $250,000 at odds of 5/2 on a horse called Washington State. This major win was not mentioned by T in his evidence, despite the fact that he must have had access to Joseph K's statement for some months prior to the hearing.

65. Below is the statement in full:

I, Joseph K, of 6/105 High Street, Caulfield, state that:

1.I have known T of 39 Byron Street, Elwood for 20 years, in which

time we have become close friends. Our families have also known each

other over this period. We are like relatives.

Our relationship has extended into business ventures, and in

providing mutual financial assistance whenever necessary.

We had joint interests in the ownership of racehorses, and in sharing

any gambling wins on horse racing. This practice has continued over

many, many years, to our mutual benefit.

One instance of our large gambling wins, which we shared the proceeds

of, was on 13th December, 1988, where we bet $100,000 to win $250,000

on Washington State. The horse won and the report of the win was

covered by the Sun Newspaper.

Other large wins we shared in were numerous, including In Wonderland,

Warned, Northern Copy, etc..

2. I have also sold, on T's behalf, the jewellery, fur coats and

other valuable possessions inherited by him following his Mothers'

death. Included in the estate was a valuable painting by A. Durer,

which I sold for him. I have estimated that the proceeds of these

sales totalled in excess of $300,000. This was received in cash and

given to T in the form of cash over a period of 2 years. T would

also lend me money to support my business when buying gold,

jewellery, antiques, coins, etc., and I would repay him when I sold

the items.

3.Because of the special relationship we had, payments to each other

were in cash, and no detailed records were needed to be kept, as we

both had an accurate idea of who owed who how much.

4.I stress that T often assisted me in financing my business

transactions of buying and selling gold, etc. and also was my partner

in gambling on horses. This relationship has existed for well over

10 years.

66. Mr Searle called a Mr Hard Yakka, who was T's supervisor in the factory which employed him as a fitter and turner. The evidence of this witness was brief and not to the point. T was a reliable worker, always available when needed, sometimes working "six, seven days a week, whatever was necessary".

67. The final witness was a TAB official through whom the TAB telephone betting account for Joseph K was tendered (exh "AM"). The record covers one day - 29 October 1988, the day Empire Rose won in Race 6 at Flemington. At the end of that day, Joseph K won, over a series of races, some $202,942.95. I don't know what use I am to make of this evidence. The whole record is ephemeral, in that it covers betting for only one day. There is no bet on Empire Rose except as part of a quadrella. In any event Joseph K is not the taxpayer and the totality of the evidence has not persuaded me that all - or any - bets made by Joseph K were in partnership with T. For good measure, there is no evidence of any payment to T of $100,000 or thereabouts. If there was an additional bet on Empire Rose of $5000 at 66/1, no attempt was made to establish this bet through bookmakers.

68. The Commissioner concluded that at the end of the betterment period, T disposed over assets worth $1,269,065. (This will need to be reduced by $2,000, the difference between the loan from Lewenberg recorded as $63,000 which was, in fact, $65,000). The applicant, on the other hand, maintains that his assets were worth only $962,000. At the end of the Commissioner's audit, the investigator included in T's assets "loans" of $130,000 in 1987, $228,000 in 1988 and $291,000 in 1989. The currency of these loans were fervently denied and the Lewenberg release pleaded as evidence that by September 1988 there were no further loans outstanding - that the parties were "quits". The auditor was not called and it is only by recourse to the notes made by the auditor (which form part of the "T" documents) that I am able to conclude that these loans were assembled, collage-like, from evidence given by T and Joseph K in other proceedings. True it is that the onus of showing that an assessment is excessive rests on the taxpayer, I believe procedural fairness demands that he be afforded the opportunity to test by cross-examination the basis upon which audits of the kind cobbled together here are brought into existence. In other words, I cannot refrain from noting my disquiet that the auditor was not called and no explanation offered for his failure to appear. Having said that, I am also satisfied that provided it can be shown that the Commissioner has properly exercised his judgment in compliance with his statutory obligation, it is for the taxpayer to show that the income thus arrived at is excessive and by how much. The matter was put succinctly by Latham CJ in Trautwein v FC of T [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63

The application of sec. 39 (now sec.177(1) is not, in my opinion,

excluded as soon as it is shown that an element in the assessment is

a guess and that it is therefore very probably wrong. It is prima

facie right - and remains right until the appellant shows that it is

wrong. If it were necessary to decide the point I would, as at

present advised, be prepared to hold that the taxpayer must, at least

as a general rule, go further and show, not only negatively that the

assessment is wrong, but also positively what correction should be

made in order to make it right or more nearly right. I say 'as a

general rule' because, conceivably, there might be a case where it

appeared that the assessment had been made upon no intelligible basis

even as an approximation, and the court would then set aside the

assessment and remit it to the Commissioner for further

consideration. (at p 88)

I believe the above statement has survived the decision in FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088 (which I analysed in detail in Case X65 90 ATC 492) and as further refined by Burchett J in Ma v FC of T. It may well be that the betterment statement in these applications include some amounts which had their source either in mother's estate, such as the sale of the Jewish paintings and/or some jewellery which came into her possession, or T's salary in 1989, or a workers' compensation payout or some holiday pay. If so, one can only express regret that no attempt was made to identify any of these matters, all of which were included in counsel's opening.

69. What has the taxpayer done to establish that the assessments are excessive? His oral testimony is virtually worthless, if only because he is incapable of distinguishing between truth and fiction. The Commissioner had material before him from which he concluded that in the three years before me, Joseph K - or someone else - owed T some $291,000. There is nothing in the evidence of the taxpayer which has persuaded me that the Commissioner has erred on this aspect of the betterment statements. Cheques payable to T from Joseph K, his girl friend and the Romy Brothers may have been loan repayments, they could be proceeds from the sale of jewellery, wins from horses or, for all I know, the proceeds from the sale of cocoa beans. It is for the applicant to establish that these monies do not emanate from an income source - and this he has lamentably failed to do. There was no evidence whatsoever - other than T's "valuation" - of the value of mother's jewellery, furs, the DHrer "painting", etc. It requires a quantum leap, given the total unreliability of T's evidence, to label any of the various payments from Joseph K as constituting the proceeds of the sale of mother's estate. In this state of the evidence, it would be nothing short of reckless to ascribe the substantial increase in T's assets as having come from that source, despite Mr Searle's assertion in his address that "it is clear beyond argument that T did come from a very, very wealthy family from Poland; did bring out very significant assets; that his mother was a very independently wealthy person and that T obtained possession of all his mother's assets in 1983."

70. As already noted, I likewise reject the submission that T's racing activities made any contribution to his assets, if only because the evidence is paltry, contradictory and what there is of it, implausible. If T did indeed lose his race book, said to have been able to confirm all his betting activities (a proposition I view with considerable suspicion), it is a misfortune, but it cannot assist him in these proceedings. I also reject T's explanation for the need to keep 14 bank accounts in different names - there certainly is no acceptable evidence that the monies in any of these accounts did not belong to T. As to the DHrer "painting" the less said about this art work the better.

71. In this state of the evidence, one can only regret the absence of Joseph K and muse whether, if called, his evidence would have assisted the taxpayer. As noted before on this aspect, Mr Searle chose to soldier on without this witness and must live with the consequences. He is thus in every sense volens. I therefore reject his attempt to persuade me that Mr Nettle's conduct, when faced with the final application for an adjournment, based on Joseph K's inability to give evidence, lured him into a sense of false security; viz the belief that cross-examination of that witness would be limited to one area of the applicant's case - the joint racing activities. I am satisfied that there was nothing in Mr Nettle's conduct before me which could have persuaded Mr Searle that Joseph K's evidence was critical only on the issue of racing wins (a not insignificant item).

72. In case the matter goes on appeal and I am said to be wrong on my interpretation of the onus provision of the Act, I propose to set it out yet again in greater particularity.

73. In this case, I have had the advantage of a very detailed address on the subject by Mr Searle who, for good measure, wrote a very learned article on the subject - "The Onus of Proof in Asset Betterment Cases"; 21 Australian Tax Review 250-258 (December 1992) - a contribution which deserves a wider readership than it is likely to receive.

74. Firstly, Mr Searle referred me to the objection - which he adopted - which states that in the Assets Betterment Statements, "they (i.e. the Commissioner) have combined assets of other family members (or assets owned jointly) which legally do not belong to the Taxpayer, and treated them as his assets." This is not borne out by any evidence. All the properties included in the Betterment Statement (set out in para 5 above) were claimed to have been purchased by T, either with loans or with his own funds. One property was said to have been acquired for the use of his son Dick, but it was not seriously suggested that any of the monies came from Dick.

75. As to the numerous bank accounts, T conceded that they were all operated by him and no attempt was made to suggest that the figures were inaccurate. Some explanations - for example why he kept an account in the name of his sister - borders on impertinence. If some accounts contain monies belonging to the wife and/or son, it is for him to identify their source. He did not do so to my satisfaction.

76. It was submitted that the Commissioner ignored the capital profits made on share sales. However, the only share transaction which could conceivably be relevant to the periods now under review relates to the sale in "1988" of 3000 Poseidon shares which formed part of a parcel of 80,000 shares acquired in "1988" and only referred to in an annexure to the letter from JP Hardwick and Associates to the respondent of 20 December 1989 (see paragraph 6 above). Since I have no evidence upon which to determine whether this profit is "caught" by sec 25(1), the (then) sec 26AAA or by Part IIIA of the Act, the applicant must fail on the onus of proof.

77. At the end of the day, given that I reject the bulk of T's evidence as a tissue of lies, I am left in a state of uncertainty as to the true position in the years now before me. Indeed, there is nothing in the evidence which, I am satisfied, would jusitfy me in determining that the assessments are excessive (save as to some $2,000 in the 1989 tax year, with which I have already dealt). That being so,

Dalco insists that a taxpayer in the position of the applicant

must show, according to the civil onus, that the assessment is

excessive. ... If, by any means, he can show the assessment is

excessive, he is entitled to seek relief. (per the court in Ma v FC

of T ).

As the judge noted in Ma, the Tribunal (i.e. me) fell into error in insisting, in a case in which increases in assets were claimed to have been the result of racing wins, the taxpayer can only succeed:

if he can prove in relation to each tax year under review

precisely what moneys were earned from gambling and, indeed, what

moneys were lost and what the precise end result was. Alternatively,

(he must) prove the precise amount of the taxable income of the

taxpayer from all sources which would give rise to the taxable

income.

78. The above statement, given in a brief oral decision, in which the Tribunal failed to make any observation on credibility, was held to be wrong in law. However, I am satisfied that, by and large, it reflects the correct position in those cases where the increase in assets is claimed to be attributable to events which are found not to exist or are not believed. This view receives support from the decision of Olney J in Ousley v FC of T 92 ATC 4486 where the learned judge noted that:

... the difference between this case and Ma is that whereas in Ma

the Tribunal did not make any findings as to credibility, and thus

denied itself the opportunity to make the sort of findings of fact

which the case called for, in the present case the Tribunal expressed

in unequivocal terms, which it repeated more than once, that the

appellant's evidence lacked any credibility. The case the applicant

mounted was that he had won money gambling as a punter, but the

Tribunal did not believe him. That was not necessarily the end of the

case. Obviously, if there was other evidence upon which the necessary

finding could be made, the applicant would have been entitled to

succeed. But there was no other evidence probative of the facts

asserted. (p 4495).

(Curiously, the decision in Ousley, in which Mr Searle appeared for the (unsuccessful) applicant both before this Tribunal, in which I presided, and on appeal to the Federal Court, fails to rate a mention amongst the many cases cited by him in the article referred to above. My first thought was that this omission was due to the fact that Ousley was handed down too late for inclusion in the article, but then I noticed that the learned author refers to Mr Beddoe's decision in Case Z32 92 ATC 297 with high praise, a decision published in September 1992, i.e. after Ousley).

79. In summary, I reject the evidence that any accretions to T's assets have their source in the sale of mother's jewellery, the DHrer "painting", furs, icons etc. It has been established by exhibit "E" that there was a good deal of jewellery in T's home at the time of the police raid in October 1987. I have no idea as to the value of this haul or what became of it. It may have been sold to or through Joseph K, if so I have no idea for how much and whether T ever received any of the proceeds. I certainly have not been persuaded that the dishonoured cheque for $150,000 led to a later payment in like amount, and even if it did, the evidence is, to say the least, contradictory as to the consideration for the payment. On the probabilities, if the amount was ever paid, I find it more likely than not - in the absence of any credible explanation - that its source was of an income nature. As to the Lewenberg "release", suffice to say that, given the surrounding circumstances and the manner of its creation, I view that document with the utmost suspicion. The torah was clearly sold and the money received before the betterment period. There may have been a painting - or several - sold overseas; if so I am unable to say when they were sold and/or when the proceeds were received. However, there is considerable doubt just when all the various paintings were sold; there is some very vague evidence that one painting was sold by T overseas for $20,000. Mr Lewenberg, who bought the "Australian batch" of the Jewish paintings, could not be more precise as to when he bought them than "about 1986" (p 332 tr). He was not pressed on the precise date. Again, I am unable to say whether (i) a cheque from Wright for coins etc in the sum of $5700 or some other amount was received during the betterment period; (ii) the proceeds from the sale of an old bible was received during the betterment period and the amount received for this item; (iii) in the relevant period, some amount in the various bank accounts belonged to the wife and/or son and did not come from an income source attributable to T; (iv) the betterment statements include amounts for workers' compensation, holiday pay, and/or wages, as to which no attempt was made to provide any evidence, Mr Searle contenting himself with statements from the bar table. As to alleged betting wins, apart from one exhibit which supports the proposition that Joseph K had a remarkable day at the races on one glorious day, there is no evidence which I find credible which would suggest that any amount or amounts claimed to have been won by T can be excluded from the Betterment Statements. Indeed, a win of the magnitude said to have been won on Empire Rose should have been capable of being established other than by the say so of T. Where were all the bookmakers I was assured would be called in support of this and other wins? It is of some interest to note that the Joseph K statement (exh AK) signed in March 1992 - I assume for the purpose of this hearing - does not refer to to the win on Empire Rose, the "deponent" contenting himself with merely noting:

one instance of our large gambling wins, which we shared the

proceeds of, was on 13th December, 1988, where we bet $100,000 to win

$250,000 on Washington State ... Other large wins we shared in were

numerous, including In Wonderland, Warned, Northern Copy, etc.

One may also well ask what became of these "wins" since they certainly do not show up in any of the numerous bank accounts under T's control. I have already dealt with the one Tattslotto win and my reason why I cannot accept it as an amount which should be excluded from the betterment statements.

80. This case is thus different from Case Z32, a case relied on by Mr Searle. In that case, Senior Member Beddoe was satisfied that (i) that there has been a deliberate and contrived arrangement for the understatement of the applicant's assessable income, (ii) that the taxpayer was a successful punter who was likely to have won more than he lost and (iii) that the Commissioner had not taken the unquantified gambling wins into account, the assessments must therefore be excessive. In the result, Mr Beddoe, rather than remit the matter back to the Commissioner to form his judgment as to the amount upon which tax ought to be levied, undertook himself, with more courage than I possess, the task of forming his own judgment as to that amount. The difference between Case Z32 and this case is that there is very little in the evidence which I can accept as truthful. It may be that the Asset Betterment Statements are excessive; if they are, nothing has been demonstrated before me which would justify me in reducing the amounts assessed, save that it has been demonstrated that the Commissioner was in error in the amount of the Lewenberg loan. It is thus obvious that Mr Searle placed more reliance on the credibility of his principle witness than was justified on the evidence, hoping that it was sufficient just to put this man in the box, have him sworn and trust to luck. Mr Nettle described T as a "pathological liar", a description I do not find inapt save that I have no evidence of any underlying pathology. In other words, unlike the case before Mr Beddoe, this is not a case where I am able to form my own judgment as to the amounts upon which, year by year, tax ought to be levied, nor do I believe it is possible for the Commissioner to do so if I were to remit the case on the offchance that the taxpayer may be able to cure some of the more glaring defects in his evidence.

81. For the above reasons, I affirm the objection decisions in respect of the 1987 and 1988 tax years and vary the objection decision in respect of the 1989 tax year by reducing the taxable income for that year by $2,000 (the Lewenberg loan difference). I see no reason for reducing the penalties assessed (subject to any proportional reduction in respect of the 1989 year due to the reduction of the taxable income for that year of $2,000).
