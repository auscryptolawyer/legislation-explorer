Re Taxation Appeals [1989] AATA 158 (30 June 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT87/3294-3299

AAT No. 5204

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Peter M. Roach(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable Deductions - Legal Expenses - Liability voluntarily incurred to preserve on-going possession of premises and equipment - Proceedings involving lessons of premises and equipment - Proceedings instituted by or against third-parties in relation to claims by third-parties to possession.

John Fairfax & Sons Pty Limited v F.C.T. [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30

HEARING

SYDNEY

30:6:1989

ORDER

Tribunal decides that the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections under review shall be varied as follows:-

(a) for the year of income ended 30 June 1981

taxable income will be reduced by $34,778 and

the assessment of Division 7 tax amended

accordingly; and

(b) for the years of income ended 30 June 1982

and 1983 the assessments of income tax and

Division 7 tax will be set aside.

DECISION

The Applicant is a limited liability company (App-co). It is one of a number of companies established with a view to helping a married couple (Dennis and his wife) achieve their financial and commercial ambitions. The issue has it origins in a partnership they had formed to carry on business in the building industry. In the course of the history of the partnership they had purchased substantial plant and equipment which came to be owned by the partnership. Later four companies were formed with three of them taking over part of the business formerly conducted by the partnership. But the partnership was not wound up; it continued to own substantial plant and equipment and made it available to the operating companies. The present problem arose out a financial crisis experienced by the four companies in June 1980.

2. The four companies were Land-co - which became registered proprietor of an industrial site from which the operations of the partnership and other companies were thereafter carried on - and Lift-co, Frame-co and Brace-co: all carrying on business within the building industry. Lift-co and Frame-co specialised in providing equipment and the services of skilled personnel to building projects. The specialty of Frame-co was scaffolding - an undertaking which involved the use of vast quantities of pipes and fittings. As loose parts, the components lacked any substantially distinguishing feature. But when assembled, they constituted a totality usually maintained in a stable condition for prolonged periods under the conditions which did not permit the ready transfer of delivery by possession. For the rest, as to Frame-co, and for the other companies, the equipment involved (such as vehicles) was more readily capable of transfer by delivery. From the incorporation of those four companies, they existed in the eyes of the law as entities distinct and separate from the partnership, but they were neither independent of it nor of each other. They were dependent upon the partnership because, to the extent to which they leased, or otherwise made available equipment to, or used it in the service of others, they had to obtain possession from the partnership. The companies were also dependent upon the partnership in that, to the extent to which they needed industrial and commercial expertise, they were dependent upon Dennis to provide it. They were also inter-dependent in that Lift-co, Frame-co and Brace-co all operated from the premises owned by Land-co. There was a further complication in that, as will appear hereafter, some steps had been taken to formally confer on the partnership an entitlement to have possession of that industrial site so that the rights of occupation and use conferred on Lift-co, Frame-co and Brace-co were directly attributable to the partnership and only indirectly to Land-co. They were also inter-dependent financially. A single bank account was operated for all companies in the group. That account was an account of Frame-co and Frame-co enjoyed substantial overdraft facilities. However, those facilities had only been made available on the basis of security for the indebtedness of Frame-co being given by the other companies in the group.

3. So it was that successful conduct of the business or businesses required:

(a) the personal organisational and technical skills of

Dennis;

(b) the services of other workers and employees;

(c) the availability for use of the industrial site;

(d) the availability of all plant and equipment;

(e) the benefit of contracts for the provision of services

to customers; and

(f) sufficient financial resources to conduct the business

- something determined by the available financial

resources committed through Dennis and his wife; by

creditors; and by financial support made available

through the bank.

4. In early June 1980 the businesses of the group were all active. Together they had substantial prospects of long-term profit. That was all imperilled by liquidity problems which were being experienced. Each of the trading companies was trading and in doing so using the skills of Dennis; the premises occupied by leave of the partnership; and the plant and equipment made available to it by the partnership. Then the bank made immediate demand for payment of a substantial sum. When payment was not immediately forthcoming the bank proceeded to appoint joint Receiver-Managers. (I shall hereafter refer to the appointees as the "Receiver-Manager").

5. The Receiver-Manager was immediately faced with substantial problems:-

(a) He had no control or authority over Dennis, without

whom it would be extremely difficult to effectively

manage the contracts which were on foot;

(b) The right of the companies to continue with the

performance of many contracts would have terminated

or have been at risk of termination on, the

appointment of the Receiver-Manager;

(c) It was asserted by the partnership that the

Receiver-Manager had no right of continued access to

and occupation of the freehold premises because the

companies only occupied those premises at the will of

the partnership;

(d) It was a further contention that the Receiver-Manager

had no right to continued possession of any plant or

equipment because its availability to the companies

was only at the will of the partnership; and

(e) The determination on the part of Dennis that the

appointments were improperly made and that the claims

of the Receiver-Manager should be resisted at any cost

and that such appointments should not frustrate the

ambitions of Dennis.

6. Not only was Dennis determined to challenge the appointments but he was also determined to continue the businesses of the companies as a business under his control. To that end the couple caused App-co to be incorporated. I accept that at all material times and in all material respects all power of decision-making was exercised exclusively by Dennis.

7. App-co quickly established itself in control of the most essential elements of the business. It had available to it the skills and services of Dennis. It was able to retain the services of such other workers and employees as it needed. The partnership provided it with the right to use the freehold premises to the exclusion of the Receiver-Manager and the partnership provided App-co with the right to possession of all plant and equipment owned by the partnership whether on site, at various jobs or in store at the premises. Further, Dennis was fairly promptly able to make arrangements with the Receiver-Manager that App-co would bear the cost of, and assume responsibility for, fulfilling existing contracts and thereon become entitled to future earnings, but with the Receiver-Manager maintaining his claim to moneys already due. The right to performance of the incomplete contracts was acquired at no cost to App-co, but in other respects it was to face substantial costs. Dennis and his wife and other workers became entitled to remuneration for their services - such remuneration to be attended by superannuation benefits in the case at least of Dennis and his wife. The right to possession of the premises and plant and equipment as conferred by the partnership only involved nominal outlays by way of rent. What proved to be the most substantial cost to be borne by App-co came through the cost of extensive litigation.

8. Between its incorporation on 2 July 1980 and January 1984 App-co was to pay to solicitors ("the Firm") over $210,000 in and about the costs of litigation. When the retainer of the Firm was discharged in January 1984, the firm presented accounts to Dennis claiming payment of a further sum of $42,743. The issues to be determined relate to the deductibility for App-co of a portion of the costs actually paid - an amount of $168,257.

9. From 8 July 1980 the Receiver-Manager was proposing arrangements which would allow App-co to carry on the performance of the contracts still in existence. At the date of the appointment of the Receiver-Manager, the companies were using the plant and equipment owned by the partnership and they operated from the industrial site owned by Land-co. Negotiations resulted in an acknowledgement by the Receiver-Manager of the ownership of the plant and equipment (as specified in its 1979 partnership return) by the partnership; that there was no equity in leases of vehicles or plant utilised in the scaffolding business; that there was a right in App-co in the short term to carry on business from the Land-co site and with it the right to use the existing telephone numbers which had been used by the companies in receivership. Ultimately App-co received the benefit of all of its actions in the performance of outstanding obligations. It did not pay anything to the Receiver-Manager or to anyone else to obtain that benefit.

10. As it already had the benefit of the services of Dennis, and was able to contract with others for their services, it remained for App-co to make secure a claim to possession and use of suitable premises and equipment. It might have dealt with unrelated entities in the market place and dealt in common place terms and conditions and by doing so have advanced the interests of those with whom it dealt. Instead it dealt with the partnership. In relation to the land, I am satisfied that there was an agreement in writing whereby App-co had conferred on the partnership a right to possession of the premises. That agreement was not produced in evidence. I am similarly satisfied in relation to a sub-lease from the partnership to App-co (cf. below). The solicitor who held the relevant documents was summonsed to produce them but, upon attending before the Tribunal, by counsel argued that the documents in his possession were privileged from production because of a claim maintained by the solicitor to a general lien. I ruled against the claim. In doing so I directed that the Tribunal would receive the documents as agent for the solicitor, thereby preserving his right to possession of the documents. In that way his right to withhold possession of the document from his client would have been fully protected. In so ruling I had regard to the decision of the Court of Appeal of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Bolster v McCallum ((1966) 85 WN (Pt 1) NSW 281). When it was indicated that the solicitor desired to move the Federal Court to set my ruling aside, Counsel for App-co determined not to press the matter further. Hence the documents were not physically produced. In reaching the finding I have made I have had secondary evidence from (inter alia) Dennis, not objected to by Counsel for the Commissioner.

11. The fact of a sub-lease from the partnership to App-co had been deposed to in an affidavit sworn by Dennis and filed in the Supreme Court of New South Wales. A copy of the sub-lease was exhibited to the affidavit at the time of its making but it seems that it was subsequently misplaced or lost by the Court. It was not among the documents produced by the Registrar of the Court to this Tribunal. When presenting his reasons for decision in that case the trial Judge acknowledged that "counsel for the Plaintiff made it clear that the credibility of (Dennis) was in question" in the case. His Honour then went on to find that:

"I accept (Dennis') evidence on all matters in this case."

(I have noted from the exhibits before me that another Judge in another case, took a completely different view in the credibility of Dennis. In that case the Judge said of Dennis:

"On a very important issue he has, in my view, been

completely discredited and shorn (sic) of being a

person worthy of being believed."

In this instance there was no challenge to the credibility of Dennis such as would cause me to doubt the accuracy of the findings I have made.

12. I accept the evidence as establishing that the documents did confer on App-co an entitlement to the possession of the industrial site as sub-lessee of the partnership for a nominal rental.

13. However, as to the matter of plant and equipment there was no such formality. I am satisfied that there was nothing in writing or otherwise expressed such as would have conferred an enforceable right at law in favour of App-co entitling it to possession of the plant and equipment. I am satisfied that Dennis simply decided that App-co could have the advantage of the use of the plant and equipment of the partnership and that the decision so made, so long as he decided nothing to the contrary, would constitute the assent of both the partnership and App-co to such a relationship. I am also satisfied that the decision was not so made as to allow either the partnership or App-co to prevent Dennis from changing his mind. The evidence of Dennis was that it was "agreed" that App-co would pay the legal costs of proceedings in which App-co and the partnership were to become engaged. In so far as the evidence was in terms suggestive of a bargain of contractual character, I reject it. In my view what happened amounted to no more than this - that App-co from time to time assumed responsibility for such legal expenses incurred in the interests of itself and of the partnership and of the other companies in the group as it pleased; and that it did so in order to prevent disruption to its business activities by reason of any claims made by third parties, whether the bank, the Receiver-Manager or others, to act in any way which would interfere with App-co's occupation of the industrial site or its possession of and/or use of any plant and equipment which it was using in the business. I find that in doing so its expenditure was dictated by its business ends - the on-going and profitable conduct of its business (cf. F.C.T. v Snowden Willson Pty Ltd [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 per Dixon C.J. at 436-7). I am sure that in July 1980 App-co did not foresee, and could not have foreseen, the extent to which there would be litigation or the amounts it would be called upon to fund in legal costs in the days ahead.

14. Since one of the arguments for the Commissioner was that the outlays on legal expenses bore the character of expenses of capital or of a capital nature, it is appropriate to consider just what the "profit-making structure" operating by App-co was. It had a small issued capital. It used the services of Dennis and of other workers. It used the premises and equipment made available to it by the partnership. It had the benefit of contracts allowed to pass to it by the Receiver-Manager. It had the goodwill of customers. By using those resources it had a capacity to earn money. But it had no such interest in any of those resources that the interest could fairly and accurately be described as capital, or of a capital nature. Its "profit-making" structure was as insignificant as it was insecure and impermanent.

15. All issues between the Bank and the Receiver-Manager on the one hand and the partnership, Land-co, Frame-co and App-co were resolved in a comprehensive settlement of April 1983. It was not suggested that the terms of the settlement are of any present relevance.

16. In considering a crucial issue as to whether the expenses in question were incurred on capital or revenue account one must be guided by the decisions of the Courts, particularly the decisions of the High Court of Australia. For that purpose there is no better starting point than a consideration of a line of authority commencing with the decision of the Court in Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v F.C.T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 and in particular, the analysis there made by Sir Owen Dixon (at page 363) in which he identified the three matters to be considered as:

(a) The character of the advantage sought, and in this its

lasting qualities may play a part,

(b) The manner in which it is to be used, relied upon or

enjoyed, and in this and under the former head

recurrence may play its part, and

(c) The means adopted to obtain it; that is, by providing

a periodical reward or outlay, coverage, use or

enjoyment for periods commensurate with the payment or

by making a final provision or payment so as to secure

future use or enjoyment.

17. The distinction there expressed was between "the profit yielding subject" and the expenses of operating it. The distinction has been applied in many cases since. Of those favouring the argument for the Commissioner it should suffice to so identify the decision in Sun Newspapers (the deductibility of a substantial outlay to prevent the publication of a rival newspaper) and John Fairfax & Sons Pty Limited v F.C.T. [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 (the legal expenses incurred by one shareholder in the course of competition with other shareholders for control of a company) and P.D.L. Marketing Pty Limited v F.C.T. 85 ATC 4416 (the cost of litigation as to the efficacy of an agreement conferring exclusive rights on the appellant and on which the whole business of the appellant depended. On the other hand, favouring the argument for the taxpayer there are such decisions as those of the High Court of Australia in F.C.T. v Snowden and Willson Pty Limited [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 (expenses incurred in resisting attacks upon its methods of business made in the Parliament and before a Royal Commission) and the decision of the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in BP Australia Limited v F.C.T. [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386 (expenses incurred by way of payment to retailers with a view to enlarging the market of the supplier).

18. But decisions which more nearly touch the circumstances of the present Applicant are the decisions of a full bench of the High Court of Australia in F.C.T. v South Australian Batterymakers Pty Limited [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 and the full bench of the Federal Court of Australia in Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v F.C.T. 80 ATC 4542. Both decisions involved circumstances in which the outlays served the immediate purposes of the taxpayer and also served the interests of others. In Batterymakers the payment of rent served the immediate purpose of the tenant in ensuring its continued right to possession under a lease. A related company was to have an option to purchase the freehold upon most favourable terms. While recognising that it could be appropriate to apportion an outgoing and to treat part of it as being of a capital nature (Gibbs A.C.J. at page 654). The majority nonetheless held that the prospect of ultimate benefit to another was not to deny the tenant a deduction for his full outgoings. As Gibbs A.C.J. said (at page 655-656):-

"It is clear that by making the payments (of rent) the

taxpayer itself did not acquire any interest in the land or

buildings except that of a lessee. The only binding

arrangement between the taxpayer and the Trust that

embodied in the lease, under which the taxpayer had the

rights and interests of a lessee and nothing more. The

taxpayer was not granted any option to purchase, and it had

no right to enforce the option granted to Property Options.

It was argued on behalf of the Commissioner that if a group

of companies of which the taxpayer was a member derived a

benefit from the making of the payments, part of that

benefit must accrue to the taxpayer. In my opinion, that

statement cannot be accepted; it is true only in the most

general sense - in the same way that it may be said that a

man benefits if his children or his neighbours prosper. Of

course, it cannot be denied that the circumstances may be

such that one company may benefit from the fact that its

subsidiary receives an advantage; .......... However,

in the present case there is no evidence upon which it

could be found that the taxpayer could benefit from the

exercise of the option."

His Honour went on to say (at page 656):

"I have said that in deciding whether outgoings made by a

taxpayer are of a revenue or of a capital nature, it is

necessary to consider the "character of the advantage

sought". In my opinion, in principle, that must mean the

character of the advantage sought by the taxpayer for

himself by making the outgoings. Of course, as I have

already indicated, a taxpayer may derive an advantage if

someone else, such as a subsidiary, acquires an asset. But

the fact that someone else incidentally derives an

advantage of a capital kind in which the taxpayer does not

share is not enough to give the outgoings the character of

capital."

19. Then in the Magna Alloys decision the question was whether an employer company could have deductions for legal expenses it voluntarily incurred in defending charges brought against some of its directors and agents. The Court unanimously allowed the deductions claimed. It is significant that his Honour Brennan J. said (at page 4554):

"Nor was the expenditure an outgoing of a capital nature.

The capital of the business was in no way increased by the

expenditure incurred. True it is that the expenditure

protected the reputation and goodwill of Magna's business,

but the attack which was made arose out of the day to day

selling activities of the business and it was the business

purpose of indicating the methods by which it was conducted

that brings the expenditure within Section 51(1). Although

goodwill is a capital asset of a business it is frequently

earned and maintained by the daily activities of those

engaged in the business. The valuable if intangible asset

of goodwill frequently grows out of activities the cost of

which is a charge on revenue account (See Sun Newspapers,

supra, at pages 360, 361). Expenditure incurred in

attempting to vindicate the business methods of the

taxpayer, overcoming the obstacle to its trading, which had

been raised by the prosecutions against property to be

regarded as a cost of revenue account."

20. Neither decision relates to circumstance so similar to the present case as to of necessity dictate the answer to the present problem. However, applying the principles of those decisions to the present case, in my view, the answer is that the expenses here in question were incurred on revenue account. None of the expenses incurred enlarged "the profit-making structure" available to App-co. What it was doing in incurring the expenses was ensuring no long term or enduring benefit, but merely its capacity to continue its operations day to day; operations made possible by the goodwill and "generosity" of Dennis and of the partnership. Its expenditure gained App-co no more than that.

21. The complexities of the litigation were considerable, prompting Master Allen of the Supreme Court of New South Wales to say, in the course of considering a claim by the solicitor to a lien:

"It is obvious that the accounting between what I may call

the (Dennis) group and the firm is complex. It follows

that the ambit of any genuine lien which can be asserted

against (App-co) is a matter almost of conjecture. Despite

some hours of evidence given before me, the picture remains

one which is shrouded in mystery ........ It also clearly

is desirable, bearing in mind the complex financial

transactions which can occur where a firm acts in a large

number of matters of what may be called group companies,

that the accounting can become complex and it can be

difficult for anyone, other than the solicitor himself, to

trace what has happened to the payments which have been

made."

22. A second indication of that complexity lies in the circumstance that there were in all no less than eighteen suits or actions and in one of them brought at the instance of a third party not previously referred to in these reasons for decision, there were five Defendants:- Frame-co; the partnership; the Receiver-Manager; App-co; and another third party not previously referred to. The last Defendant became cross-claimant against the first, second, third and fourth Defendants as cross- Defendants and against the Plaintiff as fifth cross-Defendant. Frame-co became second cross-claimant, claiming against the partnership and App-co. The partnership became third cross-claimant seeking relief against Frame-co, the Receiver-Manager and the fifth Defendant in its capacity as third cross-Defendant to the third cross-claim and agains t the Plaintiff as fourth cross-Defendant to the third cross-claim.

23. Fortunately the issues to be resolved are less complex. The claims relating to legal expenses can be identified in four parts:

(a) Possession proceedings;

(b) Equipment proceedings;

(c) Injunction proceedings; and

(d) Crane repair proceedings

Upon the hearing, the only matters remaining in dispute were the first two claims. I accept the evidence of Dennis that, where possible, App-co only retained counsel on a basis that counsel would represent all parties in common interest with Dennis. In consequence there were no significant increases in cost by reason of multiple representation. Other claims which had been in issue were resolved by agreement.

POSSESSION PROCEEDINGS

24. There were two facets to the possession proceedings. Land-co and Frame-co sought declarations against the bank and the Receiver-Manager that the appointment by the bank of the Receiver-Manager was invalid. In other proceedings, the bank brought a claim against Land-co (Receivers and Managers appointed) claiming judgment for possession of the land then occupied and being used by App-co. App-co and the partners came to be joined as Defendants. App-co met all the costs of those proceedings. In doing so it preserved from day to day its occupation of the site but it did not thereby ensure its on-going occupation of the site because it was only in possession at the will of the partnership. It follows that success in the litigation for the companies claiming possession did not ensure any long term benefit to App-co other than perhaps the continuing good-will of Dennis. I consider that its actions in effecting payment were wholly voluntary acts. It was no part of any tenancy agreement that it should do so. But the payments were acts directed as a matter of commercial expediency to the maintenance of the day to day operations of its business. It happened that ultimately the claim of the bank was deferred to the interest of the partnership as lessee of the premises but that circumstance does not in my view alter the character of the actions of App-co in incurring the expenses it did.

EQUIPMENT PROCEEDINGS

25. Tri-co, one of a number of companies which carried on business by hiring scaffolding and other construction equipment, made a claim upon Frame-co (receiver and manager appointed); the partnership; the Receiver-Manager; App-co; and another company (Quin-co) which also carried on business hiring scaffolding and other construction equipment. Tri-co made claim for delivery up to it by all concerned of all the scaffolding equipment referred to in its Statement of Claim and further claimed:

"In the event that none, or not all, of the said property

of the Plaintiff can be separately identified:

(a) a declaration that all equipment of the description

particularised in paragraph 5 over the possession or

control of the first, second, third, and fourth

Defendants or any of them which is identical and

cannot be identified separately as the property of the

Plaintiff and the property of the first, second or

fifth Defendants is held by the Plaintiff and the

first, second or fifth Defendants, or such of them as

establish its claim to ownership of such property, as

tenants in common in such proportions respectively as

the unidentifiable property of each such party bears

to the aggregate of the unidentifiable property of all

such parties.

(b) .....

(c) ....."

Among the allegations was an allegation that

"after the appointment of the receiver and manager to the

first Defendant, the fourth Defendant (App-co) without the

consent of the Plaintiff:

(a) Took possession of some or all of the said equipment;

mixed or caused to be mixed the said equipment with

other equipment of the first, second or fifth

Defendants which was identical to or indistinguishable

from the said equipment of the Plaintiff.

(b) ........."

The term "confusion" is a term of the law particularly apt to describe the situation. (c.f. Halsbury "Laws of England" 4th Edition, Vol. 35, p 635, pa 1139.)

26. App-co contested those proceedings and bore the expenses of the partnership in doing so. I am satisfied that what was in issue for App-co in those proceedings was its claim to continuing possession of plant and equipment essential to the day to day operations of its business. The strength of its claim may have been founded in establishing the entitlement of the partnership to hold possession as against the Plaintiff against others not in common interest but that was not what was at stake for App-co. In my view, the expenses incurred by App-co in resisting the claim of the Plaintiff were just as much incurred by it in the course of carrying on its business as would have been the costs incurred by the Plaintiff, Tri-co, and by Quin-co, such costs being incurred on revenue account by those companies in the course of carrying on their own businesses.

INJUNCTION PROCEEDINGS AND CRANE REPAIR PROCEEDINGS

27. These claims were initially disallowed but, upon the hearing, it was acknowledged for the Commissioner that App-co was entitled to the deductions it had sought.

QUANTUM

28. I now proceed to address the problems as to quantum. In doing so I am fully conscious of the aptness of the comments previously quoted as used by Master Allen. Neither the Applicant nor the Tribunal had the advantage of the assistance which Master Allen recognised could only be given by the solicitor concerned within the firm.

29. Faced with that problem, a young solicitor with accounting qualifications proceeded to conduct an analysis of all the financial records available to him relating to dealings between App-co and the firm. I am satisfied that he discharged that undertaking with considerable skill but he was not able to effect a complete reconciliation between the figures which had been claimed by App-co in its returns of income (R) and his own calculations (S). 30. But the fact remains that over the whole period the solicitor has only been able to identify outgoings less than the total claimed. In the circumstances I find that to that extent the Applicant must fail. Having reached that conclusion I have considered whether the shortfall in the solicitor's explanation for the 1981 year ($2,172) should be allowed to be treated as explained by some unknown factors in one of the years following but, on the whole, I am not persuaded that that would be an appropriate course to follow. For those reasons I have concluded that in relation to the "possession proceedings" and the "equipment proceedings" the deduction to be allowed in aggregate is $30,778 for the year of income ended 30 June 1981; $69,387 for the year of income ended 30 June 1982; and $61,681 for the year of income ended 30 June 1983.

31. On taking into account claims for superannuation contributions $4,000 for the year of income ended 30 June 1981 and a deduction for bad debts of $25,939 for the year of income ended 30 June 1983, now conceded by the Commissioner the result will be as follows:

(a) For the year of income ended 30 June 1981 taxable income

assessed at $36,845 will be reduced by $34,778 ($30,778

+ $4,000) to $2,067. The assessment of Division 7 tax

shall be amended accordingly;

(b) For the year of income ended 30 June 1982 the taxable

income assessed at $58,749 will be reduced by allowing a

deduction for legal expenses in the sum of $73,626

($69,387 + $2,849 + $1,390). That being so, the

assessment will be wholly discharged and the assessment

of Division 7 tax set aside. A carry forward loss of

$14,877 will be available to be brought to account in

the following year; and

(c) For the year of income ended 30 June 1983 taxable income

assessed at $97,968 will be reduced by $102,151 (legal

expenses $61,681; bad debt $25,939; and carried forward

loss $14,877). That being so the assessments of income

tax and of Division 7 tax will be wholly discharged.
