Commissioner of Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 41 FLR 385 (12 November 1979)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION v. BAYLEY [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 41 FLR 385

Employment

COURT

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

GENERAL DIVISION

Smithers(1), Keely(2) and Lockhart(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Employment - Commonwealth statutory authority - Cessation - Offer of alternative employment - Public service - Superannuation - "Eligible employee" - "Permanent employee" - Deemed involuntary retirement - Superannuation entitlement - Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.), ss. 3, 58 (3), 62 (2), 80 - Public Service Act 1922 (Cth.), s. 81ZN (2) - Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act In 1970 the respondent commenced employment with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads ("the Bureau"), a body corporate with power, inter alia, to engage employees. Employees of the Bureau were not officers for the purposes of the Public Service Act 1922 (Cth.). To effect an amalgamation of the Bureau and another Commonwealth instrumentality the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 was passed, by which Act, on its coming into operation on 30th June, 1977, the property of the Bureau vested in the Commonwealth which also assumed its liabilities.

By the Public Service Act Amendment Act 1975 (Cth.) ("the 1975 Act") the Public Service Act was amended and pursuant to a declaration of the Governor-General duly published the respondent came within a "declared class of persons" to whom Divn 9F of the Public Service Act applied. Division 9F provided a statutory arrangement whereby the Public Service Board might invite a person who was within a "declared class of persons" to join the public service, the invitee might elect to accept the invitation, and the Board was then empowered to employ such a person within the public service. On 24th May, 1977, the acting secretary of the Public Service Board wrote to the respondent setting out the details of the amalgamation and offering to him a position in the Commonwealth Public Service in the Department of Transport at the equivalent level to that which he then occupied "with effect from the day immediately following the day on which you cease duty with the (Bureau)". The offer had to be accepted in writing within a specified period expiring before the cessation of the employment with the Bureau. The respondent did not accept this offer of alternative employment.

When the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") came into operation on 1st July, 1976, the respondent became an "eligible employee" within s. 3: he was also a "permanent employee". The appellant decided that the respondent should receive only the return of his superannuation contributions, pursuant to s.80 of the Act. On reconsideration pursuant to s. 154 the decision was confirmed. By a majority the Administrative Appeals Tribunal set aside the appellant's decision and decided that the respondent was entitled to three and one half times his accumulated contributions by virtue of s. 62 (2) of the Act.

On appeal from the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal,

Held: (1) Per Smithers and Lockhart JJ. - Where an employee's employment is terminated by operation of an Act of Parliament, so that the employer/employee relationship is brought to an end, he is not "retrenched" within the meaning of s. 58 (3) (b) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

Preference of Employment case (1977), L B Co.'s Indus. Arb. Serv., C urrent Review, p. 218, referred to.

(2) Per curiam - Section 8 of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 should not be construed as applying to service contracts between the Bureau and its staff as such a construction would: (i) conflict with Divn 9F of the Public Service Act; (ii) result in employees of the Commonwealth being employed in a government department yet, in important respects, standing outside the Public Service Act; (iii) deny each individual employee the right to decide whether or not he wished to be employed by the Commonwealth Public Service.

(3) (Applying the construction of s. 8 of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 referred to in (2) above) the respondent's position or office had ceased to exist within the meaning of s. 58 (3)(d) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

(4) The respondent ceased to be a "permanent employee" and therefore an "eligible employee" within s. 3 of the Superannuation Act 1976 by reason of the abolition of his position by the operation of statute. His failure to exercise his statutory right to elect to be transferred to the Commonwealth Public Service was not the cause of his ceasing to be such an employee: he had no obligation to exercise this right.

Appeal dismissed.

Per Smithers J. - There is no room for the suggestion that a state of being an "eligible employee" might have been preserved by the acceptance of some particular employment offered by the Commonwealth. Section 58 (3) takes effect when two things happen, namely, when someone ceases to be an eligible employee because of the termination of the relevant employment and when the termination of that employment resulted from one of the circumstances specified in pars. (a) to (e) thereof.

HEARING

1977 (Cth.). Melbourne, 1979, August 1-2; November 12. 12:11:1979

APPEAL.

Appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (constituted by Messrs. Todd, Smith and Hutchinson) delivered 11th May, 1979, setting aside the decision of the appellant made on 13th January, 1978, (and subsequently confirmed upon reconsideration) that the benefit payable to the respondent out of the superannuation fund on his ceasing employment with the Bureau on 20th June, 1977, was an amount equal to his accumulated contributions. The Tribunal decided that the respondent was deemed to have retired involuntarily so that the benefit payable to him was equal to three and a half times his accumulated contributions.

J.D. Phillips Q.C. and D.G. Henshall, for the applicant.

W.E. Patterson Q.C. and M.C. Kimm, for the respondent.

Cur. adv. vult.

Solicitor for the applicant: B. J. O'Donovan (Commonwealth Crown Solicitor).

Solicitors for the respondent: Slater & Gordon.

D. LEVIN

DECISION

November 12.

The following written judgments were delivered.

SMITHERS J. In relation to this appeal I have had the advantage of reading
the judgment of my brother Lockhart and would express my agreement therein. I also make the following observations. (at p387)

2. The solution of the problem raised in this appeal depends on the construction of s. 58 (3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) (the Act). That subsection appears in Pt V of the Act which is headed "Eligible Employees Benefits" and more particularly in Divn 2 thereof which is headed "Early Retirement Benefit". Section 58 so far as immediately relevant is in the following terms: (at p387)

3. "(1) For the purposes of this Act, a person shall be deemed to have ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of early retirement

if the person is deemed by sub-section (2) to have retired voluntarily, or by sub-section (3) to have retired involuntarily.

(3) Where, before a person who is an eligible employee attains the age of 60 years, he ceases to be an eligible employee by reason that -

(b) he is retrenched . . .

(d) except in the case of a temporary employee - his position or office
ceases to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise; or . . . .

the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have retired involuntarily." (at p387)

4. Section 3 (1) of the Act provides a definition of eligible employee and so far as immediately relevant it is in the following terms:

'Eligible employee' means -

(b) a person who is a permanent employee."

By the same subsection "permanent employee" is defined as follows, namely:

'Permanent employee' means -

(a) a person who is an officer for the purposes of the Public Service Act;
and

(b) any other person employed by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority in a permanent capacity", and s. 3 (3) deems persons to be eligible employees in certain circumstances, namely: "Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and, immediately after so ceasing, again becomes an eligible employee, he shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed not to have so ceased." (at p387)

5. Other sections which may usefully be referred to include s. 62 (2) which on the Administrative Appeal Tribunal's construction of s. 58 (3) would have enabled the defendant to elect for an amount equal to three and one half times the amount of his accumulated basic contributions and s. 80 pursuant to which on the plaintiff's construction the defendant's only entitlement arises, namely, an entitlement limited to return of his accumulated contributions. (at p387)

6. In early 1977 the Public Service Board informed the defendant (respondent) who was then a permanent employee with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (the Bureau) that a decision had been made by the Government that 29th June, 1977, would be the last day of the Bureau's operations as a statutory authority and that his employment therewith would terminate on that day. On 24th May, 1977, the Board made an offer, to expire on 28th June, 1977, to the respondent, of an appointment to a position in the public service to take effect "from the day immediately following the day on which you cease duty with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads". (at p388)

7. At that stage it was the intention of the Government to terminate the existence of the Bureau pursuant to the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 (Cth.) which on 20th June, 1977, was proclaimed to come into operation on 30th June, 1977. It is clear that from 24th May, 1977, until 28th June, 1977, the respondent had the option of taking up a position as a permanent employee of the Commonwealth in the public service which would commence on the day after his existing employment with the Bureau was terminated. (at p388)

8. By virtue of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act the Bureau ceased to exist on 29th June, 1977. Had the respondent taken up the offer of new employment he would, as a permanent employee of the Commonwealth, have been an eligible employee within the meaning of that expression in the Act. It is not clear that if he had taken up the new employment there would not have been a period of time, perhaps of minimum length, between the termination of his employment with the Bureau and the commencement of his employment with the Commonwealth during which he would not have been an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act. It was clear however, that if the respondent rejected the offer of new employment and the Government adhered to its expressed intention to terminate the existence of the Bureau on 29th June the respondent's position with the Bureau would be abolished on that day and that he would thereupon cease to be an eligible employee. (at p388)

9. The respondent rejected the offer of the new appointment. On and prior to 29th June he was an eligible employee by virtue of his permanent employment with the Bureau and as a result of the termination of the existence of the Bureau on 29th June he ceased to be a permanent employee within the meaning of s. 3 (1) of the Act and thereby ceased to be an eligible employee. (at p388)

10. It is said that although his position was abolished and that he thereby ceased to be an eligible employee on 29th June, it having been within his power to continue in relevant permanent employment and thereby continue to be an eligible employee within the meaning of the Act, he ceased to be an eligible employee on 29th June by reason that he had elected to reject permanent employment in the employment which he had been offered and not by reason of the abolition of his position with the Bureau, or certainly not only by reason of such abolition. It is said therefore that when he ceased to be an eligible employee on 29th June such cessation was not, in the words of s. 58 (3) of the Act, by reason that his position ceased to exist, but that such cessation occurred by reason of his refusal to take up the new appointment or at least that his refusal was a reason for such cessation. It is said that in either case it cannot be said that he ceased to be an eligible employee by reason that his position was abolished within the meaning of s. 58 (3) of the Act. (at p389)

11. It is certainly true, in a sense, that the respondent ceased to be an eligible employee on 29th June, 1977, by reason of his rejection of the offer of new employment. It is arguable that had he accepted that employment there would have been no gap between the termination of his employment with the Bureau and the commencement of his employment with the Commonwealth (see Prowse v. McIntyre [1961] HCA 79; (1961) 111 CLR 264 and Robertson v. Federal Commissioner for Taxation [1952] HCA 71; (1952) 86 CLR 463 ). It was also contended in the alternative that if there would have been any such gap then s. 3 (3) of the Act was applicable and thereby, notwithstanding such gap, the respondent would by statutory fiction be deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee between the termination of his employment with the Bureau and the commencement of his employment with the Commonwealth. (at p389)

12. But it is a question whether the possible preservation of the respondent's status as an eligible person by his acceptance of the new appointment is really relevant to the problem which arises under s. 58 (3) of the Act. (at p389)

13. Looking at the provisions of the Act in general the basis of the scheme established is seen to be that the duties and rights of a participant in the scheme depend upon the existence or the cessation of his status as an eligible employee. But existence of that status depends upon the existence of relevant employment. It is an eligible employee who must contribute to the fund (see s. 45) and entitlements arise when the participant ceases to be an eligible person (see Pt V). In other words contributions must continue to be paid by the participant in the scheme while he is in relevant employment and he becomes entitled to benefits when relevant employment ceases. It is the effect of ss. 55,58,66 and 80 that once a person finally ceases to be an eligible employee then some entitlement accrues to him. The reason or cause of the termination of the relevant employment which brings the status of being an eligible employee to an end is only important for the purpose of measuring the extent of the participant's entitlement. (at p390)

14. In each of the sections mentioned above an entitlement arises upon cessation of the status of being an eligible employee but it varies according to the manner in which the employment upon which it depended came to an end whether by death, retirement on request, resignation, invalidity or otherwise. Those provisions of these sections which refer to the manner in which eligibility was lost are actually referring to the manner in which the relevant employment was lost. One does not suffer retirement from a state of being an eligible person but from employment. One does not resign from a state of being an eligible person but from employment, per Shylock:

"You take my house when you do take the prop that doth

sustain my house." (at p390)

15. The use in those sections of the expression "ceases to be an eligible employee" is but a convenient method of achieving in a few words what would otherwise require lengthy and complicated verbiage to cover the various kinds of employment which are comprehended within the meaning of "eligible employee" as defined in s. 3 of the Act, termination of any of which may give rise to an entitlement. (at p390)

16. It is the purpose of all these provisions to prescribe the criteria according to which the extent of the entitlement of a participant may be determined when he finally ceases to be an eligible employee. They look forward to that occasion and when it arrives the only relevant question is, what was the reason for the cessation? The answer is that it depends upon the way in which the employment upon which the existence of the status had theretofore depended came to an end. It is basically this consideration which underlines the following passage in the reasons for judgment of the majority of the members of the Tribunal, R. K. Todd and W. J. Smith: "The fact is that the applicant did cease to be an eligible employee, and that his position with the Bureau did cease to exist. It seems incontrovertible that the reason, as a matter of law, why he ceased to be an eligible employee was that the position the holding of which had given rise to the eligible employment had ceased to exist. No other cause of cessation of eligible employment was necessary. In other words, we think that the words 'by reason that' connote a causal link, in terms of legal result, between the cessation of eligible employment and the cessation of existence of the office." (at p390)

17. Reading s. 58 (3) in the light of the foregoing it seems clear that it is to be construed as providing that where, before a person who is an eligible employee attains the age of sixty years, he ceases to be an eligible employee by reason that the employment by virtue of which, while it lasted, he had been an eligible employee, has come to an end by reason of one of the circumstances set out in pars. (a) to (e) of the subsection the person shall for the purposes of the Act be deemed to have retired involuntarily. So construed there is no room for the suggestion that a state of being an eligible employee might have been preserved by the acceptance of some particular employment offered by the Commonwealth or that it is of any significance. Section 58 (3) takes effect when two things happen, namely, when someone ceases to be an eligible employee because of the termination of the relevant employment and when the termination of that employment resulted from one of the circumstances specified in pars. (a) to (e) thereof. (at p391)

18. It would be a matter for surprise that under the scheme an employee whose position is abolished by the Commonwealth should be deprived of the entitlement otherwise arising thereon by the simple expedient of the Commonwealth offering him a permanent position, at any level, at any place, on any terms and which he is under no obligation to accept. That this was appreciated by the appellant was reflected in the submission made both before the Tribunal and before this Court, namely that in some way the nature of the terms and conditions of the employment offered to and rejected by the respondent were relevant. It was urged that the terms and conditions of the employment actually offered were substantially the same as those of the respondent's employment with the Bureau and therefore reasonable for alternative employment. But if the section is to be construed as the appellant would suggest, the attractiveness or the reverse of the new employment would be quite irrelevant. The only necessary feature of the proffered alternative employment would be that it was permanent employment with the Commonwealth or an authorized authority. (at p391)

19. It was put that the section was to be construed by reference to there being discernible in the Act the concept of a Commonwealth "umbrella". The suggestion was that employment with an authorized statutory authority and with the public service of the Commonwealth were covered by the umbrella, so that as long as employment in some sphere under the umbrella was available the failure to take up that employment was a factor by reference to which the status of eligible employee would continue notwithstanding the involuntary loss of employment in a statutory authority and even although that loss occurred by that authority being abolished. There is no validity in this but it appears to have influenced those departmental officials who were required to assess the respondent's entitlements under the Act. Thus in the reasons for refusing a reconsideration of the Commissioner's decision dated 6th October, 1978, the statement appears that "the offer by the Public Service Board of employment in the Department of Transport with no change in status or duties amounts to a continuation of employment". Such a view ignores the essential character of the defendant's employment with the Bureau, namely, that employment with the Bureau was employment with an independent legal entity with its own separate legal personality. It ignores that the proposed employment was with a stranger to that employment. It ignores also that there were significant differences between the terms of the new employment and those of the old. The terms of the Public Service Board memorandum A are relevant, namely: "1. . . . 29 The Board has made offers of employment to all staff of the Bureau. These offers are on the basis that the positions to which the Bureau staff will be appointed are identical in terms of duties and classification to those occupied by the staff at present and those positions are located in Melbourne. 3. These positions have been established to bring staff into the public service. In the future a new organization amalgamating the two Bureaux will be established. It is possible that not all the existing positions in each Bureau will be recreated in the amalgamated organization. Normal re-deployment action will be undertaken and the possibility that some staff will be retrenched must therefore be acknowledged. 4. There is also the possibility that the staff will be asked to transfer to Canberra at some time in the future. Normal arrangements for such transfers will apply including twelve months' notice of the move. 5. The Board considers in those circumstances that the offers of employment under the Public Service Act constitute suitable alternative employment. Refusal to accept such an offer would prima facie amount to voluntary separation from Commonwealth Government employment in respect of which no redundancy benefits would be payable." (at p392)

20. The conclusion contained in the last sentence is not in accordance with the law of contract or with any of the provisions of any of the statutes relied upon. It proceeds on the erroneous basis that in some way there was an obligation on the appellant to accept the proffered employment. There was none. (at p392)

21. Having regard to the foregoing the correct view is that when s. 58 (3) refers to a person who ceases to be an eligible person by reason of the termination of his employment in circumstances specified in pars. (a) to (e) of that subsection the reference is to a person who has ceased to be an eligible person by reason of the employment by virtue of which he had theretofore been an eligible person has come to an end. It follows that the rejection of the offer of alternative employment in the public service after the termination of the respondent's employment with the Bureau is not a relevant consideration in the application to the respondent of the provisions of s.58(3). (at p393)

22. The appeal should therefore be dismissed with costs. (at p393)

KEELY J. The facts, correspondence and statutory provisions relevant to this appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal are set out in the reasons for judgment of Lockhart J. together with the three questions arising for consideration. (at p393)

2. Because of the opinion which I have formed as to the second and third of those questions I do not find it necessary to express any opinion as to the first question, namely, whether the defendant Mr. Bayley was "retrenched" within the meaning of s.58(3)(b) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) - a question which the Administrative Appeals Tribunal did not decide. (at p393)

3. The second question is whether the defendant's position or office had ceased "to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise" within the meaning of s.58(3)(d) of the Superannuation Act 1976. Mr. J.D. Phillips Q.C. on behalf of the plaintiff contended that the defendant was employed by the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads under an "engagement, arrangement or contract entered into by or on behalf of the Bureau as a party and in force immediately before the commencing day" of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads (Repeal) Act 1977 (Cth.) (the 1977 repealing Act). He contended that, by virtue of the provisions of s.8 of the 1977 repealing Act, the engagement, arrangement or contract was continued in force and had "effect, on and after the commencing day, as if . . . the Commonwealth were substituted for the Bureau as a party to the engagement, arrangement or contract". (at p393)

4. I am unable to accept the plaintiff's contention that the provisions of s.8 of the 1977 repealing Act have the effect in law that a person employed by the Bureau would have the Commonwealth substituted as his employer irrespective of his wishes as an employee. Section 8 should not be construed as having such an effect unless the words clearly require such a construction. The use of the words "engagement, arrangement or contract" in the section are not intended in my opinion to include the appointment of officers or the engagement of employees on terms and conditions of service determined by the Bureau with the approval of the Public Service Board under ss.18 and 19 of the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads Act 1964. Having rejected the argument based upon s.8 of the 1977 repealing Act, it follows that in my opinion the position or office of the defendant ceased to exist upon the commencement of the 1977 repealing Act. (at p393)

5. The third question is whether the defendant ceased "to be an eligible employee by reason that . . . his position or office (ceased) to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise" within the meaning of s.58(3)(d) of the Superannuation Act 1976. I agree with Lockhart J. that, although the words "by reason that" require the existence of a causal connexion between the position or office ceasing to exist and the person ceasing to be an eligible employee, it is not necessary that the fact of the position or office ceasing to exist be the sole or dominant cause of the person ceasing to be an eligible employee. (at p394)

6. I have come to the conclusion that the defendant ceased to be an eligible employee "by reason that" his position or office had ceased to exist. In arguing for the contrary view Mr. Phillips sought to rely upon the fact that the defendant had failed to accept the offer made by the Public Service Board, dated 24th May, 1979, that he might "elect to be appointed to an office in the Australian Public Service in the Department of Transport as a clerk class 9 with effect from the day immediately following the day on which (you) cease duty with the Commonwealth Bureau of Roads". He contended that if the defendant had accepted that offer he would never have ceased to be an eligible employee, relying on the principle that a day is to be considered an indivisible unit of time. (at p394)

7. I agree with the reasons given by Lockhart J. both in expressing the opinion that that principle has no application to the provisions of the Superannuation Act 1976 relating to this appeal and in expressing his opinion as to the purpose of s.3(3). (at p394)

8. I agree with the orders proposed by Lockhart J. (at p394)

LOCKHART J. (His Honour stated the facts set out in the headnote, and continued:) It is necessary to set out some of the provisions of the Act to understand the respondent's case that he is entitled to the larger benefit. (at p394)

2. Section 59 provides that where a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of early retirement, then, if the person does not make an election under s.62, he is entitled to certain retirement benefits specified in the section. (at p394)

3. Section 62(1) and (2) provides:

"62. (1) Where a person who ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of early retirement is deemed, by sub-section 58 (3), to have retired involuntarily, the person may, not later than 1 month after his last day of service, elect by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that, in lieu of pension and lump sum benefit being payable under section 59, a lump sum benefit be paid under sub-section (2) of this section and, where he had paid supplementary contributions, an additional lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section (3) of this section.

(2) Where a person makes an election under sub-section (1) of this section, the person is entitled, in lieu of pension and lump sum benefit to which, but for the election, the person would be entitled under section 59, to payment of a lump sum benefit of an amount equal to three and one half times the amount of his accumulated basic contributions." (at p395)

4. It is common ground that the respondent elected by notice in writing to the appellant that a lump-sum benefit be paid to him under sub-s.(2) of s.62. (at p395)

5. Section 58 provides, so far as relevant:

"58. (1) For the purposes of this Act, a person shall be deemed to have ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of early retirement if the person is deemed by sub-section (2) to have retired voluntarily, or by sub-section (3) to have retired involuntarily.

(3) Where, before a person who is an eligible employee attains the age of 60 years, he ceases to be an eligible employee by reason that -

(a) if his minimum retiring age is less than 60 years and he has attained that minimum retiring age - he is retired, otherwise than at his own request;

(b) he is retrenched, he is retired under section 20 of the Public Service Act or he is retired otherwise than under that Act on a ground similar to the ground on which persons may be retired under section 20 of the Public Service Act;

(c) in the case of the holder of a statutory office - the term of his appointment to that office has expired and, notwithstanding that he was eligible for re-appointment to that office and desired to be so re-appointed, he was not reappointed;

(d) except in the case of a temporary employee - his position or office ceases to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise; or

(e) he is retired under section 67 of the Public Service Act or his employment or appointment is terminated otherwise than under that Act on a ground similar to a ground specified in that section, the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have retired involuntarily." (at p395)

6. The questions that fall for determination are: 1. Whether the respondent was retrenched: s.58(3)(b);2. Whether the respondent's position or office ceased to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise: s.58(3)(d); and 3. If either 1 or 2 is answered in the affirmative, whether the respondent ceased to be an eligible employee by reason that he was retrenched or by reason that his position or office ceased to exist. (at p396)

7. I turn first to the question whether the respondent was retrenched. The word "retrench" has received little judicial consideration. In a decision of the Industrial Commission of New South Wales in the Preference of Employment case (1977) LB Co's Indus Arb Serv, Current Review, p 218 their Honours said: "Finally there are limitations in connection with retrenchment flowing from the connotation of that term. That was a matter in dispute. For the employers it was said that 'retrenchment' meant reducing the number of persons employed for a reason such as slackness of trade and that the word was not apt to include termination of employment by way of dismissal for misconduct or by way of retirement at retiring age or because of an employee becoming permanently incapacitated for work, but it was argued on behalf of the Labor Council that 'retrenchment' referred to termination of employment by an employer for any reason. In our opinion 'retrenchment' connotes cutting down or reducing for some cause pertaining to the employer's business or enterprise and is not a term apt to deal with termination of employment for reasons peculiar to an employee. We think that the meaning contended for by the employers is the correct one and that, accordingly the section limits the absolute preference which is to be given on termination of employment to termination by way of retrenchment in that sense" (1977) LB Co's Indus Arb Serv, Current Review, at p 230 . (at p396)

8. In the matter of Federated Miscellaneous Workers' Union v. Adelaide Milk Supply Co-operative Ltd. (1979) CCH Aust Indus Law Rev, vol 21, p 48 , a decision of the Commission in Appeal Session in South Australia, a comparison was made between the words "retrenchment" and "redundancy". (at p396)

9. The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary defines the verb "retrench", so far as relevant, as: "2. To cut off, 3. To cut down, reduce, diminish; esp. to curtail (one's expenses) by the exercise of economy 1625. To do away with (an item of expense) 1647." (at p396)

10. Webster's New World Dictionary of the American Language defines "retrench", in its transitive sense, as: "1. To cut down or reduce (esp. expenses); curtail. 2. To cut off or out." (at p396)

11. Whether "retrenchment" connotes termination of employment by an employer for any reason or for some reason pertaining to the employer's business and not for reasons peculiar to a particular employee, it is not necessary to decide. In my opinion, whatever else the word may mean, it is not apposite to describe the termination of the respondent's employment by an Act of Parliament, namely the 1977 Act, which brought to an end the existence of the employer and the relationship of employer and employee. (at p397)

12. Accordingly, in my opinion the respondent was not retrenched. (at p397)

13. I turn to the second question, whether the respondent's position or office ceased to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise, within the meaning of s. 58(3) (d). (at p397)

14. Before the Tribunal it was conceded by the appellant that the answer to this question should be in the affirmative. (at p397)

15. Mr. Phillips Q.C., who appeared with Mr. Henshall for the appellant before this Court, contended that the respondent's position and office did not cease to exist by reason of s. 8 of the 1977 Act which provides:

"8. An engagement, arrangement or contract entered into by or on behalf of the Bureau as a party and in force immediately before the commencing day continues in force notwithstanding the repeal of the repealed Act but that engagement, arrangement or contract has effect, on and after the commencing day, as if -

(a) the Commonwealth were substituted for the Bureau as a party to the engagement, arrangement or contract; and

(b) any reference in the engagement, arrangement or contract to the Bureau were (except in relation to matters that occurred before that day) a reference to the Commonwealth." (at p397)

16. Other relevant sections of that Act are ss. 5 and 10 which provide as follows:

"5. On the commencing day -

(a) any rights, property or assets that immediately before that day were
vested in the Bureau are, by force of this section, vested in the Commonwealth; and

(b) the Commonwealth becomes, by force of this section, liable to pay and discharge any debts, liabilities or obligations of the Bureau that existed immediately before that day."

"10. Where, immediately before the commencing day, proceedings to which the Bureau was a party were pending in any court, the Commonwealth is, by force of this section, substituted for the Bureau as a party to the proceedings." (at p397)

17. It was contended by Mr. Phillips that, as the respondent was engaged as an employee by the Bureau, that was an engagement, arrangement or contract entered into by the Bureau in force immediately before the commencing day and, therefore, was continued in force by s. 8 and had effect after the commencing day as if the Commonwealth were substituted for the Bureau as the respondent's employer. (at p397)

18. Division 9F of the 1975 Act governs the transfer of persons employed by an Australian authority (as that expression is defined by s. 81ZM (1)) to the public service. Those persons may elect to be appointed to the public service or to be employed therein in a specified department. Following such election the Public Service Board may appoint those persons to the public service or to be employed therein in the department specified in the notice of election. (at p398)

19. The Division is intended to facilitate the transfer of persons employed by a State, a State authority or a Commonwealth authority to the Commonwealth Public Service where the Commonwealth has decided to establish a new department or to reorganize or amalgamate existing departments or authorities. In each case, the person concerned has the right to decide for himself whether he becomes a member of the Commonwealth Public Service. Such membership is not thrust upon him against his will. Yet the contentions of the appellant would have it otherwise, for a person employed by the Bureau would automatically, by operation of s. 8 of the 1977 Act, cease to be an employee of the Bureau and have the Commonwealth as his new employer regardless of his wishes. (at p398)

20. This apparent conflict between s. 8 of the 1977 Act and Divn 9F in the 1975 Act is avoided if s. 8 is construed, as in my opinion it should be, as not including contracts of service or engagement between the Bureau and its employees or officers. (at p398)

21. A further reason in support of this construction of s. 8 is that the position of an officer of the public service generally is regulated by the terms of the Public Service Act (Cth.), not by contracts of service or engagement between the Commonwealth or the Public Service Board and the persons concerned. If s. 8 had the meaning and effect contended for by the appellant, the Commonwealth would find itself with former employees of the Bureau as its own employees, the relationship being governed by contract and the terms of employment would be those that previously applied between the Bureau and the employees. Those employees would be employed by the Commonwealth in a government department yet stand outside the Public Service Act in important aspects. (at p398)

22. In my opinion s. 8 is a savings provision intended to keep on foot engagements, arrangements or contracts between the Bureau and persons or corporations but does not apply to contracts of service between the Bureau and its staff. (at p398)

23. No other reasons were advanced by the appellant to support the contention that the position or office of the respondent did not cease to exist upon the commencement of the 1977 Act. (at p398)

24. Accordingly, in my opinion, the position or office of the respondent ceased to exist upon the commencement of the 1977 Act. It is not necessary to decide whether what ceased to exist was the position or the office of the respondent. It may have been either or both. (at p399)

25. I turn to the third question, whether the respondent ceased to be an eligible employee by reason that his position or office ceased to exist. (at p399)

26. The appellant contended that for a person to cease to be an eligible employee by reason that his position or office ceased to exist (s.58 (3) (d)) the former must be caused by the latter. It was contended that the abolition of the respondent's position or office was the occasion of his ceasing to be an eligible employee but not its cause. The cause was the failure of the respondent to accept the Commonwealth's offer of alternative employment. (at p399)

27. The respondent did not dispute that the abolition of his position or office must be the cause of his ceasing to be an eligible employee; but contended that it was in fact the cause. (at p399)

28. The phrase "by reason that" and the similar phrase "by reason of" have been considered judicially in various contexts, sometimes in conjunction with other words such as "or in pursuance of". Generally, the phrase has been construed so as to require a casual connexion between two or more things: see Izzard v. Universal Insurance Co. Ltd. (1937) AC 773 ; Baker v. Provident Accident and White Cross Insurance Co. Ltd. (1939) 2 All ER 690 ; Vandyke v. Fender especially per Lord Denning (1970) 2 QB 292, at p 306 and Nottingham v. Aldridge (1971) 2 QB 739 . (at p399)

29. Mr. Phillips referred to the decision of the High Court in Reseck v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 38; (1975) 133 CLR 45 and the decision of a Full Bench of this Court in McIntosh v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 25 ALR 557 where the meaning of the phrase "in consequence of" appearing in s. 26(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.) was discussed and held to include payments caused by the retirement of the taxpayer but not to be confined to such payments. (at p399)

30. Mr. Phillips distinguished those cases upon the ground that the phrase "in consequence of" requires less of a causal connexion than the phrase "by reason that". (at p399)

31. In my opinion the phrase where appearing in s. 58 (3) of the Act does require a causal connexion between the position or office ceasing to exist and the person ceasing to be an eligible employee. (at p399)

32. In my opinion it is not necessary that the occurrence of any one or more of the events specified in pars. (a) to (e) of subs. (3) of s. 58 (including the position or office of a person ceasing to exist under par. (d)) be the sole or dominant cause of a person's ceasing to be an eligible employee. It is sufficient if it be one of the causes: see Reseck's case per Gibbs J. (1975) 133 CLR, at p 51 and Vandyke v. Fender per Lord Denning M.R. (1970) 2 QB, at p 306 . (at p400)

33. Nothing turns on this question in the present case as it is the appellant's contention that there was no causal link whatever between the abolition of the respondent's position or office and his ceasing to be an eligible employee. (at p400)

34. It is common ground that the respondent ceased to be employed by the Bureau at the end of 29th June, 1977, and that his right to elect to be appointed to the Commonwealth Public Service expired on or before 28th June, 1977. The evidence is not clear as to precisely when the right to elect ceased. It may have been as early as 14th June, 1977, or as late as 28th June, 1977. Both parties conducted the appeal on the basis that nothing turned on the precise date, it being agreed that, whenever it was, it was before the date when the respondent ceased to be employed by the Bureau namely, 29th June, 1977. (at p400)

35. The appellant contended that the respondent was a "permanent employee" within the meaning of that expression in s. 3(1) of the Act in that he was employed by an approved authority (see the definition of that expression in s. 3 (1)), namely the Bureau, in a permanent capacity; and that he would have continued to be a permanent employee if he had elected to be appointed to the public service. It was his failure to exercise that right of election that caused him no longer to be a permanent employee. It mattered not, so the argument went, what period of time elapsed between the expiration of the right to elect and the abolition of his position or office. Mr. Phillips contended that the Act was one to provide superannuation for persons employed by the Commonwealth or approved authorities, that it assumed there would be, from time to time, transfers of personnel between departments of the Commonwealth and transfers from approved authorities to the Commonwealth and vice versa. The Act was not intended to enable a person to obtain three and one half times his accrued contributions merely because he chose not to move from an approved authority to the Commonwealth when he had the right to do so. (at p400)

36. The appellant contended that if the respondent had elected to be appointed to the public service, he would never have ceased to be a permanent employee because he would have been employed by the Bureau until the end of 29th June and thereafter by the Commonwealth. Mr. Phillips relied on the principle that a day is in general to be considered an indivisible unit of time and that "the beginning of a day is nothing but the end of the day before, and the end of the day is nothing but the beginning of the next": see Prowse v. McIntyre per Kitto J. (1961) 111 CLR, at p 274 . (at p400)

37. Mr. Patterson Q.C., who appeared with Mr. Kimm for the respondent, contended that the principle that a day is in general to be considered an indivisible unit of time has no application to the Act because of sub-s. (3) of s. 3 which provides: "(3) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and, immediately after so ceasing, again becomes an eligible employee, he shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed not to have so ceased." (at p401)

38. Mr. Patterson contended that sub-s. (3) was intended to apply to all cases where a person ceases to be an eligible employee, albeit momentarily, and immediately thereafter again becomes an eligible employee, and that the present case is but one such instance. (at p401)

39. Mr. Phillips contended that sub-s. (3) has no application to the present case, where the respondent never ceased to be a permanent employee, because of the principle as to a day being an indivisible unit of time. He contended that sub-s. (3) applies to cases where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and again becomes one, provided he does so "immediately" thereafter. He contended that "immediately" does not necessarily mean instanter; but rather has a meaning which may vary from case to case, sometimes allowing a gap in time of hours, days or weeks. In sub-s. (3) it has the meaning of "within a reasonably short space of time" in all the circumstances. In support of his contention, Mr. Phillips referred to the definition of the words "immediately" and "forthwith" in Stroud's Judicial Dictionary and Butterworth's Words and Phrases Legally Defined; Permanent Trustee Company of New South Wales Ltd. v. D'Apice [1968] HCA 29; (1968) 118 CLR 105 and Dorsman v. Nichol (1978) 20 ALR 231 . (at p401)

40. Mr. Phillips contended that sub-s. (3) is a deeming provision merely used for more abundant caution to put beyond doubt a particular construction that otherwise might be uncertain. He relied on St. Aubyn v. Attorney-General per Lord Radcliffe [1951] UKHL 3; (1952) AC 15, at p 53 and Hunter Douglas Australia Pty. Ltd. v. Perma Blinds per Windeyer J. [1970] HCA 63; (1970) 122 CLR 49, at pp 65-67 . (at p401)

41. A deeming provision may be included in an interpretation section in a statute for the purpose of extending the meaning of a defined term to something it would not mean in ordinary parlance: see St. Aubyn v. Attorney-General and Hunter Douglas Australia Pty. Ltd. v. Perma Blinds. In my opinion this is the purpose of sub-s. (3). It is intended to ensure that a person does not lose his status as an eligible employee merely because he ceases to be one and later becomes one again, provided the gap in time is not unreasonably large. It is designed to cover all cases where a person has a particular position or holds a particular office and ceases to do so, but "immediately" thereafter takes another position or office, in each case being a person who satisfies the statutory denotation of a "permanent employee". It matters not whether the interval of time is a second, a day, or a week provided the person again becomes an eligible employee "immediately" after ceasing to be one. The general rule that a day is an indivisible unit of time has no application to the provisions of the Act relating to this appeal. (at p402)

42. It follows that if the respondent had elected to be appointed to the public service, he would have ceased to be an eligible employee upon the abolition of his position or office and immediately thereafter again become an eligible employee upon his being appointed to the public service, and therefore deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee. (at p402)

43. The appellant's case assumes that the right of election conferred by Divn 9F of the 1975 Act was a right which, once exercised in accordance with that Division, inevitably leads to appointment to the public service. This involves reading the word "may" in s. 81ZQ as having a compulsory meaning. (at p402)

44. Whether the word "may" is used in a facultative or compulsory sense depends on the context in which it appears. It is no answer to the question to say that "may" means "shall". As Windeyer J. said in Finance Facilities Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation: "While Parliament uses the English language the word 'may' in a statute means may" [1971] HCA 12; (1971) 127 CLR 106, at p 134 . (at p402)

45. In Ward v. Williams [1955] HCA 4; (1955) 92 CLR 496 Dixon C.J., Webb, Fullagar, Kitto and Taylor JJ. said: "In considering the correctness of this interpretation it is necessary to bear steadily in mind that it is the real intention of the legislature that must be ascertained and that in ascertaining it you begin with the prima facie presumption that permissive or facultative expressions operate according to their ordinary natural meaning. 'The authorities clearly indicate that it lies on those who assert that the word "may" has a compulsory meaning to show, as a matter of construction of the Act, taken as a whole, that the word was intended to have such a meaning' per Cussen J. in Re Gleeson [1907] VicLawRp 71; (1907) VLR 368, at p 373 . 'The meaning of such words is the same, whether there is or is not a duty or obligation to use the power which they confer. They are potential, and never (in themselves) significant of any obligation. The question whether a Judge, or a public officer, to whom a power is given by such words, is bound to use it upon any particular occasion, or in any particular manner, must be solved aliunde, and, in general, it is to be solved from the context, from the particular provisions, or from the general scope and objects, of the enactment conferring the power' per Lord Selborne in Julius v. Bishop of Oxford (1880) LR 5 AC 214, at p 235 . One situation in which the conclusion is justified that a duty to exercise the power or authority falls upon the officer on whom it is conferred is described by Lord Cairns in his speech in the same case. His Lordship spoke of certain cases and said of them '(they) appear to decide nothing more than this: that where a power is deposited with a public officer for the purpose of being used for the benefit of persons who are specifically pointed out, and with regard to whom a definition is supplied by the Legislature of the conditions upon which they are entitled to call for its exercise, that power ought to be exercised, and the Court will require it to be exercised' (1880) LR 5 AC, at p 225 " (1955) 92 CLR, at pp 505-506 . (at p403)

46. In my opinion the exercise of a right of election is but a condition precedent to the appointment by the Public Service Board to the public service of a person formerly employed by an approved authority, such as the Bureau. If the Commonwealth intends that an approved authority is to cease to exist and its functions carried on by the Commonwealth, Divn 9F will ensure the smooth transfer of personnel from the authority to the public service; but only of those people who elect to be appointed to the public service. There may be any number of reasons why the Public Service Board should have the right to decide who will and who will not be accepted as members of the public service. The context in which the word "may" appears in s. 81ZQ, and 81ZR does not refute the prima facie presumption that it is to operate according to its ordinary and natural meaning as a facultative expression. (at p403)

47. It follows that, if the appellant's case were founded merely on the word "may" having a compulsory meaning, as the right of election is merely a right to be considered for appointment to the public service, the appellant would be constrained to argue that the cause of the respondent's ceasing to be an eligible employee was his failure to exercise a right which was but a condition precedent to his appointment by the Public Service Board to the public service. In my opinion this emphasizes the fallacy inherent in the appellant's contention that it was the respondent's failure to elect that caused the cesser of his status as an eligible employee. (at p403)

48. However the appellant proceeded to contend that, on the assumption that the word "may" where appearing in s. 81ZQ, was used in a facultative sense, the evidence established that the Public Service Board had undertaken to appoint to the public service, employees of the Bureau who duly exercised their right of election under Divn 9F. The appellant relied in particular upon the letter from the acting secretary of the Board of 24th May, 1977. The contrary was not contended. In my opinion the evidence establishes the correctness of this contention. So I have proceeded on the basis that, on the facts of this case, if the respondent had elected to be appointed to the public service, he would in fact have been appointed thereto by the Public Service Board. (at p404)

49. In my opinion, the respondent ceased to be a permanent employee, and therefore an eligible employee, because his employment ceased to exist on 29th June, 1977, with the abolition of his position or office. His non-exercise of the statutory right of election afforded by Divn 9F of the 1975 Act played no causative role whatever in his ceasing to be a permanent employee. He ceased to be a permanent employee when he was no longer employed by the Bureau. The abolition of the respondent's position or office was more than the occasion for his ceasing to be a permanent employee. The respondent wished to continue in the employ of the Bureau; but both the Bureau and the respondent's office and position were abolished by Act of Parliament. It was the operation of the 1977 Act which led to the respondent's ceasing to be a person employed by an approved authority. The fact that the respondent had a statutory right of election under Divn 9F of the 1975 Act and that he chose not to exercise it, cannot be the source of his ceasing to be an eligible employee. (at p404)

50. The definition of "permanent employee" looks to the person in his employment. It is intended to encompass a wide range of persons under Commonwealth aegis, whether officers of the public service or employees of the Commonwealth or of an approved authority, in a permanent capacity. Once a person ceases to answer the description of a permanent employee that is sufficient to remove him from the definition, subject to the provisions of sub-s. (3) of s. 3. (at p404)

51. For these reasons, in my opinion, the respondent is deemed to have retired involuntarily within the meaning of s. 58 (3) and deemed to have ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of early retirement under s. 58 (1) and therefore, having made an election under s. 62(1), to be entitled to payment of a lumpsum benefit of an amount equivalent to three and one half times the amount of his accumulated contributions. (at p404)

52. The appeal should be dismissed. (at p404)

ORDER

Appeal dismissed with costs.
