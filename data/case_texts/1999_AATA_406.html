Decision
The objection decisions under review are affirmed.

CATCHWORDS

Contributions by a bankrupt made under Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 - contributions made prior to the coming into force of Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act by agreement between the Applicant and the Trustee - whether deductible under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936; section 51(1)

Bankruptcy Act 1966; Division 4B

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975; section 37



Re Gillies; Ex parte Official Trustee in Bankruptcy [1993] FCA 289; (1993) 115 ALR 631

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Official Receiver (1956) 95 CLR 300

Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay’s) Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295

W Nevill & Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290

Case P101 82 ATC 520



TD93/10



REASONS FOR DECISION



10 June 1999
Mr J Block, Senior Member




  1. The objection decisions in this matter relate to the disallowance of objections dated 4 February 1998 against assessments numbered 236969/002 and 236968/002 issued on 22 December 1997 in respect of the years ended 30 June 1996 and 30 June 1997 (collectively the “relevant years” and each a “relevant year”).
  2. The Applicant was represented by Mr C. Lonergan of counsel instructed by Tesoriero Pegler, Solicitors; Mr Martin Dwyer, an officer of the Respondent, appeared on behalf of the Respondent.
  3. The Tribunal had before it the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and, in addition, exhibits as follows:
  • Exhibit A1 consists of a letter by the Applicant’s solicitors addressed to the Tribunal enclosing a bundle of correspondence and documents entitled “Exhibit referred to in paragraph 6 of the Applicant’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions”.

  • Exhibit A2 is a copy of a letter by the Trustee (as defined hereafter in these Reasons) to the Applicant dated 12 March 1992.

  • Exhibit A3 is a Notice of Objection to Discharge by the Trustee (as defined hereafter in these Reasons) dated 21 February 1997.

  • Exhibit A4 is a copy of a letter addressed by a bank to the Applicant’s solicitors dated 5 February 1997.

  1. The Applicant gave evidence on his own behalf and was not cross-examined by the Respondent. His evidence can conveniently be summarised as follows:

(a) The Applicant has been a chartered accountant for some 28 years;



(b) He was the senior partner in an accounting firm bearing his own name until 1 August 1991; he then left that firm in order to set up on his own practise as a private consultant;



(c) The Applicant was made bankrupt on 4 December 1991 (“bankruptcy date”) and a partner in a well-known firm of bankruptcy practitioners was appointed as his Trustee in Bankruptcy. That partner is referred to in these Reasons as the “Trustee”. The Applicant continued in practice as a consultant accountant after the bankruptcy date, and derived income in consequence;



(d) On 10 January 1992, the Applicant met with the Trustee in order to discuss matters relating to the bankruptcy administration. At that meeting, and in anticipation of amendments to the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (“Bankruptcy Act”), and in particular the introduction of Division 4B into the Bankruptcy Act, the Applicant and the Trustee agreed (having regard to enquiries by the Trustee as to the level of the Applicant’s income) that the Applicant would pay contributions of $1,000 per month. In the result, an amount of $4,000 in aggregate was paid thereafter up to the end of June 1992. In the years which followed, and including the relevant years, the Applicant paid contributions under section 139J of the Bankruptcy Act. There is a difference between the parties as to the aggregate amount of all contributions; the Applicant contends that the aggregate amount is $314,736.86 while the Respondent contends that the aggregate amount paid was $312,598.88; this latter amount appears in the Trustee’s Account of Receipts and Documents (T18 at page 72 of the T Documents). The difference between the two figures amounts to $2,137.98; the Tribunal is prepared to accept that the amount specified by the Applicant is correct.



  1. The break-up of the aggregate contributions as to each year in which contributions were paid is set out at page 12 of the T Documents as follows:
“1992 $4,000

1993 $21,044.85

1994 $38,297.38

1995 $36,981.41

1996 $36,259.53

1997 $178,153.69”


  1. The accounts filed by the Trustee indicate that he collected (inclusive of contributions by the Applicant) an aggregate amount of $538,527.41; of that amount, $125,124.07 was paid to the Respondent as the preferred creditor in the bankrupt estate, while the balance was utilised in respect of costs. Page 9 of the T Documents indicates that the claim of the Respondent against the Applicant arising prior to the bankruptcy debt was in respect of “group tax debts”. The Tribunal was not furnished with evidence as to the debts of the Applicant at the bankruptcy date and more particularly the amounts, the names of the creditors and when those debts were incurred. Nor indeed, having regard to clause 8 below, was any such evidence necessary for the purposes of this matter.
  2. Both Mr Lonergan and Mr Dwyer furnished the Tribunal with written submissions. In addition, Mr Lonergan furnished the Tribunal with an extract from the Explanatory Memorandum (the “Explanatory Memorandum”) in respect of the Bankruptcy Amendment Act 1991 which introduced (inter alia) Division 4B into the Bankruptcy Act.
  3. (a) Mr Lonergan accepted that in respect of the additional tax assessed by the Respondent, the relevant return was lodged late, and moreover that the additional tax was correctly calculated by the Respondent having regard to the amount assessed. It was agreed between the parties that the additional tax would be remitted if the Applicant were successful, but not if the objection decisions were affirmed.

(b) The representatives of the parties agreed also that in respect of all contributions, (and having regard to the fact that all contributions were made by the Applicant after the bankruptcy date) there was only one issue to be resolved by the Tribunal, and that is as to whether the contributions are deductible under s51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“Tax Act”). Section 51(1) is set out as a matter of convenience in this sub-clause (b) as follows:



“51(1) [Deductions for losses and outgoings] All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private, or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”


(c) Mr Lonergan contended (and Mr Dwyer accepted) that although $4,000 was paid by way of contributions prior to the coming into force of Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act, the payments which constituted that aggregate amount were stamped with the same character as those made after 1 July 1992, and pursuant to section 139J of the Bankruptcy Act.



(d) Notwithstanding the Applicant’s objections (T13 and T14 of the T Documents) and references therein to section 80 of the Tax Act, it was agreed on behalf of the parties that neither section 80 of the Tax Act, nor section 79E of the Tax Act are, in fact, relevant, in that the only relevant issue in respect of all contributions is that set out in sub-clause (b) of these Reasons, and being as to whether the contributions are entitled to deductibility under section 51(1) of the Tax Act.



(e) Nor for that matter, and having regard to the preceding sub-clauses, was there any argument or issue before the Tribunal as to whether the contributions were voluntary; see in this context TD93/10 and references to it in the T Documents.



  1. (a) Exhibit A1 consists, in the main, of correspondence between the Applicant and the Trustee as to the contributions which were paid by the Applicant. The bundle is large and it is not necessary for the Tribunal to deal with it in detail. Exhibit A1 indicates that:

(1) on various occasions, the Trustee sought information from the Applicant as to income derived and income expected to be derived;



(2) assessments, and including revised and amended assessments, were levied by the Trustee against the Applicant in respect of contributions payable under section 139W of the Bankruptcy Act;



(3) in respect of certain amounts, the Applicant did not make the required payments on their due dates;



(4) the Applicant sought time extensions within which to make payments;



(5) a letter from the Trustee dated 25 July 1994 indicates that the Trustee contended that the Applicant had underestimated his income “by some 66% to 82% in the previous 2 years”.



(b) Exhibit A2 records the arrangement entered into between the Applicant and the Trustee as to payments of $1,000 per month. The second paragraph indicates that the payment made on 6 March 1992 amounted to $800 only.



(c) Exhibits A3 and A4 relate to objections by the Trustee and a bank respectively against the Applicant’s early discharge from bankruptcy.



  1. In respect of the relevant years, the Applicant’s objections (T13 and T14 of the T Documents) indicate that the Applicant sought deductions for his contributions. In respect of the first relevant year, he claimed a deduction of $136,583.17 ($36,259.53 in respect of that year and the balance in respect of contributions paid in prior years, and including the year ending 30 June 1992). In respect of the second relevant year, the Applicant claimed a deduction of $314,736.86 ($178,153.69 in respect of that year and the balance in respect of contributions made in the prior years, and including the first relevant year).
  2. Mr Lonergan, both in his written submissions and in his closing argument, dealt at some length with Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act; it is sufficient in this regard to note that:

(a) prior to the introduction of Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act, a bankrupt was entitled to retain the benefit of his income. The Trustee could apply to court for an order compelling contributions. Clause 24.2 of the Explanatory Memorandum noted that an application to court was an expensive process and that frequently the Trustee would have no money available with which to commence court action. Clause 24.2 went on to note that there were no statutory criteria guiding the exercise of the discretion of the court in determining an appropriate level of contributions.

(b) Clause 25.1 of the Explanatory Memorandum makes it clear that Division 4B was introduced in order to require a bankrupt who derives income during bankruptcy to pay contributions towards his estate.



(c) Clause 25.2 sets out a number of definitions which are relevant for the purpose of assessing the extent of contributions.



(d) Mr Lonergan referred the Tribunal most specifically to clause 25.17 of the Explanatory Memorandum in relation to the proposed new section 139R. That section is now enshrined in the Bankruptcy Act and has the effect that unpaid contributions are recoverable by the Trustee against the bankrupt even after the bankrupt has been discharged from bankruptcy. Mr Lonergan emphasised in particular the fact that the contribution regime appears to be independent of the bankruptcy process itself.



  1. (a) Mr Lonergan cited Re Gillies; Ex parte Official Trustee in Bankruptcy [1993] FCA 289; (1993) 115 ALR 631 and referred specifically to certain passages from pages 633 and 635 of the judgment. The first passage cited (at page 633) refers to the position which applied before Division 4B was introduced and reads as follows:
“... in FCT v Official Receiver (1956) 95 CLR 300 where Williams J (Dixon J agreeing) said that the question whether the bankrupt’s income in excess of that necessary for his support vested in the official receiver was academic because “if the official receiver claims any part of these earnings he must apply to the Bankruptcy Court for an order”. In the absence of such an order, it was said, “the bankrupt is free to dispose of the whole of those earnings.”


(b) The second passage from Gillies cited by Mr Lonergan (at page 635) reads as follows:

“The amending Act inserted into the Bankruptcy Act 1966 a new Div 4B entitled “Contribution by bankrupt and recovery of property”. A statement of its objects is set out in s139J. They are:

(a) to require a bankrupt who derives income during the bankruptcy to pay contributions towards the bankrupt’s estate; and

(b) to enable the recovery of certain money and property for the benefit of the bankrupt’s estate.

The Division establishes a liability on the part of a person who is bankrupt to make a contribution out of the excess of his income over a threshold amount which is a function of the maximum basic rate of pension payable under the Social Security Act 1991 (Cth). The Division does not impose a liability to contribute in respect of the entire excess. In the explanatory memorandum relating to the Bill it was said that s131 had not been a particularly useful instrument for trustees because of the cost of court processes which it was necessary to invoke and the self-defeating nature of the custodial sanction for non-compliance with an order once made. Reference was made in the memorandum to the class of bankrupts who earned large incomes after bankruptcy and for all practical purposes were not required to make any repayment to creditors from that income. The memorandum went on to outline the statutory mechanism established by the amendments for imposing upon bankrupts a liability to contribute. There was no suggestion in the memorandum that the amendments would effect a vesting of after-acquired income in the trustee.”


(c) As to why so much time and attention was devoted to a detailed consideration of Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act is not clear to the Tribunal. Its ambit and scope is clear. Without the need for a court order, as was previously the case, a trustee in bankruptcy is entitled to require a bankrupt to make contributions to the estate. Those contributions are related (and indeed it is difficult to imagine how they could be calculated in any other fashion) to the level of the Applicant’s income.



  1. (a) Both parties referred, at some length, to the decision of the High Court in Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47.

(b) Reference was made in particular to the following passages:



(1) At page 56:

“For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words “incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income” mean in the course of gaining or producing such income. Their operation has been explained in cases decided under the provisions of the previous enactments: see particularly Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay’s) Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1935) [1935] HCA 81; 54 CLR 295] and W Nevill & Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1937) 56 CLR 300].”


(2) At page 57:

“In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income...”


(c) In his written submissions the Respondent contended, in clause 9, that:

“The Commissioner maintains that there is insufficient nexus between the expense of paying the contributions and the derivation of the applicant’s income as an accountant for the deduction to be allowable under subsection 51(1) as the expense was not incurred “in the course of gaining or producing” the assessable income. (Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavays) v FCT [1935] HCA 81; 54 CLR 295 per Latham CJ at page 303 and Dixon J at page 309).”


(d) Case P101 82 ATC 520 was not cited by either party. The Tribunal notes that it agrees with clause 18 of the decision of the President, Mr H.P. Stevens, reading as follows:

“The only remaining unconsidered deduction item is that relating to the bankruptcy payments of $5,040. Although I do not consider it really arises (para. 5) I will deal with it briefly. The facts in respect of this item are that when the taxpayer returned to Australia he was a bankrupt. A scheme was submitted to, and accepted by, a meeting of creditors whereby payments of $420 per month would be paid to the trustee in bankruptcy until 50c in the $ had been paid. This scheme was approved by the Court in December 1997 and the requisite payments were made in each of the years concerned. It is claimed that the amounts are allowable in that the taxpayer acted as a finance consultant in terms of How To Survive A Financial Crisis and Then Make More Money Than Ever (a book with that title being published in 1977) and that a financial adviser conducting a Financial Crises Centre can do so much more successfully if he is not an undischarged bankrupt. For the Commissioner it was said the amounts were not allowable as the necessary connection with the production of assessable income had not been shown whilst the amounts were, in any event, of a private nature. Whilst I agree with the taxpayer that it is easier to conduct business if one is not an undischarged bankrupt I do not accept that this makes the relevant outgoings allowable. The expenditure relates to a stigma (if that is correct) or, at least, a disability, attaching to the individual personally and the outgoing, in my view, is disqualified for deduction even if it could be said to satisfy the positive limb of sec. 51(1).”


  1. It was contended on behalf of the Applicant that the contributions were analogous to a fee which the Applicant was required to pay in order to allow him to derive assessable income. Clause 16 of Mr Lonergan’s written submissions reads as follows:
“In short the agreement entered into with the Trustee as and from January 1992 is analogous to a fee that the Applicant must make in order to allow him to continue to derive assessable income from his profession. It is a fee to allow him to continue to carry on his profession - that is to say to incidental and relevant to the gaining or producing assessable income, or alternatively, was most certainly necessarily incurred by the Applicant in carrying on a business for the purposes of gaining or producing such income. In no way could it be said to be a voluntary payment. The circumstances of the contributions paid to the Trustee have criteria that even raise the suggestion that it represents private expenditure of a capital nature.”


It is to be noted that the Applicant, in that clause 16, stated that the payment could not be characterised as voluntary. The final sentence of that paragraph 16 raised the possibility that the payments may have been private or of a capital nature.



  1. The Tribunal entirely agrees with Mr Dwyer’s submissions as set out in clause 13(c) of these Reasons. The contributions by the Applicant had no connection whatever with the business conducted by the Applicant, that being his practice as a consultant accountant. The income from his practice was relevant only insofar as it figured in the quantification of the contributions levied against him. The income from his practice, in other words, furnished the Applicant with the wherewithal which enabled him to pay contributions. As Mr Dwyer pointed out, a person may earn income which is used in part to make mortgage payments, and without which he would not be able to make those payments. But it is, of course, perfectly clear that the fact that the mortgage payments are made out of earned income does not have the effect that they are deductible. Put in another way, the Applicant paid the contributions to his bankrupt estate for the benefit of his pre-bankruptcy creditors for one reason and one reason only; he was required through the operation of Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act to pay contributions in accordance with the regime prescribed by that Division. He did not do so voluntarily; Exhibit A1 indicates that he did so precisely because the Trustee exercised his powers under that Division. In respect of the aggregate amount of $4,000 paid prior to the coming into force of the Division, Mr Lonergan contended (and the Tribunal agrees) that, as set out previously, the payments making up that amount were stamped with the same character. Had the agreement of 10 January 1992 not been made, the Trustee might presumably have compelled payment through a court order. But to contend (as did the Applicant) that the payments were incurred in the conduct of the Applicant’s business (within the second positive limb of section 51(1)), is untenable. It is not the creditors of that (on-going) business who would receive the benefit of those contributions, but rather the pre-bankruptcy creditors. It is unnecessary to refer to the numerous cases which followed Ronpibon. The fact that the required connection or nexus between the contributions and the income derivation is absent is, in the view of the Tribunal, altogether apparent.
  2. To the extent that it is or may be relevant, the Tribunal agrees with Mr Lonergan’s contention that the contributions could not be characterised as voluntary. Exhibit A1 indicates that the payments were exacted from the Applicant.
  3. The Tribunal has previously noted that the parties were agreed that neither section 79E nor section 80 of the Tax Act could apply, and accordingly it is unnecessary for the Tribunal to deal with the operation of those sections.
  4. In summary, and in respect of the only issue to be resolved by the Tribunal, the contributions made by the Applicant both under section 139J of the Bankruptcy Act, and in the period of a few months (up to 30 June 1992) which preceded the introduction of Division 4B of the Bankruptcy Act, cannot be characterised as falling within either positive limb of section 51(1) of the Tax Act. The deductions claimed were correctly disallowed; the Applicant has (as set out previously) agreed that the additional tax was correctly calculated, and should not be remitted if (as is the case) the Applicant is unsuccessful.
  5. Accordingly, the objection decisions under review are affirmed.

I certify that the 19 preceding paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of Mr J Block, Senior Member
