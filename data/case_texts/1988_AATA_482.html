ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - proportion of mortgage interest referable to home office/study used by teacher after ceasing to reside in the home

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s. 51(1)

Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 CLR 478

Handley v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 16; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 182

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 C LR 203

Swinford v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803

) No. NT86/10274

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

3 May, 1988 Mr. J.R. Gibson (Senior Member)

  1. In his return of income for the year ended 30 June 1984 the applicant claimed a deduction of $832, described as a proportion of rent for an office/study. In assessing the applicant to income tax on income derived during that year the Commissioner of Taxation disallowed the deduction. An objection to the assessment was lodged by the applicant and it was disallowed. The applicant has applied to the Tribunal for review of the decision of the Commissioner disallowing the objection.
  2. The office/study referred to is a room which the applicant fitted out as a study in a home formerly owned by the applicant and his wife and occupied by them and their two children. The applicant ceased to reside in the home just prior to the year of income in question and the marriage was later dissolved; however, his former wife and the children were still living in the home during the year ended 30 June 1984 and the applicant continued to use the room as a study. The applicant also continued to make payments due under a mortage on the home and it is a proportion of the mortgage interest paid during the year of income that was claimed as deductible. The question for the Tribunal is whether the amount claimed is deductible under S.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as having been incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of the applicant and not being of a capital, private or domestic nature.

3. The evidence at the hearing comprised the oral evidence of the applicant, the documents lodged with the Tribunal by the respondent and written evidence from the applicant's former wife, his superior at a school at which he taught and the mortgagee.

4. The applicant is a teacher by profession and has for some years been employed as a science teacher and as a science co-ordinator at a private High School in the metropolitan area and also, on three nights each week, as a science teacher at a College of Technical and Further Education. In addition to his income from those sources, the applicant has also derived small amounts by way of fees for coaching pupils outside school hours. He said that he would have preferred not to do coaching but had done so, at a relatively small fee, to oblige some people or to help some students in need of assistance in their work. On very few occasions he gave coaching at the home of the pupil but otherwise always at the home office/study.

5. The applicant gave evidence that he needed time after school hours and when he was not at the college for the purpose of preparing lessons for both school and college, for co-ordinating the science teaching at the school and for other tasks arising out of his teaching duties. He also spent a little time on coaching. He estimated that in term time he spent a total of about 21 hours per week out of school and college hours in this way in his office/study. Although he had a desk and book case available at the school, they were situated in a large room shared with various other teachers and were inadequate and inconvenient. He could only carry out the work satisfactorily at the home. He was expected by his employers to do the preparatory and other work outside school hours and at his home.

6. The applicant said that the family home had been purchased about one year before the year of income. It contained four bedrooms and it was because he had in mind having a proper office or study that a four bedroom home was acquired. He converted one of the bedrooms, which was at one end of the building and quiet, into a study and purchased and installed in it a large desk, a bookcase and cabinet, a filing cabinet, a book shelf for reference books, a cabinet for his microscope and two chairs. He used the room for the activities required by his employment and for some coaching periods. When he left the home in about May 1983 he had no premises to which he could take the contents of the study and he arranged with his wife that he should have exclusive use of the office/study. It was more important than before that he should be able to use the study because the home was near the school and his new accommodation was much further away from it. His evidence was that he did continue to use it to the same extent and for the same purposes during the year ended 30 June 1984 and for a year or so afterwards.

  1. The applicant's former wife confirmed in writing that, in 1983 and 1984, the room was used only as a study and contained furniture for a study. She also confirmed that the applicant had paid the mortgage installments for the period 1983/1984. There was also written evidence from the mortgagee that the amount of interest on the mortgage for the period 1983/1984 was $4,543.53. The principal of the school confirmed that the applicant was expected to do preparatory work out of school hours and at his home.
  2. The applicant impressed as a completely truthful witness and nothing was put to him in cross examination to suggest the contrary. His evidence is accepted by the Tribunal.
  3. The question whether an outgoing falls within s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as being incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or as necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income and is not of a capital, private or domestic nature, is to be determined by reference to the essential character of the outgoing. (Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 CLR 478 497, Williams, Kitto and Taylor JJ.) In Handley v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 16; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 182 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1980-91) 148 CLR 203, the Full Court of the High Court was concerned with the deductibility of outgoings, a proportion of mortgage interest in the former case, and a licence fee in the latter, relating to a room in the home of a barrister set aside and used for part of his income-earning activities. In both cases the Court, by majority (Mason, Murphy and Wilson JJ.; Stephen and Aickin JJ. dissenting) held that the outgoings were not allowable deductions under s. 51(1). In Forsyth's case, Wilson J. said that the application of s. 51(1) was a matter of fact and degree in each case (148 CLR 60 at 210, 213). He concluded that the outgoings in that case were not incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business; also that they were of a domestic or private nature. Wilson J. thought that an important consideration was the relationship of the study to the home as a whole; he thought that the room was indistinguishable from the other rooms in the home and intimately related in a physical sense to the life of the family. He also took into account that there was no compulsion for the taxpayer to work at home, that he did not require the service of a secretary there and that solicitors and clients hardly ever came there; the home was not the taxpayer's business premises [1981] HCA 15; (148 CLR 203 at 213). In Handley's case, Wilson J. said -

"In my opinion, the appeal cannot succeed. The room used as a study does not cease to be part of the taxpayer's home merely because as a matter of convenience he uses it for professional purposes for twenty hours per week during forty-five weeks of the year. It is true that in choosing for purchase in 1969 this particular residence as a home for himself and his family the taxpayer was influenced by the fact that there was in it a room which he considered to be suitable for use by him as a study. But it remained essentially part of his home."

In both cases Mason J. as he then was, was in general agreement with the reasons of Wilson J. In the course of his reasons in Handley's case Mason J. said -

"Here the study is a room in the taxpayer's home, not separate from it in any way, having no distinctive physical characteristics, readily capable of other use for family purposes, and in fact used for non-professional purposes from time to time. Moreover when used for professional work it is ordinarily used only for professional work that can be done at home, in the evenings and at weekends, e.g. working on briefs and preparing opinions. Expenditure related to the study is therefore referable to the home. The "essential character of the expenditure", to take up the expression used in Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra), is therefore that of a "capital, private or domestic nature".

In both cases, Murphy J. was of the opinion that the expenditure was of a domestic nature and therefore excepted from deductibility by the express provisions in s. 51(1) excepting expenditure of a capital, private or domestic nature. He thought that there might be some basis for the allowance of an outgoing of the kind under consideration in a case where part of the home used in gaining assessable income in a real sense the place of business.

10. The decisions in the cases just referred to and in some other cases decided by single Judges of the High Court and the Supreme Court were reviewed by David Hunt J. of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Swinford v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803. In that case it was held that rent relating to a room in the home of a script-writer which was in effect her place of business, was deductible under s. 51(1).

  1. Although the study used by the applicant was an integral part of the home, that home was not the residence of the applicant during the year of income in question and so it was not a case of him using the study because it was convenient for him to carry out the work at the place where he resided. He had no other suitable place to use for his purposes. On my assessment of his character, he probably would have paid the mortgage installments even if his wife had effectively prevented him using the study, but, if she had threatened to do so, he would have had a strong basis for negotiating an arrangement such as was in fact made. There can be no doubt on the applicant's evidence, which I accept, that when he was using the office he was engaged in the activities from which he derived his income. The use made of the study by the applicant could be said to be part and parcel of his employment and, to the extent that his coaching was a business, the study was the principal place at which it was conducted. Seen in the light of the particular circumstances referred to above, some part of the mortgage interest has, in my view, the essential character of an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and not of a private or domestic nature.
  2. For these reasons, the Tribunal finds that the applicant is entitled to a deduction in respect of portion of the mortgage interest. The amount claimed was described in the return as one-fifth of the rent of the home but the amount is less than one-fifth of the mortgage interest paid. No particulars were given of the basis of the apportionment, but presumably it was on the basis that the home contained four bedrooms and a group of other rooms. If so, that estimate of the proportion referable to the study was rather generous. It was not, however, challenged by ther respondent. It seems to me that in all the circumstances only the amount claimed of $842 should be allowed as a deduction.

13. The decision of the Tribunal is that the objection decision under review be varied to the extent that the taxable income as assessed be reduced by $832.
