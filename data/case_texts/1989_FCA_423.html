HEARING

PERTH

12:10:1989

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr A Templeman QC with Mr G Windsor

Solicitor for the Applicant: Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr R O'Connor

Solicitor for the Respondent: Kott Gunning

ORDER

The appeal be upheld.

The Tribunal's decision be set aside and the assessments confirmed.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

I have read and adopt the detailed account of the facts and the provisions of the Workers Compensation and Assistance Act 1981 (W.A.) ("the Compensation Act") set out in the reasons of Lee J. I shall mention only those features which seem to me of most significance. When, in November 1983, the respondent was found to be disabled by pneumoconiosis (for which disability he has been compensated) he had been in retirement for more than a year and had not worked as a fitter for more than 30 years; the exposure to asbestos occurred during his work as a fitter. When his entitlement to compensation began, he had ceased to have any intention to work for a living, whether as a fitter, a police inspector (the job he held when he retired) or in any other capacity.

2. The payments of compensation, taxation of which is in issue, were calculated by subtracting the minimum wage from the fitter's wage as at November 1983. That calculation had no practical connection with the appellant's circumstances because he would, pneumoconiosis or no, not have worked for a wage in November 1983.

3. It is therefore difficult accurately to characterise the payments of compensation made. Before attempting to do so, however, it seems to me convenient to attempt to analyse what was decided in the recent appellate decisions relevant to this topic of income tax on compensation payments and the like.

4. The first is Tinkler v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 88; (1979) 29 ALR 663. It was there decided that compensation payable under a statute at fortnightly intervals, on account of an injury suffered in a traffic accident, was taxable as income. The amount of compensation was 80% of the injured person's prior average weekly earnings; see s.25(1) (p 664). The Court held that the sums paid were in substitution for lost salary or wages; there was no relevant entitlement to compensation unless there was a "loss of income in the capacity of employee".

5. So considered, the main point is Tinkler does not touch the present, for here compensation was payable although there was no loss of income consequent upon the disease. Further, it is not possible in this case to hold that the payments were in substitution for wages which would otherwise have been earned. For one thing, the compensation payable to the respondent was calculated without reference to recent earnings, and for another, had there been no disability, the respondent would still have earned nothing during the relevant period.

6. However, what might be described as a subsidiary issue in Tinkler has some relevance to the present case. The statute in question in Tinkler (the Motor Accidents Act 1973 (Vic.)), apart from the provision (s.25(1)) under which compensation was in fact awarded, contained a s.25(2) which provided for payments in respect of "... a reduction in ... capacity to earn income ..." It was argued that such payments could not be taxable and therefore, to avoid incongruity, payments for actual loss of income should not be taxed. Brennan J. said he -

"... would not wish, however, to conclude that there

is any difference in the nature of payments under

the respective sub-sections" (p 667).

7. He went on to suggest, in effect, that there were reasons to doubt whether payments under the loss of earning capacity provision (s.25(2)) would be tax free. In the other set of reasons, that of Deane and Fisher JJ., it was said that:

"... we are not persuaded that the payments made

pursuant to s.25(2) of the Act should be

characterised as capital payments for loss or

impairment of earning capacity as distinct from

payments in partial substitution for earnings which

would have been earned but for the relevant

accident" (p 672).

The judges noted that the sum payable under s.25(2) was quantified as a proportion of pre-injury income; I return to this point below.

8. The second case I shall mention is the decision of the High Court in The Commissioner of Taxation v. Smith [1981] HCA 10; (1981) 147 CLR 578. That concerned the deductibility of insurance premiums paid by a doctor to guard against disability and, in addition, the exigibility to tax of payments made under the policy. In the principal judgment, there was discussion of an argument advanced that "the moneys received under the policy represent payments made for the loss of a capital asset" (p 582). The Court was prepared to accept that the insurance was against "loss of an ability", a capital asset (p 583), but said that did not show that the moneys paid were other than in substitution for income. The Court did not hold that the circumstance that the benefits payable were not directly related to the amount of lost earnings helped the taxpayer. The judges concluded -

"... that the purpose of the policy is to diminish

the adverse economic consequences of injury by

accident. It was to provide a monthly indemnity

against the income loss arising from the inability

to earn" (p 584).

9. Smith appears to me to reinforce the basic rule applied in Tinkler, that payments to compensate for lost income are themselves income. It also gives some support to the idea expressed in Tinkler that a payment made to compensate for lost capacity or ability may be income, at least if there is some correspondence between the amount of the payment and income lost or likely to have been lost.

10. The third case in the line, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slaven (1984) 52 ALR 81 is, however, not at first sight quite so easy to reconcile with Tinkler. There, the Court had to consider an amended version of the statute dealt with in Tinkler. What the legislature had done was to:

(i) define the payment under s.25 of the relevant statute as

the lesser of a fixed stated sum ($20,800) and "such amount

as ... will adequately compensate that person for the

deprivation or impairment of earning capacity which he has

suffered";

(ii) require that, for the purposes of determining the amount

just mentioned, regard be had to "the loss of earnings

which that person has incurred and the likely loss of

future earnings which that person will incur by reason of

the injury".

11. There, the taxpayer had received less than the fixed sum of $20,800, by a series of payments at irregular intervals. The relevant authority had, in ascertaining the taxpayer's pre-accident earnings, fixed a weekly amount which "approximated very closely to the appellant's pre-accident earnings but was not identical with those earnings" (p 83); the compensation was based on the nett amount of those earnings - i.e. the after-tax earnings. It does not appear that the Full Court regarded the slight difference between the pre-accident earnings and the payments of compensation as decisive or, indeed, significant.

12. It is not immediately obvious why the altered statute in question in Slaven should have produced any different result from that considered in Tinkler, particularly when one has regard to the fact that in the principal judgment in Tinkler it was suggested that the same result would follow as to the then s.25(2), which was expressed to provide compensation for "reduction in ... capacity to earn income". The only difference of any consequence between the s.25(2) payment considered in Tinkler and the payments considered in Slaven was that, in the latter, there was no statutory formula by which the loss of earnings referred to in the statute could be calculated: see p 91 line 25 of 52 ALR. The Court remarked:

"But the Board's task is essentially to determine

the compensation payable to a person having regard

to the deprivation or impairment of his earning

capacity by reason of the injury. The distinction

between loss of earnings and loss of earning

capacity is well established; it is by no means

fictional" (p 92).

13. In my respectful opinion, it is hardly possible to reconcile what was said by Deane and Fisher JJ. about s.25(2) in the earlier case with the line of reasoning in Slaven. In the latter case, much emphasis appears to have been placed upon the legislative description of the payment - i.e. as one for deprivation or impairment of earning capacity, whereas a similar description in the earlier section ("reduction in ... capacity to earn income") was not stated by Deane and Fisher JJ. to have any importance.

14. It appears that the treatment by the Court of compensation for loss of earning capacity in Tinkler should be treated as obiter. However that may be, Slaven seems to be authority against the taxability of payments which are:

(a) described in the compensation statute as being for loss of

earning capacity;

(b) related to lost earnings, but not by a formula; (c) empowered, but not required, to be made periodically.

15. As to point (a), some further analysis of the Compensation Act here in question is necessary. Under s.34, entitlement to payment of the relevant sort depends upon a worker becoming "disabled from earning full wages", which suggests that the question is whether he has lost earning capacity, rather than whether he has lost income. However, clause 7(2) of schedule 1 of the Act, dealing with cases of "partial incapacity", creates an entitlement to -

"... a weekly payment during the partial incapacity

equal to the amount by which the total weekly

earnings of the worker calculated and varied in

accordance with this Schedule would exclude the

weekly amount ... which he is earning or able to

earn in some suitable employment or business after

the occurrence of the disability".

I have deleted part of the provision, which seems to me not relevant to the present point. Under clause 11 of schedule 1, the "weekly earnings" in the provision just partially quoted are taken to be, in such a case as the present -

"... the total wages, salary or other remuneration

payable, or which would have been payable if the

industrial award or industrial agreement to which

the work was subject at the time the disability

occurred were still in operation, at the time of

the incapacity, for a week's work in such

employment, under the industrial award or

industrial agreement plus any over award or service

payment payable on a regular basis as part of the

worker's wages, salary or other remuneration".

16. As pointed out above, the respondent had not worked as a fitter for more than 30 years and it was during his work as a fitter that the exposure to asbestos occurred. The respondent has been compensated on the basis that "the time the disability occurred" within the meaning of clause 11(1) (partially quoted above) was the time when he worked as a fitter, rather than the time when the pneumoconiosis first manifested itself, many years later. It appears that it is unnecessary to determine whether that view was correct. Acting upon it, the conclusion was reached that the respondent was entitled to compensation on the basis of the award wage for a fitter, less the amount which he was "able to earn in some suitable employment or business" within the meaning of clause 7(2), the latter being, for reasons which are not entirely clear, assumed to be the minimum legal wage.

17. Reading the relevant provisions together, it seems to me that one cannot accurately say that the Compensation Act describes the compensation to which the respondent was held to be entitled as being for loss of earning capacity. It is true that, not only in this particular case, but generally, compensation may be payable under the statute in amounts not truly representative of lost earnings. However, the provisions in schedule 1 (in particular clauses 7 and 11 referred to above), seem on their face to be designed to compensate for lost earnings: the idea appears to be that the worker should be paid the difference between what he would have got from doing the work which brought about his disability, and the lesser sum he is in fact able to earn. The fact that clause 7 of schedule 1 makes the entitlement to compensation dependent upon the existence of "incapacity for work" does not seem to me, in this respect, to take the case into the Slaven class.

18. A broader consideration is that, as a matter of common sense, what the compensation statute calls the rights created should not be decisive, for the purpose of exaction of tax. Therefore, although I do not think that, as to description in the compensation statute, the payments in question should be classed with those in Slaven, that is not in itself a powerful reason for distinguishing Slaven.

19. As to point (b), the earnings here were related by a formula to what might be described as notional lost earnings. It will be recalled that in Slaven, the statute required the fixing of a sum to compensate the injured person for deprivation or impairment of earning capacity and required that regard be had to the loss of earnings and likely loss of future earnings. Here, in contrast, the statute sets out fairly precisely in clauses 7 and 11 how the payments are to be determined.

20. In this respect, then, the case is distinguishable from Slaven.

21. As to the third point, (c), there are provisions in the statute enabling the injured person to accept a lump sum in redemption of the obligation to make weekly payments. The respondent availed himself of these and in consequence was paid $25,834 on 29 October 1987. The consequence of the redemption was that the respondent ceased to be entitled to receive weekly payments, but it is not the sum just mentioned which is in issue here. The only question is whether the respondent was properly taxed on payments made on a weekly basis during the fiscal years ended 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986. It is true that, according to the evidence, when compensation was first paid, a substantial payment (said to be $3,000) was made, but that was merely the total of the periodic payments due up to that date.

22. It therefore appears to me that, in this respect also, the present case is distinguishable from Slaven.

23. The conclusion at which the Tribunal arrived was based principally upon Slaven and upon the view that -

"... the payments received by (the respondent) were

paid to compensate (the respondent) for his

disablement and not in respect of loss of income."

The dichotomy is, in my opinion, not helpful. It was true of the payments in all the three cases mentioned above that they were to compensate the taxpayer for disablement.

24. Here, the payments, if one looks at the practicalities, are not able to be neatly categorised as being either in respect of lost income, as in Tinkler and Smith, or in respect of lost earning capacity, as in Slaven. I cannot, with respect, agree that the solution is to describe them as for "disablement".

25. To so describe them may carry the implication that the statutory purpose was to compensate for the injury or disease itself, irrespective of economic consequences. That does not appear to me to be so. Whereas, in the peculiar facts of the particular case, it turned out that the payments did not compensate the respondent for lost income, since he lost no income, the essential purpose of the statute, so far as it provided for periodic payments, appears to have been to compensate for lost income resulting from disability. In Tinkler, the principal judgment, in dealing with s.25(2) of the Act there in question, makes the point that the compensation was quantified at 4/5ths of average pre-injury after tax weekly income, and that where -

"... as in the present case, the method of

calculation comprises an integral part of the

provisions under which the entitlement arises, it

can legitimately be regarded as a relevant factor

in determining the character of the payment"

(p 672).

So here: schedule 1 creates an entitlement to weekly payments equal to the amount by which "total weekly earnings", as defined, exceeds the amount which the person in question is earning or able to earn and it seems proper to treat that as a factor relevant to characterization.

26. Further, in Smith, the High Court noted that features of the policy made it:

"unlikely that the moneys received under the policy

will bear any direct correspondence to the loss of

earnings suffered by the insured."

One such feature was that if the disability continued beyond two years, the monthly indemnity continued, whatever the insured was then earning. Their Honours remarked:

"Even after the period of two years has elapsed, the

inference of loss still remains. The loss of

'ability to earn' in one's own calling is most

likely to be reflected in an actual loss of

earnings" (p 584).

27. This line of reasoning appears to me applicable here. Although disabled within the meaning of the statute, the respondent was losing no income and was thus comparable with the case dealt with in Smith of the person who, after two years' disablement, happened to be earning income. The proper reading of the High Court's reasons is that, for this purpose, one looks at the essential character and purpose of the compensation provisions rather than whether or not, in particular situations, those provisions necessarily do no more than restore lost earnings. It is true that the present is an extreme case, in that there was not merely a lack of correspondence between the compensation and the lost earnings; there were no lost earnings at all.

28. It would, of course, be anomalous to treat the compensation payments received by an injured worker who has in truth lost earnings less favourably for tax purposes than those received by a person who is fortunate enough to have lost no earnings. Rather than accepting that distinction, it appears to me that the proper course is to characterise the payments received by the respondent as periodic payments made to compensate the respondent for a weekly loss of income presumed to have occurred.

29. In short, I think the case should be decided by following Tinkler and Smith and distinguishing Slaven.

30. I therefore agree, although for slightly different reasons, with the conclusion expressed by my brother Lee. In my opinion the proper orders are that the appeal be allowed, the order of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal set aside, and the assessments in question confirmed.

I agree with the orders proposed in the judgment of Lee J. I do so for the reasons given by his Honour.

This is an appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal") which set aside assessments of taxation issued by the appellant to the respondent for the years ending 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986.

2. The Tribunal decided that payments of workers' compensation received by the respondent during those years of income were not assessable income pursuant to the provisions of sub-s.25(1) or sub-s.26(j) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). In August 1984 the respondent received a sum of approximately $3,000 as a composite payment of compensation payments that had accrued between November 1983 and August 1984 at a rate of $128.70 per week. Thereafter, the respondent was paid fortnightly payments of compensation calculated at the rate of $128.70 per week to 7 April 1985, $221.80 per week to 10 November 1985 and $230.20 per week thereafter.

3. The respondent was employed as a fitter and turner in workshops of the Western Australian Government Railways between November 1948 and February 1950, and between August 1950 and May 1951. In the course of his employment, the respondent was exposed to asbestos dust.

4. In 1951 the respondent commenced employment with the Western Australia Police Department and remained in that employment until 1 September 1982 when he retired at the age of 60. At the time of retirement, the respondent held the rank of inspector and his salary was $720 per week. After retirement the respondent received superannuation payments of $356 per week and obtained temporary employment with the Crown Law Department. He was paid a salary of $540 per week and was provided with the use of a motor vehicle. The temporary position ceased after several months and thereafter the respondent obtained no other employment.

5. Prior to his retirement the respondent had regular radiographic examinations of his chest. As a result of such an examination in July 1982 the respondent was advised that he was suffering from asbestosis. Asbestosis is a particular form of pneumoconiosis, a disease of the lungs caused by inhalation of dust. After his retirement in September 1982 the respondent lodged a claim with Westrail, formerly the Western Australian Government Railways, for compensation under the Workers' Compensation and Assistance Act 1981 (W.A.) ("the Compensation Act"). On 18 October 1982 the respondent was examined by a medical panel appointed pursuant to s.38 of the Compensation Act. The panel determined that the respondent was not suffering from asbestosis.

6. In the following year, the respondent had another chest examination and was informed once more that he was suffering from asbestosis. The respondent lodged another claim for compensation with Westrail. In November 1983 he was examined by another medical panel appointed under the Compensation Act.

7. Pursuant to s.38 of the Compensation Act, the panel was required to determine:

a) whether the respondent suffered from

pneumoconiosis;

b) whether he was thereby disabled from earning

full wages;

c) to what extent did pneumoconiosis cause

impairment of his ability to undertake physical

effort; and

d) whether he was fit for work and, if so, at what

level - "light, moderate or heavy".

8. The panel determined that the respondent was suffering from pneumoconiosis and was thereby disabled from earning full wages. It also determined that asbestosis and chronic bronchitis caused impairment of his ability to undertake physical effort to the extent of 40 per cent and that the respondent was fit for work at a moderate level. Pursuant to sub-s.38(4) of the Compensation Act, the determination of the medical panel was final and conclusive and binding on the respondent, the employer and any tribunal in which the determination was relevant.

9. Section 33 of the Compensation Act provided that if a worker was disabled from earning full wages by reason of suffering from pneumoconiosis and the disease is, or was due to the nature of any employment in which the worker was employed at any time prior to the date of the disablement, a disability of the worker thereby occurred, the disability being pneumoconiosis, and the Compensation Act applied to that disability. Pursuant to s.34 of the Compensation Act, if the association of chronic bronchitis with pneumoconiosis caused a worker to be disabled from earning full wages, he was deemed to be disabled by pneumoconiosis.

10. Section 5 of the Compensation Act defined a disability in various ways. One part of the definition of disability was "a disabling disease to which Pt.III Division 3 applies". Pneumoconiosis was such a disease.

11. Pursuant to s.18 of the Compensation Act, if a disability of a worker occurred an employer was, subject to the Compensation Act, liable to pay compensation in accordance with Schedule 1 of the Act. Section 21 of the Compensation Act further provided that an employer was liable to pay compensation from "the date of incapacity resulting from the disability".

12. Section 33 of the Compensation Act defined the occurrence of a disability by reason of a disease of slow progression such as pneumoconiosis as the point at which a worker was disabled from earning full wages. Section 5 of the Act defined the phrase "disabled from earning full wages" as meaning rendered less able to earn full wages. Therefore, for the purposes of s.33, a disability occurred when the impact of the disease on the worker was of such a degree that it lessened the worker's ability to earn the level of wages that was commensurate with his capacity to obtain employment if he were not affected by the disease. A worker so disabled may in fact continue to earn full wages but remain disabled within the meaning of the Compensation Act in that the effect of the progressive disease has been to render him less able to earn such wages if he were obliged to compete in the open market in the fields of employment for which he had been reasonably suited by training, experience, skill and ordinary fitness.

13. The Compensation Act did not require, as a matter of fact, that less than "full wages" be earned. It was concerned with a loss of the capacity to earn such wages and upon it being demonstrated that such a loss was a consequence of the disease pneumoconiosis, a disability occurred under the Compensation Act and a liability to pay compensation arose under s.18. However, the amount of compensation payable and the date on which the employer was liable to pay compensation under s.21 depended upon the worker being able to show the existence of a total or partial incapacity for work resulting from the disability.

14. Clause 7 of Schedule 1 of the Compensation Act set out how the compensation to be paid in respect of a disability was to be calculated.

15. The measurement of compensation was tied to an incapacity for work resulting from a disability. If there were either a total incapacity (sub-cl.7(1)) or partial incapacity (cl.7(2)) for work which resulted from the disability, a weekly payment was to be made to the worker during the period of incapacity. Pursuant to sub-cll.7(1), (2) of Schedule 1, the amount of the weekly payment was calculated by reference to the weekly earnings of the worker as defined in cl.11 of the Schedule. The weekly earnings of a worker were the amount of wages, salary or remuneration payable in the employment in which the disability occurred excluding overtime, bonuses and allowances. In respect of pneumoconiosis, disability occurred when a worker became disabled from earning full wages, but sub-s.41(1) stated that in the case of such a disease, compensation was recoverable from the employer "who last employed the worker, in the employment to the nature of which the disease is, or was, due". Section 42 of the Compensation Act provided that the amount of compensation was to be calculated with reference to the earnings of the worker under the employer from whom the compensation was recoverable. It appears, therefore, that in respect of a disease contracted gradually such as pneumoconiosis (see sub-s.41(4)), the relevant weekly earnings of a worker "in the employment in which the disability occurs" referred to in cl.11 of Schedule 1 was to be taken to be the earnings of a worker employed in the work in which the worker was engaged when he contracted the disease rather than the employment in which the worker was engaged when the disease rendered him less able to earn full wages. Therefore, although a worker may have left the former employment many years before and increased his earning capacity by acquiring further skills, as had the respondent, the calculation of weekly earnings would be related only to the wages paid in the original field of employment.

16. Sub-clause 7(2) of Schedule 1 stipulated that where partial incapacity for work resulted from the disability, a weekly payment was to be made to the worker equal to the amount by which the total weekly earnings of the worker exceeded the amount the worker was earning, or able to earn, in some suitable employment or business after the occurrence of the disability.

17. Section 56 of the Compensation Act provided that entitlement to weekly payments of compensation for an incapacity for work resulting from a disability ceased upon a worker attaining the age of 65 years, subject to exceptions set out in Schedule 5 of the Compensation Act. Schedule 5 provided that a worker who attained the age of 65 years and was able to show that if the incapacity which resulted from the disability had not occurred he or she would have continued to be a worker after attaining that age, was entitled to receive a weekly payment described as a "supplementary amount". Such a payment was made only during a period of total incapacity which resulted from the disability during the time in which the worker would have continued to have been in employment but, in any event, no such payments were made after the worker attained 70 years of age (Schedule 5 cl.2). The "supplementary amount" was a fixed amount prescribed pursuant to the Schedule and was not related to any amount of earnings lost by the worker.

18. Clause 3 of Schedule 5 made special provision for workers suffering incapacity for work resulting from the disabilities of mesothelioma or pneumoconiosis. One such provision (sub-cl.3(4)) enabled a worker who was so disabled and who before attaining the age of 65 years was entitled to weekly payments for any incapacity which had resulted from that disability, to receive weekly payments after attaining 65 years of age. The entitlement to weekly payments was not limited to workers who had suffered total incapacity for work. There was no requirement for the worker to show that he or she would have continued to be a worker after attaining the age of 65 years if the incapacity which had resulted from the disability had not occurred. The effect of cll.4 and 8 of Schedule 5 was to allow a worker disabled by pneumoconiosis to receive weekly payments for compensation calculated pursuant to Schedule 1 for a period of 1 year after the age of 65 years and if in that time there had been no election to take a "redemption amount" as a lump sum, to be paid the "supplementary amount" during his or her lifetime. The "redemption amount" was defined in cl.1 of Schedule 5 as the sum of $20,000 varied by prescribed adjustments or the difference between the maximum amount of weekly payments able to be paid under the Compensation Act and the amount of weekly payments already made if such difference was less that the sum of $20,000 varied by adjustments.

19. The employer's insurer apparently relied upon the finding of the medical panel appointed in November 1983 that pneumoconiosis had caused impairment of the respondent's ability to undertake physical effort to the extent of 40 per cent to conclude that a partial incapacity for work had resulted from the disability. The weekly payments of compensation were purportedly calculated pursuant to sub-cl.7(2) of Schedule 1 of the Compensation Act by determining the difference between the amount paid to a person employed as a fitter by the respondent's former employer in November 1983 and the amount of the minimum wage paid to adult males in the State at that date. The fortnightly payments received were net amounts after deduction of tax by Westrail.

20. In April 1985 the respondent's condition was reviewed by another medical panel appointed under the Compensation Act. That panel determined that the respondent was suffering from asbestosis and chronic bronchitis and that that condition impaired his ability to undertake physical effort to the extent of 60 per cent. It concluded that the respondent was fit for "light" work.

21. After April 1985 the amount of compensation paid to the respondent was increased to the difference between one half of the State Male Minimum Wage and the wage payable to a fitter employed by his former employer.

22. On 1 September 1987 the respondent reached the age of 65 years and elected to take the "redemption amount" under Schedule 5 of the Compensation Act. He received the redemption amount in October 1987.

23. The expression "incapacity for work" is not defined in the Act but its meaning has been considered in the context of other like legislation on a number of occasions. It had been held to denote a physical incapacity for doing work in the labour market in which the employee was employed or might reasonably be expected to be employed. Although a physical incapacity may be demonstrated, that incapacity may not necessarily attract compensation if it results in no loss of earning power (Williams v. Metropolitan Coal Co. Ltd. [1948] HCA 8; (1948) 76 CLR 431 per Dixon J. at p 449).

24. The discussion of the meaning of the expression "incapacity for work" as used in earlier authorities remains relevant, notwithstanding that the Compensation Act repealed earlier legislation and introduced some new concepts. In Thompson v. Armstrong and Royse Pty. Ltd. [1950] HCA 46; (1950) 81 CLR 585 the meaning of the words was examined in detail. At pp 601-603 McTiernan J. said as follows:

"The phrase 'incapacity for work' is a set form of

words used in Acts of this type and its meaning is

well settled. There is nothing in the present Act

which shows that the phrase was intended to have a

special significance different from its recognized

meaning. The decisions in which the meaning of

the phrase is discussed are numerous. It is

hardly possible to refer to all of them. The

respondent's argument on the meaning of the phrase

may be tested by reference to some of the cases.

Cardiff Corporation v. Hall (1911) 1 KB 1009

shows that the principle upon which compensation

is given under a Workers' Compensation Act is

based on the diminution of earning power by reason

of the injury. In that case Buckley L.J., at

p 1027, said that the Act makes the employer 'an

insurer of 'capacity for work''. This capacity

is the worker's power to earn wages. The

principle was again stated in Harwood v. Wyken

Colliery Co. (1913) 2 KB 158.

...

The reasoning in Williams v. Metropolitan Coal Co.

Ltd. [1948] HCA 8; (1948) 76 CLR 431 is in line with the

decisions. In that case Dixon J. said, at p 449,

'but it is not true that incapacity is a

conception covering nothing but incapacity for the

man's former work or for work in his former

industry'.

The respondent's submission on the meaning of the

phrase 'incapacity for work' is not supported by

authority. The phrase does not merely mean

inability to work for the employer in whose

service the worker was injured. An injury results

in incapacity for work, according to the intention

of the Act, when it takes away or diminishes the

power of the worker to earn wages in some suitable

employment."

At pp 615-616 Fullagar J. said:

"The words cannot, as I have said, be read as

referring to anything but a physical condition,

and the question of the existence or non-existence

of a physical condition cannot be answered by

reference to the terms of a particular contract or

to what is required for the performance of a

particular contract. It is true, of course, that

the fact that a worker is being paid full wages by

his employer is relevant to the question whether

an injury has resulted in incapacity for work.

But this is only because prima facie employers do

not pay full wages to men who are unable to do any

work. And it is never conclusive evidence. It

would, of course, be conclusive evidence if

'incapacity for work' meant inability to obtain

wages from a particular employer. But the

expression means inability for physcial reasons to

sell his labour in the open market. This is the

whole point of Funnell v. Allen West & Co. Ltd.

(1947) 177 LT 220. In that case Morton L.J. (as

he then was), speaking for the Court of Appeal,

said:- 'The workman appeals, and his counsel

contend that the county court judge failed to

apply Lord Loreburn's definition of 'incapacity

for work' in Ball v. William Hunt & Sons Ltd.

(1912) AC 496 at p 499. They contend that he

wrongly took the view that there cannot possibly

be a partial incapacity for work if a man is

employed by his old employers at his old work, and

is in receipt of the same wages as those received

by men of his standing who have never had an

accident. We agree that a partial incapacity for

work may exist in the circumstances just stated;

for instance, the employers may be paying the

workman, out of compassion, more than his labour

is actually worth in the open market'. I would

add that they might be paying him because an

industrial award compelled them to pay him, in

which case the position would be the same, though

a question might arise as to whether s.13 of its

counterpart would be invoked. Morton L.J.

proceeded:- 'No doubt the fact that a man is doing

his old work, and is in receipt of the same wages

as those received by men of his standing who have

never had an accident, is strong evidence that

there is no 'incapacity for work' within Lord

Loreburn's definition; but it is not conclusive

evidence'. His Lordship then proceeded to examine

the reasons which the county court judge had given

for his decision, and came to the conclusion that

he had not misunderstood or misapplied Lord

Loreburn's definition. Certain passages in the

judgment indicated that the learned judge had 'had

the open market in view'. If he had not had the

open market in view, but had acted on the

assumption that the question of incapacity for

work was conclusively determined by reference to

the relation between the worker and his continuing

employer, the case would have been sent back to

him. The well-established position is clearly put

by Starke J. in Williams v. Metropolitan Coal Co.

Ltd. (1948) 76 CLR, at p 444. His Honour

said:- 'Compensation is not payable for the injury

but for the loss of power to earn caused by the

injury, that is, for incapacity for work which

results from the injury. The question is whether

the injury has left the worker in such a position

that in the open labour market his earning

capacity in the future is less than it was before

the injury (Birmingham Cabinet Manufacturing Co.

v. Dudley (1910) 102 LT 619; Jackson v. Hunslet

Engine Co. (1915) 84 LJ KB 1361.' The words

'in the future' in this passage refer, of course,

to the time of commencement of the incapacity, and

it does not matter whether ther duration of the

incapacity is one week or many years.

...

The Act has always been construed from the point

of view that its nature and object are those of an

Act to benefit the worker. In Williams v.

Metropolitan Coal Co. Ltd. (1948) 76 CLR, at

p 444, in a passage following immediately on that

which I have quoted above, Starke J. said:- 'It is

erroneous to say that the whole object of the Act

is to compensate a worker for injury whether by

disease or otherwise only to the extent to which

he is thereby incapacitated from earning his full

wages in the employment in which the injury arose,

or that the clear intention of the Act is to limit

its operation to the matter of restoring the

financial position of the worker in relation to

the industry in which he had been working at the

time of the injury.' Striking examples of a

refusal to introduce any implication which would

take away or limit the right of the worker to

compensation if the statutory conditions are

literally fulfilled are to be found in McCann v.

Scottish Co-operative Laundry Association Ltd.

(1936) 154 LT 503, and the cases collected in

the judgment of Lord Thankerton at p 506. These

cases show that compensation is payable during

incapacity resulting from an accident

notwithstanding that the worker, even if he had

met with no accident, could not have earned

anything because of some disease unconnected with

the accident, or because of another accident

unconnected with the employment, or because he is

undergoing a term of imprisonment, or because of a

strike. As Hamilton L.J. (as he then was) said in

Harwood v. Wyken Colliery Co. (1913) 2 KB, at

p 170:- 'The Act is a guarantee of workmen against

the risk of accident. It is not founded on

indemnity, and the ideas of retribution for

wrong-doing and or restoratio in integrum are

foreign to it.' Hamilton L.J. did not, of course,

mean that (apart from provisions which correspond

to s.16 of the New South Wales Act) the Act gave

compensation for injury as such. He meant that

the Act must be allowed to speak for itself, and

that its own express provisions with regard to the

consequences of an accident are not to be

qualified or limited by the importation of ideas

developed in the law of tort and in equity."

It may be noted that s.72 was inserted in the Compensation Act to suspend a worker's entitlement to weekly payments of compensation during any period of imprisonment. Similarly, s.56 of the Compensation Act modified the right that a worker may otherwise have obtained under the Act by providing that the entitlement to receive weekly payments of compensation ceased upon the worker attaining the age of 65 years.

25. The argument that there was no entitlement to workers' compensation unless an actual loss of wages was demonstrated was rejected in Thompson v. Armstrong and Royse Pty. Ltd.. As Kitto J. stated at pp 621-622:

"Loss of wages is in most cases a result of, but it

does not itself constitute, the relevant economic

fact. That fact is the inability, or the reduced

ability, by reason of a physical deficiency, to

sell work for wages. 'It is now accepted that by

incapacity for work is meant incapacity to earn

wages by working' (Birch Brothers Ltd. v. Brown

(1931) AC, at pp 626, 627, per Lord Macmillan).

Thus compensation is awarded, not for loss of

wages, nor for impairment of physical condition

per se, but for the economic aspect of that

impairment, namely a lost or diminished ability to

obtain wages by working.

This is made abundantly clear by Lord Loreburn's

frequently quoted definition in Ball v. William

Hunt & Sons Ltd. (1912) AC, at pp 499, 500:-

'There is incapacity for work when a man has a

physical defect which makes his labour unsaleable

in any market reasonably accessible to him, and

there is partial incapacity for work when such a

defect makes his labour saleable for less than it

would otherwise fetch.' The inquiry, then, must

be as to the result of a man's physical defect in

relation to the market for his labour. If his

physical defect is such that he cannot go into the

market at all, because he cannot offer to perform

any labour, it seems to me to follow necessarily

that he has a total incapacity for work. He has

no labour to sell; and to say that nevertheless he

is not incapacitated for work, because he is

getting wages under a continuing contract of

employment which in the circumstances does not

require him to give any labour in return for

wages, appears to me to involve a complete

desertion of Lord Loreburn's definition. His

Lordship's expression 'any market reasonably

accessible to him' has often been paraphrased as

'the open market'; see, for example, Funnell v.

Allen West & Co. Ltd. (1947) 177 LT 220. It is

the general market in which the worker, but for

his injury, could reasonably have offered his

labour. To concede, as I think one must, that

total incapacity for work exists whenever an

injury makes a worker's labour unsaleable in the

general market for such labour, is to admit that

its existence is not disproved by the fact that a

particular employer is paying him wages for doing

nothing. Indeed, the fact that a worker, after

receiving an injury, is found to be in receipt of

wages is never decisive of capacity for work.

This fact may or may not point towards the

conclusion that he has capacity for work; and

whether or not that is the proper conclusion

depends upon the circumstances. To the extent to

which the wages are produced by anything other

than work, e.g., by the compassion of the

employer, the receipt of them is irrelevant to the

question of capacity for work: Funnell v. Allen

West & Co. Ltd.; Hawkins v. Australasian United

Steam Navigation Co. Ltd. (1938) 12 WCR 99, at

pp 108-111; and the clearest possible case of

wages being produced by something other than work

would seem to be the case where wages are paid

because of a legal obligation to pay them for a

period in which no work is performed."

26. In respect of partial incapacity for work, wages and compensation may be mutually exclusive in that the statutory measurement of compensation excludes the amount of wages earned, or able to be earned, but that mutual exclusivity results from the statutory method of calculation and does not in itself necessarily provide the character of any payment of compensation made under the Compensation Act. As Kitto J. said at pp 622-623:

"Again, in Birch Bros. Ltd. v. Brown (1931) AC,

at p 630, Lord Macmillan said: 'Even the actual

obtaining of employment by a handicapped workman

may not prove his recovered economic capacity to

earn wages, for the job may have been given to him

out of philanthropy or be merely nominal. In such

a case the compensation is diminished or ended not

because the workman is really proved to have

recovered his earning capacity but because wages

and compensation are mutually exclusive.' But

this means, I think, what the same learned Lord

said in McCann v. Scottish Co-operative Laundry

Association Ltd. (1936) 1 All ER 475, at

pp 482-483:- 'It is true that if the workman has

resumed work after his accident, and is in fact

earning the same wages as before his accident, the

employer is freed from liability so long as that

state of matters continues, even if the workman is

still partially incapacitated, but this is because

the statutory method of calculating compensation

yields nothing.' Thus it is true to say, in a

case of partial incapacity, that wages and

compensation are mutually exclusive, not in the

sense that the receipt of wages necessarily proves

capacity for work and therefore negatives a right

to be paid compensation, but in the sense that

post-injury wages reduce the amount of

compensation payable because the weekly

compensation must not exceed the difference

between the workers' average weekly earnings

before the injury and his average weekly earnings

after the injury: see s.11 of the New South Wales

Act. The doctrine of mutual exclusiveness affords

no touchstone for, and indeed has no relevance to,

the determination of the question whether a worker

is entitled to be paid compensation as being

totally incapacitated for work."

27. Sub-clause 7(2) of Schedule 1 of the Compensation Act described the test to be applied to determine the amount of compensation to be paid for a partial incapacity for work caused by the physical incapacity that has resulted from the disability and it accorded with the usual measure to be found in such legislation.

28. The nature of that test was well illustrated in the following passage in the joint judgment of Mason, Wilson, Deane and Dawson JJ. in Arnotts Snack Products Ltd. v. Yacob [1985] HCA 2; (1985) 155 CLR 171 at p 177:

"In Thompson v. Armstrong and Royse Pty. Ltd.

[1950] HCA 46; (1950) 81 CLR 585, where the concept of

'incapacity for work' in the context of s.9 of the

Act - the principal provision providing for

payment of weekly compensation - arose for

consideration, the majority of the Court

(Latham C.J., McTiernan, Fullagar and Kitto JJ.)

decisively rejected the proposition that the

receipt post-injury of the same or higher wages

than those received pre-injury denied the

existence of partial incapacity for work.

That 'incapacity for work' means a physical

incapacity for actually doing work, resulting from

'injury (or disease) and that, subject to ss.11

and 13, compensation is awarded for that

incapacity where it reduces the employee's ability

to sell his labour in the open market, is brought

out in the judgments of the majority, especially

those of Latham C.J. and Fullagar J. Latham C.J.

said at p 595:

'The phrase 'where total or partial

incapacity for work results from the

injury' must refer to physical injury

resulting in physical incapacity for

actually doing work. That incapacity is

relevant where it produces an incapacity

to earn his living as he did before the

injury (per Evershed L.J. in Ruocco v.

Surrey County Council (1947) 177 LT

613, at p 616) in a market for his

labour which was reasonably accessible

to him...'

Fullagar J. said at p 613:

'A man is totally incapacitated for work

when he is, by reason of his injury,

physically unable to work. The words in

their natural and primary sense mean

that. When their meaning has been

expounded by reference to inability to

earn wages, the purpose has been to make

the meaning more specific, and the

result has been to extend rather than

restrict the meaning.'

The same point was made, though less directly, by

Kitto J. who remarked at p 621:

'Loss of wages is in most cases a result

of, but it does not itself constitute,

the relevant economic fact. That fact

is the inability or the reduced ability,

by reason of a physical deficiency, to

sell work for wages.'

(See also McTiernan J. at pp 602-603.)

The judgments in Thompson were discussed by Jacobs

J. (with whom Gibbs, Stephen and Aickin JJ.

agreed) in The Commonwealth v. Muratore (1978) 141

CLR 296, at pp 300-301, which related to the

Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees)

Act 1971 (Cth). His Honour said (1978) 141

CLR, at p 300:

'It has always been recognized that

'incapacity for work', those words being

taken to refer to physical incapacity,

is only relevant where it produces an

economic incapacity.'

He was speaking, as the passage at the foot of

p 301 makes plain, of compensable incapacity for

work, so that the employee's incapacity is

'measured by the extent that the amount per week

that he is able to earn in some suitable

employment or business is less than his average

weekly earnings before his injury'.

It follows that the concept of partial incapacity

for work is that of reduced physical capacity, by

reason of physical disability, for actually doing

work in the labour market in which the employee

was working or might reasonably be expected to

work."

See also Steggles Pty. Ltd. v. Vandenberg [1987] HCA 35; (1987) 163 CLR 321 per Mason C.J., Wilson, Deane, Toohey and Gaudron JJ. at pp 325-326.

29. In the respondent's case the respondent's former employer was liable to pay compensation upon the occurrence of the disability (s.18) and was liable to pay compensation from the date of incapacity (s.21). The measure of compensation was established by cl.7 of Schedule 1 (s.18).

30. It was necessary for the respondent to demonstrate what amount he was able to earn if he were required to do so, but the employer waived that requirement and accepted that the respondent was only able to earn the minimum weekly wage payable to an adult male. (See J. & H. Timbers Pty. Ltd. v. Nelson [1972] HCA 12; (1972) 126 CLR 625.) Both parties were satisfied that a notional calculation represented the appropriate measure of the compensation payable under the Compensation Act in respect of the reduction in the respondent's capacity to earn income.

31. The Compensation Act did not state in its terms that the worker had an entitlement or a right to receive compensation upon the occurrence of a disability. It did state in s.18 that an employer was liable to pay compensation upon such an occurrence and s.21 stipulated the date from which payments of compensation were to commence pursuant to that liability. The provisions of the Compensation Act, however, assumed that a worker had a right to recover compensation as a correlative to the liability of the employer to pay compensation. For example, s.56 and sub-cl.7(3) of Schedule 1 of the Act refer to circumstances in which a worker's "entitlement" to weekly payments of compensation under the Compensation Act would cease.

32. The scheme of the Compensation Act was to provide a worker with an entitlement to receive compensation and to impose a liability upon an employer to pay compensation when a disability occurred (s.18). The measurement of the payment of compensation and the date of commencement of such payments were related to the incapacity which resulted from the disability (s.21; cl.7 Schedule 1). Those provisions did not limit the right to obtain compensation but prescribed the method of determining the quantum. (Williams v. Metropolitan Coal Company Limited per Starke J. at p 444 and per Dixon J. at p 450.) The measure of compensation depended upon the extent to which an incapacity for work had been demonstrated.

33. The incapacity for work was not restricted to incapacity in the field of employment in which the worker was engaged when the disability occurred. An incapacity may be demonstrated if there has been an impairment of the worker's capacity to obtain employment on the open market in any field of employment reasonably open to that worker.

34. Where the disability has been caused by the onset of progressive disease rather than a traumatic injury by accident, the assessment of the amount of compensation payable pursuant to Schedule 1 may have little relationship to any loss of earnings suffered by a worker. Clauses 7 and 11 of Schedule 1 measure the amount of compensation payable by reference to the earnings available in the field of employment in which the worker was engaged at the time the disease was contracted and not the earnings of the worker at the time the disease-caused disability was manifested.

35. If the physical incapacity resulting from the disease does not appear until after the worker has retired from his usual employment, the lack of an attempt to obtain further employment would have no bearing on the worker's right to obtain compensation under the Compensation Act. The measure of compensation payments would depend upon the worker showing what amount he was able to earn on the open market having regard to his physical incapacity and his particular circumstances. (See Williams v. Metropolitan Coal Co. Ltd.)

36. The amount paid to the respondent by way of weekly payments of compensation was a notional calculation. There was no assessment of the respondent's actual capacity to obtain earnings in some suitable employment or business after his retirement to determine how the weekly payment of compensation for the partial incapacity was to be calculated within the meaning of sub-cl.7(2) of Schedule 1.

37. The respondent did not intend to obtain regular employment after his retirement. His claim for compensation under the Compensation Act was lodged at the suggestion of a social worker. The claim may have involved an assumption that there was entitlement under the Compensation Act to be compensated for the injury caused by the disease contracted in the course of his original employment. In that Schedule 5 of the Compensation Act made special provision for workers suffering from the disability of pneumoconiosis by providing greater compensation benefits for such persons, there may have been some ground for the respondent's assumption.

38. In determining whether the compensation payments received by the respondent had the character of income under the Act, the provisions of the Compensation Act will be of importance. However, although the terms of the Compensation Act may have indicated a purpose for the payments, those provisions will not determine the character of the payments without regard to the circumstances in which the respondent received the payments.

39. In the absence of any other evidence there may be a conclusion that the payments were duly made in the terms of the Compensation Act and carried the purpose provided by that Act, but other evidence may show the payments to have a different character. As the Full Court said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slaven (1984) 52 ALR 81 at p 93:

"...the purpose of a statutory payment, as

disclosed by the terms of the statute itself, must

be a powerful, though not conclusive, aid to the

determination of the character of the payment and

in particular as to whether its receipt

constitutes income in the hands of a taxpayer."

40. The evidence in this case showed that no assessment was made in the terms required by sub-cl.7(2) of Schedule 1 of the Compensation Act. The respondent was not required to demonstrate the extent to which his earning capacity had been impaired at the time he lodged his claim for compensation.

41. The respondent's former employer, through its insurer, calculated the payment of compensation by comparing the notional weekly earnings of a person carrying out the respondent's former occupation, a fitter, and the notional possible earnings of the respondent. In fact, the employment obtained by the respondent after his retirement, although temporary in nature, provided earnings substantially in excess of those payable to a fitter and there was no evidence that the respondent was unable to obtain employment providing earnings equivalent to that of a fitter if he wished to do so. There was no evidence that the disease from which he was suffering had disabled him from obtaining employment for which he was suited by his particular experience before his retirement and which required no major physical exertion.

42. In making a calculation based upon notional earnings to assess the respondent's entitlement to weekly payments of compensation for his disability of pneumoconiosis, the employer's insurer proceeded to an assessment that disregarded whether, in fact, a partial incapacity for work had resulted from the disability and a loss of earnings had been suffered by the respondent during such incapacity. As was set out in the evidence adduced before the Tribunal, the calculation of the weekly payments of compensation paid to the respondent represented an assessment of the diminution of the respondent's capacity to earn income and was made in consequence of the finding of the medical panel that the disease of pneumoconiosis had impaired the respondent's ability to undertake physical effort by 40 per cent.

43. No loss of income was established or sought to be established nor inferred from the facts. The calculation proceeded as if the respondent had an entitlement to an assessment for compensation which represented the extent of the impairment of his ability to gain income by personal exertion. As such it was an assessment of the loss of capacity to earn income and not a calculation of a payment to serve as a contribution to offset an actual loss of income. In that regard it was directed more to the matters with which Schedule 2 of the Compensation Act was concerned, namely calculation of lump sum payments for compensation for the impairment of a worker's ability to earning income by reason of bodily injuries.

44. The fact that the amount of a compensation payment is a notional calculation will not prevent the payment being of a revenue nature if it is designed as a contribution to diminish the adverse economic consequences of a disability, that is to say the income loss caused by it.

"From his review of the policy, Wickham J.

concluded that 'this is clearly an insurance

against loss of an ability'. So much may be

conceded, as also the proposition that capacity or

ability to earn is a capital asset. But, with

respect, these conclusions do not carry the

taxpayer far enough to establish the contrary of

the Commissioner's assertion that the moneys paid

under the policy were paid in substitution for

income and therefore take the place of a revenue

receipt. If the ability to earn is the tree, and

income the fruit thereof, a policy of insurance

against impairment of the fruit-bearing capacity

of the tree may well take the form of providing

the fruit until such time as the tree recovers its

proper role. The degree of correspondence, if

any, between the monies payable under the policy

of the actual pecuniary loss of revenue suffered

by the insured is a relevant factor, but it is not

necessary to look for an indemnity measured with

any precision against the loss. Any fruit is

better than none, whether or not it represents

adequate compensation for the loss."

(Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Smith (1981)

[1981] HCA 10; 147 CLR 578 per Gibbs C.J., Stephen, Mason and

Wilson JJ. at p 583.)

In that case the payments retained their character as income even though the loss of income they were designed to mitigate was offset by earnings received from other employment. The character of the payments was established when a loss of income occurred and the purpose of replacing that lost income was shown to be inherent in the payments.

45. In the respondent's case, there was no loss of income to which the payments were directed as a substitution for such earnings and the payments had their genesis in an assessment of a loss of earning capacity.

46. For this reason any one payment may have had the character of capital rather than revenue. (See Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slaven at p 93.) This was the conclusion of the Tribunal and in my opinion the Tribunal did not err in so finding.

47. For the same reason, sub-s.26(j) of the Act could have had no application to the circumstances of this case.

48. Sub-section 26(j) provided as follows:

"any amount received by way of insurance or

indemnity for or in respect of any loss

(i) of trading stock which would have been

taken into account in computing taxable

income; or

(ii) of profit or income which would have

been assessable income,

if the loss had not occurred, and any amount so

received for or in respect of any loss or outgoing

which is an allowable deduction;"

49. The respondent did not suffer any loss of earnings and did not claim that he had. He no longer sought to earn income by personal exertion. For sub-s.26(j) to have applied, it would have been necessary to demonstrate that an actual loss of income had been indemnified. In the respondent's case no such loss was shown and nor was it necessary. The respondent qualified for the payment of compensation when he established that he was less able to earn the ordinary wages payable to a fitter if he were to seek employment on the open market. Whatever the width of the meaning of "insurance or indemnity" may be, the respondent did not receive any amount for, or in respect of, any loss of income.

50. The facts of this appeal may be distinguished from those considered by the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Smith where it was decided that in addition to being income within the meaning of s.25 of the Act, the benefits received by the taxpayer under a policy of insurance were also assessable income pursuant to sub-s.26(j). In that case the payments made to the taxpayer under the policy were an indemnity against actual losses of a revenue nature suffered by the insured. The necessary characteristics were present for sub-s.26(j) to apply. The Tribunal correctly decided that sub-s.26(j) had no application.

51. It is now necessary to determine whether such payments for compensation calculated as weekly payments and paid regularly, took on the character of income by virtue of their periodicity. On this question the Tribunal found that periodicity of payment alone was not decisive and that no other factors were present to make the payments income according to ordinary concepts. Set out below are the paragraphs of the Tribunal's reasons which express the Tribunal's opinion:

"30. Returning to the facts of the present case it

is clear from the provisions of the Act that

the payments received by the applicant were paid

to compensate the applicant for his disablement

and not in respect of loss of income. The fact

that the Act provides for weekly payments in

respect of the disablement does not by itself

convert a payment of a capital nature into income.

Where there are other circumstances which also

suggest the payment to be of an income nature then

the periodicity of the payments would need to be

taken into account. However, periodicity without

more is not decisive on the authorities already

referred to. In this case there is no payment for

lost income which would otherwise have been

derived except for the disablement. The applicant

was in receipt of a superannuation pension as a

retired police officer and he continued to be paid

such pension.

31. The payments made were wholly and exclusively

for the permanent disablement, no loss of income

being involved. A payment for such disablement is

clearly a payment of a capital nature except where

other factors intrude. No factors intrude here

except periodicity of payment which alone is not

decisive. The payments were not income according

to ordinary concepts nor did they come within the

terms of paragraph 26(j)."

52. With respect to the Tribunal, there were surrounding circumstances more than capable of characterizing the periodical payments as income. Although the payments had their origins in capital, they were not in the nature of payments by instalments of a fixed sum due and owing. The payments were intended to serve the purpose of providing a regular income supplement to the respondent notwithstanding that the payments were generated by calculations which related to capital considerations. Although it is the character of the receipt in the hands of the taxpayer as recipient that must be determined (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slaven at p 93), part of the consideration of that matter must involve consideration of the motive of the payer (Hayes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47 per Fullagar J. at p 55) and the part the receipt played in the payee's affairs.

53. Although the respondent may not have relied upon the payments to meet his regular expenditures (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Harris [1980] FCA 60; (1980) 30 ALR 10 per Bowen C.J. at p 15), the payments were calculated as weekly payments by reference to notional weekly earnings and were received fortnightly by the respondent over a significant period of time. The Compensation Act provided for the payments to be calculated as weekly payments and paid regularly. The calculation of such payments by reference to, and as part of, a weekly income and regular receipt thereof may by sufficient to attract the character of income to the payments. (See Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Slaven at p 92.) In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540 at pp 567-568, Fullagar J. said the following:

"It seems to me that the appellant's receipts from

Macdonald, Hamilton & Co. must be regarded as

having the character of income. They were regular

periodical payments - a matter which has been

regarded in the cases as having some importance in

determining whether particular receipts possess

the character of income or capital in the hands of

the recipient, see e.g. Seymour v. Reed (1927)

AC 554 at p 570 and Atkinson v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 64; (1951) 84 CLR 298.

This consideration, while not unimportant, is not

decisive. What is, to my mind, decisive is that

the expressed object and the actual effect of the

payments made was to make an addition to the

earnings, the undoubted income, of the respondent.

What the employing firm decided to do, and what it

really did, in relation to the respondent and

others in the same position, was 'to make up the

difference between their present rate of wages and

the amount they will receive'. What is paid is

not salary or remuneration, and it is not paid in

respect of or in relation to any employment of the

recipient. But it is intended to be, and is in

fact, a substitute for - the equivalent pro tanto

of - the salary or wages which would have been

earned and paid if the enlistment had not taken

place. As such, it must be income, even though it

is paid voluntarily and there is not even a moral

obligation to continue making the payments. It

acquires the character of that for which it is

substituted and that to which it is added."

That passage was explained by Bowen C.J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Harris at pp 15-16:

"Fullagar J took a somewhat different view. He

said at p 564: 'The fact of the respondent's

employment explains the selection of him as a

recipient, but it in no degree characterizes the

payment. The payment does not partake in any

degree of the character of a reward for services

rendered or to be rendered.'

His Honour, in referring to the respondent's

employment, appears to be referring to his

employment with the firm. Certainly the payment

was not the product of that employment. In

contrast with the judgment of Dixon CJ and

Williams J, his Honour's judgment seems to place

no weight on the fact that the payment was

incidental to or flowed from his enlistment and

service in the military forces.

In arriving at the conclusion that the receipts

were assessable income Fullagar J appears to have

had regard to three considerations: first, they

were 'regular periodical payments'; secondly, the

expressed object and the actual effect of the

payments was 'to make an addition to the earnings,

the undoubted income' of Mr Dixon; and, thirdly,

the payments were intended to be, and in fact

were, 'a substitute for - the equivalent pro tanto

of - the salary or wages which would have been

earned and paid if the enlistment had not taken

place'. The reference to the payment taking the

character of that for which it is substituted

contains an echo of the successful argument of

Fullagar QC (as he then was) in C of T (Vic) v.

Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 55 CLR 144.

Certainly, the payments to Mr Dixon had a

significant periodical character as an addition to

his wages. But it seems to have been the

coincidence of the fact that payment was an

addition to wages and the fact that the payment

was in substitution for wages he would have earned

if he had remained with the firm, which his Honour

regarded as important. His Honour, at p 568,

said: 'It acquires the character of that for which

it is substituted and that to which it is added.'"

54. Periodicity of payment alone may not be determinative of the question of whether the payments are in the nature of income but such a circumstance is important and additional circumstances may make it clear that the periodical payments do have such a character. Although the payments made to the respondent were not in substitution for wages lost, the payments were intended to be a supplement to whatever income the respondent enjoyed and, furthermore, each payment as it was made was intended to operate as a weekly amelioration of any realization of his impaired capacity to earn a weekly income. (See Tinkler v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 88; (1979) 10 ATR 411 per Brennan J. at p 414.)

55. Soon after the payments commenced, the respondent sought to redeem his entitlement to compensation for his incapacity, measured under the Act in the terms of weekly payments, by redeeming his entitlement to such payments in the manner provided by s.67 of the Compensation Act. However, the respondent could not reach agreement with the insurer of his former employer as to the sum to be paid to discharge the employee's liability under the Act. If the respondent had been able to commute his entitlement, the sum received may have been a capital receipt as far as the respondent was concerned having regard to the particular circumstances of his case, but the receipt of regular periodical payments pursuant to the scheme for such payments provided by the Compensation Act was able to give what otherwise may have been a capital receipt the character of income. The circumstances and manner of payment may convert what would otherwise be capital payments into income. (See Egerton Warburton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 40; (1934) 51 CLR 568 per Rich, Dixon and McTiernan JJ. at pp 572-573.)

56. The Tribunal erred in finding that periodicity of payment stood alone and that no other pertinent factors were present. For the reasons set out above, the Tribunal was obliged to consider a number of factors which in fact gave the periodical payments the character of income.

57. In the light of the foregoing, it is unnecessary to determine the Commissioner's contention that the payments were assessable pursuant to sub-s.26(e) of the Act. That argument was not addressed to the Tribunal and the appellant having been content to conduct its case in that way before the Tribunal must have some difficulty in contending that the Tribunal erred in failing to find that the payments were so assessable particularly where the appeal is restricted to a question of law. (Smith v. Baker [1891] UKHL 2; (1891) AC 325, cf. Pittalis v. Grant (1989) 2 All ER 622 at p 627 et seq.) However, the following comments may be made on the argument.

58. For the reasons set out above, which led to the conclusion that no single payment bore the character of income in ordinary terms because the payments were in discharge of a statutory obligation to recompense the respondent for his loss of capacity to earn income, it could not be said that payments were in the nature of a reward given or granted in respect of the respondent's employment possessing a value capable of being included in his assessable income. Quite simply, payments of compensation in replacement of a loss of earning capacity occasioned to an employee by virtue of his or her employment cannot be regarded as a perquisite of employment in the nature of a benefit or allowance. Such compensation was not a discretionary payment which, upon receipt, had the effect of enhancing the employee's remuneration and it is to that form of "compensation" to which sub-s.26(e) is directed. The payments made under the Compensation Act were not "compensations allowed, given or granted" to the respondent in respect of his employment. The payments represented the recovery of an entitlement under the Compensation Act. The employer did not make such payments as a matter of discretion. The employer was under a statutory liability to make such payments in the same way that an employer may have been liable at common law to pay damages to an employee. (See Constable v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 64; (1952) 86 CLR 402 per Dixon C.J., McTiernan, Williams and Fullagar JJ. at p 418.) It is unnecessary to resolve whether sub-s.26(e) only applies to receipts which were in the nature of income according to ordinary concepts. (See Hayes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation; Scott v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514 per Fullagar J. at pp 525-526; Reseck v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 38; (1975) 133 CLR 45 per Gibbs J. at p 48; Smith v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1987] HCA 48; (1987) 164 CLR 513 per Brennan J. at pp 521-523.)

59. In conclusion, it is necessary to deal with the argument presented on behalf of the respondent that the appeal was not competent in that it was not on a question of law as required by sub-s.44(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cwth). The nature of such an appeal was considered in detail in TNT Skypack International (Aust.) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1988] FCA 119; (1988) 82 ALR 175 by Gummow J. at pp 178-182. In its amended notice of appeal, the appellant contended that, inter alia, the appeal was based on the question of law of whether the payments of compensation were assessable income within the meaning of sub-s.25(1) of the Act and stated as a ground of appeal that the Tribunal should have held that the compensation payments were income according to ordinary concepts by virtue of the regularity and periodicity of the payments.

60. In the circumstances of this case, the determination of whether the compensation payments were assessable income within the meaning of sub-s.25(1) of the Act, required more than a mere conclusion of fact. Firstly, the Tribunal had to construe the terms of the Compensation Act to ascertain as a starting point what character was given to the payments by the provisions of that Act. Secondly, the Tribunal had to determine whether the principle of periodicity as expressed by various cases, made the payments assessable income within the meaning of sub-s.25(1) of the Act. The first issue was a question of law and the second issue involved a combination of law and fact. An appeal which alleges that a determination on the latter question has been made in error by a failure to duly apply relevant principles of law will be an appeal on a question of law. The question of whether the payments were affected by the principle of periodicity was not a simple question of fact. (See Lombardo v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 66; (1979) 28 ALR 574 per Bowen C.J. at pp 576-578; TNT Skypack International (Aust.) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation at pp 102-103; Farmer v. Trustees of the Late William Cotton (1915) AC 922 per Lord Parker and Waddington at p 932; Hayes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation at p 51; Hope v. The Council of the City of Bathurst [1980] HCA 16; (1980) 144 CLR 1 per Mason J. at p 7.)

61. A finding in this case that there were no relevant factors to allow any analysis to be undertaken as to the significance of the periodicity of the payments of compensation to the respondent necessarily involved a misunderstanding of the relevant principles of law to be applied. (See Lombardo v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation per Bowen C.J. at p 578; Jolley v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 86 ALR 297, per Burchett and Lee JJ. at p 299.)

62. Accordingly, the appeal raised a clear question of law and was competent.

63. For the reasons expressed above, I am of the opinion that the appeal should be upheld. Although the question of law in this matter arises out of a mixed question of fact and law which may normally require the matter to be remitted to the Tribunal for further consideration in accordance with these reasons (see Jolley v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation at p 309), the conclusion of fact is inescapable and this Court should set aside the Tribunal's decision and confirm the assessments. (See Statham v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 89 ATC 4070 at p 4075.) The appellant having agreed to bear the respondent's costs of the appeal, there will be no order for costs.
