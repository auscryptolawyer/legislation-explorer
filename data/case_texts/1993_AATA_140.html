Eric Trusty and Repatriation Commission [1993] AATA 140; (1993) 30 ALD 422 (5 May 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ERIC TRUSTY v. REPATRIATION COMMISSION

No. P92/514

AAT No. 8684

Number of pages - 3

Veterans' Affairs

[1993] AATA 140; (1993) 30 ALD 422

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

VETERANS' APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Veterans Affairs - benefits claim - short period of service - discharged on ground of false medical information given at time of enlistment - whether applicant falls within the terms of s 69 - whether applicant entitled to apply for pension under Part IV

Defence Act 1903, 44, s 124(1)(a)

Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (Cth) s 69(1)(d)(ii), 69(5)

Secretary, Department of Social Security v Salvona 18 ALD 289

Ziems v Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of New South Wales [1957] HCA 46; (1957) 97 CLR 279

HEARING

SYDNEY, 22 April 1993

5:5:1993

Solicitor for the Applicant: Lega Aid Commission (Ms C Weeks)

Solicitor for the Respondent: Department of Veterans' Affairs

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

B.J. McMAHON The applicant lodged a claim for benefits under the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 in relation to "shrapnel wound, perforated ear drums, loss of IQ and insomnia". The applicant enlisted in the Australian Army on 15 August 1978 and was discharged on 11 May 1979. Although it is conceded that he engaged himself for the usual period of 3 years' service, in fact his actual period of service was some 9 months.

2. The Repatriation Commission, noting the truncated period of service, and noting that the grounds for discharge were that he had given false medical evidence at the time of his enlistment, found that he was not entitled to benefits under the Act, pursuant to s 69. This determination was affirmed by the Veterans' Review Board and is brought before this Tribunal for further review.

3. Section 69 defines those members of the Defence Force to whom Part IV of the Act applies. This part deals with pensions for members of the Defence Force and their dependants. The section is expressed to apply primarily to persons who have completed 3 years effective full time service as a member of the Defence Force. An ameliorating provision has been included in sub-paragraph 69 (1)(d)(ii). If a person's service is terminated before he completed 3 years effective full time service, "by reason of the person's death or the person's discharge on the ground of invalidity or physical or mental incapacity to perform duties" then the Part will nevertheless apply to that person.

4. An exception to the ameliorating provision is to be found in sub-section 69(5) which is in the following terms -

"s69(5)Paragraph (1)(d) or (e) does not apply in respect of a

person if the person's service as a member of the Defence Force

was terminated by reason of the person's discharge, or the

termination of the person's appointment -

(a) before the person had completed 12 months' effective full-time

service; and

(b) on the ground of invalidity or physical or mental incapacity to

perform duties, being invalidity or incapacity caused, or

substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition that -

(i) existed at the time the person commenced continuous full-time

service as a member of the Defence Force; and

(ii) had not been aggravated, or materially aggravated, by that

service."

5. Thus it will be seen that in order to qualify for pension, a person must show either that he has completed 3 years' service or (relevantly) that he was discharged "on the ground of invalidity, or physical or mental incapacity to perform duties". Consequently the further concession provided by sub-section (5) is available only if the reasons for discharge are "on the ground of invalidity or physical or mental incapacity to perform duties".

6. Section 124(1)(a) of the Defence Act 1903 empowers the Governor-General to make regulations prescribing matters in relation to the discharge of members of the Defence Force. Pursuant to that power, regulation 176 sets out comprehensively the only reasons available for the discharge of a soldier. Paragraph (h) provides that a soldier may be discharged if "the soldier is medically unfit". This is defined in sub-regulation (3) to include "unfit because of mental incapacity". Paragraph (p) sets out the ground relied upon by the Army, namely : "that the soldier gave false information to a person to whom the information was required to be given in connection with his enlistment".

7. Following several medical and psychiatric examinations, steps were taken to have the applicant discharged. There was, however, no formal finding that he was medically unfit. A medical board was recommended (Exhibit 1 page 10) but before the recommendation could be acted upon, it was discovered that the applicant had had a past history of severe mental illness. Because this had not been disclosed upon his enlistment, it was decided to discharge him. The order for his discharge, signed by a lieutenant colonel, set out in paragraph 2 -

"The reason for discharge is AMR (Australian Military Regulations)

176 (1)(b) having given false information in connection with his

enlistment (ie failed to disclose full medical history)."

The particulars added to the ground were given in conformity with the Army's own practice direction which appears in Exhibit 3 at page 26.

8. Prima facie, therefore, the applicant does not comply with even the first requirement of s 69. He was not discharged on the ground of invalidity, or physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties. Nevertheless, it was submitted on his behalf that he should be regarded as qualifying.

9. Although the applicant's solicitor agreed that the lieutenant colonel's decision could not be reviewed in this Tribunal, she nevertheless submitted that it was based on a misapprehension and that the applicant ought to be given the opportunity of being accepted under the first part of s 69 so as to test whether his disability fell within sub-section 69(5). This submission was based upon some observations by Dr Grady, a psychiatrist, who examined the applicant prior to his discharge. Dr Grady found that although the applicant admitted that he had been in a psychiatric ward at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital in 1977, when the records show that he was severely disturbed and probable schizophrenia was diagnosed, Mr Trusty told Dr Grady that he was "stiff from too much running and had an injection which fixed him up". He also denied that he had a nervous break down on this occasion and alleged that he simply had ear trouble and was medically misdirected and then upset when he found himself in the psychiatric ward. Dr Grady found that although a firm diagnosis could not be made beyond immature and inadequate personality, the facts were that Mr Trusty had had 2 break downs with grossly abnormal behaviour and in the face of his total denial of feeling disturbed in any way, had been inaccessible for treatment.

10. Upon the basis of these observations it was submitted that the applicant did not have the necessary will when he enlisted to make a false statement as to his previous medical history. The requirement for full knowledge and capacity to exist for an allegation of giving false information to be supported was considered in passing in Secretary, Department of Social Security v Salvona 18 ALD 289. The solicitor for the applicant also referred to Ziems v Prothonotary of Supreme Court [1957] HCA 46; (1957) 97 CLR 279 as an example of the circumstances in which courts would go behind ostensible facts.

11. In my view this submission can not succeed. Whether or not the applicant told deliberate lies on his enlistment, whether the lieutenant colonel should have satisfied himself as to the intentional basis of the applicant's conduct before issuing his order and whether or not the grounds for discharge were appropriate are not matters that are reviewable in this application. The words of the sub-section are quite clear and must be given their ordinary natural meaning. The applicant was not discharged for any of the statutory reasons.

12. There has been no medical finding whether or not the applicant was in fact physically or mentally incapable of performing his duties. He was discharged on a ground specifically provided for in the regulations and of which particulars were given to him. In my view, there is simply no basis open to this Tribunal to review this ground or the manner in which it was arrived at.

13. The submissions put by the solicitor for the applicant may go to the question of mens rea where criminal charges are brought. In my view, they are irrelevant to the considerations presently before the Tribunal. Section 44 of the Defence Act provides that where a soldier has been discharged for one of the prescribed reasons and a delegate of the chief of the general staff is satisfied that the soldier could probably have been discharged for another prescribed reason, then he may at the request of the soldier, take such steps that are necessary to ensure that he is treated as having been discharged for that other reason. It may be open to the applicant to apply under this section, although as I have said there is no concluded medical evidence before me determining that the applicant was incapable of performing his duties for medical reasons. This, however, is a matter for the applicant to pursue if he wishes. For the present, it seems to me that he does not fall within the terms of s 69 and that consequently he is not entitled to apply for pension under Part IV.

14. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
