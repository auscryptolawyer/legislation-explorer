Victor Henry Starr and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria [1997] AATA 144 (1 May 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

VICTOR HENRY STARR v. TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF VICTORIA

No. V96/496

AAT No.

11824

Number of pages - 7

Taxation



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

G.L. McDONALD (Deputy President) AND C. RE (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - registration as tax agent - criteria for registration - "relevant experience" - agent worked as ASC officer - duties involved some taxation issues - whether employment involved "substantial" involvement "in the preparation and examination of income tax returns"

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) ss.251BC, 251JC

Income Tax Assessment Regulations - 1936 (Cth) reg.156

Re Civiti and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria (1990) 90 ATC 2039

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 9 April 1997 (hearing), 1 May 1997 (decision)

1:5:1997

Counsel for the applicant: E. Power

Solicitor for the applicant: Andrew H. Freadman & Associates

Solicitor for the respondent: S. McBurney, Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

G.L. McDONALD, Deputy President and Dr C. RE, Member

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. The applicant is applying for the review of a decision dated 9 April 1996 made by the respondent, refusing his re-registration as a tax agent.

2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr E. Power, of counsel, and the respondent was represented by Mr S. McBurney, of the Australian Government Solicitor's Office. The Tribunal had before it affidavits from the applicant, Mr D.J. Clements, Mr R. Brown, Mr O.M. Boak, Mr G.M. Wilson and Mr L.K. Wilmot, all filed on behalf of the applicant. The applicant, Mr Clements and Mr Brown gave oral evidence in support of the application. Mr F. Pietka, the secretary of the Tax Agents' Board of Victoria ("the Board"), gave oral evidence on behalf of the respondent. The Tribunal had before it the documents filed for the purposes of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the "T" documents) which included a supplementary set of documents, as well as other documentary evidence tendered during the course of the hearing.

3. The applicant was employed by the Australian Taxation Office ("ATO") between 1972 and 1988. During this period of time, he completed a Bachelor of Business (Accountancy) and a Graduate Diploma in Taxation at the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology. In 1988 he left the ATO and commenced employment with the then National Companies and Securities Commission ("NCSC"). In November 1989 he applied for and obtained registration as a tax agent. At the time of that registration his work and experience within the ATO were determinative in securing his registration given that his duties included assessment of income tax payable by personal, corporate and trust entities. His registration was renewed in 1992 for a further period of three years and, again, his background in the ATO was a determinative factor in securing re-registration.

4. Since joining the NCSC, the applicant engaged in a part-time capacity in work as a registered tax agent. In 1992 the NCSC was replaced by the Australian Securities Commission ("ASC") and the applicant has continued to be employed by that body in a full-time capacity as an investigator, as well as continuing to maintain his part-time practice as a tax agent. He runs that practice from his home where he has a computer that allows him to electronically submit returns to the ATO on behalf of his clients. He told the Tribunal that he belongs to the Taxpayers Association and receives publications from the Australian Society of Certified Practising Accountants, as well as having access to the ASC library, and the Supreme Court of Victoria library. He can access ATO Rulings by using his computer.

5. Section 251JC of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") sets out the criteria to be fulfilled in order for the Board to grant re-registration of a tax agent as follows:

" 251JC The Board shall re-register the applicant as a tax agent if the applicant satisfies the Board that:

(a) if the applicant is a natural person:

(i) the applicant is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters; and

(ii) the applicant is not an undischarged bankrupt;

(b) . . . ."

Section 251BC of the Act provides the following guidance with respect to determination of who is a ". . . fit and proper person."

" 251BC (1) Without limiting the generality of an expression used in this Part, but subject to this section, a person is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters, as at a particular time, if:

(a) the person is not a natural person;

(b) both the following conditions are satisfied:

(i) the person was not registered as a tax agent, or a nominee, for the purposes of this Part immediately before the commencement of section 39 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (N( 2) 1988;

(ii) the person does not hold such qualifications (whether academic, by way of experience or otherwise) as are prescribed:

(c) the person has not attained the age of 18 years;

(d) the person is not of good fame, integrity and character;

(e) the person has not been convicted of a serious taxation offence during the previous 5 years; or

(f) the person is under sentence of imprisonment for a serious taxation offence."

The above disqualification provisions are to be read compendiously. It is not disputed, and the Tribunal accepts, that the applicant does not stand to be disqualified under any of the provisions except for sub-section (1)(b)(ii) (sub-clause (b)(i) not being applicable to his circumstances). Regulation 156(2) deals with prescribed qualifications for the purposes of fulfilling the provisions of s.251BC(1)(b)(ii) of the Act. Regulation 156(1) deals with both the need to fulfil academic qualification as well as prescribing what will be required by way of "experience". It is not disputed that, and the Tribunal accepts, the applicant's academic qualifications satisfy the requirements of the regulation. The regulation refers to the necessary experience being obtained by way of "relevant employment" and in this case that relevant employment must be engaged on a full-time basis for not less than a total of two years in the preceding five years or its equivalent (regulation 156(2)(c)(ii)). Regulation 156(2) sets out the following prescription for "relevant employment":

" 156 (2) In this regulation `relevant employment' means employment by a person or a partnership or as a member of a partnership in the course of which there has been substantial involvement in income tax matters including:

(a) the preparation or examination of a broad range of income tax returns;

(b) the preparation or examination of objections to assessments issued in respect of such returns; and

(c) the provision of advice in relation to income tax returns, assessments and objections."

It is against the criteria set out in regulation 156(2) that the Tribunal, standing in the shoes of the Board, is to reach its determination on the evidence before it as to whether or not the applicant should be registered as a tax agent.

6. On behalf of the applicant it is submitted that his employment with the ASC, as an investigator, constitutes "relevant employment" which either is, or should be, regarded as equivalent to relevant employment for the purposes of the regulation. In his affidavit, as elaborated upon in his oral evidence, the applicant outlined as part of the investigative work he carries out with, initially the NCSC and, subsequently, with the ASC as involving him in examining the accuracy of accounting and auditing records, including those to do with income tax liability for the purposes of assessing the accuracy and adequacy of the fiscal disclosures made for purposes of satisfying the Corporations Law. He exampled an investigation relating to a gold mining company issuing a Part A takeover statement, where the applicant said that, as a result of his examination of the accounts, it was determined that the "properly claimable expenditure exceeded taxable income giving rise to tax losses". It was the evidence of the applicant that in examining company returns there were also subsidiary returns concerning partnerships and individuals which sometimes also needed to be examined.

7. The applicant also said that his duties involved examining accounting records to assess the liability of directors for companies placed in liquidation to see whether any offences had been committed, e.g. whether the company had traded while insolvent. In relation to this type of activity the applicant stated (affidavit, para 5.1):

" Therefore it was crucial to determine when and why companies debts accrued to the Australian Taxation Office and to determine whether or not directors caused a company to trade in circumstances contrary to the law."

The applicant also outlined the circumstances in which, in the course of carrying out an investigation, he determined the party which should bear the responsibility for the payment of sales tax. Other cases with which he had more recently been concerned dealt with an examination of a company's balance sheets and profit and loss statements to determine whether or not there were sufficient funds to meet the company's current liabilities, including any income tax which may be payable.

8. The applicant stated that he was known amongst the ASC investigators as somebody to whom questions relating to taxation matters could be addressed. He was regarded as having some expertise in this area. He was supported on this point by the evidence of Mr Clements and Mr Brown.

9. The applicant was also asked to research and prepare on behalf of the ASC a paper concerning the Victorian Regional ASC office's liability for the payment of fringe benefit tax. Since August 1995, the applicant kept a diary, setting out the time spent within the ASC looking at taxation issues, and that is set out at annexure 1 to his affidavit. The diary entries overwhelming show that the majority of time spent was spent in the research and preparation of the fringe benefit's tax paper. Additionally, some of the other entries, as was also evident from the material contained in the applicant's affidavit, dealt with sales tax. Neither fringe benefits tax nor sales tax can, in the Tribunal's opinion, be regarded as "income tax matters" and, accordingly, do not fall within the terms of regulation 156(2).

10. However, the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant in his capacity as an investigator with the ASC is engaged in carrying out investigations in which he was required to examine income tax liability for companies and that this involved him in examining income tax returns. The Tribunal also accepts that the applicant, because of his previous history with working at the ATO, was regarded as a resource for other investigators when they had queries concerning income tax matters. The Tribunal is also satisfied that the applicant, in his work as an investigator with the ASC, had an involvement in the examination of a range of income tax returns. That finding alone, however, is not sufficient to fulfil the requirements of the regulation. As Deputy President Forrest pointed out in Re Civiti and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria (1990) 90 ATC 2039 (at 2043), it is necessary for an applicant to show both "substantial" involvement in the examination and preparation of income tax returns as well as that a broad range of such returns has been undertaken.

11. The Tribunal is satisfied that the evidence of the applicant and his witnesses was not sufficient to show that he had a "substantial" involvement in the examination of income tax returns. The use of the word "substantial" in this context relates to "of ample or considerable amount, quantity or dimensions" (the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary on Historical Principles) and requires more than simply that the applicant being involved from time to time as part of his work as an ASC investigator in the examination of income tax returns. The Tribunal is fortified in its view by considering the objective sought to be achieved by the introduction of the current regulatory regime for tax agents.[1] These provisions are aimed at ensuring people who are engaged in the preparation of income tax returns and advice for reward have appropriate knowledge of the law and practice (see Re Civiti (supra), p.2042). An ASC investigator has the major responsibility of investigating whether there have been breaches of the Corporations Law which may involve, as an incidental function, an examination from time to time of whether there have been breaches by corporations relating to information contained in income tax returns. The two functions are different. In the Tribunal's view, the applicant's position as a full-time investigator within the ASC, in which he carries out some examination of income tax returns of companies, does not sufficiently equate to result in him having a "substantial" involvement in the examination of income tax returns.

12. In reaching its conclusion the Tribunal must also look at the work that the applicant has carried out over the years in his part-time capacity as a tax agent. In that capacity he has told the Tribunal that he has prepared income tax returns on behalf of individuals, trusts and corporations. He has also prepared accounts for businesses and given advice with respect to taxation matters, including advice as to whether or not self-funded superannuation schemes should be established to achieve inter alia some income tax relief. The applicant has also prepared four notices of objection. He is, as is evidenced by the affidavits filed by Mr Boak, Mr Wilson and Mr Wilmot, highly regarded for the work which he has carried out on behalf of those clients. The Tribunal accepts that in carrying out his part-time practice he has prepared a "broad range of income tax returns" as required by regulation 156(2)(a).

13. The evidence showed that he had carried out this work on behalf of approximately 30 clients. Some of those clients were partnerships and that may have involved him in the preparation of partnership returns as well as returns for the individuals constituting the partnerships. There was no evidence to suggest other than the clients on whose behalf he had carried out the work were small business operators. The affidavits of the parties mentioned confirm this. Annexure 2 to his witness statement sets out the time that he spends involved in carrying out the accounting and taxation work on behalf of his clients. It shows that he has spent approximately 380 hours, between 1 July 1996 to 22 March 1997, engaged in this work. He told the Tribunal that he incurred a tax loss from this activity. From the totality of the evidence before the Tribunal, the Tribunal is satisfied that again the applicant has not been able to satisfy the Tribunal that he has had a "substantial involvement" in income tax matters on behalf of individual clients.

14. The Tribunal has considered whether the work he does within the ASC combined with the work that he carries out as a tax agent together satisfy the requirement of there being substantial involvement. Again, bearing in mind the objectives to be achieved by the Act, the Tribunal remains unconvinced that the applicant's involvement from both activities could be described as "substantial".

15. In reaching its conclusion the Tribunal has taken into account the fact that the applicant is seeking re-registration rather than registration ab inito. However, it is apparent from the provisions of the Act that those registered must have a current appreciation of the law and practice involved with income tax. Both the law and practices in this area change rapidly over time. While the applicant may well have had current knowledge at the time of his initial registration in 1989 and at the time of the 1992 re-registration, unless he can show a substantial involvement so that he or she is considered to have a current understanding of the law and practice at the time the application for registration is considered, then the applicant will not satisfy the registration requirements.

16. It was also suggested to us that, if the applicant was not re-registered, he stands to lose an ability to earn income, particularly since, as the evidence of both the applicant and Mr Clements showed, the applicant's position with the ASC is precarious. There was evidence presented to the Tribunal that the applicant may be made redundant at the ASC. The evidence suggested that the ASC is facing considerable financial difficulty, and may have to retrench several staff members at its Melbourne office. The applicant falls within a class of officers who will, according to the evidence presented to the Tribunal, be targeted for redundancy. There was no suggestion, and the Tribunal accepts that this was related to the applicant's work performance. The applicant also said that he is, and had been, building up his business as a tax agent to enable him to have the flexibility to turn to it on a full-time basis should he lose his job with the ASC. This is not a factor adverted to in the regulations, but is one which in cases where a person's sole livelihood arises from his or her work as a tax agent needs to be considered. Whatever may be the future of the applicant's employment with the ASC is a matter of some speculation. Currently the applicant does not rely on his tax agent's work for his income. Indeed, the evidence shows that he makes a tax loss from it (albeit, as he said, as part of building his practice up). Accordingly, this aspect does not arise for consideration in this particular instance.

17. For the reasons given, the decision under review is affirmed.

FOOTNOTE

[1] By the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No 2) 78 of 1988, with effect from 1 November 1988
