REASONS FOR JUDGMENT

GOODMAN J:

A. INTRODUCTION

  1. On 27 February 2026, a Registrar of this Court made an order for the winding up in insolvency of, and appointed a liquidator to:
(1) Oldfields Holdings Ltd (OLH) in proceeding NSD1663/2025; and

(2) Oldfields Pty Ltd in proceeding NSD1654/2025.

  1. OLH was listed on the Australian Securities Exchange Limited (ASX). Oldfields is a subsidiary of OLH. I will refer to OLH and Oldfields as the companies.
  2. Each order was made on the application of the plaintiff, the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation.
  3. The basis of each order was the insolvency of the company the subject of the order, a matter which was deemed by operation of s 459C of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) as a result of the failure of that company to comply with a written statutory demand which the Commissioner had served upon it.
  4. On 2 March 2026, an interlocutory application was filed in NSD1663/2025 in the name of OLH (as first applicant), Oldfields (as second applicant) and EQM Holdings Pty Ltd (as third applicant). EQM Holdings holds more than 40 per cent of the issued shares in OLH.
  5. On 6 March 2026, I heard the application – by reference to an amended interlocutory application – which was advanced on behalf of EQM and Mr Frank Lesko, a director of OLH and Oldfields. Leave was sought to file the amended interim application.
  6. The orders sought in the amended interlocutory application include relevantly: (1) an order adding EQM and Mr Lesko as parties to NSD1663/2025; (2) orders granting leave to Mr Lesko to bring an application pursuant to 35A of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth) (FCA Act) for review of the Registrar’s orders; (3) orders staying, or setting aside the Registrar’s orders; (4) orders adjourning the hearing of the Commissioner’s creditor’s petitions; and (5) orders staying the winding up of the companies. The amended interlocutory application called in aid s 35A of the FCA Act and s 482 of the Corporations Act.
  7. There are several procedural irregularities in the manner in which the application was made (including the absence of a separate interlocutory application in NSD1654/2025). However, no such point was taken and the arguments made in opposition to the orders sought focussed upon issues of substance rather than form. I have adopted the same approach.
  8. The amended interlocutory application was opposed by the Commissioner, the liquidator and the companies.

B. JOINDER OF PARTIES

  1. Mr Lesko is, as noted above, a director of each of the companies. Recognising the operation of s 198G of the Corporations Act, he seeks to be added as a party to each proceeding so that he can pursue a review application under s 35A of the FCA Act on behalf of OLH and Oldfields.
  2. Section 198G of the Corporations Act, provides in so far as is presently relevant:
198G Exercise of powers while company under external administration
Powers of officers while company under external administration

(1) While a company is under external administration, an officer of the company must not perform or exercise a function or power of that office.

...

Exceptions

(3) Subsections (1) and (2) do not apply to the extent that the officer of the company is acting:
...

(b) with the written approval of ... the Court;

...
Definitions

(9) In this section:
external administration of a company has the same meaning as in Schedule 2.
  1. The companies are each in “external administration” because of the appointment of the liquidator: s 5-15 of Schedule 2 to the Corporations Act.
  2. The effect of s 198G(1) and (3) of the Corporations Act is that the directors of OLH and Oldfields are unable to cause those companies to pursue an application for review without, relevantly, the written approval of the Court.
  3. The considerations relevant to the exercise of the discretion to grant such leave include: the strength of the case to be argued on review, the protection of the assets of the company and the “constitutional imperative” of the right to review the Registrar’s decision in the original jurisdiction of the Court: see, e.g., HVAC Construction (QLD) Pty Ltd v Energy Equipment Engineering Pty Ltd [2002] FCA 1638; (2002) 44 ACSR 169 at 181 ([40] to [41]) and 181 to 182 [44] (French J); Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Soiland Pty Ltd (In Liq) [2010] FCA 168 at [16] ff (Barker J); and Woori International Pty Ltd, in the matter of TJM Holdings Group Pty Ltd (In Liquidation) [2025] FCA 1276 at [17] to [19] (Owens J).
  4. The Commissioner and the liquidator opposed the grant of leave principally on the basis that it would expose OLH to the costs of defending the application. However, Mr Hall, solicitor, who appeared on behalf of Mr Lesko and EQM, indicated that an exposure to an adverse costs order was a risk that Mr Lesko would assume upon being made a party to the proceeding. In these circumstances – and taking into account: (1) the “constitutional imperative” of the right to review, as described by French J in HVAC at 181 to 182 [44] by reference to the observations of Finn J in Official Trustee in Bankruptcy v Nedlands Pty Ltd (In Liquidation) [2000] FCA 599; (2000) 99 FCR 554; and (2) the fact that the case to be argued on review does not appear to be so lacking in merit that leave ought not be given – I am satisfied that Mr Lesko should be granted leave to be joined as a party to each proceeding so that he may pursue the s 35A application for review in each proceeding.
  5. EQM is a creditor of OLH. As such it has standing under s 482 of the Corporations Act to seek a stay of the winding up. I am satisfied that it should be added as a party to proceeding NSD1663/2025. As the evidence does not establish that EQM is a creditor of Oldfields, no such leave should be granted in proceeding NSD1654/2025.

D. REVIEW UNDER SECTION 35A OF THE FCA ACT

  1. Against that background, I turn first to the application for review under s 35A of the FCA Act. That section provides, in so far as is presently relevant, that:
35A Powers of Registrars
(1) Subject to subsection (2), the following powers of the Court may, if the Court or a Judge so directs, be exercised by a Registrar:
...

(h) a power of the Court prescribed by Rules of Court.
...

(5) A party to proceedings in which a Registrar has exercised any of the powers of the Court under subsection (1) may, within the time prescribed by the Rules of Court, or within any further time allowed in accordance with the Rules of Court, apply to the Court to review that exercise of power.

(6) The Court may, on application under subsection (5) or of its own motion, review an exercise of power by a Registrar pursuant to this section and may make such order or orders as it thinks fit with respect to the matter with respect to which the power was exercised.

...
  1. An application under s 35A of the FCA Act may be made by a “party to proceedings in which a Registrar has exercised any of the powers of the Court under subsection (1)”. In the present proceedings the companies are parties. As noted above, leave is to be given to Mr Lesko to pursue a review of the Registrar’s decisions on behalf of the companies. Further, the Registrar was exercising a power prescribed by the Rules of Court: see r 16.1 of and Schedule 2, item 62 to, the Federal Court (Corporations) Rules 2000 (Cth).
  2. The review of a Registrar’s decision is a rehearing de novo in which the Court may receive fresh evidence and depart from factual findings made by the Registrar. It is also a complete rehearing in which the Commissioner, as the moving party before the Registrar, bears the onus of satisfying the Court that a winding up order should have been made. The Court is involved in a fresh exercise of the power to make the winding up order based upon the facts and law as at the time the review is undertaken; and the decision to be made on review is a decision by the Court as to how it would exercise the power and not whether error on the part of the Registrar has been demonstrated: see Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Melking Holdings Pty Ltd [2019] FCA 988; (2019) 137 ACSR 411 at 414 to 415 ([11] and [14]) (Colvin J); Bechara v Bates [2021] FCAFC 34; (2021) 286 FCR 166 at 176 [27], 185 to 186 [74], 189 to 190 [88] and 190 [92] (Allsop CJ, Markovic and Colvin JJ); and Robson v Body Corporate for Sanderling at Kings Beach CTS 2942 [2021] FCAFC 143; (2021) 286 FCR 494 at 502 [21] (Allsop CJ) and 525 ([151] to [152] per Colvin J).
  3. It is common ground that the companies are insolvent and not in a position to pay the debts they owe to the Commissioner.
  4. In IOC Australia Pty Ltd v Mobil Oil Australia Ltd [1905] ArgusLawRp 98; (1975) 11 ALR 417 at 427 Gibbs J, with whom Stephen and Jacobs JJ agreed, explained that “[t]he authorities show that as a general rule a creditor who cannot obtain payment is, as between himself and the company that owes the debt, entitled to a winding-up order as a matter of right ...”.
  5. The reservation of a discretion not to order an insolvent company be wound up is consistent with the terms of the Corporations Act: see in particular s 459A and s 467.
  6. The essence of the present application is the submission on behalf Mr Lesko that the Court should exercise its discretion so as to adjourn the hearing of the winding up applications in circumstances where, he contends, there is a genuine refinancing or capital raising proposal capable of satisfying the debts owing to the Commissioner within a short timeframe.
  7. The principal authority cited in support of this contention is the decision of Murphy J in Re Presha Engineering (Aust.) Pty. Ltd. (1983) 1 ACLC 675. In that case, his Honour noted at 676 and 677 that the usual position upon proof of insolvency arising from non-compliance with a statutory demand is that the defaulting company is wound up. However, his Honour noted, there may be exceptional cases in which the Court will exercise its discretion so as to adjourn the application. At 677, his Honour stated:
There are, however, clear expressions from the highest authority to the effect that the Court has a discretion and that in exceptional circumstances where there would be better prospects existing for the creditors as a whole if the company was allowed to trade on then there would be if the company was wound up, the Court has exercised its discretion, notwithstanding the entitlement of the petitioning creditor, and adjourned the petition to enable time to elapse for the Court to see whether or not the aspirations of the company or its directors will be realised, see, e.g. St. Thomas’ Dock’s case, (1876) 2 Ch.D. 116, per Jessel M.R.

(italic emphasis in original)
  1. In that case, His Honour made an order adjourning the application. Similar approaches have been taken in other cases: e.g., Re DTX Australia Ltd (1987) 11 ACLR 444 (Master Seaman QC); Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v T D Preece Pty Ltd [2013] FCA 1365; (2013) 96 ATR 361(Griffiths J); and Ace Contractors & Staff Pty Ltd v Westgarth Development Pty Ltd [1999] FCA 728 (Weinberg J).
  2. In other cases, such an argument has not found favour: e.g., Crema Pty Ltd (ACN 005 668 011) v Land Mark Property Developments Pty Ltd [2006] VSC 338; (2006) 58 ACSR 631 (Dodds‑Streeton J); Xat Ky v Australvic Property Management Pty Ltd [2007] FCA 1541 (Middleton J); Hickie v Land Enviro Corp Pty Limited, in the matter of Land and Enviro Corp Pty Limited [2014] FCA 1386 (Jacobson J); and T-S Capital Partners LLC v Paltar Petroleum Limited (administrators appointed), in the matter of Paltar Petroleum Limited (No 1) [2019] FCA 635 (Stewart J).
  3. Of course, each case turns on its particular facts. Nevertheless, it is instructive to consider the manner in which the assessment of whether to grant an adjournment has been made in previous applications. For example:
(1) in Re Huon Foam Pty Ltd [2000] TASSC 99, Justice Blow stated at [8] that “[a]s a general rule, a creditor of an insolvent company has a right ex debito justitiae (ie, as a matter of legal right) to have the company wound up: Mercantile Credits Ltd v Foster Clark (Australia) Ltd [1964] HCA 66; [1965] ALR 574 at 575; IOC Australia Pty Ltd v Mobil Oil Australia Ltd (1975) 49 ALJR 176 at 182; Re London Suburban Bank (1871) 6 Ch App 641 per Mellish LJ at 643; Re Presha Engineering (Aust.) Pty Ltd (1983) 1 ACLC 675. However, there are exceptional cases in which courts will refrain from making winding up orders in such circumstances”. His Honour declined the application on the basis that the company’s position was hopeless and there was no prospect of it ever trading again ([9]);

(2) in Westgarth, Justice Weinberg stated at [55] that “[w]hile as a general rule an applicant is entitled to a winding up order against a company which has failed to comply with a statutory demand it may, in appropriate circumstances, be better for the creditors as a whole to allow the company to trade on rather than have the company wound up.”;

(3) in Crema, Justice Dodds-Streeton stated at 656 [168] that “[t]he court may, in the exercise of that discretion, adjourn the application when it is credibly asserted that the company has good prospects of recovery in order to allow time to elapse ‘for the aspirations of the company or its directors to be realised’” (see also Paltar at [51]);

(4) in Australvic at [29(viii)], Justice Middleton stated that “[t]here is no material to demonstrate that the company, in its attempts to refinance or obtain sufficient funding, will succeed in the foreseeable future or at all”;

(5) in CP York Holdings Pty Ltd v The Food Improvers Pty Ltd [2009] NSWSC 409 at [9], Justice Barrett described each of Re Presha and Fire & All Risks Insurance Co Ltd v Southern Cross Exploration NL (1986) 10 ACLR 683 as “a case in which a court called upon to determine a winding up application adjourned the hearing on the footing that the defendant company's financial position looked likely to improve in such a way that winding up might be avoided”; and

(6) in Hickie, Justice Jacobson at [88] stated that “[c]ases such as Fire & All Risks, in which an adjournment was granted demonstrate that the discretion to order an adjournment should only be exercised so as to permit a short adjournment. This is consistent with the policy of the statutory scheme embodied in Part 5.4 of Div 4 of the Act that winding up applications should be determined expeditiously. In that regard, s 459R(1) provides that an application for a company to be wound up in insolvency is to be determined within six months after it is made.”

  1. The essential question for determination in the present case is whether an adjournment would likely produce a position in which the companies are returned to solvency sufficiently quickly and thus provide a better outcome for their creditors than winding up.
  2. I have reached the conclusion that the answer to that question is “no” and that each of the companies should be wound up, for the reasons which follow.
  3. Each is insolvent and this provides a very strong reason to make winding up orders: see IOC Australia ([47] above).
  4. I am also not persuaded that the allowance of more time is in the interests of the creditors of OLH and Oldfields, for the following reasons.
  5. First, the winding up applications were commenced on 12 and 15 September 2025. Section 459R of the Corporations Act provides that the applications are to be determined within six months (i.e. by 12 or 15 March 2026) unless the Court – upon satisfaction of the existence of special circumstances – makes an order extending that period. For the reasons which follow, the existence of special circumstances has not been established.
  6. Secondly, the winding up applications have already been adjourned numerous times, with the consequence that the companies have had almost six months in which to put in place an arrangement which will provide a better outcome for creditors than winding up. Previous estimates of the time for completion of the proposed capital raising have proven to be incorrect.
  7. Thirdly, the present circumstances do not engender confidence that a further adjournment would produce a better outcome for creditors than winding up. In particular:
(1) OLH’s shares are suspended from trading by dint of its failure to lodge its Appendix 4D and financial statements as at 31 December 2025 ([42] above). This is a matter that was brought to the attention of the Court by the Commissioner and was not addressed in the evidence adduced by EQM and Mr Lesko. There is also no evidence as to likely effect of OLH’s failures to comply with its obligations upon the likelihood of the proposed capital raising (a) going ahead and (b) being successful, but it seems likely to present a further and perhaps significant obstacle;

(2) there is considerable uncertainty as to the proposed capital raising and in particular:

(a) the terms on which the capital is to be raised. In this regard:
(i) the Clime letter provided:
Subject to all necessary approvals, due diligence and successful execution of binding agreements, Clime intends to raise between AUD $2.5 million and $5 million from its fund under management (subject to meeting investment mandate requirements) and / or capital under management to be utilised as capital to support the acquisition and ongoing operation of the combined group. For the avoidance of doubt, Clime is not committing to, guaranteeing or providing capital directly from its corporate balance sheet. We anticipate the capital raising process to run for at least a 60-day period. ;
(ii) the evidence does not disclose whether Clime has satisfied itself as to the existence of “all necessary approvals, due diligence and successful execution of binding agreements” or as to whether the anticipated 60 day period has commenced to run and if so, what Clime’s present view of the likely time period is;

(b) as to the compliance issues raised by the ASX, whether, when and how such issues will be resolved; and

(c) taking the above matters, and the unreliability of previous time estimates, into account the Court cannot be satisfied that the proposed capital raising will be complete by any particular date.

61 Fourthly, the PURE Asset 13 November 2025 letter does not contain anything of substance relevant to an assessment of the likelihood of a capital raising being successful. Further, its expression of support for OLH is now more than three months old, and there is no evidence that this remains its position.

  1. Fifthly, the loan deed referred to in the 27 February 2026 announcement was not in evidence and the nature of the conditions precedent is unknown.
  2. Finally, I do not accept the submission made on behalf of EQM and Mr Lesko that the Registrar’s actions in granting previous adjournments reflected a view that he had reached that the proposed capital raising was viable and needed only to clear some regulatory hurdles. This does not necessarily follow from the fact that an adjournment was granted. In any event, and with great respect, the Registrar’s views (assuming for the moment that such views were formed) are not of great significance given the de novo nature of the review.
  3. As I am satisfied that the companies should be wound up, the Registrar’s decisions should be affirmed.

E. SECTION 482 OF THE CORPORATIONS ACT

  1. I turn next to the application under s 482 of the Corporations Act. That section provides, in so far as is presently relevant:
482 Power to stay or terminate winding up
(1) At any time during the winding up of a company, the Court may, on application, make an order staying the winding up either indefinitely or for a limited time or terminating the winding up on a day specified in the order.

(1A) An application may be made by:
(a) in any case—the liquidator, or a creditor or contributory, of the company; or
...

  1. As noted above, EQM is a creditor of OLH and has standing to bring an application under s 482 of the Corporations Act.
  2. The application was argued on the basis that the considerations which inform the exercise of the discretion under s 482 of the Corporations Act to stay (or terminate) the winding up of OLH are materially the same as those considered as part of the review of the Registrar’s decision addressed above. It follows that I am not satisfied that a stay or termination of the winding up is appropriate.

F. CONCLUSION

  1. For the reasons set out above, the applications brought by EQM and Mr Lesko must be dismissed with costs. I will make orders accordingly.
