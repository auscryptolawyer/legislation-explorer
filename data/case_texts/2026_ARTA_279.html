Tribunal Number: 2024/5333

Tribunal: General Member C Willis

Decision: The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter for reconsideration in accordance with the orders that:

  1. An assessment of administrative shortfall penalty for false or misleading statement is to be issued at the rate of 50% of the tax shortfall amount for the year ended 30 June 2020.
  2. An assessment of administrative shortfall penalty for false or misleading statement is to be issued at the rate of 50% of the tax shortfall amount for the year ended 30 June 2021, with this base penalty amount to be increased by 20%.

    1. For the income tax years ended 30 June 2020 and 30 June 2021 in an amount equal to 40 penalty units for each income year.
    2. For the activity statement periods from 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2021 in an amount equal to 40 penalty units for each activity statement period.

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. Ms Yuen (the Applicant) is seeking remission of certain penalties imposed by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) arising in relation to the possession and use of an electronic sales suppression tool (‘ESST’) by a company called Trio Harmony Investments Pty Ltd (‘Trio Harmony’) during the income years ended 30 June 2020 and 30 June 2021 (the ‘Relevant Years’). The Applicant was a director of Trio Harmony and has also worked as a manager in the restaurant business run by Trio Harmony.
  2. ESSTs are software or devices which interfere with electronic sales records in various ways, such as deleting, modifying or hiding records of sales. Broadly, the Respondent’s position is that the Applicant aided and abetted the possession and use of the ESST by Trio Harmony. The Respondent therefore imposed penalties under specific provisions of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 1953) which apply to ESST’s (the ESST Penalties). The Respondent also imposed administrative shortfall penalties (the Shortfall Penalties) in relation to false or misleading statements it said were made by the Applicant in relation to her income tax affairs.
  3. The Applicant lodged an objection (Objection)[1] against the assessments relating to these penalties. The Respondent disallowed the Objection in its objection decision of 26 June 2024 (the Objection Decision).[2]
  4. The Applicant seeks the remission of the ESST Penalties and Shortfall Penalties on the basis of her personal circumstances, including health conditions, lack of education and family history, such that the imposition of penalties was unjust and unduly harsh.

BACKGROUND

  1. The Applicant was born in Hong Kong and came to Australia in 1991. Her formal education ceased at grade 5 level in Hong Kong and she worked in clothing factories after leaving school, including as an outworker in the clothing sector after her arrival in Australia. She described her main duty as being a mother. She has difficulty with reading, has never undertaken studies in business or tax and has limited proficiency in English.[3]
  2. Trio Harmony was originally established in 1996 by the Applicant’s ex-husband, Mr S, who was a director of Trio Harmony from its incorporation until December 2011, and then again from February 2012 to July 2018.[4] Mr S left Australia to work in China around 2006, and the Applicant and Mr S divorced in 2011. After the divorce Mr S transferred his shares in Trio Harmony to the Applicant and their children, Ms Tham and Ms VS.
  3. The Applicant was a director of Trio Harmony between May 2006 and January 2020, and then worked as a manager of the Restaurant in 2020 and 2021.[5] The current director of Trio Harmony is Ms VS.
  4. Trio Harmony was trading as the operator of a restaurant (the ‘Restaurant’). It is not in dispute that Digital Data Assist (DDA) software was contained in the Restaurant’s ‘point of sale’ (POS) system during the Relevant Years and in other years.[6] The DDA software could delete and renumber sales transactions, and after deletion of a transaction it could not be recovered. The DDA software is regarded as an ESST by the Respondent, and this characterisation was not challenged by the Applicant.
  5. The Applicant stated that she was subject to the control and influence of Mr S during their marriage and followed his instructions, including in relation to business and financial matters. She also stated that Mr S had failed to provide agreed financial support after their divorce, and by 2020 he had ceased making monthly payments such that she withdrew funds from the Restaurant business to cover mortgage and living expenses for herself, her daughter and granddaughter.
  6. The Applicant said that the ESST in question was introduced by Mr S around 2005, while he was still a director of Trio Harmony and before she became a director. It was only after Mr S returned to China that she became involved in the Restaurant business.[7] Mr S provided the Applicant with what was described as ‘basic training’ in the operation of the Restaurant’s POS system before he left Australia.[8] The ESST continued in use until it was discovered by the Respondent as part of audit activities relating to the Applicant and Trio Harmony. The Applicant said that after she was informed by the Respondent of their concerns about the POS software at the Restaurant, she contacted the supplier of the software and arranged for the removal of the improper features of the software.[9]
  7. The Respondent had commenced an audit of Trio Harmony in relation to GST reporting, business income and payments to directors in 2021.
  8. The Respondent then commenced an audit of the Applicant’s income tax affairs in 2022. The Respondent determined that there was an income tax shortfall amount in each of the Relevant Years and sent an audit finalisation letter to the Applicant on 22 March 2022 advising that it would issue default assessments for the Relevant Years and impose an administrative penalty for making a false or misleading statement at 75% of the tax shortfall. The Respondent therefore issued to the Applicant on 28 March 2022:
    (a) Notices of amended assessment for each of the Relevant Years (the First Amended Assessments).[10]

    (b) A notice of assessment of a shortfall penalty each of the Relevant Years (the Shortfall Penalty Assessment).[11]

  9. As part of earlier audit activity relating to the taxation affairs of Trio Harmony[12] the Respondent visited the Restaurant premises in March 2022 and discovered the DDA software on the Restaurant’s POS system. The Respondent therefore commenced a second phase of audit activities in relation to the Applicant.
  10. On 12 July 2022 the Respondent wrote to Ms Tham advising that some anomalies had been found in documentation, and, in particular, reported sales did not match POS data, indicating that cash had not been banked. The Respondent said that they would speak to the Applicant’s tax agent but also asked if Ms Tham could discuss this with her mother and sister.
  11. On 13 July 2022 Ms Tham sent an email to the Respondent which included the following:[13]
    I relayed to her exactly what you said, almost word for word, and she admitted that she was aware of the ESST function and had been using it. I asked on the quantum, she said mostly about $1,000 to $2,000 a month most time of the year, however in the peak season being November and December, it would be $3,000 to $5,000 when the restaurant is opened.

    I also questioned her about the $0.00 invoices. There are 2 types of these invoices.

  • a process introduced by ex-manager [name] to use the POS system to notify our kitchen staff that customers ... has been seated ... That will later be superseded by an actual order with food and beverage ... These type of $0.00 invoices can be reconciled and confirm on the POS data that you’ve got ...
  • Second type is the ones that she deleted.
    I asked where the funds has gone given that her wages is sufficient to cover the loan repayment of her home loan and she has borrowed $145,000 from [Restaurant] as Directors Loan to cover all of her expenses (mostly credit cards spending) at grocery and fresh food shopping, utility bills etc. She said the cash is to cover a shortfall such some petty cash expenses and out of pocket medical costs / gaps.

    She said she now knows it is wrong to do so and she is happy to arrange a short Zoom meeting with you if you want to speak to her directly. She acknowledges her wrong-doing and will never do it again, now she would like to pay what she owes and so she could put this behind and focus on her health.

    Please kindly reassess the position and would like to apologise for her wrong doing.

  1. The Respondent sent a letter to the Applicant dated 3 May 2023, attaching a further audit paper which set out the Respondent’s updated position that there were additional amounts which should have been included in the Applicant’s assessable income, such that the income tax shortfall amount had increased in each of the Relevant Years. The Respondent made a finding of evasion in relation to the 2020 income year, such that the period of review for that year was extended.
  2. The Respondent also determined that the Applicant would be liable for ESST penalties.
  3. An audit finalisation letter followed on 28 June 2023 and the Respondent then issued to the Applicant:
    (a) Further notices of assessment for the Relevant Years dated 6 July 2023, which included the additional amounts of income (the Further Amended Assessments)[14].

    (b) Further notices of assessment of a shortfall penalty for making a false or misleading statement as a result of intentional disregard of a tax law dated 7 July 2023 (the Further Shortfall Penalty Assessments). This reflected the increased income tax shortfall amount and a 20% increase to the base penalty amount in relation to the 2021 year.[15]

    (c) Notices for ESST penalties for the Relevant Years dated 4 July 2023 (the ESST Penalty Assessments). Two of the notices related to a penalty for ‘aiding or abetting the incorrect keeping or altering of records using an ESST’ and the third notice related to a penalty for ‘aiding or abetting the possession or having control of an ESST.’[16]

  4. ESST penalties and tax shortfall penalties were also imposed on Trio Harmony as set out in separate correspondence from the Respondent to Trio Harmony dated 23 June 2023.[17]
  5. The Applicant’s Objection dated 4 September 2023[18] was lodged by her former tax agent on her behalf. The stated reason or grounds for Objection were a ‘request for the Commissioner to exercise his [discretional] power to partially remit the administrative penalties and the full remission of ESST penalties.’ The Objection form also requested the remission of General Interest Charge (GIC) imposed on the Applicant.
  6. In a submission accompanying the Objection, the tax agent referred to the Applicant as having been diagnosed with a medical condition in September 2021 which required significant surgery and left her in considerable pain.[19] They also submitted that the Applicant’s lack of education and knowledge of taxation laws, together with the personal and financial difficulties she had faced as a consequence of her marital issues and divorce, were circumstances that should be taken into account by the Respondent in determining whether to exercise his discretion to remit all or part of the shortfall penalties imposed. They also noted that the Applicant had been forced to take on the role of director of Trio Harmony and become involved in the management of the Restaurant after Mr S’s unexpected departure. She had merely continued with the business methods established by her ex-husband.
  7. The Applicant’s advisers further submitted that it would be appropriate for the shortfall penalty to be imposed at the rate of 50% rather than 75% based on similar considerations of her circumstances. They noted that the Applicant was unable to earn an income due to her age and health condition[20] and that she had few assets other than her principal place of residence, which she would have to sell if the shortfall penalties were to be imposed in their full amount as assessed.
  8. In relation to the ESST Penalty Assessments, the Applicant’s advisers requested remission of those amounts[21] on the basis that the Applicant was acting in her capacity as director of Trio Harmony and therefore should not be treated as acting separately and distinctly from the company in relation to the ESST/DDA software. In their view, for the Applicant to be penalised in this context would represent a double application of penalty provisions. This argument and the Respondent’s response is discussed further below.
  9. In its Objection Decision the Respondent:
    (a) Disallowed the Applicant’s objection against the Further Shortfall Penalty Assessments, confirming the imposition of the Shortfall Penalties on the basis of intentional disregard and finding that there were no circumstances that warranted a remission of the Shortfall Penalties.

    (b) Disallowed the Applicant’s objection against the ESST Penalty Assessments, on the basis that the Respondent did not consider the Applicant’s circumstances warranted a remission or reduction of the ESST Penalties. The Respondent had regard to matters set out in its Law Administration Practice Statement PSLA2022/1 in reaching this decision.

  10. On 29 July 2024 the Applicant lodged an application with the Tribunal for review of the Objection Decision.[22] The Applicant said that penalties had been imposed on both Trio Harmony and the Applicant in her capacity as a director of that company for what was essentially the same act, and this ‘piercing of the corporate veil’ which treated her separately to Trio Harmony was not permitted or contemplated by the tax law. The Applicant also referred to what she perceived as delays in the Respondent completing its audit and objection processes. Reference was also made to GIC imposed and a director penalty notice (DPN) issued to one of her daughters.[23]
  11. In addition to the T Documents, the following materials were filed with the Tribunal:
    (a) A Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions prepared by the Respondent dated 4 November 2024 (‘Respondent SFIC’).

    (b) A Statement from the Applicant as to why the penalties imposed were excessive or should be remitted dated 16 December 2024 (‘Applicant Statement’).

    (c) Extracts from the MASCOT database with corporate history and details relating to Trio Harmony, a company called Larvange Pty Ltd (Larvange) and a company called ASOA Investments Pty Ltd (ASOA) were provided by the Respondent.

    (d) Land title searches for two properties in Melbourne (as at 10 July 2025), one registered in the name of Larvange and the other in the name of the Applicant were provided by the Respondent. A mortgage had been registered in April 2025 in relation to the latter property.

  12. From the MASCOT database materials and evidence and submissions provided during the hearing, the Tribunal understands that:
    (a) A liquidator has been appointed to Trio Harmony. Ms Tham said this occurred after the lease of the premises occupied by the Restaurant expired in early 2024 and Trio Harmony was unable to reach agreement with the landlord in relation to rent increases and renovations to the premises.[24]

    (b) Larvange and family members of the Applicant currently hold the shares in Trio Harmony.[25]

    (c) Ms Tham is currently the sole director and shareholder of Larvange, although the Applicant previously held 50% of the shares and was a director between September 2018 and January 2022, and from 17 February to 1 October 2025.

    (d) A home previously owned by the Applicant was transferred to Larvange in August 2021.

    (e) ASOA was registered in August 2024. Ms Tham is the sole director and shareholder of ASOA. ASOA owns the house in which Ms Tham and the Applicant now live. Ms Tham told the Tribunal that ASOA is owned by her and has been funded from her salary and wages in her employment in the finance industry, that is, unrelated to the Restaurant business.[26]

    (f) The registered address and principal place of business of Larvange and ASOA was the Applicant’s previous residential address.

  13. The matter was heard by video on 17 November 2025. The Applicant represented herself with the support of her daughter Ms Tham and the assistance of an interpreter provided by the Tribunal. On the basis that Ms Tham’s support might involve her providing explanations or information to the Tribunal about her mother’s circumstances the Tribunal asked Ms Tham to make an affirmation as a potential witness.
  14. The Tribunal agreed to receive written closing submissions from the parties. These were filed by the Respondent on 16 January 2026 and by the Applicant on 13 February 2026.

REMISSION OF PENALTIES

  1. Part 4-25 of Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 contains provisions relating to ‘charges and penalties’ including:
    (a) Division 284, and in particular Subdivision 284-B, which sets out the circumstances in which administrative penalties will be imposed for false or misleading statements made by a taxpayer to the Respondent and the manner in which those penalties are calculated.

    (b) Division 288 which sets out miscellaneous administrative penalties that apply in specific situations, such as in relation to ESSTs.

    (c) Division 298 which contains ‘machinery provisions’ applying to penalties, including the provision which provides a discretion to remit all or part of a penalty.

  2. The Applicant did not lodge an objection against the First Amended Assessments or the Further Amended Assessments. That is, the Applicant did not object against the underlying income tax shortfall amounts, and for the purposes of this proceeding is taken not to dispute the quantum of the shortfall amounts identified by the Respondent in the Further Shortfall Penalty Assessments.[27]
  3. The Applicant has also acknowledged having made ‘false or misleading statements’ to the Respondent in relation to each of the Relevant Years, said that she accepts that the administrative penalties were lawfully imposed and confirmed that she is not asking the Tribunal to revisit liability for penalties.[28]
  4. The issue therefore remaining for the Tribunal is whether the discretion to remit all or part of the ESST Penalties and/or the Shortfall Penalties should be exercised.
  5. Subsection 298-20(1) provides that the Respondent (and therefore the Tribunal standing in the shoes of the Respondent in a merits review) ‘may remit all or part of a penalty.’
  6. Section 298-20 does not prescribe particular matters that a decision-maker must take into account when considering whether to exercise the discretion to remit a penalty. However there is case authority which indicates that the Tribunal should consider whether it is appropriate to remit the penalty on the basis that the ‘outcome is harsh, having regard to the particular circumstances of the taxpayer’ or ‘in all the circumstances.’ Nevertheless the power to remit should be exercised for a proper purpose, having regard to the objects of the TAA 1953. The legislative scheme does not require ‘special circumstances’ to be established.[29]
  7. The Respondent has published guidance on aspects of the imposition and remission of penalties, which its staff are required to follow: see PSLA 2012/5 in relation to penalties for false or misleading statements and PSLA 2022/1 in relation to ESST penalties. The Tribunal is not bound by the Respondent’s guidance, although consistency of decision-making is generally regarded as desirable.[30]
  8. Subsection 298-20(3) provides for a right of objection against a refusal by the Respondent to remit any part of a penalty, where the amount of the penalty payable after the refusal is more than two penalty units. That threshold was met in the present case in relation to the Shortfall Penalties and the ESST Penalties. The Objection Decision is a ‘reviewable objection decision’ which is eligible for review by the Tribunal.[31]
  9. Section 298-20 applies to requests for remission of penalties for false or misleading statements and of ESST penalties. In the present case many of the factors or circumstances that the Tribunal should consider in determining whether to remit a penalty happen to be common to both the Shortfall Penalties and the ESST Penalties, but the two types of penalties are addressed separately below.

The ESST Penalties

  1. Section 288-130 provides that a person is liable to an administrative penalty of 30 penalty units where they are required by law to keep or make a record and they acquire or have possession or control of an ESST or right to use an ESST. A person is also liable to the same administrative penalty if they ‘aid, abet, counsel or procure’ such conduct.
  2. Section 288-135 provides that a person is liable to an administrative penalty of 60 penalty units where they are required by law to keep or make a record, the record is kept, made or altered with the use of an ESST, or is prevented by the use of an ESST from being kept, made or altered and as a result of the use:
    (a) the record does not correctly record and explain the matter, transaction, act or operation to which it relates; or

    (b) the person does not keep or make the record in accordance with the taxation law.

Similarly, a person is liable to an administrative penalty of 60 penalty units if they aid, abet, counsel or procure such conduct.

  1. Under section 298-20 the Respondent may remit all or part of a penalty imposed under sections 288-130 or 288-135.[32]
  2. The Applicant does not dispute that an ESST was used at the Restaurant in each of the Relevant Years. Based on advice from her previous tax advisor, the Applicant had argued that where Trio Harmony is subject to penalties under Division 288 for the possession and use of the DDA software at the Restaurant, she should not also be subject to penalties relating to the use of that ESST. It was contended that her conduct was not sufficiently distinct from that of Trio Harmony and the relevant taxation laws do not contemplate or permit the piercing of the ‘corporate veil.’
  3. The Applicant had therefore submitted that the discretion under section 298-20 to remit all or part of the penalties imposed should be exercised:
    (a) Because Trio Harmony was the entity responsible for the possession and use of the ESST, and by extension the entity responsible for the records which were affected by the ESST. The Applicant argued that she was only acting in her capacity as director of that entity and the ‘corporate veil’ should not be pierced such that she is subject to a penalty under these provisions.

    (b) On the basis of her personal circumstances, which made it unjust and unduly harsh for her to be subject to the penalties.

  4. In relation to the ‘corporate veil’ contention, the Tribunal understood from Ms Tham that this argument was based on advice provided by a previous tax agent who no longer represented the Applicant. This is consistent with the Objection documentation having been prepared by that tax agent. At the hearing Mr Pillay, representing the Respondent, explained the basis for the Respondent imposing ESST penalties on both the Applicant and Trio Harmony, even though the penalties arose from the same ESST software used in the one location. As set out in the Respondent’s SFIC, Mr Pillay said that Division 288 expressly contemplate that more than one person or entity may be liable to penalties relating to the operation of the same ESST. This is because Division 288 imposes penalties on those who actually possess and/or use an ESST and also imposes penalties on those who aid or abet the possession and/or use of an ESST. The ESST Penalties had not been imposed on the Applicant in her capacity as a director of Trio Harmony, but as a person who was viewed by the Respondent as having aided or abetted contraventions of Trio Harmony. In its SFIC the Respondent referred to statements in the Explanatory Memorandum to the Bill which introduced Division 288 which draw an analogy with provisions of the criminal law which recognise a primary offence, and create separate or additional offences for aiding or abetting the commission of the primary offence.[33]
  5. After hearing this explanation the Applicant did not make any further submissions at the hearing, and the Applicant’s closing written submissions did not refer to the ‘corporate veil’ argument or otherwise dispute the lawfulness of the imposition of the penalties, although the Applicant referred to the cumulative effect of multiple penalties as a matter in favour of remission (and this is discussed further below).
  6. For the avoidance of doubt, the Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s contentions that the language of Division 298 allows for the imposition of an ESST penalty on one entity which acquires or possesses an ESST or which incorrectly keeps records by using an ESST, and for the imposition of a (further or separate) penalty on another entity which ‘aids or abets’ that first entity in doing those things in relation to that ESST. That other entity may or may not be a director or other officeholder of the first entity. The question is whether the other entity ‘aided or abetted’ the first entity, regardless of their legal relationship with the first entity.[34] Therefore, for example, the Respondent was able to impose a penalty on Trio Harmony for the possession and use of the ESST at the Restaurant, and also impose a penalty on the Applicant for aiding or abetting Trio Harmony to possess or use that ESST at the Restaurant.
  7. Moving to the issue of the discretion to remit the ESST Penalties, the Respondent identified[35] the following factors as relevant to its decision not to remit any of the ESST Penalties in either of the Relevant Years:
    (a) The Respondent did not agree that the Applicant was merely a ‘silent director’ in Trio Harmony. The Applicant had continued as a manager of the Restaurant after she ceased to be a director of Trio Harmony because of her ‘experience, respect and authority.’[36] As the manager and former director the Applicant had over a decade of experience and had oversight and management of Trio Harmony’s operations.[37]

    (b) There was no corroborating evidence to support the Applicant’s claim that she was subject to the instructions or control of her ex-husband.[38]

    (c) Although it had been suggested that the Applicant had not realised that the use of an ESST was wrong until after the commencement of the audit by the Respondent, the Applicant had made other statements during cross-examination suggesting that she had understood that the deletion of transactions was wrong.[39] The Applicant had also indicated that had the Respondent not conducted its audit, she would have likely continued using the ESST.[40]

    (d) The fact that the Applicant may not have been aware of the exact consequences of her conduct was not relevant.

    (e) The Applicant used the funds from omitted transactions for her personal expenditure.[41]

    (f) The Respondent drew the Tribunal’s attention to what it believed were inconsistencies between the information the Applicant gave the Respondent at objection and what she told the Tribunal during cross-examination relating to the withdrawal of financial support by her ex-husband, such that she struggled to pay her home mortgage without the additional money from the Restaurant.[42]

    (g) The Respondent did not accept that the Applicant lived a life of limited means. A new BMW SUV had been purchased in the name of Trio Harmony with the Applicant meeting the loan repayments on the vehicle during 2019. This vehicle had subsequently been acquired by Larvange and was available for the use of the Applicant.[43] Otherwise, the Applicant had not provided any evidence in support of her claim that she would be in financial hardship if made to pay the penalties. However there was evidence that the Applicant had financial support from her daughters and received payments totalling approximately $60,000 from Larvange.[44] She had also sold an investment property and shares to Larvange in August 2021, as well as selling her former home in May 2025.

    (h) The Respondent accepted that the Applicant suffered poor health but did not view this as a reason for remission of any penalty amount. The Respondent’s position was that the Applicant had engaged in conduct which resulted in the non-payment of tax across an extended period, at least from 2018. The Respondent drew a distinction between the Applicant’s circumstances and those considered by the former AAT when it exercised the discretion to remit penalties in Kizquart and Commissioner of Taxation.[45]

    (i) The Respondent believed it was likely that the ESST was used to conceal sales transactions and assessable income in periods prior to the Relevant Periods, and this was confirmed by the Applicant during cross-examination. However the Respondent had only sought to impose ESST Penalties for the Relevant Periods (and did not intend to reopen earlier years).

  8. The Applicant asked the Tribunal to consider the following matters:
    (a) The Applicant had very limited education, working as a child labourer prior to emigrating to Australia and thereafter working in factories or at the Restaurant set up by her ex-husband before he left her. She had no formal education in business, accounting or taxation matters. She has limited proficiency in English, and difficulty with reading and numeracy more generally. These matters went to whether the Applicant had the capacity to understand her statutory obligations, including the legal status of the use of an ESST and penalty consequences. The Applicant disputes any submissions made by the Respondent suggesting that the Applicant was knowledgeable or sophisticated.

    (b) The Applicant had surgery in September 2021 for a serious medical condition, with subsequent treatment, which left her in pain, fatigued, stressed and needing medication to sleep. She had further surgery for another medical condition in July 2025. She is now 70 years old, has limited mobility and is unable to live independently. Although the Applicant is not arguing her medical conditions as a cause of her conduct, they are relevant to her vulnerability, resilience and to the severity of the penalties imposed.

    (c) Although the Applicant acknowledged that she knew the use of the ESST was wrong, this statement was made by the Applicant as a lay person, in a general sense, rather than demonstrating that she understood the statutory or legal obligations yet chose to ignore them. Having regard to her limited education and English proficiency, her age and her history as a ‘worker’ rather than a ‘decision maker’ an inference should not be drawn that she intentionally disregarded the taxation law.

    (d) At most, the evidence supported a finding of ‘recklessness’ rather than intentional disregard.

    (e) The ESST was in the POS system of a business established by her ex-husband. The Applicant did not acquire, install or configure it. She lacked the sophistication to understand its legal implications. When the Respondent’s officers advised the Applicant that features of the ESST were unlawful, she immediately contacted the POS supplier to have this functionality removed.[46]

    (f) The Applicant followed the instructions of her ex-husband in relation to the operation of the ESST. Her history of working in factories since she was nine or ten years old meant that she was inclined to listen to what others told her to do.[47] She had relied on her ex-husband for financial support and when he ceased providing that support she withdrew ‘modest’ amounts from the Restaurant business via the POS system to cover her living expenses.[48] She believed that this compensated her for payments that otherwise would have been made to her, and her husband had said that he did not have money to give her so she could use money from the Restaurant.[49] She told the Tribunal that she was not drawing a large enough salary from the Restaurant to meet her own living expenses because the Restaurant needed to cover staff salaries, supplier costs and rent.[50] The Applicant now relies on financial support from her daughters to supplement the age pension.[51]

    (g) The disposals and transfers of property described by the Respondent occurred because the Applicant could not service mortgages or holding costs associated with those assets. These transactions reflected ‘financial necessity’ and there was no ‘discretionary enrichment’ of the Applicant.[52] The Applicant disposed of the shares when her medical condition became apparent in 2021, which also coincided with a time when the Restaurant was closed due to COVID-19 restrictions. This was before the audit by the Respondent of the Applicant commenced.

    (h) The Relevant Years included periods when COVID-19 lockdown restrictions were in force in Victoria. The Restaurant was closed or had restricted operations during these periods and the business switched to cashless transactions, such that the amounts that the Applicant took via the ESST were ‘negligible.’ The Applicant appreciated that this did not excuse her conduct, but it was relevant to issues of proportionality of the penalties and the personal benefit she obtained. Although the Applicant was not challenging the imposition of the ESST Penalties, the fact that the Restaurant was closed during some of the Relevant Years suggested that the amount of these penalties could be excessive.[53]

    (i) The Applicant had co-operated with the Respondent’s audit and made admissions. She engaged with the Respondent’s officers while she was undergoing a course of medical treatment. A number of emails evidence correspondence between the parties to this effect. This was inconsistent with the Applicant being disengaged or intentionally non-compliant.

    (j) Although the Applicant was no longer making submissions about the ‘corporate veil’ preventing the imposition of ESST penalties on her, the fact that multiple penalties had been imposed for the same underlying conduct across the Relevant Periods was a relevant matter in the context of remission.

    (k) The penalties imposed were disproportionate to the Applicant’s circumstances.

  9. The Respondent correctly submitted that the burden of proof is on the Applicant to demonstrate a basis for exercise of the discretion to remit any portion of the ESST Penalties. The Tribunal also agrees generally with the statement in the Respondent’s guidance PSLA 2022/1 that in considering whether to remit any of an ESST penalty, the decision-maker must decide ‘whether the penalty outcome is just and reasonable having regard to the facts and circumstances of the case.’
  10. The Applicant confirmed in her closing written submissions that she accepted that the administrative penalties were lawfully imposed.[54] The Tribunal understands her contentions relating to whether her behaviour reflected ‘recklessness’ or ‘intentional disregard’ to relate to the more general issue of her degree of culpability or responsibility in the context of the discretion to remit the penalties.
  11. Having regard to all of the circumstances of this case the Tribunal has decided that it is appropriate to remit the ESST Penalties in part. The Tribunal specifically refers to the following matters:
    (a) The Objection Decision contained a very limited indication of the matters upon which the Respondent relied in refusing to remit any of the ESST Penalties. In its reasons the Respondent referred in general to the factors set out at Section 6 of PSLA 2022/1 (‘Remitting Penalties’) and ‘Appendix A of the PSLA 2022/1’ which contains ‘Examples’ of ESST related topics.[55] The Tribunal believes that some factors relevant to the Applicant’s circumstances were not fully considered in the Objection Decision. Some of these factors were raised in the Applicant’s Objection but not discussed in the Objection Decision, although the Respondent subsequently sought to address these before the Tribunal.

    (b) The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence relating to her very limited education, her difficulties with literacy (including in her first language of Cantonese) as well as her lack of proficiency in English. These matters were not challenged by the Respondent during hearing. They were only mentioned in passing in the Objection Decision, with the Respondent asserting that a ‘reasonable person’ involved in the management of a business for 14 years and having ‘control of its financial record keeping practices’, even one without a ‘high level of education’ or who is not ‘an expert in taxation law’ should be expected to know the taxation effect of not declaring all sales.[56] The Tribunal believes that the Applicant’s particular educational and literacy circumstances place her in a different position to a taxpayer who has simply not completed higher education or has not been exposed to taxation issues, and for this reason the imposition of the full amount of the penalty may lead to an unjust or harsh result. Although the Applicant acknowledged an awareness of the ESST functionality and that it was ‘wrong’, the Tribunal accepts that her level of understanding of the implications or gravity of what she was doing may have been limited.[57] She told the Tribunal during cross-examination that did not realise the ‘kind of trouble’ that it would cause and that she ‘had to’ take the money via the ESST because she did not have money to live on.[58]

    (c) The Respondent did not dispute that the Applicant had faced health challenges but said that the Applicant’s medical issues were not relevant to penalty remission on the basis that the documentary evidence indicated that her major surgery took place at the time of the Respondent’s audit or after the end of the 2021 income year.[59] The Tribunal is not willing to dismiss the Applicant’s medical circumstances as entirely irrelevant to the issue of remission. Copies of medical documentation provided to the Respondent with the Objection confirm that major surgery was being arranged for the Applicant as of September 2021, and it is reasonable to assume that her medical condition may have been having an impact on her for a period prior to that date. The medical documentation indicates ongoing tests and procedures occurred during 2022 and 2023 when the Applicant engaging with the Respondent about her taxation affairs.[60]

    (d) In its Objection Decision the Respondent noted the Applicant’s contentions relating to Mr S’s involvement in purchasing and installing the ESST at the Restaurant and his influence over her roles at the Restaurant and with Trio Harmony.[61] They were not addressed in the Objection Decision reasons for refusing to remit the ESST Penalties. During the Tribunal proceeding the Respondent argued that there was no evidence to corroborate the Applicant’s claims. It is difficult for the Tribunal to make findings in relation to the involvement of the Applicant’s ex-husband or the degree of influence he might have exerted over the Applicant. Mr S was not called to give evidence, and it was not clear whether he was in Australia or other location where he would have been able to give evidence. To the extent that there were allegations that he was responsible for the initial installation of the ESST, it is possible that his own legal position would be a consideration for him during the giving of evidence. There is insufficient evidence for the Tribunal to find that the Applicant was put under duress or coerced by her ex-husband in relation to the use of the ESST, or that she was merely a ‘silent director’ at all relevant times. However, based on the Applicant’s evidence about her limited education and English proficiency, her cultural background and statements made by Ms Tham, the Tribunal believes it is plausible that the Applicant acted in accordance with Mr S’s directions and was influenced by him to some extent.

    (e) However the Respondent did state in the Objection Decision as a reason for refusing to remit the ESST Penalties that the Applicant ‘did not engage in any remedial actions such as disposing of the POS system containing the ESST.’[62] This does not appear to align with evidence from the Applicant, which was apparently not challenged by the Respondent, that after being told by the Respondent that the ESST was unlawful, she immediately went to the supplier of the POS system and had the prohibited functionality removed. This is relevant to the factor set out in PSLA 2022/1 as to whether the entity had taken any remedial action, although the Tribunal accepts that such remedial action only happened after the Respondent discovered the ESST.[63]

    (f) The Respondent also gave as a reason for refusing to remit the fact that the Applicant did not make a voluntary disclosure to the Respondent or self-amend her income tax returns prior to the commencement of the audit. The Tribunal agrees that the Applicant did not make a voluntary disclosure in the formal sense of that term prior to the audit being commenced. However a factor in PSLA 2022/1 is whether the taxpayer ‘cooperated with [the Respondent’s] investigations.’ The documentary evidence before the Tribunal, and in particular Ms Tham’s email of 13 July 2022 indicates that the Applicant acknowledged her use of the ESST at the Restaurant, and this was done in response to an informal request by the Respondent for clarification of records. The Applicant also stated that she knew deleting sales transactions was ‘the wrong thing to do’ both in her oral evidence to the Tribunal and in her written submissions and would not repeat the behaviour. She has not challenged the Further Amended Assessments or the basis for imposition of the ESST Penalties. The Tribunal has previously indicated that genuine ‘remorse, co-operation and seeking to correct the problems going forward’ can be a circumstance in favour of remission.[64] This may be contrasted with circumstances where an applicant lacks self-awareness of, or fails to take responsibility for their conduct.[65]

  12. There were other matters raised in relation to the Applicant’s circumstances which the Tribunal did not view as tending significantly for or against remission, or in relation to which there was insufficient reliable evidence for the Tribunal to draw a conclusion. This included:
    (a) In relation to whether the Applicant had a good compliance history, the Respondent contended that it was likely that the Applicant had used the ESST prior to the Relevant Years. The Applicant firmly denied having used the ESST to delete transactions prior to 2018, stating that there were other managers at the Restaurant in those years. She initially said that she started to use the ESST after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic due to a lack of money, but under cross-examination agreed that it was probably around 2018 when she started using the ESST to compensate for not receiving money from her ex-husband.[66] There was no corroborating evidence of when the Applicant’s involvement with the ESST began, but on her own admission she had removed funds from the Restaurant via the ESST at times other than during the Relevant Years.

    (b) The Tribunal does not believe that possible financial hardship is a matter in favour of remission in this case. Although the Tribunal accepts that the Applicant’s current financial circumstances may be limited, including due to her age and poor health, she did not provide evidence in support of serious financial hardship. The Respondent’s representative asked the Applicant a number of questions about whether she had given any of the money she took via the ESST to anyone else, her access to the BMW and her property transactions. The Respondent asked the Applicant about whether any of the money had been applied to paying the mortgage loans on family properties. The Applicant’s evidence was that she had not shared any of the money with her daughters (other than paying for groceries and meals when the family ate together). She had contributed amounts to the mortgage on an investment property she co-owned with Ms Tham (which was sold to Larvange in August 2021) and to the mortgage on her former home (which she sold in early 2025, for a profit, before moving in with Ms Tham).[67] To the extent that the Respondent was seeking to question the Applicant’s claim of financial hardship, the Tribunal has indicated that it is not treating financial hardship as a matter in favour of remission. To the extent that the questions related to the activities of the Applicant’s daughters, the Tribunal’s focus is on the Applicant’s circumstances.[68]

    (c) The Respondent also asked the Applicant why she did not simply increase the amount of her drawings or directors fees from Trio Harmony to cover her living costs. She said that she took money from the Restaurant business via the ESST when it was available.[69] The Tribunal views this line of questioning and the Applicant’s evidence as confirming matters that were already largely known, being that the Applicant removed funds from the Restaurant business via the ESST to meet personal living expenses.

    (d) In relation to the quantum of amounts the Applicant is said to have removed from the business via the use of the ESST, the Applicant suggested that the unreported income amounts as determined by the Respondent were inconsistent with the amounts suggested by Ms Tham in the email she sent to the Respondent on 13 July 2022, such that the shortfall amounts were overestimated, particularly in the context of COVID-19 conditions.[70] The Applicant believed that the Respondent had overstated the amounts removed by her via the ESST and said that this is a factor that should be taken into account in considering remission.[71] The Tribunal views Ms Tham’s email, relaying what she said her mother had told her the previous day, as speaking to what the Applicant recalls removing from the Restaurant business in general terms rather than in relation to the Relevant Years specifically. The Tribunal accepts that during periods of COVID-19 restrictions there may have been very few (or at certain times, nil) sales transactions. However the Tribunal understands the Respondent to have determined that there were unreported cash amounts on the basis of a mismatch of sales records and POS data obtained during the audit, including data indicating that receipts were issued to customers confirming purchases made, which suggested unbanked cash amounts.[72] In the absence of evidence from the directors of Trio Harmony of the amount of those deleted sales, the Respondent was required to calculate the amount based on the information it had.[73]

    It is also important to understand that the ESST Penalties are calculated by reference to a specific number of ‘penalty units’ for activities which contravene provisions of Division 288, with each penalty unit being a fixed amount established by statute rather than being based on a tax shortfall or similar amount. In this context, it is the contravening activity which attracts the penalty rather than quantum of income not disclosed. In any event, the Tribunal has no basis upon which it could conclude that the Respondent’s assessment of unreported income amounts was incorrect.

    (e) The Tribunal believes that the decision in Kizquart can be distinguished from the Applicant’s circumstances. Although medical and financial issues of the director of the taxpayer in that matter were given as a reason for remission, the AAT in that case saw a relationship between the late lodgement of returns by the taxpayer and the director’s personal issues. This Tribunal also believes that the use of an ESST is a more serious matter than late lodgement of returns.

  13. Although the Applicant did not press the ‘corporate veil’ argument, she remained of the view that it was unjust for multiple penalties to be applied to herself and Trio Harmony for the same underlying conduct. In the present circumstances the Tribunal believes the clear language of the TAA 1953 provides for multiple penalties to be applied, particularly in the context of ESST penalties which look to both possession of ESST’s as well as their use in incorrectly keeping records, and the separate recognition of administrative penalties for tax shortfalls arising from such conduct.
  14. The Tribunal does not wish to downplay the seriousness of ESSTs. These tools are not only intended to falsify sales transactions but also erase the evidence of falsification of records. Their use is not only a breach of taxation law but reflects dishonest or fraudulent business record keeping practice. The objectives of the administrative penalty regime include encouraging taxpayers to take reasonable care in complying with taxation obligations and the promotion of consistent treatment of taxpayers.[74] In considering a remission of penalties circumstances that might be viewed as mitigating the taxpayer’s behaviour or reducing the taxpayer’s culpability are likely to be of relevance.[75] The Tribunal would not suggest that financial hardship or ill health is generally an acceptable reason for non-compliance with taxation laws. However there are particular features of the Applicant’s circumstances, including her educational background, the timing of her health challenges, and what the Tribunal accepts as her attempts to address the offending conduct, which suggest that partial remission of the ESST Penalties would not be inconsistent with the objectives and intent of Part 4-25 of Schedule 1 and in particular Division 288.
  15. The Tribunal does not believe it is appropriate to remit the entirety or majority of the ESST Penalties. However, a weighing of the matters described in paragraphs 51 and 52 above supports partial remission of the ESST Penalties as follows[76]:
    (a) In relation to the ESST Penalty Assessment for an ESST Penalty imposed under subsection 288-130(2) for ‘aiding and abetting the possession or having control of an ESST’, remitting the penalty to an amount equivalent to 20 penalty units.

    (b) In relation to the ESST Penalty Assessments for ESST Penalties imposed under subsection 288-135(2) for ‘aiding or abetting the incorrect keeping or altering of records using an ESST’:

    (i) Remitting the penalty to an amount equivalent to 40 penalty units for each of the 2020 and 2021 income tax years.

    (ii) Remitting the penalty to an amount equivalent to 40 penalty units in each of the activity statement periods from 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2021.

  16. This essentially reflects a remission of one-third of the ESST Penalties imposed. The Tribunal also considered the Examples given by the Respondent in PSLA 2022/1, such as Example 2 where a full remission was suggested as appropriate for a taxpayer who was compliant with audit requests (albeit in circumstances where the taxpayer had been unaware of the ESST) and Example 3 where a 50% remission was suggested as suitable for a taxpayer who took remedial action (albeit prior to an audit commencing).

Penalties for false or misleading statements

  1. A person will be liable to an administrative penalty if they make a statement to the Respondent that is false or misleading in a material particular because of things in it or omitted from it[77] but will not be liable for a penalty if they took ‘reasonable care’ in connection with the making of the statement.[78]
  2. The Applicant has acknowledged making one or more false or misleading statements and has not raised any contentions that she took reasonable care in connection with such a statement.
  3. In determining the amount of any penalty to be imposed:[79]
    (a) The ‘base penalty amount’ is calculated by applying a percentage to the ‘shortfall amount.’ A shortfall amount will arise where a tax-related liability, as worked out on the basis of the false or misleading statement, is less than it would be if the statement were not false or misleading.

    (b) The percentage to be applied reflects the type of behaviour that resulted in the shortfall amount. That percentage will be 75% of the shortfall amount if the shortfall arose from the intentional disregard by the taxpayer of a taxation law. It will be 50% of the shortfall amount if the shortfall resulted from ‘recklessness’ by the taxpayer or their agent as to the operation of a taxation law.

  4. In the present case the Respondent imposed the Shortfall Penalties on the basis of ‘intentional disregard.’ The Applicant has told the Tribunal that she is not challenging whether the Shortfall Penalties were lawfully imposed or seeking to revisit this liability. However, she did highlight for the Tribunal certain matters, albeit in the context of her request for remission of penalties, which she believes were inconsistent with a finding of intentional disregard (and at most indicated recklessness). In particular she told the Tribunal that although she may have agreed that her conduct in relation to the ESST was wrong, her educational background, lack of English, health and other vulnerabilities meant that she did not understand the legal consequences of what she was doing and therefore she had not deliberately or intentionally disregarded a taxation law.
  5. The Applicant otherwise referred to the same factors and circumstances in support of her request for remission of both the Shortfall Penalties and the ESST Penalties.
  6. The Respondent likewise relied on the same matters it outlined in relation to the ESST Penalties as supporting its position that there should be no remission of the Shortfall Penalties. However, the Respondent additionally noted the Applicant’s involvement in a number of property transactions, and the Applicant having been a director of Lavarnge, which held significant family assets, for some years. The Respondent queried how the Applicant could be involved in such matters and be unaware of the consequences of under reporting her income for tax purposes.
  7. The Tribunal refers to its findings as set out in paragraphs 51 and 52 in relation to the ESST Penalties. The Tribunal also accepts that the Applicant was an office holder of Trio Harmony and other family companies over a number of years and agrees that a failure to appreciate the full legal consequences of conduct is generally not a sufficient answer to a finding of intentional disregard or in support of remission of a penalty. However, for similar reasons to those set out in relation to the ESST Penalties the Tribunal believes there are particular features of the Applicant’s circumstances which make it appropriate to remit the Shortfall Penalties in part.
  8. Those reasons are also consistent with the matters set out in the Respondent’s guidance on the remission of penalties for false or misleading statements.[80]
  9. The Tribunal believes it is appropriate to remit the Shortfall Penalties to 50% of the shortfall amount in each of the Relevant Years. In relation to the 2021 year, the Tribunal believes a 20% uplift of that amount remains appropriate, reflecting the continuation of conduct from the prior year.
  10. To be clear, the Tribunal is not setting aside or varying the Objection Decision to reduce the base penalty amount for the Shortfall Penalties to reflect a finding of ‘recklessness’ rather than intentional disregard. Had the Applicant challenged the basis for imposition of the Shortfall Penalties she would have borne the burden of proof to demonstrate that a finding of recklessness (or lack of reasonable care, or absence of a false or misleading statement) was appropriate. The Tribunal believes that she would have had difficulty in doing so based on the evidence before the Tribunal. The Tribunal’s decision in this instance is made under the discretion to remit in section 298-20.

OTHER MATTERS

  1. During the hearing the Applicant told the Tribunal that she disagreed with the Amended Assessments in relation to her income tax. In her application to the Tribunal the Applicant also raised matters relating to the length of time taken by the Respondent in finalising its audit and objection processes, the imposition of GIC and the issue of a DPN to Ms Tham.
  2. The Applicant’s closing written submissions confined the issues in dispute to the exercise of the discretion to remit the ESST Penalties and Shortfall Penalties. However, for the sake of completeness, the Tribunal confirms that:
    (a) The Objection Decision which is the subject of this application relates only to the ESST Penalty Assessments and Shortfall Penalty Assessments. The Tribunal also understands that the Applicant did not lodge an objection against the income tax Amended Assessments. The Tribunal would have been unable to make findings in relation to other assessments that may have been issued to the Applicant on the basis that there is no ‘reviewable objection decision’ which could be the subject of an application for review by the Tribunal in relation to those Amended Assessments.

    (b) Except to the extent that delays or other procedural issues on the part of the Respondent may be relevant to the circumstances to be taken into account in deciding whether remission of a penalty is appropriate, the Tribunal is unable to make any decisions or determinations about the appropriateness of the administrative processes of the Respondent.

    (c) The Tribunal is not empowered to conduct a review of any decisions relating to the imposition of GIC. A decision of the Respondent to impose or refuse to remit GIC is not a ‘reviewable decision’ for the purposes of the ART Act.

    (d) The Tribunal is unable to conduct a review of a decision which applies to a person other than the Applicant in this proceeding. If another person has a concern about a decision of the Respondent which applies to them, that person would need to take advice as to whether they are able to make their own separate application for review to the Tribunal, even if that other decision arises from the same or similar facts to those arising in this proceeding.

CONCLUSION

  1. The combination of a number of circumstances specific to the Applicant has led the Tribunal to conclude that it would be appropriate to exercise the discretion under section 298-20 of Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 to remit the penalties imposed on the Applicant as set out at paragraph 55 (in relation to the ESST Penalties) and paragraph 65 (in relation to the Shortfall Penalties).
  2. The Tribunal wishes to reiterate that poor health or financial stress suffered by an Applicant would not ordinarily be sufficient reasons for remission of penalties. Other processes such as an application for release of a tax debt on the basis of serious hardship or administrative arrangements such as payment plans negotiated with the Respondent may be more appropriate options for consideration.
