Re Taxation Appeals (No 7) Aat [1986] AATA 289 (2 October 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS (No. 7)

AAT No. 2909

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

K.P. Brady (Senior Member)

J.E. Stewart (Senior Member)

D.J. Trowse (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - public accuntant - overseas travelling expenses

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 51(1), 190(b).

F.C. of T. v. Hatchett 71 ATC 4184

F.C. of T. v. Klan 85 ATC 406C

Magna Alloys & Research Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. 80 ATC 4542

HEARING

CANBERRA

2:10:1986

DECISION

The question for determination in this reference is whether the cost of an overseas trip to the United States undertaken by the taxpayer/ applicant, a self-employed public accountant, and hereinafter called the applicant, is an allowable deduction pursuant to s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

2. Upon the Commissioner's disallowance of the claim, and of the objection against the disallowance, that latter decision was, at the request of the applicant, referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board concluded its hearing of the matter prior to 1st July, 1986, and reserved its decision. Under the provisions of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, the functions of Taxation Boards of Review were transferred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal with effect from 1st July, 1986. As a consequence, the decision in the instant case, together with reasons in writing for the decision, are given pursuant to the provisions of s. 43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

3. In giving his evidence, the applicant told us that he conducted his own accountancy practice in a large provincial city. The firm's name was his own name with the standard appendage "& Associates". In preparing his income tax return for the year of income in issue, which was that ended 30th June, 1978, he advised the Commissioner of his claim in the following terms:

"Business trip to U.S.A. to study tourist facilities, hotels and

motels, tourist promotion and presentation, advertising,

agricultural trends and markets, the preparation and presentation

of foods, transport, service stations and anything that may be of

benefit to my clients, $2371."

The above expense, together with more mundane business expenses, was deducted from gross fees received from clients amounting to $79,972, to arrive at the net income of the firm for the year in issue. In processing the applicant's return, the Commissioner disallowed all of the cost of the trip to the U.S.A. on the basis that the expenditure was not incurred by the applicant in gaining his assessable income, nor was it necessarily incurred by him in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income.

4. The applicant went on to tell us that he went alone on the trip, which was a package tour open to the public at large and apparently organised by American Express. The tour was pre-arranged as to the places to be visited and as to the dates on which those visits would take place. The tour party arrived in the United States on 6th May, 1978, and returned to Australia, via a stopover in Hawaii, some three weeks later. The applicant told us that, prior to making his booking, he examined a number of brochures detailing a wide range of available tours to the U.S.A. but he chose the American Express tour because he considered that that company would have more contacts in America than any other travel organisation, and so its tour was best suited for his purposes. The decision to go by way of a package tour instead of travelling privately was mainly dictated by the cheaper cost of the tour. Another attraction of the American Express arrangement was the considerable amount of free time which the tour party enjoyed. The applicant made use of that free time to talk to Americans having the same sorts of business interests as many of his clients. It seems too that he did not undertake all of the pre-arranged tours within the United States; rather, he preferred to use that time to talk with local inhabitants, be they motel staff, taxi drivers or simply passers-by in the street, and ascertain their views over a wide range of topics in order to add to his own stock of general knowledge. Somewhat curiously, the applicant did not arrange to call on particular businessmen or inspect specific businesses before leaving Australia. He told us that he did not need anyone to point him in a possible direction; rather, he preferred to make enquiries in his own way.

5. The amounts expended on the trip comprised:

Fares and accommodation $1,820

Travel and entertainment in the

United States 551

Additionally, the applicant made other outlays totalling $750 which he categorised as non-business expenditures, and for which he made no claim.

6. Included in the applicant's file and tendered as Exhibit A was a copy of a lengthy letter from him addressed to the Taxation Office advising details of his trip in response to a request for further information made by that office. In giving his evidence, the applicant basically elaborated on the content of his letter. Essentially it appeared to us that the trip involved visits to the main tourist places of California, interspersed with a plane flight to Las Vegas and over the Grand Canyon and Boulder Dam.

7. Whilst the tour was of the package type and the places visited by the applicant were in the main tourist attractions, we accept the applicant's evidence that he made the trip, at least in part, in order to better inform his clients on a whole host of business-related matters. Whilst in the United States, he observed that the adage "the customer is king" is accepted by all engaged in business and finds expression in the efforts taken by business houses to always please the customer in order to ensure that his or her patronage is kept. That information he passed on to his own clients upon his return so that they, in their turn, would provide a better service to their clients. He also told us that he predicted the 1982/83 economic downturn in Australia on the basis of what he had seen and heard in the United States and so advised his clients "to batten down the hatches". More particularly he told us that, resulting from visits he made to wineries and various tourist centres, he was able to pass on specific items of information to clients who were engaged in those same industries. As regards his own practice, he told us that his U.S. trip demonstrated to him the benefits to be obtained from advertising and from constantly seeking to service a client's every need.

8. The problem which we have with the fact situation of the instant case is that we do not believe that the applicant has discharged the onus imposed on him by s. 190(b) of the Assessment Act of showing that his assessment is excessive. In other words, we do not consider that he has demonstrated that the various expenditures outlaid on the trip were incurred in deriving his assessable income or, in the alternative, were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining assessable income. Paraphrasing what Mr. Justice Menzies stated in F.C. of T. v. Hatchett 71 ATC 4184 at p. 4187, we believe that probably the applicant was better able to inform his clients on a wide range of matters resulting from the trip, but that general type of consideration is not sufficient, in our view, to make the outlays deductible. We are not able to see any worthwhile nexus between the applicant's expenditures on the trip and the accounting firm's fee income.

9. A parallel, we believe, exists between the applicant's situation and that of an accountant in practice who takes his family for a drive on a Sunday afternoon and calls in at a vineyard with the thought of obtaining information as to its operation with a view to helping a client who also operates a vineyard. The reason for the call does not convert an essentially private matter into a business transaction and so make the costs associated with the use of the car on the afternoon visit deductible for tax. Such costs, we consider, would not be relevant and incidental to the production of the accountant's assessable income, and neither, we believe, were the expenditures outlaid by the applicant in travelling abroad in the instant case.

10. In answer to a question put to the applicant in cross-examination as to whether his practice derived any additional income as a result of his overseas trip, he gave an affirmative reply and, in amplification, he stated:

"I have told a lot of people, hundreds, probably thousands, that I

have been to America. I have explained to them how I see business

in America and how it operated in America and various things that

would apply to Australia, and of opportunities in America."

He then advised us of a specific case where a client had made money because of advice he had given to him on a particular waste oil project which, in general terms, was a subject on which he had acquired knowledge when in the United States. But what the applicant failed to tell us was how his own income was affected by his client's response to his advice and, if it were so affected, to what extent.

11. Throughout all of the applicant's evidence, we were not able to discern any direct relationship between the tour outlays and his assessable income in the sense of seeing how those outlays were incidental and relevant to the gaining of that income. We believe that one of the purposes of the applicant's trip was to increase his stock of general business knowledge so as to better service his clients' various needs, but, as was stated in F.C. of T. v. Klan 85 ATC 406C, the applicant's state of mind is but one item of evidence which must be examined in conjunction with all other items of evidence. In that case, Ormiston J., in discussing the matter of the taxpayer's purpose in making an overseas trip, cited the following dicta of Brennan J. stated in Magna Alloys & Research Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. 80 ATC 4542 at pp. 4551/4552:

"Given a sufficient identification of what the expenditure is for and

the character and scope of the taxpayer's income-earning undertaking

or business, the question whether expenditure is incurred for the

purpose of carrying on a business or for the purpose of gaining or

producing assessable income does not depend upon the taxpayer's

state of mind. The relationship between what the expenditure is for

and the taxpayer's undertaking or business determines objectively

the purpose of the expenditure. In cases to which a reference to

purpose is required or appropriate, objective purpose will be found

to be an element in determining whether expenditure is incurred in

gaining or producing assessable income or in carrying on business."

12. In our view the expenditures incurred by the applicant in making the tour had insufficient connection with the applicant's income to be properly characterised as outgoings incidental and relevant to it. Rather, they related to the acquisition of a fund of knowledge, some aspects being of a commonplace nature, some of a business kind, but very general in their scope. Thus all aspects, in our view, tended to stamp the character of the outgoings as of a private nature.

13. For the reasons outlined above, we propose to affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection under review.
