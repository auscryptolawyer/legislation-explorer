Cameron and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 590 (9 August 1995)

Cameron and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 590 (9 August 1995)

Last Updated: 24 September 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

RETIREMENT BENEFITS - Superannuation - elections - discretion to recognise late election



Superannuation Act 1976 ss 137(1), 157(1)



Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 50 FCR 150

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman 50 FCR 236

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456

Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT 9987, 6 February 1995

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: DONALD RALPH CAMERON



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION



Tribunal: Dr D J Grimes, Senior Member

Mr N J Attwood, Member

Mr G D Stanford, Member

  1. This is an application to review a decision of the delegate of the respondent made under subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) not to direct that the election made by the applicant on 17 February 1992 under subsection 137(1) of the Act be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that subsection. The Act provides as follows:

“s137(1) A person who:



(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or



(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;



may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect by notice on writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.



...



s157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

  1. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr G Lunney of Counsel and gave oral evidence. The respondent was represented by Mr A Merrilees of the Commissioner for Superannuation. The Tribunal had before it documents provided pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and exhibits produced at the hearing.
  2. The applicant was born on 3 May 1945. He was educated to university level, receiving a Batchelor of Economics degree from the University of Sydney. He entered the Public Service as a graduate clerk in 1969 and became a contributor of the superannuation fund established by the Superannuation Act 1922 on 7 July 1969 when with the Public Service Board.
  3. On 1 July 1976, at the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976, he became an “eligible employee” under that Act. On that occasion he was required to make a decision about his equity in reserve units under the Superannuation Act 1922 and on 24 June 1976 made an application for a refund of contributions on 12 reserve units. In doing so he completed and signed a form SB2A. The applicant gave evidence he has no memory of the form or a summary of benefits available to a contributor who resigned under the heading “Outline of Conditions for Preservation of Superannuation Rights” (Document T7).
  4. The applicant took leave without pay to travel overseas from 12 April 1977 to 11 July 1977. In February 1977 he wrote a letter to the Commissioner for Superannuation informing him of his intentions and enclosing a cheque for $108.72 being for contributions to his superannuation while he was away (Exhibit R2).
  5. In 1982 the applicant, while employed by the Department of Housing and Construction, was seconded to the Master Builders Association in the ACT for 9 months during which time his salary was paid by the Department. This was to gain experience in the operations of the building industry in the ACT, in particular in the industrial relations and personnel areas. He came to the realisation that he may well develop a career in private industry and made specific enquiries (Document T10) about the sum of money he would receive by way of a refund if he resigned.
  6. He returned to the Department of Housing and Construction until September 1984, when he resigned. He gave evidence he believed he would benefit from further experience in the Master Builders Association so, when offered a position, he accepted it.
  7. The applicant gave evidence that he intended to continue a career in industrial relations but that he only intended to gain experience with the Master Builders Association and then return to the public service. He told his immediate supervisors, John Kay and Stephen Polywoda. He also gave evidence that he also spoke of his plans to someone in the personnel department but that he could not recall his name.
  8. He gave evidence that he was interested in returning to the public service in a middle management position and thought his chances of eventually securing such a position were “moderately good”.
  9. On his resignation in September 1984 the applicant completed Form S2A (Document T13). This form is headed “Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights”. In a box on the form the applicant is required to “delete whichever is applicable” of one or more of three options, namely: “apply for a refund of accumulated contributions; apply for payment of a lump sum benefit; elect for preservation of superannuation rights”. He completed the form by scoring out two options leaving the option of an application for refund of accumulated contributions.
  10. The applicant gave evidence that until he saw form S2A his knowledge of preservation rights was nil. He believed that the option meant that the accumulated contributions were left in the fund and gathered interest. On form S2A the applicant directed that the refund of contributions be sent to his then solicitors, Phillips & Company, Canberra City, ACT 2601. This was to enable them to use the funds to reduce a mortgage he had obtained to purchase a new house.
  11. At the time of his resignation the applicant and his wife were expecting another child and had been seeking a new house. They found the house they desired and purchased it for $220,000 using funds obtained from the sale of their previous house, a bequest received by the applicant’s wife, funds in the applicant’s possession and a $40,000 mortgage. The refund was used to reduce this mortgage.
  12. The applicant gave evidence he worked for the Master Builders Association for two, to two and a half years. The Association had no superannuation scheme. He made enquiries of the AMP about a private superannuation scheme but did not proceed with it as he did not consider it a good investment.
  13. Six months before leaving the Master Builders Association a new person was placed in charge of industrial relations. The applicant said he did not see “eye to eye” with the new person, life became strained and he resigned. In answer to a question from the Tribunal the applicant agreed he had applied for the manager’s job himself and if he had been successful he would have continued to work there.
  14. After resigning from the Master Builders Association the applicant sought industrial relations positions with his old department. He did not succeed and after seeking professional recruitment advice, sold insurance for a time and continued to apply for public service positions, eventually securing a middle management position one grade below his previous position. The position was in the Insurance and Superannuation Commission, in a policy area, and the applicant again became an “eligible employee” on 29 August 1988. His experience at the Master Builders Association had given him a knowledge of occupational superannuation which assisted him in his new position.
  15. The applicant gave evidence that on returning to his employment in the Public Service he had no induction day. He assumed he had no right to have his previous superannuation considered in rejoining the superannuation scheme and made no enquiries about it. His evidence was that he only learnt of the late election option in a chance conversation with a fellow employee in late 1991. This caused him to make investigations and ultimately make the application which led to this appeal.
  16. Enquiries were made by the Commissioner for Superannuation of the Department of Administrative Services when Mr Cameron applied for his late election, seeking information about what would be available to the applicant on retirement in 1984.
  17. Mr Cameron’s file at the time of his resignation could not be obtained but the Department (Document T26) advised that:

“Preservation options were not generally well understood by Personnel staff at the time and if the question was raised, Mr Cameron would most likely have been referred to your Office.

...as Mr Cameron would have been required to make an election on the Form S2A/S2R he would have been aware of all the options available to him.”

  1. The Insurance and Superannuation Commission was asked about information given to new members and their superannuation rights and they responded at Document T24:

“I refer to your letter reference 2317671 dated 28 February 1992 regarding an application from the above officer to repay his previous superannuation refund and have all his service recredited.

It is the Commission’s policy to advise all new members of their superannuation rights. All new members are supplied with the “new member” information kits which has now superseded the new contributor leaflet. Should a member mention previous employment or superannuation scheme membership all possible information is supplied, however, should Personnel not know the answer to the staff member’s query it is our practise to contact your office to obtain the correct information.

An intensive search of Mr Cameron’s file has revealed that he has not requested any information regarding this matter either in writing or verbally.

Should you have any queries regarding this matter please contact Maggie Treleaven...”



  1. At the time of his resignation from the Public Service the applicant was a clerk and had a Batchelor of Economics degree from Sydney University. On his evidence, Form S2A alerted him to the existence of the option of preservation of rights but he chose to make his own interpretation of what this meant rather than make enquiries. This was despite the fact he had on an earlier occasion elected to contribute additional units under the previous superannuation scheme (Superannuation Act 1922) and had later applied successfully for a refund of these contributions.
  2. The applicant used the refund of contributions he received in 1984 to reduce the mortgage on a recently purchased family home and he arranged for the refund to be paid directly to his solicitors for this purpose.
  3. The Tribunal accepts that there was no pressing need for the funds to purchase the house, it was “convenient” to use them for this purpose, and no attempt was made by the applicant to enquire as to the possibility of an alternative use.
  4. The applicant gave evidence he intended to return to the Public Service but conceded that had he succeeded in becoming the industrial relations manager at the Master Builders’ Association he would have accepted the position. No mention of his being a candidate for this position was made in an affidavit sworn by the applicant on 15 March 1995 for the Tribunal (Exhibit A1).
  5. The applicant gave evidence that on returning to the Public Service in 1988 he used the fact that he had “become extremely familiar” with the topic of occupational superannuation at the Master Builders’ Association. The Tribunal finds it surprising that this being so he made no enquiries about part or continuing entitlement until alerted to the possibility of late election in a chance conversation in 1991. This is surprising in the light of the applicant’s evidence that his knowledge of superannuation was partly responsible for his job with the Insurance and Superannuation Commission.
  6. Factors to be considered by the Tribunal were discussed by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 50 FCR 150. In referring to the application of section 157 Davis J (at 156) said:
“In the application of s 157, it may be of little consequence that the employee did not inquire as to his or her right to elect under s 137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case. Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s 157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances need not be of a medical character. ...

The superannuation scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights. But the entitlement to participate in the scheme and to receive the benefits of the scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the s 157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded.”
  1. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman 50 FCR 236 von Doussa J said at 244:
“... the connotation of ‘desirable’ in the context of s 157(1) is that of being fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election, and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.”

His Honour continued:

“Whether a late election is ‘desirable’ is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case. The suggested meaning ‘something worthy of achievement’ does not imply other than an objective evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in the end, whether the recognition of a late election is ‘something worthy of achievement’ must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.”
  1. The evidence before the Tribunal is that the applicant was conscious of the desirability of superannuation and the workings of the superannuation schemes. He had previously taken out and later obtained refunds of contributions under the scheme in existence at the time.
  2. Deputy President Forgie stated in Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 at 473:
”However, what the form does do is to alert the reader to the fact that he or she must choose one of three things: a refund of accumulated contributions, a lump sum benefit or preservation of superannuation rights. Certainly, no information can be gleaned from the form itself about these options but the form does, in my view, put the reader on notice that he or she should make further enquiries as to whether these options apply to his or her circumstances and generally find out more information about them.”
  1. At the time of his resignation in 1984 the applicant was alerted to the possibility of preservation of benefits by form S2A but chose to put his own interpretation on it rather than make further enquiries.
  2. On resignation the applicant sought payment of his refund to his solicitors to reduce his mortgage payments and was happy with that course of action. Despite the lack of superannuation in his new position he made only one enquiry about private superannuation schemes and rejected that possibility.
  3. On return to the Public Service in 1988, despite having acquired experience in superannuation and taking a position in the area of superannuation he again made no further enquiries as to his superannuation entitlements and gave evidence of only discovering the late election option in a casual conversation in 1991.
  4. The Tribunal is satisfied that had the applicant succeeded in becoming industrial relations manager at the Master Builders’ Association he would have been content to stay in that employment with or without superannuation and his return to the Public Service was prompted by his failure to obtain that position.
  5. The applicant submitted to the Tribunal that it should take into account the applicant's lack of knowledge of the existence of the preservation of rights option before making the election of Form S2A in 1984 and that when his attention was drawn to the option he misinterpreted it. He further claimed that on rejoining the Public Service he was inadequately informed as to the possibility of making a late election, only discovering this by chance at a later date.
  6. The respondent contended that the applicant, despite being alerted to the right to preserve superannuation rights, had made a conscious decision to take his refund of contributions and invest it in another manner, namely to reduce his mortgage. Despite previous knowledge of the superannuation scheme he made no enquiries as to the definition of his rights except to seek the total of his entitlement should he accept a refund on resignation. When alerted to the option of preservation he chose to rely on his own understanding and on rejoining the public service made no enquiries as to his entitlements despite having acquired a knowledge of occupational superannuation.
  7. The respondent submitted that the applicant's education, level of public sector experience and admission that he was put on notice of the possibility of preservation should have resulted in his seeking further information rather than relying on his own understanding.
  8. The Tribunal relies on the summary of principles provided by Deputy President McMahon in Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT 9987, 6 February 1995 at paragraphs 17 to 19 and which there is no need to reproduce here.
  9. Having regard to the broad principles outlined in Ward the Tribunal has come to the following conclusions:
    • (a) The applicant, an educated and experienced public service employee was alerted to the possibility of preservation of superannuation entitlements on leaving the public service in 1984 but chose to rely on his own interpretation rather than make enquiries.
    • (b) The applicant made a conscious decision to use the refund of contributions subsequently obtained to reduce a mortgage on a recently purchased home.
    • (c) Despite expressing a knowledge of the importance of superannuation the applicant made only one enquiry about private superannuation while out of the public service and rejected that option.
    • (d) The applicant gave evidence that had he been successful in obtaining the position he sought in private employment, he would have continued there rather than return to the public service.
    • (e) The applicant made no enquiries as to the possibilities of obtaining credit for previous employment with the public service when he returned in 1988.
  10. At the time of his resignation and since, the applicant has had the use of his refunded contributions for the purpose he chose and if permitted to elect he would receive a gain against other members of the scheme who remained in the scheme.
  11. The Tribunal finds that it was not presented with a reasonable explanation for the delay and in all the circumstances it is not desirable to recognise the applicant's late election. For the reasons given the Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
