Re Taxation Appeals [1989] AATA 213 (27 September 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. WT88/525

AAT No. 5394

Sales Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

G.L. McDonald(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Sales Tax - whether goods manufactured "by manufacturer in the course of carrying on a business and applied to his own use" - activities in question - manufacture of display fixtures and ticket writing for own use found to be incidental to overall retail business.

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 S.37

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 sections 3, 11 17,25AA

Brayson Motors Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 85 ATC 4125

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Nimrod Theatre Co Ltd 85 ATC 4092

In the matter of the Totalisator Board of Queensland 88 ATC 5025

Adams v Rau and Another [1931] HCA 43; (1931) 46 CLR 572

HEARING

PERTH

27:9:1989

ORDER

The decision of the respondent is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with a direction that the applicant is not required to pay tax under the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 on the goods set out in the assessment dated 4 December 1987.

DECISION

Background

The applicant, a retailer, is disputing liability to pay sales tax on goods produced in its factory and cards printed in its printing office. The items produced in the factory and printing office are used by the applicant in the course of its retailing business.

2. The facts are not in dispute and an agreed statement of facts was filed. Additionally, the Tribunal received the documents filed under S37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and other documents tendered in evidence and heard from the person occupying the compendious position of Finance Director Secretary and Public Officer of the applicant company as well as its Operational Services Manager. I find the facts to be as follows.

Facts

3. The applicant is an incorporated company and operates a number of retail department stores, of which one is located in the city and the others in the suburbs, selling clothing, fashion accessories, home furnishings, homewares, haberdashery and dress fabrics. Prior to 3 August 1981, the City Store was owned and operated by a related company. Included in that related company's operations was a factory and ticket writing office which provided services not only to the City store but to suburban stores in the same chain. On 3 August 1981, the related company sold the whole of its operations to the applicant which until that time had controlled the suburban stores. From that date the applicant has run the business including the operations of the factory and the ticket writing office.

4. Display fixtures and fittings including counters, benches, fitting rooms, back walling, stands, boxes, racks and other like items (examples of which are set out in exhibit "S") are made at the factory for use by the applicant in it's stores. Some of the items made are free standing and some are installed as fixtures. No items of furniture are made for the purposes of re-sale to the public. When items made in the factory are no longer needed for use they are either sent to the rubbish tip or sold by auction. The factory is located separately from the retail stores. In September 1987 there were approximately 3 to 4 employees engaged in the factory (Transcript P41). The running of the factory was the responsibility of the applicant's Operations Manager which involved him in visiting the factory several times a day. Work was carried out by carpenters, joiners or french polishers as required by the work schedules which were prepared and submitted to the workshop by the various Store Departmental Managers. Some of the work involved repairs.

5. The display signs at the time they are purchased by the applicant are either blank cards of varying sizes or cards with a border trim and the name by which the applicant is generally known in the retailing business was printed in one corner. Sales Tax was paid on the cards at the time they were purchased by the applicant. At the applicant's ticket writing office, product description, price and conditions of sale are written onto the display cards. This is done mechanically or by hand. Request for the cards comes from the person responsible in the retail store for the sale of the goods in relation to which the promotional card has been requested. No signwriting is done for any purpose other than display in the applicant's retail stores. The cards are normally used for short term display purposes and are then destroyed. The printing office is located in premises leased by the applicant but separate from the retail stores and the factory.

6. As best as can be calculated the value of the tickets and goods produced in the factory together represent .116% of the applicants total turnover for the year ending 1988.

7. Each of the stores in the chain has its own profitability requirements. In case of work done by the factory the cost is directly attributed to the store involved. In the case of printed cards the cost is not directly attributed but an allocation is made to each store based on the proportion of the revenue generated by that store (P43 Transcript).

8. The items produced at the factory and the printing office are produced solely for the use by the applicant in its retailing business with the following one exception.

9. The applicant is part of a company in which the shares are largely held by the members of the one family. Occasionally, the factory repairs furniture for members of the family or installs minor items of furniture at shareholders' homes. The operational services manager who is also a carpenter by trade told the Tribunal that in the 3 and 1/3 years that he had been with the applicant company he can only recall such requests being made 3 or 4 times. They related to the repair of some older furniture and the making of some laundry and kitchen cupboards. When such items were made for shareholders' homes they were costed and sales tax paid on them.

10. The applicant conducts a retail business in the course of which it sells, and has always sold, curtain fabrics. Until 1981, the company offered a service of making curtain fabric into curtains on measurements given by the customer. From 1981 the service was still offered to customers but the work sub-contracted to people or firms unrelated to the applicant. At that time, no sales tax was paid for making up the curtains. In 1983, when sales tax was applied in this area, the sub-contractor would make the curtains, render the applicant an account for labour, the applicant would record those details and apply the appropriate rate of sales tax to the total cost to be paid and pay the tax accordingly. The manufacture of curtains represented .33% of the applicant's turnover in 1988.

11. On 13 October 1981, the applicant registered as a manufacturer of "display fixtures and printing (ticket writing)" pursuant to the provisions of sll of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 ("the Sales Tax Act"). The applicant has paid sales tax on both the work carried out by the factory and the printing operations since that time.

The Application and Sales Tax Act Provisions

12. On 21 October 1987, the applicant requested a special assessment pursuant to s25AA of the Sales Tax Act in respect of merchandising of work carried out in the factory and ticket office for the month of September 1987. A sales tax assessment of $853.23 dated 4 December 1987 was issued for the goods so manufactured in the month of September 1987. The assessment was based on the sale value of the goods (cost $355.14 plus 20% mark-up) and the tax assessed at the general rate of 20%. On 28 January 1988 the applicant lodged a Notice of Objection in respect of that assessment and it is that matter which is now before the Tribunal for determination.

13. The relevant Sections of the Sales Tax Act are as follows:

"S3 "Manufacturer" means a person who engages,

whether exclusively or not, in the

manufacture of goods and includes a printer,

publisher, lithographer or engraver, and a

person (not being an employee) who

manufactures goods, whether or not the

materials out of which the goods are

manufactured are owned by him, but where one

person manufactures goods for another,

wholly or in part out of material supplied

by that other and goods are not for the use

of, but are for sale by, that other, the

person supplying the material shall be

deemed to be the manufacturer, and the

person who shall manufacture the goods shall

be deemed not to be the manufacturer;

"S17(1) . . . sales tax . . . shall be levied and paid upon

the sale value of goods manufactured in

Australia by a taxpayer and sold by him or

treated by him as stock for sale by retail

or applied to his own use.

"S17(2) . . . the reference in sub-section (1) to goods

manufactured in Australia by a taxpayer and

applied to his own use shall be read as a reference

(a) to goods manufactured in Australia by a

manufacturer in the course of carrying

on a business and applied to his own

use, whether for the purposes of that

business or for any other purpose and

whether or not the goods are of a class

manufactured by the manufacturer for sale;

14. The questions which arise for consideration are whether the items in issue are:

(a) goods;

(b) manufactured;

(c) by a manufacturer;

(d) in the course of carrying on a business; and

(e) applied to his own use.

15. The parties are agreed that the items produced in the factory and from the printing operation are "goods" and that they are "manufactured" in accordance with the way those terms are defined in S3 of the Sales Tax Act. Items (c), (d), (e) are however in dispute. For the sake of completeness I have addressed the arguments with respect to each item.

16. The fact that the applicant is registered as a manufacturer pursuant to sll of the Sales Tax Act does not per se lead to the conclusion that the applicant is a "manufacturer". Registration does not create the liability to pay sales tax - that exists independently of registration. If a person or company is registered in circumstances in which registration is not in fact required then that person or company does not by virtue of the registration become a "manufacturer". Rather the liability to pay sales tax depends on an analysis of whether the person or company is engaged in taxable transactions - whether wholly or as part of the business in which they are otherwise engaged (Brayson Motors Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 85 ATC 4125).

Are the goods manufactured by a "manufacturer"?

17. The applicant asserts that the goods were not manufactured by a "manufacturer" and relies on the cases of the Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Nimrod Theatre Co Ltd 85 ATC 4092 ("Nimrod") and the Totalisator Board of Queensland 88 ATC 5025 ("TAB Q").

18. In Nimrod's case the Full Court of the Federal Court (Fox, McGregor and Wilcox JJ) dismissed an appeal from a decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales which found that Nimrod, which carried on the business of presenting theatrical productions to the public, did not become a manufacturer in circumstances in which it produced (only) its own scenery and stage props. In that case, the Court concluded that a person can only be said to be a manufacturer if manufacturing is that person's "vocation or business" (McGregor J at page 4101) and the Act did not have any application to persons essentially engaged in the course of some other activity who, from time to time, produced some goods for their own use.

19. In the matter of TAB O the Supreme Court of Queensland made a declaration that the Totalisator Agency Board of Queensland was not liable to pay sales tax on material printed in its own printer room where the material printed was for its own use, whether as stationery for general office use, or tickets, receipts etc.

20. McPherson J in the course of his judgment made the following comment (at page 5028):

"I do not think that, in a matter like

this, I should decline to follow a

decision of the Full Court of the Federal

Court even if I believed it to be wrongly

decided. The factual differences between

that case and this cannot be denied: but

they do not affect the ratio of the

decision as disclosed by the reasons of

Fox and McGregor JJ to which I have

referred. It follows in my opinion that

the TAB is not a "manufacturer" and

consequently that it is not within s17 of

the Act liable to pay sales tax on the

value of the printed matter produced by

it and applied to its own use".

21. In the instant case, the applicant's relatively trivial operations with respect to the making up of curtains and repair of furniture for the shareholders' houses are indicative only of manufacture and do not enable the overall business in which it engages to be characterised as one of a "manufacturer". The applicant's activities must be regarded as a whole in determining how the applicant is to be categorised and not "disintegrated" into their component parts (Adams v Rau and Another [1931] HCA 43; (1931) 46 CLR 572). I am satisfied the services the applicant offers in arranging the making up of curtains and the minor services given to shareholders are not such that they are sufficient, by themselves or in combination with any of the other activities carried out by the applicant, to sustain a conclusion that the applicant's overall business can properly be categorised as one of a "manufacturer".

22. From the facts before me I am satisfied that the correct description of the applicant's overall business activities is that of a "retailer". Whilst there may be some activities carried out by the applicant which may suggest otherwise (eg the provision of credit, locating coffee shops within its stores etc) these activities are all part of normal services offered by retailers and the fact the applicant engages in them does not detract from the overall proposition that it is a retailer.

23. Just as Nimrod made stage props and TAB O published its own stationery, cards and tickets and those activities were found to be incidental to the overall activities of the respective bodies so here the level and nature of the activities carried out in the factory and by the ticket office in comparison to the applicant's overall retail business are, on the facts, more consistent with those activities being but an incidental adjunct to the overall retail business than with them being regarded as separate manufacturing businesses. The evidence before me clearly indicated that the printing and ticket writing activities would not exist but for the retailing activities.

24. Whilst there may be some differences on the facts between the instant case and the facts in Nimrod and TAB O - eg the incidental activities in those cases related to service industries and here the incidental activities relate to a retailer - those differences are not such that I am justified in not following the ratio enunciated in those decisions. For those reasons I find the applicant is not a "manufacturer" for the purposes of the Sales Tax Act.

Are the Goods manufactured "in the course of carrying on a business"?

25. This concerns the application of S17(2) of the Sales Tax Act in relation to goods manufactured in the course of carrying on a business and applied to one's own use.

26. Mr O'Sullivan submits that the use of the indefinite article "a business" distinguishes the phrase from a necessary connection to "carrying on the business of manufacturing" and that this Tribunal should not read that interpretation into the definition. In my view this argument was clearly open in both the Nimrod and TAB O cases and was one which, in light of the decisions, did not find favour with either court. The courts expressly found that s17 of the Sales Tax Act required sales tax to be levied on and paid by "persons engaged in the business of manufacturing, and not entities who are engaged in a different business . . . but as part of such occupation produces goods for its own use in that occupation". (Nimrod (supra) per McGregor J at page 4102-4103).

Are the goods manufactured by the applicant "applied to his own use"?

27. We are again here concerned with the application of S17(2) of the Sales Tax Act. In Nimrod (supra) McGregor J (at P4102) gave an example of an instance where tax would be payable on goods manufactured by a manufacturer and applied to his own use as follows:

"Presumably a person engaged in the

business of manufacturing electric light

globes, which he treated as goods for

sale, for his own use might apply some of

them to light his own factories. If such

goods were not allocated to the business

of the manufacturer for its own purposes,

the manufacturer would need to purchase

them from another. This transaction

could thus attract tax . . . . ."

28. In other words His Honour held that the provisions of S17 are relevant only in cases where a manufacturer in the course of carrying on a business of manufacturing applied the goods manufactured to his own use. Here that is not the case because, as I have found, the applicant is not in the business of manufacturing.

Conclusion

29. For the above reasons the application should succeed and the decision of the respondent be set aside.

30. I remit the matter to the respondent with a direction that the applicant is not required to pay tax under the Sales Tax Act on the goods set out in the assessment dated 4 December 1987.
