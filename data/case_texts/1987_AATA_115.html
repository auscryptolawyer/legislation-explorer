Taxation - eligible deductions s51(1) - immigrant - expenses involved in setting up consultancy and securing income earning employment.

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant arrived in Australia as an immigrant in April 1983 and immediately began to seek sources of income. He concentrated on finding positions which would allow him to exercise his skill as a business management consultant. To open up opportunities for earning income, he contacted various companies, went to exhibitions, talked to people likely to help him and attended functions where businessmen congregated.

He was not successful in obtaining any consultancy work for the year ended 30th June 1984. However, in response to a newspaper advertisement, he was employed as an accountant in a company on a full-time basis from December 1983 until the end of the financial year in question.

He continued throughout the year contacting likely prospects who might engage him for consultancy work.

In his return of income for the year ended 30th June 1984, he claimed a deduction of $4,927.86 and gave details of it n the following terms:

"The following expenditures were incurred to overcome unemployment and obtain the job through which the taxable income was earned in 1983/84:

$

  1. Rental of electric typewriter from 491.78

4/6/83 to 3/1/84 (receipts held)

  1. Stationary, supplies etc. 219.43 (receipts held for $124.23)
  2. For room used exclusively as office
    • - Rent 30 % of 4,950.00 1485.00
    • - Utilities 30 % of 281.27 84.38

(receipts held) 1569.38

  1. Cost of Telephone with 10 % of

metered charges deducted for private

use (receipts held for 393.61) 442.41

5. Cost of Postage

(receipts held for 195.37) 248.37

  1. Mileage for Subaru Stationwagon, 1800 cm , from July to December 1983, 8216 km
    • - 2922 km for private use
    • - 5294 km @ 21.5 1138.21
  2. Cost of repair of damage from an

automobile accident on 3 Aug. 83

(receipts held) 818.28

TOTAL FOR ITEM 17 4927.86"

The rental of the electric typewriter, the stationery, telephone and postage relate to letters to prospective clients and in some cases, relate to "free work" done to encourage prospective clients to give him a remunerative consultancy.

The monies claimed for the Subaru Stationwagon relate to journeys which he made "from north to south and east to west-all around Sydney" visiting companies, talking to business people and attending meetings where he thought he might make useful acquaintances.

On one of these trips, he was Involved in a motor accident not due to his own fault. As he was unable to recover the cost of repairs from the other driver, that sum has been included as item 7 in his claim.

His claim for a deduction, pursuant to section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 was disallowed by the respondent. This application is brought to review that decision.

The applicant's objection, which was referred to this Tribunal, is in the following terms:

"The objection is based on the grounds that

  1. there was a business necessity to incur the expenses.
  2. natural justice demands, that these business expenses be accepted by the Australian Taxation Office as legitimate deductions from income as it is obvious, that the income could not have been generated at all, if the source of it had not been generated at all, if the source of it had not been found first to generate the income. The context of incurred business expenses and subsequent income is obvious, timely and logical.
  1. equal treatment of all taxpayers is warranted, as these type of business expenses as claimed are acceptable in other cases, where a taxpayer incurs expenses while he has a source of income, for the purpose to change it or obtain an additional source of income. The disallowance in this case, therefore, is a denial of fair and equal treatment and should be corrected.
  1. disallowance of these business expenses by the Australian Taxation Office would - de facto -establish, that the Australian Government is encouraging immigrants to come to Australia and to go on the dole there, and stay on the dole, rather than try to establish a reasonable work situation that generates self-supporting income. If the Australian Taxation Office were to disallow legitimate expenses to generate taxable income in such cases as this one, it would be a betrayal of the Australian Government on these immigrants as well as on all Australian taxpayers, who have to foot the dole payments.

Therefore, a correction of the assessment of Taxable Income by $ 4,110 and $ 818 is warranted."

At the hearing, the applicant stated that he had claimed similar expenses since he started to earn monies as a business management consultant and that these claims, in subsequent years, had been allowed. He could not understand why expenses of the same nature could not be allowed for the year ended 30th June 1984.

If the claims were not allowed, then, in his submission, the tax laws were discriminatory and were "not in line with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights". He contended that the effect of the law was to discriminate against immigrants and that this constituted discrimination on the grounds of national origin. He submitted, further that if the Tribunal could not decide the issue in his favour, then it should set the assessment aside and obtain clarification of the discriminatory aspects of the legislation from a higher court. A subsidiary but relevant aspect of discrimination (in his submission) was that the fiscal year June to July was adopted in his case. Had it been January to December, he submitted that his expenses would have been allowable.

It has been pointed out on many occasions that the expenses contemplated by section 51(1) are those which are incurred in the course of gaining assessable income. Expenses incurred in getting, not doing, work are not deductible. For this, we have the authority of Commissioner of Taxation v Maddalena 71 ATC 4161, a decision of five judges of the High Court of Australia. Their view in that case, where a professional footballer incurred expenses in travelling to Sydney to negotiate with a metropolitan club to obtain a new position, was that the expenditure had come at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income.

The court was following a well worn path established as long ago as 1958 in Lunney v Commissioner of Taxation 100 CLR 478.

Preliminary expenses incurred in seeking a source of income, whether it be by way of salary or by way of fees as a self employed consultant, must be regarded as setting up expenses and therefore as capital. Section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 is in the following terms:

"51(1) [Deductions for losses and outgoings] All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

Expenses of the applicant in the present case are no -different in nature from the claim for the cost of newspapers and public transport fares in seeking employment considered in Case L63 79 ATC 496 where the taxpayer argued that in order to retain his eligibility for unemployment benefits, he was required to demonstrate that he was actively seeking a job. His claim was not allowed. Neither was the claim of the taxpayer in Case F71, 74 ATC 424, which dealt with an accountant who paid a fee to an employment agency which found employment for him.

A claim for expenses of this nature is often made and objections relating to them are often dealt with by this Tribunal and its predecessor, the Taxation Boards of Review. A recent restatement of principles (this time dealing with travelling expenses) can be found in a decision of this Tribunal, No. 003084 of 10 December 1986. At paragraph 5 the Tribunal said:

"The answer to the first question lies in the fact that the travelling expenses so incurred are not incurred 'in the course of gaining an assessable income, but rather in an endeavour to secure an opportunity to gain an assessable income from employment. I acknowledge that that hardly provides any incentive for an unemployed person to incur expense and seek employment, but the distinction has been long known and it is not for me to try to upset it. As the law stands, unemployed persons are entitled to unemployment relief so long as they are willing to accept work. They are not obliged to incur expense in seeking work and they can neither claim expenses of seeking employment as expenses of securing that unemployment relief, nor is expenses to be allowed against any income which may come to be earned as a result."

In his address, the applicant touched upon the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. This was adopted by the General Assembly of the United Nations on 10 December 1948. The Declaration does not itself impose any legal obligations upon member states of the United Nations, nor does it prescribe any legal rights for individuals. It merely forms the basis of subsequent covenants drawn up under the auspice of the United Nations and of the European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms. Thus, although Australia is a signatory to the Declaration, it does not form part of the domestic law of this country and certainly it does not invalidate any statute to the extent of any inconsistency.

The applicant may have views as to the fairness of the result of the decisions to which I have referred. There are many decisions refusing deductions under section 51(1) which produce similar feelings of unfairness. Many, for example, would say that deductions for child minding expenses should be allowed to working mothers. Some might say that special "setting up" expenses should be allowed to immigrants to enable them to settle into the community. These are aspirations and views more of a political than of a legal nature. Neither the Commissioner nor this Tribunal can deal with objections on other than legal grounds. If other approaches are to be generally accepted in the community as fairer approaches than those that presently obtain, then they must come about as a result of a political process not as a result of any decisions of this Tribunal.

The applicant's expenses must be characterised as being of a capital nature and are therefore excluded from eligibility for deductions under section 51(1).

The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
