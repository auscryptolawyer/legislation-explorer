Re Cyril Thompson and Commissioner of Superannuation [1985] AATA 179 (17 July 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CYRIL THOMPSON

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q85/45

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

A.N. Hall (Deputy President)

D.R.S. Craik (Member)

W. De Maria (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - history of gastric ulcer - surgical treatment - "post-gastrectomy state" condition by reason of which applicant admitted to Provident Account - retirement on grounds of invalidity - determination that retirement incapacity principally due to "post-traumatic stress disorder" which was contributed to by personality disorder - issue of benefit classification certificate - deemed to be in force prior to applicant's retirement - whether retirement incapacity caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition "connected with" post-gastrectomy state - whether applicant's personality disorder connecting link - decision of Commissioner set aside -

Superannuation Act 1976, ss. 66(2)(c), 154(4), 184(2), 184(7)

Re Bailey and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 666

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1

HEARING

CANBERRA

17:7:1985

ORDER

1. The Tribunal decides to:-

(i) SET ASIDE the decision taken by a delegate of the

Commissioner for Superannuation to issue, pursuant

to subsection 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976

("the Act"), a benefit classification certificate in

respect of the applicant AND IN SUBSTITUTION for

that decision, the Tribunal decides that the issue

of a benefit classification certificate was not

authorised by the Act; and

(ii) SET ASIDE the decision taken by a delegate of the

Commissioner for Superannuation to confirm the

decision under paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act, that

the incapacity which was the ground for Mr.

Thompson's retirement was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a physical or

mental condition or conditions specified in the

benefit classification certificate or by a physical

or mental condition or conditions connected with

such a condition or conditions AND IN SUBSTITUTION

for that decision, decides that the incapacity which

was the ground for the applicant's retirement was

not so caused or contributed to.

2. The Tribunal reserves liberty to apply for any necessary

further or consequential orders to carry its decision into effect.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation upon reconsideration pursuant to s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"), the effect of which was to deny the applicant full superannuation benefits following his invalidity retirement from the Commonwealth Public Service as from 6 November 1981.

2. The applicant was born on 4 June 1935. He joined the Public Service on 29 May 1972 when he commenced temporary employment as a motor vehicle driver with the Postmaster-General's Department in Adelaide. On 19 January 1973 he underwent a medical examination for the purpose of enabling a decision to be made, firstly, by the Public Service Board as to his fitness for permanent appointment to the Service, and, secondly, by the Superannation Board as to his health and fitness for acceptance as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account established under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act").

3. A medical report in respect of that examination prepared by Dr Leeson, a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO"), disclosed that the applicant had a history of gastric ulcer and that he had undergone surgical treatment for that condition in February 1971. In accordance with the guidelines contained in the Handbook for the Guidance of Commonwealth Medical Officers (see paras.107-110 - Ex. T5), Dr Leeson obtained a report from the Queen Elizabeth Hospital, Woodville, South Australia, where the polya-gastrectomy was performed (see report 31 January 1973 signed by Dr I.G. Hislop - Ex. T6). In accordance with the guidelines, Dr Leeson deferred for eleven months a decision as to whether or not Mr. Thompson should be accepted as medically fit.

4. On 16 November 1973 a further medical examination was conducted by Dr Carter, another CMO. The medical report shows that Dr Carter had available to him Mr. Thompson's leave file (T8/1). Dr Carter certified, following his further examination of the applicant, that Mr. Thompson was fit for permanent appointment to the Public Service. A notation "accept for Provident Fund" also appears above Dr Carter's signature. At the foot of the medical report, again under the signature of Dr Carter, appear the following comments:-

"There is a possibility of ulcer formation on the

anastomosis but I am quite sure it won't be within

three years, if at all, it ever occurs. This

ulceration would have nothing to do with the

previous gastric ulcer as that area is permanently

removed. However a possibility exists but only

very remote one as it would have recurred by now."

5. On 18 January 1974 a delegate of the Superannuation Board determined that the health and physical fitness of the applicant justified his acceptance as a contributor to the Provident Account under Part III of the superseded Act (see Ex. T27/1).

6. In the course of his employment as a mail van driver, Mr. Thompson was twice nearly involved in armed hold-ups (in 1974 and 1975) and was then actually involved in an armed robbery in 1978. He developed a severe anxiety condition as a consequence of these events. On 29 October 1980 the applicant was examined by Dr MacMillan, a further CMO, to enable the applicant's fitness to continue duty to be determined. As a result of this examination and following consideration of a report from Dr H. Barry, a specialist physician, dated 12 November 1980, Dr MacMillan formed the opinion that Mr. Thompson was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity (Ex. T9/2). Dr MacMillan identified the conditions contributing to Mr. Thompson's incapacity as:-

"Peptic ulceration, gastrectomy, dumping syndrome

75%; depressive state 25% (Ex. T9/2)."

(In a later clarification of this report, Dr MacMillan explained that, in his opinion, dumping syndrome was the relevant condition contributing 75% of the applicant's retirement incapacity. The ulceration and gastrectomy had been referred to as "leading to this condition" - see Ex. T17).

7. "Dumping syndrome" is defined in Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th Edn., as:-

"Disagreeable or painful epigastric fullness,

nausea, weakness, giddiness, sweating,

palpitations, and diarrhea occurring after meals in

patients who have had gastric surgery which

interferes with the function of the pylorus."

The "pylorus" is defined as the circular opening of the stomach into the duodenum. In the expert medical evidence before us (see Ex. T17/5) the symptoms of "late dumping syndrome" "are very similar to those of a severe fright or acute anxiety due to the outpouring of adrenalin etc.. The person becomes pale, shaky, sweaty and faint".

8. On 24 November 1980, Dr Schmidt, the Senior Medical Officer ("SMO") and adviser to the Commissioner for Superannuation, adopted Dr MacMillan's recommendation. As earlier noted, Mr. Thompson's retirement on the grounds of invalidity took effect from 6 November 1981.

9. The provisions of the 1976 Act of particular relevance to this application for review are subsection (2) and para. (7)(c) of s.184 which provide as follows:-

"184.(2) Subject to sub-section (4), where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who,

immediately before the commencing day, was

(otherwise than by reason of an election made

under section 81 of the superseded Act) a

contributor to the Provident Account ceases to

be an eligible employee by reason of death or

retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than

20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to

be an eligible employee, he has not attained

his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the

death of the person or the incapacity which was

the ground for the retirement of the person was

caused, or was substantially contributed to -

(i) by the physical or mental condition of

the person that was relevant for the

purposes of the superseded Act or, if

there was more than one such condition,

by those conditions or one or more of

those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or

conditions of the person connected with

the physical or mental condition of the

person that was relevant for the

purposes of the superseded Act or, if

there was more than one such condition,

with those conditions or one or more of

those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a

benefit classification certificate in which there is or

are specified the physical or mental condition or

conditions of the person that was or were relevant for

the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes

of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have

been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in

force in respect of the person immediately before his

death or retirement."

"184.(7) In this section -

.....

(c) a reference to the physical or mental

condition or conditions of a person that was

or were relevant for the purposes of the

superseded Act shall be read as a reference to

the physical or mental condition or conditions

of the person that, in the opinion of the

Commissioner, was or were the physical or

mental condition or conditions of the person

by reason of which -

(i) the Superannuation Board was not

satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the

superseded Act that the health and

physical fitness of the person were

such as to justify his being accepted

as a contributor to the Fund; or

(ii) the person was not, by virtue of a

provision of the superseded Act (other

than sub-section 5(1) or 79(2) of the

superseded Act), accepted as a

contributor to the Fund,

.....".

10. On 21 April 1982, a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation determined (i) that the physical condition that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act within the meaning of s.184(7)(c) was "history of gastric ulcer" and (ii) that pursuant to s.184(2)(c)(i) he was of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr. Thompson's retirement was caused by or was substantially contributed to by the history of gastric ulcer. It followed from these decisions that a benefit classification certificate was required to be issued pursuant to s.184(2) of the 1976 Act; that the certificate was deemed to have been in force for the purposes of s.16 of the Act immediately prior to Mr. Thompsons retirement; and that, accordingly, the applicant's superannuation benefits on retirement were limited by virtue of s.66(2) of the Act.

11. The applicant's solicitors requested reconsideration of these decisions by letter dated 23 June 1982 claiming, essentially, that Mr. Thompson's invalidity was not attributable to the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate but rather to the severe anxiety condition that he developed as a consequence of having been involved, in the course of his duties in 1978, in the armed robbery. Following further investigations, including a medical examination by Dr J.D. Dickey, a specialist gastroenterologist, and the obtaining of a report from Dr D. Jenkins, Mr. Thompson's treating psychiatrist, the Commissioner concluded that the incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement was, as to 80%, a condition described as "post-traumatic stress disorder" and, as to 20%, a condition described as "post-gastrectomy state". The delegate of the Commissioner formed the opinion, however, that the applicant's retirement incapacity was substantially contributed to by a mental condition connected with the applicant's "post-gastrectomy state" and decided therefore to affirm (although on different grounds) the original decision to issue a benefit classification certificate. As a consequence, the applicant's retirement benefits remained limited in accordance with s.66 of the Act.

12. The formal decision of the Commissioner's delegate on reconsideration was to:

"(i) vary the decision taken by a delegate on 21

April 1982 to issue, pursuant to subsection

184(2) of the Act, a benefit classification

certificate in respect of Mr. Thompson

specifying the condition 'History of Gastric

Ulcer' by substituting for that condition

'Post-Gastrectomy State'; and

(ii) confirm the decision taken by a delegate on

12 April 1982 to be of the opinion under

paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act that the

incapacity which was the ground for Mr.

Thompson's retirement was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a physical

or mental condition or conditions specified

in the benefit classification certificate or

by a physical or mental condition or

conditions connected with such a condition or

conditions."

13. The applicant applied to this Tribunal for review of the second of those decisions, contending that his retirement incapacity was not caused or substantially contributed to by his "post-gastrectomy state". At the conclusion of the hearing the Tribunal, having formed the opinion that the decisions under review should be set aside, and being concerned, having regard to the applicant's psychiatric state, that a decision in the matter should be given without delay, proceeded to state orally the substance of its reasons for decision. The Tribunal now reduces those reasons to writing for the assistance of the parties.

14. The questions that arise in this matter, pursuant to these provisions, are essentially three. Firstly, what was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of Mr. Thompson that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, and now in the opinion of this Tribunal, was or were the physical or mental conditions by reason of which he was not accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established under the superseded Act? That condition we refer to for the sake of brevity as "the relevant condition". The second question is: what was the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement? And the third question is: is the Commissioner, and now this Tribunal, of the opinion that Mr. Thompson's retirement incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by the relevant condition or by a physical or mental condition connected with that relevant condition? It is only if this last question is answered in the affirmative that the power to issue a benefit classification certificate arises pursuant to s.184(2).

Question 1 - The Relevant Condition

15. In the present case, as is often the position, there is no direct evidence before us as to the condition by reason of which the applicant was not accepted as a contributor to the former Superannuation Fund. We therefore adopt the approach referred to in the decision of the Tribunal in Re Bailey and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 666:-

"In a case such as the present, where there is

no direct evidence to establish the physical or

mental condition or conditions by reason of which

the Superannuation Board did not accept the

applicant as a contributor to the Superannuation

Fund, it appears to us that the proper test to

apply when considering the material before the

Board when it reached its decision is - what

condition or conditions must have influenced the

Board to accept the person into the Provident

Account rather than the Fund. Unless, on a fair

consideration of that material, the Tribunal can

feel satisfied that a particular physical or mental

condition was, in fact, a reason which influenced

the Board's decision, the Tribunal would not, in

our view, be justified in finding as a fact that

the condition should be taken as being specified in

the benefit classification certificate which is

deemed, by s184(2) of the Act, to be in force in

respect of the applicant."

16. We find, on the probabilities, that when deciding whether Mr. Thompson should be accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account, the delegate of the Superannuation Board had before him the medical examination report of Dr Leeson dated 5 February 1973 (Ex. T7); the report of Dr Hislop dated 31 January 1973 (Ex. T6); and the report of Dr Carter dated 16 November 1973 (Ex. T8). These reports disclosed that prior to undergoing a partial gastrectomy in February 1971, Mr. Thompson had had a history of symptoms of gastric ulcer. 17. Dr. Hislop's report outlined the history of Mr. Thompson's condition. So far as presently relevant, and omitting formal parts, his report was as follows (Ex. T6/1):-

"He first presented in July, 1970. He had an

insidious onset of epigastric pain which had

persisted intermittently for about 12 years. Just

prior to admission he had passed several melaenas.

His haemoglobin however, was 13 gm. Gastroscopy

was carried out within 48 hours and revealed a

chronic benign lesser curve gastric ulcer of

moderate size. He was treated with bed rest in

hospital and re-endoscopy one month later showed

the ulcer to have almost completely healed. He

was readmitted in January, 1971 with further pain

and the ulcer was found to have recurred on

gastroscopy. When surgery was performed three

weeks later the ulcer could not be found but a

polya-gastrectomy was performed.

About five weeks after the operation he began

to have episodes of vomiting and diarrhoea

associated with a little windy abdominal pain.

These symptoms persisted until he was commenced on

Amitriptyline to which he respondend rapidly.

Four months later he stopped the antidepressant and

within two months has suffered a recurrence of pain

and vomiting. There was some improvement on

recommencing the tablet.

He remained on this therapy until July, 1972

when he was symptom free. At this stage he had

changed his job and his whole outlook had increased

in confidence and security. He was taken off all

medication and when last seen in September, 1972

was asymptomatic and appeared to be in excellent

health. I regard his post-gastrectomy symptoms as

being primary psychogenic in origin and were

largely related to his unsatisfactory life

situation at that time. Since the change in

occupation he has been very well and I expect him

to continue his satisfactory progress."

17. When the matter was first considered by the Commissioner's Office, a delegate of the Commissioner found, as we earlier noted, that the relevant condition, for the purposes of s.184(2), was "history of gastric ulcer". On reconsideration this was changed to "post-gastrectomy state". On the evidence before us this description is not a term of art. It was the description adopted by Dr Dickey to encompass Mr. Thompson's history of gastric ulceration, gastrectomy and post-operative dumping syndrome.

18. The delegate, in fact, agreed with Dr Dickey's view that "history of gastric ulcer" was an inappropriate description of Mr. Thompson's post-operative condition. Accordingly he varied the condition stated in the benefit classification certificate to "post-gastrectomy state". A fair reading of Dr Dickey's very comprehensive and careful analysis of Mr. Thompson's medical history indicates, however, that the description "post-gastrectomy state" encompassed or was intended to encompass no more than the physiological sequelae of the gastrectomy operation. These sequelae included, on the evidence before us, the dumping syndrome, which is evidently not uncommon in such cases, and the risk, at some future date, of further surgical treatment to restructure the gastric anastomosis. So understood, it appears to us that "post-gastrectomy state" is as apt a description as can be found for the post-operative condition of a patient who has undergone a gastrectomy.

19. Reviewing the medical reports which we have found on the probabilities to have been before the delegate of the Superannuation Board, we are satisfied that the condition by reason of which Mr. Thompson was admitted to the Provident Account must have been the fact that he had undergone surgery for a gastric ulcer. That this was the primary focus of the CMOs' consideration is borne out, firstly, by the reference in the initial report by Dr Leeson, dated 5 February 1973, to paragraph 108 of the guidelines relating to peptic ulcers; secondly, to the fact that Dr Leeson obtained a report from the Queen Elizabeth Hospital where Mr. Thompson was treated and where ultimately the polya-gastrectomy was performed; and thirdly, to the fact that Dr Leeson then deferred a final decision in the matter for eleven months so that Mr. Thompson's work record could be considered. In so doing, Dr Leeson followed the guidelines relating to cases of peptic ulcers to the letter (see paras. 108 and 110 of the Handbook). Dr Carter who conducted the review examination on 27 November 1973, was clearly concerned with assessing the degree of risk of post-operative complications flowing from the gastrectomy, a risk that he dismissed as no more than a "possibility" (see para.4 above). No mention was made of any psychiatric or personality disorders.

Question 2 - The Retirement Incapacity

20. It is, we think, fair to say that it was common ground that, in the light of the specialist reports from Dr Dickey and Dr Jenkins, the incapacity, which was the ground for the applicant's ultimate retirement in 1981, was the effects of, firstly, post-traumatic stress disorder as to 80% and, secondly, post-gastrectomy state as to 20% (see Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1). Having regard to the whole of the evidence before us, we are content to find accordingly.

21. The applicant, who gave evidence on his own behalf, has plainly been shattered by the experience of having been twice nearly involved, and finally actually involved in 1978, in an armed hold-up in his former capacity as a PMG driver. Following the first incident in Adelaide in 1974, he had moved with his family from Adelaide to Brisbane. In 1975, he had arrived at the Kalinga Post Office just after an armed hold-up. These events made him fearful for his own safety. His worst fears were realised in the 1978 incident when he was grabbed from the rear, a revolver was pushed into his ribs and a knife was placed at his throat. He was blindfolded with surgical tape and bound. He was later released by another employee.

22. Dr Jenkins, Mr. Thompson's treating psychiatrist since May 1981, described these events as the trigger to the post-traumatic stress disorder from which the applicant now suffers, the effects of which were the principal reason for his retirement incapacity. Dr Jenkins also said that, in his opinion, Mr. Thompson had a basic personality disorder of a schizoid type with obsessional and paranoid features which had, in turn, contributed to the severity of the stress disorder.

Question 3 - The Retirement Incapacity/Provident Account

Condition

23. In his s.37 statement, the Commissioner conceded that, in the circumstances, he could not contend that the "post-gastrectomy state" had "caused" or "substantially contributed" to the retirement incapacity. It was said by the Commissioner, however, that there was a connection between the post-traumatic stress disorder, that was the principal ground for Mr. Thompson's incapacity on retirement, and the post-gastrectomy state which was the relevant condition that led to his admission to the Provident Account. The foundation for this argument was the report from Dr Hislop dated 31 January 1973 (see para.17).

24. Mr. Anforth, on behalf of the Commissioner, pressed the argument that because there were symptoms of a stress reaction post-operatively, that was sufficient to establish that those symptoms were part of the "condition" by reason of which the applicant was admitted to the Provident Account. It was his submission that the occurrence of those stress reaction symptoms, after the gastrectomy, justified the conclusion that the applicant's personality disorder must have played a part in his condition at that stage and that the personality disorder should be found therefore to be connected with both the post-gastrectomy state and the retirement incapacity, namely the post-traumatic stress disorder.

25. From the statements in Dr Hislop's report, the Commissioner submitted, firstly, that the post-gastrectomy symptoms, namely, vomiting, diarrhoea, and windy pain, that occurred after the operation, formed part of the "condition" by reason of which Mr. Thompson was admitted to the Provident Account; that, being psychogenic in origin, the symptoms should be seen as evidencing a psychiatric state; and that this psychiatric state should be found to have been contributed to by Mr. Thompson's underlying personality disorder (namely, schizoid type with obsessional paranoid features) and thus to be "connected with" the post-gastrectomy state. It was then argued that it was this same personality disorder which underlay, predisposed the applicant to, and exacerbated the post-traumatic stress disorder, the effects of which gave rise to the incapacity that was the ground for his retirement. The Commissioner submitted, therefore, that the incapacity that was the ground for Mr. Thompson's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a mental condition, namely a post-traumatic stress disorder, that was, in turn, contributed to by the applicant's personality disorder and that this personality disorder was also connected with the post-gastrectomy state by reason of which the applicant was admitted to the Provident Account. There are, in our view, some major assumptions in this somewhat attenuated argument for which there is either no support, or little support in the evidence before us.

26. As earlier indicated, Dr Dickey's report, in our view, lends no support to the conclusion that the post-gastrectomy state that he described, was intended to, or did, in fact, encompass any psychogenic element. Dr Dickey appears to have accepted that the symptoms referred to by Dr Hislop were probably attributable to stress and that they probably related to Mr. Thompson's "unsatisfactory life situation", whatever that may have been. In other words, those symptoms related to a stress reaction, not to the gastrectomy. Dr Dickey dismissed the suggestion that gastric ulcers are psychosomatic. He also said that there was little evidence, if any, to show that anxiety and depression caused gastric ulcers.

27. At p.6 of his report, Dr Dickey makes it clear that the post-gastrectomy symptoms described by Dr Hislop were "quite trivial". Having pointed out that a good deal of specialist gastroenterological practice deals with gastro-intestinal symptoms related to depression and anxiety, Dr Dickey said:-

"I do not think that the ulcer or the subsequent

surgery has caused depression or anxiety but merely

allowed the symptom of vomiting to occur more

readily than would happen in a person with a normal

stomach." (emphasis added) (Ex. T17/8)

28. Dr Dickey's report also made clear the difficulty of unravelling symptoms of "late dumping" from symptoms of anxiety because of their similarity. In our view, however, his report leaves no room for doubt that he saw the post-gastrectomy symptoms of vomiting and diarrhoea described by Dr Hislop as stress related, and as having no connection with the gastrectomy except in the way he described, namely, that the surgical intervention to cure the ulcer allowed the symptoms of anxiety and stress, such as vomiting, to occur more readily. The fact that the symptoms of a stress reaction may occur more readily because of a gastrectomy does not, in our view, lead to the conclusion that the stress reaction itself is therefore "connected with" the post-gastrectomy state. We did not understand the Commissioner to so contend. Furthermore, the evidence with respect to this "stress reaction" is, to say the least, inconclusive as to whether it truly evidenced a psychiatric condition, and as to whether any personality disorder from which the applicant may then have suffered was a contributing factor.

29. It is true that, in his report of 26 July 1984, Dr Jenkins expressed the opinion that, on the probabilities, the symptoms described by Dr Hislop were connected with some underlying mental condition. His written answer, with respect, left much to be desired as to what precisely was the underlying mental condition that he had in mind. In his evidence before the Tribunal, however, Dr Jenkins resiled from expressing any opinion as to the applicant's mental state in 1971. His evidence ultimately went no further than to say that Mr. Thompson's personality disorder was something that he "grew into". How disordered that personality was in 1971, and what contribution, if any, it made to the stress reaction described by Dr Hislop, Dr Jenkins was unable to say. Thus, Dr Jenkins' evidence, in our view, fails to establish that the applicant had a psychiatric condition in 1971. In our view, the evidence establishes no more than that, whilst Mr. Thompson was in hospital recuperating from his gastrectomy, he exhibited symptoms of a stress reaction related to his unsatisfactory life situation. There is no evidence to establish that the stress reaction was abnormal. We do not know what the "unsatisfactory life situation" was. The circumstances giving rise to that reaction have not been investigated or explained.

30. Looking back to the medical evidence available to the delegate of the Superannuation Board in 1973, we are satisfied, as we have said, that the condition by reason of which a decision to admit Mr. Thompson to the Provident Account was made, was the fact that he had recently required surgery for gastric ulcer. The CMOs' reports in 1973 gave no hint of any nervous or mental state having been observed in Mr. Thompson. On the contrary, Dr Leeson wrote "no" in answer to the question, "Is there any evidence of nervous or mental troubles" (Q.6(b)). Dr Hislop, in his letter of 31 January 1973, gave a very positive prognosis for the applicant, saying that he had been symptom free since July 1972, and that since his change in occupation he had been very well. In the circumstances, the absence of any reference in the CMOs' reports to consideration of a possible psychiatric condition, points overwhelmingly, in our view, to the conclusion that both the CMOs, at the time of their examinations in 1973, dismissed the post-gastrectomy psychogenic symptoms as of no consequence. In our view, therefore, those symptoms could not have been part of the "condition" by reason of which the applicant was admitted to the Provident Account.

31. Finally, there being no satisfactory evidence to establish that the applicant had a psychiatric condition in 1971 and, if he did, that any personality disorder contributed to such a condition or to his gastric ulcer, the alternative basis for the Commissioner's case also collapses. Put at its highest, Dr Hislop's report raises, with the wisdom of hindsight, a suspicion of a possible anxiety-based condition. This possibility was evidently not considered worthy of investigation by the CMOs who examined Mr. Thompson in 1973. They had the advantage of seeing the man and assessing his psychiatric state at that time. There is, ultimately, no medical evidence to support the suspicion. In the circumstances, the Commissioner's contention that the applicant's personality disorder is the underlying, and thus the connecting, link between the applicant's post-gastrectomy state and the post-traumatic stress disorder cannot be sustained. Question 3 must therefore be answered "No".

32. Late in the hearing Mr. Bain, who appeared for the applicant, applied for and was granted leave to amend the application for review so as to challenge the variation of the benefit classification certificate, by which the Commissioner's delegate substituted for "history of gastric ulcer", the words "post-gastrectomy state". His concern was that the Commissioner had attempted to give that expression a psychiatric connotation. We indicated, in giving our reasons orally, that having regard to the sense in which we have construed the expression "post-gastrectomy state" as excluding psychogenic symptoms, we would not have seen any need to vary that description. However, given our conclusion that the requisite connection between the Provident Account condition and the retirement incapacity has not been established, it follows that the decision to issue the benefit classification certificate must be set aside.

33. It also follows, for the reasons we have given, that the second decision of the Commissioner pursuant to s.66(2) of the Act must also be set aside. In our view, the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement, was not caused or substantially contributed to by the physical condition, namely post-gastrectomy state, that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act, nor by any physical or mental condition connected with that condition.

The Ground for the Retirement Incapacity

34. At the conclusion of the case, Mr. Anforth, on behalf of the Commissioner, invited the Tribunal to consider the question whether the Commissioner had acted correctly in deciding, as he did, that the incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement, was the effects of post-traumatic stress disorder 80%, and the effects of post-gastrectomy state 20%. Mr. Anforth pointed out that Dr MacMillan, the CMO who examined the applicant prior to his retirement on 29 October 1980, had recommended that the applicant be retired because of dumping syndrome, 75%, and anxiety, 25%. We were told that the Commissioner believed he had acted correctly but that the question nevertheless recurred as to whether he had power to go behind the grounds specified at the time of retirement.

35. In fairness to the Commissioner, it must be said that if he had accepted Dr MacMillan's assessment without further investigation, there would have been a much clearer causal relationship between the incapacity that was the ground for Mr. Thompson's retirement (namely, the effects of dumping syndrome), and the condition that had been found to be relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act (namely, post-gastrectomy state).

36. By acting as he did, the Commissioner has ensured that the real facts are considered. In the present case, this has worked to the advantage of the applicant. Mr. Anforth informed us that the Commissioner's view was that he was not bound by the CMO's recommendation, or by the condition, or conditions, that may have been stated in the instrument of retirement, and that he was entitled to investigate the circumstances for himself and obtain, and act upon where appropriate, other medical opinion. No point in this regard had been taken, of course, by Mr. Bain on behalf of the applicant.

37. In our view, this is not an appropriate case in which to attempt to decide that question. The Commissioner has very properly been prepared to argue the case on the basis of the real facts as to the applicant's retirement incapacity as established by expert medical opinion. He has not sought to take the point that he is bound by the CMO's recommendation, or by whatever may have appeared on any instrument of retirement.

38. Without the benefit of proper argument, we are not prepared and, indeed, consider it inadvisable to express any concluded view on the matter. It suffices for present purposes to say that, in our view, there is an arguable basis for the construction of section 184(2)(c) for which the Commissioner contends. It is a construction that is not demonstrably wrong. We are content to adopt that construction for the purposes of this decision and to leave the matter to be raised in some future case, if a decision on the point comes necessary.

39. The decisions under review will be set aside accordingly.
