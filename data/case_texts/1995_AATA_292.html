DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT94/47

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 4 October 1995

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and allows the applicant’s objection to the assessment of penalties pursuant to section 221YHH of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 in full.



.....(Sgd).Mr B. H. Pascoe.....

Senior Member



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - prescribed payments system - payments by cake and pastries manufacturer to owner-drivers for delivery of products - whether prescribed payments - whether activity involves the exclusive use of a vehicle

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Income Tax Regulations

REASONS FOR DECISION

4 October 1995 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection lodged by the applicant, a company, against an assessment of penalties imposed pursuant to section 221YHH of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). The penalties were imposed as a result of the respondent having decided that the applicant was an eligible paying authority under the Prescribed Payments System pursuant to Division 3A of Part VI of the Act during the period 1 January 1992 to 31 March 1992 and had failed to deduct the required amounts from prescribed payments made. In the notice of assessment the respondent had remitted the flat component penalty under section 221YHH(1)(a) to 45% of the gross amount. The applicant objected pursuant to section 221YHT(2).
  2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr T Murphy of counsel and the respondent by Mr J de Wijn of counsel. Evidence was given by the managing director of the applicant, a chartered accountant who advised the applicant and three owner-drivers who distributed the applicant’s product.
  3. The applicant is a manufacturer of cakes and pastries. The delivery of the products to retailers and other end users is carried out by some 35 owner-drivers who are described as distributors. Each distributor has a specific area for deliveries and is remunerated by commission of between 10% and 15% of gross sales. Within limits each distributor has a discretion to discount prices and provide free samples to attract new customers or larger orders. Any such discounts of normal wholesale prices reduce the commission payable. Each distributor loads his van at the factory usually between 3:00 am and 5:00 am in the morning. The average time taken to complete delivery of the products is between three and four hours. The most common type of vehicle used is a one-tonne van with no special requirements for such things as shelving or refrigeration. Some vehicles have the applicant’s logo displayed on the exterior.
  4. The managing director gave evidence that, at the relevant time, no formal agreement had been entered into with the distributors. Since that date a formal agreement has been prepared and entered into for 75% of the distributors. It was stated that the oral agreement in force at the relevant time placed no restriction on the use of the vehicle by a driver for other purposes or delivery of other good either while delivering the applicant’s goods or outside the hours in which the applicant’s goods were being delivered. The only restrictions sought to be imposed were that competitor’s goods and fish or frozen goods were not carried nor deliveries made of other goods to retailers to whom the applicant’s goods were delivered.
  5. Following an investigation by officers of the respondent in 1991, the applicant sought declarations from each of the distributors as to whether or not their vehicle was exclusively used to earn income from the applicant and whether it was used to earn income from another party. Of the thirty-five distributors, eight provided a statement that the vehicle was used for purposes other than the delivery of the applicant’s goods. The assessment in question was raised to levy penalties for failure to deduct required amounts from payments to the remaining twenty-seven owner-drivers.
  6. The basic issue in this case is whether the applicant was a “prescribed person” within the meaning of Regulation 126 of the Income Tax Regulations. Under section 221YHA(1) of the Act, a prescribed payment means a payment of a kind declared by the regulations to be a prescribed payment for the purposes of Division 3A. Regulation 126 sets out the declared payments and the relevant provisions relating to this case are:
“126(1) A payment, being a payment within the meaning of Division 3A of Part VI of the Act, that is made, or is liable to be made, under a contract the performance of which, in whole or in part, involves the performance of work that consists of the carrying out for a person who is a prescribed person of any activity to which subregulation (2) or (3) applies is declared to be a prescribed payment for the purposes of that Division.

126(2) ...

126(3) This subregulation applies to the following activities:

(a) the transportation by road of goods or materials (including construction materials, agricultural produce, livestock, waste, natural resources or merchandise of any description);

(b) ...

(c) ...

(d) ...

(e) ...

(f) ...

(g) ...
  • (h) ...


126(4) In subregulation (1), ‘prescribed person’ means:

(a) ...

(b) in relation to the carrying out of any activity referred to in paragraph (3)(a):

(i) ...

(ii) a person who, in the course of carrying on a business or in providing services otherwise than in connection with the carrying on of such a business, engages a person carrying out that activity to carry out that activity on a regular daily basis, where the performance of that activity involves the exclusive use of a vehicle or vehicles provided by the person so engaged for the purpose of carrying out that activity;”
  1. It was accepted by both parties that the activity involved here is within Reg. 126(3)(a). What is in dispute is whether the applicant is a prescribed person and, more particularly, whether “the performance of that activity involves the exclusive use of a vehicle ... for the purpose of carrying out that activity”. There is no doubt that the applicant in the course of carrying on a business engages distributors to carry out the transportation by road of goods on a regular daily basis. Does the performance of that activity involve the exclusive use of a vehicle?
  2. It was submitted for the applicant that the activity does not involve the exclusive use of a vehicle for pick-ups and delivery of the applicant’s goods in the early hours each morning. Apart from a few exceptions, the distributor can use his vehicle for any other purpose outside these hours and, with limitations, even within those hours. The contract is a contract for pick-up and delivery of specific goods not the exclusive use of the vehicle. It was said that the applicant would not know and has no reason to seek to know whether the distributor uses his vehicle for other purposes within or without the time in which the vehicle is being used to deliver the applicant’s goods. Whether or not the vehicle is used for other purposes was said to be irrelevant. The Tribunal was urged to interpret Regulation 126(4)(b)(ii) as only including arrangements where the applicant controls the use of the vehicle and, therefore, the question to be asked is whether the vehicle can be used for other purposes. The vans in question are simple vans, able to be used for a variety of other purposes and the activity for which the distributor is engaged does not require the exclusive use of a vehicle.
  3. For the respondent, Mr de Wijn submitted that Regulation 126(4)(b)(ii) requires an examination of the manner in which the activity is carried out. If the performances of the activity involves the use of a vehicle by the independent contractor and the vehicle is used solely in that performance, then that activity involves the exclusive use of the vehicle. It was submitted that the wording of the regulation cannot be intended to exclude any other use of the vehicle such as private use. Where the vehicle is used every day in the performance of the activity of transporting the applicant’s goods by road and the owner-driver cannot point to its use for any other activity it was said that this activity involves the exclusive use of the vehicle. The submission argued that the applicant’s view involved an hypothesis that the vehicle could have been used for other activities whereas the simple facts were that it was not.
  4. Support for the applicant’s view was sought in a ruling issued by the respondent in 1983 on the interpretation and operation of the prescribed payments system, IT2056. Paragraphs 16 and 17 of the ruling states:
“ 16. An exception to the general intra-industry basis in the road transport industry applies where a person who is not himself engaged in that industry engages another person to transport goods, etc. and that transport is carried out on a ‘regular daily basis’ involving the ‘exclusive use’ of a vehicle or vehicles provided by that other person. In practice, this test will only apply in relation to persons who are engaged under contractual arrangements that involve the transportation of goods virtually on an all day, day in, day out basis for an indefinitely continuing period. Examples of industries making use of these types of arrangements were given in Schedule A to the Prescribed Payments System Information Booklet.

17. Payments under arrangements involving the intermittent or irregular use of a person’s truck of trucks will not be prescribed payments. Examples of these types of arrangements are:

(a) ...

(b) ...

(c) ...

(d) ...

(e) where the activity is performed for only part of a day on a regular and exclusive basis, e.g., a general carrier under a contract for the delivery of milk or newspapers for, say, 3 hours every day.”

Whilst Mr Murphy accepted that the ruling is an expression of view by the respondent and not the law, he submitted that it should be seen as an authoritative view of the meaning of the regulations. Not surprisingly, the respondent stressed that the ruling had no legal or binding status and that the paragraph 17 referred to, related to “intermittent or irregular use” and there are difficulties in relying on examples provided.

  1. Much of the argument related to the interpretation of the words “exclusive use”. The Macquarie Dictionary, Second Revision, defines exclusive as “not admitting of something else; incompatible”. The New Shorter Oxford English Dictionary 1993, defines exclusive as “pursued, employed etc., to the exclusion of all else; sole, only.” On one view, many of the distributors did use their vehicles for delivery of the applicant’s products to the exclusion of any other use. However, the regulation requires more than that. It requires that the performance of the activity “involves” the exclusive use of a vehicle. The Macquarie Dictionary defines involve as “to include as a necessary circumstance, condition or consequence; imply; entail”. In my view the expression used in the regulation connotes that the performance of the activity itself, not the manufacturer of the goods nor the owner-driver of the vehicle, has as a consequence, or entails or makes necessary the exclusive use of the relevant vehicle.
  2. The evidence of the managing director and the drivers who gave evidence is that the delivery of the applicant’s goods does not require the exclusive use of the vehicle. It involves the regular delivery of those goods by a particular time in the morning. The time of pick-up is to a large extent at the discretion of the distributors. Subject to certain limitations the activity does not require a specific time between pick-up and delivery, does not unduly restrict the distributor from delivering other goods to other destinations and does not restrict the use of the vehicle for other purposes outside the three to four hours required for delivery of the applicant’s goods. Whether or not the distributor chooses to use his vehicle exclusively in the activity of delivering the applicant’s goods is not the question to be asked. It is whether that activity “involves” the exclusive use of the vehicle. If the activity required a vehicle of a special design or fitted out in a special way so as to be unsuitable for the carriage of other goods then it may well be said that the activity involves the exclusive use. That is not the position in this case.
  3. Consequently, I find that the performance of the activity of the transportation by road of the applicant’s goods does not and did not in January, February and March 1992 involve the exclusive use of a vehicle provided by the person so engaged for the purpose of carrying out that activity. It follows that the applicant is not a prescribed person under Reg. 126(4) and the payments to the distributors were not prescribed payments.
  4. The decision under review, therefore, is set aside. As the applicant did not fail to deduct any amount required to be deducted under Division 3A of Part VI, no penalty is payable under section 221YHH.
