Introduction

1. This is an application under Section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"), lodged on 7 August 1986, for review of two decisions of a delegate of the respondent made on 2 July 1986:

(a) confirming the decision taken by another delegate on 2 October 1984 to issue pursuant to sub-section 184(5) of the 1976 Act, in respect of Mr Chose, a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") specifying the condition "asthma"; and

(b) confirming the decision taken by a third delegate on 3 October 1984 to be of the opinion under paragraph 66(2)(c) of the 1976 Act that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Ghose's retirement was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the BCC or by a condition connected with that condition.
  1. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to Section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and numbered T1 to T19, together with other material lodged at the hearing, to some of which it will be necessary to refer. Evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant, and by Dr McInnes, a senior medical officer with the Commonwealth Department of Health, Dr Doss, the applicant's treating general practitioner, Dr Calanchini, a retired Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr Sutherland, a physician, and Dr Tighe, who was formerly the applicant's treating general practitioner. The applicant was represented by Mr K.H. Bell of counsel, and the respondent by Mr R.C. Whithear, Assistant Commissioner.
  2. The applicant was born in India on 12 March 1922. He said that he served in the Indian Army during World War II after leaving university, and was trained in the Army as an electronics engineer. After the war he served as a communications officer in the Federal Ministry of Communications for six or seven years. He then worked in private industry as an electronics engineer, moved into management and became a senior executive. He left India and worked as a manager in an import-export business before coming to Australia as a consultant to a similar business in Melbourne. He worked under contract in New Guinea with the Department of Territories and the Department of Overseas Trade. Just before the independence of Mew Guinea he returned to Australia.

4. After a holiday in India, he applied for a position as an employee of the Commonwealth and on 24 March 1975 he was examined by Dr Calanchini as a prerequisite to a decision under section 5 of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") as to whether his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the 1922 Act ("the Fund") or to the Provident Account. Dr Calanchini recommended that Mr Ghose should "be subject to chest X-ray - deferred for 11 months". The reasons for that recommendation he gave as: "1. Recent history of bronchitis. 2. History of asthma". Pursuant to that recommendation, Dr Calanchini's examination of Mr Ghose was resumed on 15 January 1976 and after completion of the examination Dr Calanchini recommended that he be accepted for the Superannuation Fund. It appears that he was accepted as a contributor with effect from 19 March 1976. On 13 March 19S4 he was retired on the grounds of invalidity with effect from 19 July 1984.

  1. Section 5 of the 1922 Act provided that a person could not become a contributor to the Fund unless the Superannuation Board (or the Public Service Board) was "satisfied, after considering the report of the medical practitioner [in this case Dr Calanchini] that the health and physical fitness of the person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund".
  2. The relevant legislation is found in sub-sections 66(2) and 134(1), (2), (5) and (7) of the 1976 Act, in which the 1922 Act is referred to as "the Superseded Act". Those provisions, so far as relevant, read as follows on 2 July 1936, the date of making of the decision under review:

"66(2) ... where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled -

[to substantially reduced benefits].

184(1) This section applies to a person who is, or at any time has been an existing contributor ... .

(2) ... where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately before the commencing day, was ... a contributor to the Provident Account ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to -

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions, the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was. or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement.

184(5) Where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies ... ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) the person was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Fund ...

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied -

(i) that, at or in connexion with a medical examination which the person underwent for the purposes of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information; and

(ii) that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information ... there would have been deemed to be in force in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement, a benefit classification certificate in which there would have been specified the physical or mental condition or conditions which caused, or substantially contributed to, the death or retirement, or a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would have been the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified in the benefit classification certificate that the Commissioner would have issued ... in respect of the person under sub-section (2) if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information ... and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(11) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement.

...

(7) In this section -

(a) a reference to a contributor to the Fund shall be read as a reference to a contributor to the existing Fund under Part III of the superseded Act;

(b) a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account shall read as a reference to a contributor to the Provident Account established under the superseded Act; and

(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which –

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund; or

  1. By the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No 2) 1986, sub-section 184(5) was repealed and replaced by sub-sections 184(5), (5A), (5B) and (5C), with effect from 18 December 1986, after the making of the decisions under review, but before the hearing of this application for review.
  2. The principal distinctions of substance, for present purposes, between the old and new provisions are:

- the substitution throughout of "give" and its congeners for "furnish" and its congeners;

- the addition throughout of "or misleading" after "false".

9. In the circumstances of the present case, the only one of those distinctions which is likely to be significant is the replacement of "false" by "false or misleading". The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary provides two principal relevant meanings of "false", namely "erroneous" and "purposely untrue". It is clear from the decision in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229 (as to which see paragraph 29 infra) that the meaning to be given to "false" in sub-section 16(11) is "purposely [or knowingly] untrue". The verb "to mislead" from which the adjective "misleading" derives, is defined in the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary as "To lead astray in conduct; to lead into error", with no requirement of purpose, knowledge or intent.

10. In view of the conclusion set out in paragraph 35 infra, that amendment is not of significance in relation to the present case. Accordingly we refrain from embarking on the elaborate investigation which would be necessary to resolve the question of whether the previous provisions or the amended provisions are applicable to this matter, and we shall proceed on the basis of the legislation as it stood at the date of the decision under review.

11. It is not in issue that the applicant was, prior to his retirement, an "existing contributor" in terms of the definition in sub-section 3(1) of the 1976 Act: that is to say, a person who immediately before the commencement of that Act on 1 July 1976 was an employee for the purposes of the 1922 Act and had continued to be such an employee: and who, immediately before that commencement, was a contributor for the purposes of the 1922 Act. He was thus a person to whom section 184 of the 1976 Act applied, by virtue of sub-section 134(1).

  1. Nor is it in issue that paragraphs 184(5)(a), (b) and (c) of the 1976 Act applied to the applicant, in that, being a person to whom Section 184 applied, he ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity, at a time when his period of contributory service was less than 20 years, and had not attained his maximum retiring age.
  2. Briefly, the submission of the respondent is:
    • (a) that at his medical examination by Dr Calanchini, Mr Ghose, in terms of paragraph 184(5)(d)(i) of the 1976 Act, failed to furnish information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information;
    • (b) that had he not done so, the Superannuation Board would have been aware that he suffered from the condition of asthma, and by reason of that condition would not have been satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the 1922 Act that his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund, but he would have been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account;
      • (c) that the condition of asthma was thus a condition of Mr Ghose that was "relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act" in terms of sub-paragraph 184(7) (c)(i) of the 1976 Act;
      • (d) that that condition, or a condition or conditions connected therewith, in terms of sub-paragraph 184(5)(d)(ii) of the 1976 Act, caused or substantially contributed to his retirement;
      • (e) therefore that, had Mr Ghose been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account on the basis set out in paragraph (b) supra, a BCC specifying the condition "asthma" would, on his retirement, have been issued in respect of him pursuant to sub-section 184(2) of the 1976 Act and would, by virtue of that sub-section, have been deemed to be in force in respect of him immediately before his retirement;
      • (f) that the Commissioner is therefore required to issue, under sub-section 134(5) of the 1976 Act, a BCC specifying the condition "asthma", which certificate is, by virtue of that sub-section, to be deemed to have been, for the purposes of paragraph 66(2)(b) of the 1976 Act, in force in respect of Mr Ghose immediately before his retirement; and
      • (g) that, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c) of the 1976 Act the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Ghose's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the condition of asthma.

The Medical Examination

14. Mr Ghose said at the hearing that he suffered from asthma as a child and in his teenage years, chiefly as an accompaniment to bronchitis. Asthma was a common condition in his family; his grandmother suffered from it, as did his brother, his sister and his brother's children. He had no asthma between his teenage years and 1975, but he did have bronchitis "off and on".

15. The relevant part of the General Medical Standard for Examination of Candidates for Admittance to The Commonwealth Service read as follows at the relevant time:

"44(a) Asthmatics suffering from frequent or severe attacks or those with emphysema should be rejected.

44(b) Candidates with a history of asthma in the past and those stated to be suffering from infrequent mild attacks and who are otherwise healthy should be deferred for the maximum period before a final recommendation is given. If there is freedom from attack during this period and the Medical Officer is satisfied that the candidate is otherwise healthy, a recommendation for acceptance with Superannuation benefits may be made.

44(c) During this period of deferment if no more than two asthmatic attacks of slight to moderate severity occur, involving absence from work for not more than four days in all, and provided that allergic symptoms and signs are absent at examination, a recommendation for acceptance with Provident Account benefits may be made.

44(d) Careful investigation should be instituted before recommending acceptance of cases of asthma where onset has occurred after middle life, as there may be associated chest infection or heart disease.

......

48. Acute bronchitis, if recurring, calls for rejection. Similarly chronic bronchitis should be viewed with suspicion in view of its poor prognosis in middle life and the possibility of frequent absences from duty on sick leave. Candidates with chest signs at repeated examinations should be rejected.

49. Emphysema, silicosis, or any other chronic lung disease requires rejection."

16. Mr Ghose completed a medical examination form in connexion with the initial component of the medical examination on 24 March 1975. He answered "Yes" to the question:

"Have you now or have you previously had any of the following conditions:

Asthma?

Bronchitis?"

In answer to the question "When did you last receive medical or surgical treatment and for what condition?" he wrote "February 1975 - bronchitis". Dr Calanchini's comments noted on the form, which would, he said, have derived from what he was told by Mr Ghose, were:

"Asthma. None since 20 years old

Bronchitis. February 1975 - cleared in 10-14 days."

As to the asthma, Dr Calanchini's clinical notes read: "asthma in childhood and up to 20 years old: claims none since". At the foot of his clinical notes, he wrote "History of asthma and recent bronchitis: defer 11/12". It is to be assumed that Mr Ghose did not inform Dr Calanchini that, as he told the Tribunal, he had had a history of bronchitis "off and on" since his teenage years.

17. Mr Ghose answered "Nil" to the question on the medical examination form "Details of absence from school or work due to sickness over the past two years". He had been employed by the Australian Development Assistance Agency ("the Agency") from 26 February 1973 to 6 March 1975, and his sick leave record from that period showed two days absence in August 1973 and five days absence in July 1974. No evidence was before the Tribunal as to the reasons for those periods of absence, which were not disclosed on the record. He agreed in cross-examination that he had teen in hospital while on recreation leave from the Agency, and a report from Fairfield Infectious Diseases Hospital indicates that he had been admitted to that hospital suffering from a condition which was diagnosed as "acute bronchitis" on 5 February 1975 and discharged on 11 February 1975. This is apparently the episode of "recent bronchitis" referred to in paragraph 16 supra, and is discussed further in paragraph 33 infra.

  1. Dr Tighe's clinical notes showed that Mr Ghose had consulted him on 4 February, 17 February and 24 February 1975: on the second occasion he had been having "wheezes" for three days. On the third occasion, exactly one month before the initial component of the medical examination, Dr Tighe wrote "now has asthma" and prescribed a Ventolin inhaler. Dr Calanchini said that if he had been told by Mr Ghose at the initial examination on 24 March 1975 that he had seen his general practitioner three times in the previous month for asthma, he would have noted it on the clinical card although not necessarily on the form.
  2. In evidence Mr Ghose described his consultations with Dr Tighe about asthma symptoms, presumably referable to those dates in February 1975, in the following manner:

"Now in 1975 you decided that you should go and see Dr Tighe in relation to some asthma symptoms that you were experiencing; is that right? Dr Tighe was then your treating practitioner? --- Correct.

Right, and you decided to go and see him about asthma; is that right? --- Yes, I did.

Now what symptoms were you experiencing that led you to do that?---It was the fact that first of all it caused a bit of a shock to me to know that asthma had caught up with me again so to speak because I thought I had seen the last of it.

Right? --- And that I was not going to have it any more.

Right?--- So I was concerned and when I found that I was getting wheezing symptoms, which are the first symptoms of asthma with me, which results from congestion of the lungs I sought his help. That is what caused me to go and see him because I did not want it to progress into attacks.

Right. Now did you consider the wheezing symptoms to be attacks? --- No, I did not.

What do you consider to b attacks? --- Attack is something which is, if I may say so, crippling. By crippling I mean something which restricts my movement, for example, which causes me some panic for breathing for air and which, for example, disturbs my sleep at night and I cannot sleep and I go to sit up - that is what I call an attack.

All right. Now you said earlier that - just that little bit earlier - that you were disturbed by the fact that asthma had caught up with you. Now what did you regard as being the event that suggested that asthma had caught up with you again? --- In fact the attack just before I joined Telecom.

Right? --- That bronchitis - - -

So it was the one that caused you to go to hospital? --- That is right, yes.

And the diagnosis here is acute bronchitis? --- Yes, but I had wheezing symptoms then.

Right?---And although maybe Dr Tighe did not take much notice but I did because I knew that wheezing meant that asthma was catching up with me.

All right?---And so I sought his help.

Right. So you went to see Dr Tighe and did you come to know of an organisation called the Asthma Foundation by that time? --- I did,yes.

And did you ask him for a referral to the Asthma Foundation? --- I did."

20. In a referral letter to the Asthma Foundation dated 30 April 1975, Dr Tighe wrote:

"He is a recent onset asthmatic, quite severe at times, although he has settled. Recently required one admission to Fairfield Hospital ... He did have asthma as a child but has been free of it until just recently."

21. Dr Sutherland, who was a medical consultant at the Asthma Foundation from approximately 1969 to 1977, saw Mr Chose on 18 June 1975 and wrote in inter alia the following terms to Dr Tighe on 19 June:

"Mr Ghose does not remember much about his childhood except that asthma did occur quite frequently, often every night, and was a major problem. At about the age of 20 the symptoms subsided considerably and only occasional attacks occurred over the subsequent few years. Then for about 25 years he had no symptoms at all except for mild wheeze on strenuous activity. During his trip abroad in December and January last, he had two episodes of respiratory infection with mild wheeze. On his return, more severe symptoms occurred and he had a quite severe attack lasting most of one night. As you mentioned, he was then investigated in Fairfield Hospital. Since then he has had regular though relatively mild symptoms, with wheeze most nights. Symptoms are not very troublesome by day, and he has had no real disability. For the last few days he has been quite free of symptoms."

22. On 12 November 1975 Dr Sutherland wrote to Dr Tighe again: his letter reads in part:

"I saw Mr Ghose again at the Asthma Advisory Centre on November the 12th 1975, and he mentioned that in the last few months he had had mild asthma only. He has had up to ten days of complete freedom on occasions, but usually wakes at two or three o'clock in the morning with symptoms, and again at about five a.m. Occasionally at other times of the day he has similar symptoms. Ventolin usually satisfactorily relieves these symptoms.

On examination faint rhonchi were audible at end expiration, and pulmonary function testing by Spirometry showed mild airways obstruction.

There seems little doubt that Mr Ghose has mild chronic asthma, which becomes worse at certain times of the day. Symptomatic treatment with occasional Ventolin does seem reasonable, though in view of his persistent abnormality, regular treatment with something like Tedral S.A. twice daily might be worthwhile. It could conceivably remove his symptoms altogether. If it was of no benefit, the regular use of Intal might be worthwhile. I am not sure that Mr Ghose is convinced that his symptoms warrant regular treatment."

23. Dr Tighe's notes of visits from Mr Ghose are consistent with Dr Sutherland's reports. They continue with the following entries:

"1 December 1975 wheeze since yesterday. Has run out of Ventolin...To start on Tedral S.A. as recommended. Place on pump ... 20 December 1975 – wheeze every night 3 a.m."

Dr Tighe said that the "pump" referred to is a Ventolin inhaler for the acute treatment of wheezing; the Tedral had been recommended by Dr Sutherland. In a report to the respondent of 28 June 19S4, Dr Tighe said:

"Mr Amiya Ghose first presented, as far as can be seen from records, in February 1975 and reported a severe bronchitis. He had a history of asthma as a child and in the ensuing two weeks developed wheezing which required treatment. Chest X-ray at this stage was normal. He was referred to the Asthma Foundation when he seemed to have developed a recurrence of his earlier asthma condition. Following this he had mild attacks of asthma which seemed to be controlled by the normal methods.

He was seen again in December 1975 with wheezing. In February 1976 he had a tennis elbow and his anti-asthma drugs repeated again. "

  1. Mr Ghose said that Dr Sutherland informed him that he had "mild chronic asthma" and that he understood this to mean that he had to live with the symptoms, but that he should not fear any crippling attacks. He thought that he could do without medication and he thought that his body could fight the asthma without any help. The asthma was not affecting his life, except that when he was woken by the wheezing he took a puff of Ventolin.
  2. Sick leave records before the Tribunal relating to Mr Ghose showed only two absences from work in the period between the two parts of the medical examination, each described as for "doctor's appointment". One absence, of one and a half hours on 18 June 1975, no doubt referred to the visit to Dr Sutherland referred to in paragraph 21 supra; the other, a full day on 1 December 1975, appears to refer to the occasion described in Dr Tighe's notes in paragraph 24 supra when Mr Ghose was placed on the pump.
  3. Some time was spent at the hearing on discussion of what constituted an "attack" of asthma (the expression used in the extract from the General Medical Standard which appears in paragraph 15 supra; and also, perhaps for that reason, in the question asked by Dr Calanchini at the resumed medical examination described in paragraphs 27 and 28 infra). Part of the transcript of Mr Whithear's cross-examination of Mr Ghose reads as follows:

"I think previously you said the things that you would consider would constitute an attack was something that was crippling or panic or disturbed your sleep?---Right.

I think you recounted to Dr Sutherland that your sleep had been disturbed on most nights between two and three and again at five, so therefore one would have to say that what you had been experiencing met your own definition of an attack?---When I said, when it disturbs my sleep I need to amplify that. I did not imply that waking up from sleep to take Ventolin is an attack because - - -

You are saying that that did not disturb your sleep?---No, I am saying that disturbance of sleep, which meant waking up and not being able to lie down; to have to get up and sit hunched up to get over an attack of – during the............that is what I meant by disturbance of sleep. That is what I meant. But if you are - just wake up and take a puff of Ventolin and go back to sleep again, according to me, that is my symptom.

Yes. Now, you have said that you were a - that you - well, I withdraw that. If you had not had the Ventolin would it have been an attack? What would have happened if you did not have your Ventolin, and in fact you ran out of Ventolin at one stage, as I recall, that is why you had to go on the pump. What happens if you do not have the Ventolin? --- This question is not fair to me. I agree that if I do not have Ventolin I suffer.

Well, what happened that time when you ran out of Ventolin and you had to go on the pump? --- I did not run out of Ventolin, sir.

I see. So Dr Tighe's note is probably - I probably did not interpret it properly:

Has run out of Ventolin; place on pump.

But you cannot recall that?---I can recall that. But I recall having been placed on the pump, yes."

27. Mr Ghose said that the resumed medical examination on 15 January 1976 was very brief. Dr Calanchini asked him how he had been and he said "well, I have had no bronchitis since you last saw me. I have had some mild symptoms which have been controlled by medication". Dr Calanchini asked "Anything else? Any attacks?" and Mr Ghose replied "No". He said that he knew Dr Calanchini to be asking about bronchitis and asthma, because his history of those conditions was the reason why the examination had been deferred. Dr Calanchini examined his chest.

28. Dr Calanchini's report of the resumed medical examination on 14 January 1976 reads:

"Mr A.K. Ghose has been medically re-examined. No further attacks of asthma or bronchitis have occurred. Sick leave record is satisfactory. Recommendation - Accept Superannuation Fund."

His clinical notes, also before the Tribunal, were to the same effect. Understandably, he said at the hearing that he has no independent recollection of Mr Ghose. Asked what he understood by the expression "attack of asthma" he said "an attack of shortness of breath, difficulty in breathing, possibly wheezing". An "attack of bronchitis" was not much different. Lay people used the word "attack", in a medical context, to mean "an episode of a condition". The extract from Dr Sutherland's report of 12 November 1975, cited in paragraph 22 supra, was read to Dr Calanchini, and he said that if he had received that report at the resumed medical examination he did not think that he would have said that Mr Ghose was free of asthma attacks. If Mr Ghose had told him then that he had had mild symptoms of asthma since the initial examination, he would have rechecked his chest. He would not have referred to Mr Ghose's doctor for a report: he never did so in the case of deferments. As a matter of routine he would have asked whether Mr Ghose had had any asthma attacks. If Mr Ghose's chest was clear, he would have done nothing further: if he had had any signs of chest condition, he would have noted it.

29. In Fry's Case (supra) the Full Court of the Federal Court said at p.237 of sub-paragraph 16(11)(d)(i) of the 1976 Act, which was in similar terms to sub-paragraph 184(5)(d)(i):

"The language of Section 16(11)(d) makes it clear that the Act does not impose an obligation upon an employee to furnish information generally but limits his obligation to information required to be furnished by him at or in connection with' a medical examination. Under Section 16(2) the Commissioner may require the employee to undergo such medical examination or examinations as he determines. A medical examiner is at liberty to ask such questions as he wishes.

There must be a requirement made of the employee to furnish information before any obligation arises. The extent of the obligation depends upon the questions asked of him and the actual state of his knowledge in relation to them.

The obligation upon him is to furnish information. 'Furnish' means 'to provide or supply with (something necessary, useful, or desirable)' and 'information' is something 'of which one is apprised or told'. (See the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary).

To fall within the scope of the section an employee must have failed to provide or supply something required of him of which he has been apprised or told.

...

An employee complies with the section if he answers the questions truthfully according to his actual knowledge and state of mind."

The Court did not accept the submission of the Commissioner that "false" should be interpreted as meaning "false in fact", rather than "false to the knowledge of the employee".

  1. The Tribunal, presided over by Mr Thompson, Deputy President, said in Re Sosnowski and Commissioner for Superannuation (reported in note form only at (1987) 12 ALD 734) after referring to the passage cited above from Fry's case:

"[W]here an employee, having given to a question the requisite degree of attention that the completion of the form properly requires, genuinely misunderstands it and that misunderstanding is the reason why the answer to the question is not what it should have been, the employee ought not to be held to have failed to furnish the information or to have furnished false information (or to have given false or misleading information); we consider that this accords with the principles which underlie the decision in Fry's case and Sweeney J's reference to "state of mind'."

And later, the majority of the Tribunal, referring to the applicant whose matter was before them said:

"In spite of his evidence to the contrary we do not rule out the possibility that, when he completed the form and during the medical examination, he may possibly have decided not to refer to the injuries in 1969 and 1976 as he had suffered no sequelae from either of those injuries after a period of about a month in the first case and a few days in the second. That, however, would not justify his failing to do so; if he knew that the information was being sought, or would have known if he had given the question proper attention, he should have supplied it. We find, therefore, that in connection with his medical examination he failed to furnish information required of him and he furnished false information."

31. Mr Ghose's evidence as to what he understood by the expression "attack of asthma" is set out in paragraph 26 supra: Dr Calanchini's in paragraph 28. Dr McInnes said "An attack would be any discrete episode of asthma ... something bounded in time by a start and a finish". By an "episode of asthma" he said that he meant "a marked degree of breathlessness ... any episode that was characterised by breathlessness of sufficient severity to cause the sufferer some distress is an attack". Dr Sutherland said that he would describe being woken up from sleep and using Ventolin as an "attack". On the evidence, the word "attack", in this context, does not appear to us to be a term of art with a precise and generally recognised meaning among either the medical profession, or the general community.

  1. Mr Ghose is an intelligent, educated, experienced and articulate man, familiar with the symptoms of asthma. He knew at the initial component of the medical examination in March 1975 that he had consulted his doctor in February 1975 in respect of asthmatic symptoms, been diagnosed as having asthma and been treated with Ventolin. He did not disclose this information at the medical examination. Nor did he disclose his history of bronchitis prior to February 1975.
  2. Further, the evidence shows that the episode of February 1975 involving admission to hospital and diagnosed as "acute bronchitis", in fact involved an episode of asthma which was seen by Dr Tighe as sufficiently severe to be investigated in hospital. Commenting on the Fairfield Hospital report of that admission (referred to in paragraph 17 supra) Dr Sutherland said at the hearing that the statement in the hospital report "His cough persisted, and he became easily short of breath" suggested to him an asthmatic reaction to the bronchitis. It is not clear whether Dr Sutherland saw that report (which was addressed to Dr Tighe and dated 25 March 1975) at the time when Mr Ghose consulted him, or whether Mr Ghose informed him that the admission to hospital had resulted from an asthmatic attack. But both Dr Tighe and Dr Sutherland were aware in 1975 that that was the reason for the admission and it is clear from the extract in paragraph 18 supra that Mr Ghose was well aware, at the time of the admission, of that fact, which he did not disclose at the initial examination.
  3. Mr Ghose was also well aware of the purpose of the resumed medical examination and of what was required of him on that occasion. He was sufficiently concerned about his asthma, in April 1975, (or possibly in February; the evidence is not clear) to seek a referral to the Asthma Foundation. In December 1975 he was placed on the pump for acute wheezing, and he referred to this episode when asked in cross-examination whether he had had anything in this period which "amounted to what you call an attack". He had frequent symptoms of asthma throughout the period between the two parts of the medical examination. None of this was disclosed to Dr Calanchini at the resumed examination. Mr Ghose's contribution to the dialogue described in paragraphs 27 and 28 supra is not, in our view, a sufficient compliance, in the circumstances of this case (including in those circumstances the actual knowledge and state of mind of Mr Ghose) with the obligation of an employee under sub-paragraph 184(5)(d)(i) as described by the Federal Court in Fry's case.
  4. For the reasons set out in the preceding paragraphs we are satisfied, on the evidence, that at both parts of the medical examination Mr Ghose, "failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information" in terms of the manner in which that expression has been interpreted in the authorities to which we have referred. We are further satisfied, on the basis of the extract from the General Medical Standard which is cited in paragraph 15 supra, that had he not failed to furnish that information, the Superannuation Board, by reason of his condition of asthma, would not have been satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the 1922 Act that his health and physical fitness was such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund, but that he would have been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account.
  5. Having reached that conclusion on the evidence referred to, we note also the letter from Dr Dorner, Acting Senior Medical Officer, Department of Health, who advised the respondent on 31 January 1986, after reading reports from Dr Sutherland and Dr Tighe, that he felt "that a recommendation should have been made that he be issued with a BCC (or equivalent) for the conditions 'Asthma' and 'Bronchitis'". Dr Dorner was not called to give evidence before the Tribunal, but the letter was not challenged by counsel for the applicant.

The Retirement

  1. The next question for us to consider is whether the condition of asthma, or a condition or conditions connected therewith, caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Ghose's retirement. In terms of paragraph 66(2)(c) of the 1976 Act the answer to this question will, it would appear, also produce an answer to the similar question posed by sub-paragraph 184(5)(d)(ii) of that Act, namely: whether the condition of asthma, or a condition or conditions connected therewith, caused or substantially contributed to Mr Ghose's retirement. That second question looks more directly than does the first question to the relationship between the condition or conditions and the retirement, without reference to the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement. While it may be possible to imagine a set of facts in which the answers to the two questions would not be the same, we do not consider that the facts in this case are of that kind. If we are to consider the steps in the respondent's argument in the order required by the terms of the legislation, the second question falls to be considered before the first (see sub-paragraphs (c) and (g) of paragraph 13 supra). However, in the nature of things, as we have said, the answer to the first question will produce an answer to the second question, and it is for that reason that we approach them in that order.
  2. It is necessary first of all to determine what was the "incapacity which was the ground for his retirement". The Tribunal, presided over by Mr Todd, Senior Member, as he then was, said in Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 411: decided on 30 September 1980: note only at (1980) 3 ALN N1 at paragraph 22:

"The phrase 'incapacity which was the ground for ... retirement' is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. It would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between 'incapacity' and a 'condition' is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions. We understand Dr Kay's difficulty with the phrase 'coronary insufficiency' as having been that he did not regard it as a disease process. In Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th Ed. coronary insufficiency is defined as 'Prolonged precordial pain or discomfort without conventional evidence of myocardial infarction; subendocardial ischaemia due to a disparity between coronary blood flow and myocardial needs'. It is thus a result, a symptom, which is precisely what Dr Kay meant when he declined to regard coronary Insufficiency as a disease. But it is we think appropriate to regard it as having constituted the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. Likewise, coronary sclerosis may on the evidence be aptly regarded as having been the physical condition which caused or substantially contributed to the coronary insufficiency."

  1. Mr Chose said that early in 1983 he found that he was becoming short of breath, and tired at the end of a working day. He became worse after a car accident in October of that year, in which he suffered a punctured lung; after that he found that he could not walk to work from Museum Station without becoming breathless. He gave up smoking on his doctor's advice: he had been smoking about 45 cigarettes a day for a long time. It was because of the breathlessness that he asked to see a Commonwealth Medical Officer with a view to retirement, and it was following that request that he was examined by Dr McInnes.
  2. Dr McInnes examined Mr Ghose on behalf of the Commonwealth on or about 10 April 1984, and ticked the box opposite the printed statement "I consider that the examinee is unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity". Under the printed statement "I consider that the examinee is suffering from the following conditions (in order of severity) and with estimated percentage each contributes to the incapacity", he listed "Asthma and Bronchitis 100 per cent". His record of history read:

"Had asthma in childhood, to age 20. Recurred about 1974 and has got progressively worse, with frequent attacks of bronchitis. Attacks are now disabling, and he is breathless on exertion between attacks. Has to use Ventolin by respirator. Had fractured ribs and pneumothorax in car accident in October and was seriously ill. Still has muscular pains in chest and shoulder. O/E thin man. BP 130/90. Heart size borderline. Occasional extrasystole. Chest - poor air entry. Likely to worsen."

The form concludes with Dr McInnes' formal statement, signed by him as Advisor to the Commissioner for Superannuation, which reads "Retirement Recommended".

  1. At the hearing Dr McInnes was asked why he had, in effect, said that each of the two conditions, asthma and bronchitis, contributed 100 per cent to the incapacity. He said that he considered that the two conditions, asthma and bronchitis, were so closely associated as to form one single entity, rather than two separate disorders. He had entered the ICD code 491.2 opposite the names of the two conditions: that code describes "obstructive bronchitis" or "asthmatic bronchitis" as one single entity. There would always be breathlessness during an attack of asthma. Dr Sutherland agreed that the symptoms of asthma and bronchitis were often indistinguishable.
  2. On the evidence of Mr Ghose, the incapacity which led to his seeking a medical examination with a view to retirement was breathlessness: to adopt the words of the Tribunal in Burr, breathlessness was "the outward result" that led to Mr Ghose's "being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform ... his duties". Similarly, the incapacity apparent from Dr McInnes' report is disabling attacks of asthma and bronchitis (which may, on the evidence, be taken to mean, in the present context, episodes of disabling breathlessness) and breathlessness on exertion. Mr Ghose was, as has been said, retired on invalidity grounds on 18 April 1984, eight days after Dr McInnes' examination of him and formal recommendation of retirement. We have no reason not to assume that he was retired as a direct result of that recommendation.
  3. Accordingly, we find that the incapacity which was the ground of Mr Ghose's retirement, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c) of the 1976 Act, was breathlessness.
  4. The question to which we now turn is whether the condition of asthma, or a condition or conditions connected therewith, caused, or substantially contributed to, that incapacity (in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c)) and thus to Mr Ghose's retirement (in terms of sub-paragraph 184(5)(d)(ii)).
  5. Dr McInnes' view was, as has been said, that the asthma and bronchitis were effectively one entity, and that that entity was the cause of the incapacity. The submission of the applicant was that the cause of the incapacity was emphysema, and that there was not a connection of the required kind between asthma and emphysema.
  6. The applicant relied on the evidence of his treating doctor, Dr Doss, to support this submission. In the light of the other medical evidence before it, the Tribunal found Dr Doss to be somewhat confused in his understanding of the nature and diagnosis of the several conditions generally described by the several expressions "asthma", "bronchitis", "emphysema" and "chronic obstructive airways disease". The Tribunal did not find the evidence of Dr Doss to be of assistance to it. His evidence was put to Dr Sutherland, and after hearing the evidence of Dr Sutherland, we see no reason to find that the diagnosis of Dr McInnes, set out in paragraph 40 supra, was incorrect.

47. Accordingly, we find that the condition of asthma caused, or substantially contributed to, the incapacity, namely breathlessness, which was the ground for Mr Ghose's retirement (in terms of paragraph 66(2) (c)) and thus to the actual retirement (in terms of sub-paragraph 184(5)(d)(ii)).

Conclusion

48. That being so, the several steps in the submission of the respondent, which are set out in sub-paragraphs (a) to (g) of paragraph 13 supra, are established (see also paragraph 35 supra). For the reasons given, the decisions under review will be affirmed.
