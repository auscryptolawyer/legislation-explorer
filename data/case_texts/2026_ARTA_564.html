Rajput and Tax Practitioners Board (Practice and procedure) [2026] ARTA 564 (19 March 2026)

Applicants: PARAMPREET SINGH RAJPUT

P&G ACCOUNTING SOLUTION PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION DERRIMUT PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION CRAIGIEBURN PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION TASMANIA PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION SHEPPARTON PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION SOUTH MORANG PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION SYDNEY PTY LTD

KPG TAXATION BRISBANE PTY LTD

Place: Melbourne

Date: 10 April 2026

Decision: For the reasons given orally at the conclusion of the Interlocutory hearing on 19 March 2026, an amended transcript of which follows, the Tribunal refuses the stay application under section 32(2) of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth).

Deputy President P Britten-Jones



Catchwords

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – Stay application under s32 of Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) – public interest weighing– standard of professional and ethical conduct expected of the tax profession – Stay application refused

Legislation

Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth)

Tax Agent Services Act 2009 (Cth)

Cases

Scott v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2009] AATA 798; (2009) 51 AAR 114

Kaur v P & G Accounting Solution Pty Ltd [2025] FedCFamC2G 30

Statement of Reasons

  1. At the conclusion of the hearing of the above matter, the terms of the decision intended to be made and the reasons therefore were stated orally. After the giving of the oral reasons, the Respondent requested a written statement of reasons pursuant to section 112(5) of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (ART Act).
  2. The oral reasons for the decision have been transcribed. Some minor amendments and additions have been made to that transcript. Whereas those oral reasons, as amended, may reflect the inelegance of an ex tempore decision, they are in fact the reasons for the said decision.
  3. The said transcript is annexed hereunto and furnished to the Applicant and the Respondent as it is the reasons for the Tribunal’s decision.

ORAL DECISION OF DEPUTY PRESIDENT BRITTEN-JONES

  1. DEPUTY PRESIDENT: The Applicant has sought a stay of various decisions that have been made. A stay is available to be granted under section 32(2) of the ART Act where the Tribunal considers that it is desirable to do so for the purpose of ensuring the effectiveness of the review, so that is the underlying test. But of course, it takes into account those matters that are well known by the parties, in the Scott decision.[1]

  1. I will just provide a little bit of background, most of which is not in dispute. On 4 December 2025, the Tax Practitioners Board, who I will refer to as the Respondents, made decisions under the Tax Agent Services Act 2009 (Cth), I will refer to that as TASA. The decisions were to terminate the tax agent registrations of Mr Rajput, and eight of his companies, and to impose four-year periods, during which Mr Rajput and one of those companies may not apply for registration. On 15 January 2026, the Applicants each applied, pursuant to section 32(2), for stay orders. On 4 February 2026, the Tribunal ordered by consent that the decisions be stayed on an interim basis.

  1. Some further procedural background. Between 23 July and 1 October 2025, the Respondent made investigations under the TASA into Mr Rajput and the companies. Mr Rajput had the opportunity to provide submissions through his lawyers, in response to some of the matters that were being raised by the Respondent as part of the investigation.

  1. Following those investigations on 4 December 2025, the Respondent determined that it was satisfied that Mr Rajput had breached section 30-10(1) and subsection (2) of the Code of Professional Conduct in the TASA and had ceased to meet the tax practitioner requirement of being a fit and proper person. There was also a determination that the second named Applicant had, in addition to matters that Mr Rajput had breached, ceased to meet the tax practitioner registration requirements, that it had a sufficient number of individuals, being registered tax agents, to provide tax agent services to a competent standard, and to carry out supervisory arrangements as required under the TASA.

  1. There were also other matters found, with respect to the other Applicants. The Respondent then made the decisions which are under review, and which the Applicant will, at some stage, have an opportunity to seek to set aside. The decisions terminated Mr Rajput’s tax agent registration and determined that he may not apply for registration for a period of four years. There were corresponding terminations which applied to the other companies.

  1. In terms of the facts and the background material, the Respondent relies on findings made in a decision involving the Applicants in the Federal Circuit and Family Court (Division 2), by Judge Forbes, in Kaur v P & G Accounting Solution Pty Ltd [2025] FedCFamC2G 30.

  1. Given the importance of that decision, I will spend some time on it. Judge Forbes, in the introduction to his reasons, said that it is a case of two competing and largely irreconcilable narratives, and that the facts are complex and that it is, fundamentally, a credit case. Judge Forbes said, in a nutshell, that the applicant, Ms Kaur, is an Indian national and temporary resident of Australia, who had worked for a period of time for the respondent company in that case, who was also an Applicant in this case. Judge Forbes said that the applicant in the Kaur case pleads that the respondents engaged in a pattern of unlawful conduct, which she contends reached the threshold of being serious contraventions within the meaning of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth), and she sought orders for the imposition of significant penalties.

  1. Specifically, Judge Forbes said that Ms Kaur alleges that the respondents engaged in egregious and illegal practices, including what was described as, ‘a kickback arrangement’, whereby she and/or her brother paid money to the respondents, which was then transferred back to her as salary. Put simply, the applicant alleges that she was required by the respondents to pay her own wages. Judge Forbes, later in his reasons, dealt with the alleged kickback arrangement, and said that it underpins the allegation that the respondents in the Federal Circuit Court had engaged in serious contraventions of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth), and that the applicant sought to expose his business model by relying on tendency evidence from three former employees, who claimed to have participated in a similar arrangement during their employment.

  1. Dealing with some of the findings, in particular with regard to credit, Judge Forbes said that the applicant in the Federal Circuit Court presented as a mostly credible witness, who seemed to have a reasonably sound recollection of past events about which she had direct knowledge. But having said that, he went on to say, ‘I am not persuaded that she gave a full account of those events, or that she was a completely reliable historian’. With respect to Mr Rajput, Judge Forbes said that he did not accept the submission that Mr Rajput was thoroughly discredited as a witness. Judge Forbes accepted a lot of his evidence, but not all of it. Finally, he said, ‘In respect of some matters, I have found that Mr Rajput was less than truthful’.

  1. In terms of findings with respect to the kickback arrangement, Judge Forbes confirmed that the kickback arrangement was said to have the effect of the applicant in the Federal Circuit Court proceedings funding wages that were paid to her by the respondent in those proceedings. Judge Forbes was satisfied that during the period of the applicant’s employment, she was required to make payments to Mr Rajput, to cover her employer’s costs of employing her. This requirement became a condition of the sponsorship arrangement and the applicant’s ongoing employment.

  1. The judge’s findings in relation to the kickback arrangement was expressed by him as having the following consequences: that the applicant in the Federal Circuit Court, in effect, funded her own wages during the course of her employment, and funded the superannuation contributions which the respondent was required to make on her behalf. His Honour found that by reason of the kickback arrangement, the respondent in the Federal Circuit Court had not discharged his obligations to make contributions as required by the award.

  1. A further consequence was that by requiring payments from the applicant, the respondents knowingly misrepresented the applicant’s workplace rights under the award and the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth), in contravention of that Act. Further and most importantly, the applicant in the Federal Circuit Court, by reason of her migration status, was subjected to undue influence, or undue pressure, to agree to a deduction from the amounts to which she was lawfully entitled, in relation to the performance of work being a contravention of the Act.

  1. When considering accessorial liability, Judge Forbes confirmed that Mr Rajput was centrally involved in the kickback arrangement and knew that the kickback arrangement was unlawful. Judge Forbes said:

Indeed, the arrangement was designed to give the appearance of compliance with employment and migration laws. The mere design of the arrangement to give such a false appearance implies a consciousness of the contravention it was intended to conceal. When the kickback was challenged, Mr Rajput agreed to a fictional extension of employment, in the hope that his involvement would not be exposed.

  1. They are findings made in the Federal Circuit Court, and they are findings that I place significant weight on. Whilst there is a potential for an appeal, there is no reason on the material before me to not place significant weight on those findings, which remain undisturbed. The Tribunal has not been provided with any material of weight, in terms of the grounds for an appeal, and therefore the Applicants in the Tribunal matter have not established that any less weight should be given to those findings.

  1. Some of the other relevant facts then, besides those set out in the Kaur decision in the Federal Circuit Court, relate to media coverage. The circumstances involving Mr Rajput, as were found in the Federal Circuit Court judgment, were the subject of adverse print and television media attention from A Current Affair. There was further adverse media attention as a result of similar claims by other women who were subjected to kickback arrangements, including a Ms Khokar. She provided a statement and the related documents to the Respondent in the Tribunal proceedings, concerning her claims about Mr Rajput’s behaviour, and a similar kickback arrangement.

  1. Other facts relevant are that the ATO conducted investigations of Mr Rajput, and his related entities. And on 28 August 2025, the ATO finalised a covert audit of Mr Rajput’s GST returns, and business activity statements, and income tax returns for the period of 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2024. The audit determined the numerous matters relating to certain breaches of tax laws, but in particular that Mr Rajput had incorrectly reported his taxable income in his income tax returns for the relevant period. He was found in the audit to have under-reported his income in the relevant period by about $5.3 million, resulting in a tax shortfall of about $2.48 million.

  1. Further, that there was an avoidance of tax due to evasion, for the income tax returns for the income years 2020, 2021 and 2022, and the quarterly BAS periods 2020 and 2021. The Commissioner considered that the under-reporting of sales and income and acquisitions deductions constituted evasion. I note that Mr Rajput has objected to the assessments of income tax and some of the related matters, on 27 October 2025.

  1. In terms of the decisions under review, the findings that were made were that Mr Rajput had failed to act honestly, and with integrity. The Respondent determined that it was satisfied on the balance of probabilities that Mr Rajput breached the Code by failing to act honestly and with integrity, by being found to have been involved in the contraventions identified in the Kaur decision in the Federal Circuit Court, and by failing to notify the Respondent in the Tribunal proceedings in writing, within 30 days of that Kaur decision, being handed down on 23 January 2025, and by engaging in conduct stipulated in a statement from Ms Khokar. In addition, there were findings made in the decisions, that Mr Rajput had breached section 30-10; subsection (2) of the Code, by failing to comply with the taxation laws in the conduct of his personal affairs.

  1. Further, the Respondent was satisfied in those decisions on the balance of probabilities that Mr Rajput was not a fit and proper person, as required by the TASA, and then considered it appropriate to terminate Mr Rajput’s registration and impose a four-year non-application period. On 22 December 2025, the Respondent notified Mr Rajput of the decision, and that termination would take effect from 27 February 2026. There were also decisions with respect to the other entities, in particular the second named Applicant, regarding a failure to act honestly and with integrity.

  1. An additional matter found with respect to the second named Applicant, P&G, is that P&G ceased to meet the tax practitioner registration requirements of TASA, that it must have a sufficient number of individuals, being registered tax practitioners, to provide services to a competent standard and to carry out supervisory arrangements. I will not go through the decisions, insofar as they relate to the other Applicants, because they deal with the same matters that were dealt with involving Mr Rajput, and P&G.

  1. There is some additional conduct that the Respondent has identified, which is relevant to the decisions under review, regarding further covert audits and the debt that Mr Rajput and his related entities are said to have. As at 6 March 2026, Mr Rajput and some of the related entities have debts on the calculation of the Commissioner of Taxation, totalling in excess of $18.8 million.

  1. Now, I will just briefly refer to the relevant statutory framework. The object of TASA is to support public trust and confidence in the integrity of the tax profession and of the tax system, by ensuring the taxation services are provided to the community in accordance with appropriate standards of professional and ethical conduct.

  1. The statutory framework includes certain tax practitioner registration requirements, including that of being a fit and proper person to practice. The TASA sets out criteria to which the Respondent must have regard, in deciding whether it is satisfied that an individual is a fit and proper person, which relevantly include whether the individual is of good fame, integrity and character. There has been a significant amount of judicial consideration of the meaning of the words, ‘fit and proper’, which is set out in the Respondent’s written submissions, and I will not go through them, because they are not in dispute.

  1. Further, with respect to the statutory framework, the Code of Professional Conduct is contained in part three of the TASA, and that applies relevantly to all registered tax agents, and includes an obligation to act honestly and with integrity, and to comply with taxation laws and the conduct of your personal affairs, both of which are relevant to the conduct of Mr Rajput, and his related entities.

  1. Turning now to the Tribunal’s power to grant a stay, as I said earlier in these reasons, section 32(2) of the ART Act provides that the Tribunal has the power to make an order staying the operation or implementation of the decisions under review, if it considers that it is desirable to do so for the purpose of ensuring the effectiveness of the review.

  1. When so considering, the Tribunal must take into account the interests of any persons who may be affected by the review of the decisions. The power can be exercised only for the purpose of securing the effectiveness of the hearing and determination of the applications for review. The Tribunal must be satisfied in every case that the stay is being sought for the requisite purpose. The power is not available simply because the Applicants or anyone else will experience hardship. In considering whether to grant a stay, both parties accepted that the factors set out in the well-known decision of Scott and the Australian Securities and Investment Commission are to be considered, namely the prospects of success of the substantive applications for review, the consequences for the Applicants should the application for a stay be refused, the public interest, the consequences for the Respondent in carrying out its functions, should the stay be granted or refused, and whether the substantive applications for review would be rendered nugatory.

  1. The Applicant made oral and written submissions to the Tribunal that addressed the key factors from its perspective of the background leading to the decisions under review, including the Kaur decision in the Federal Circuit Court, the failure to notify the Respondent of that judgment, the allegations made by Ms Khokar in her statement, the audit methodology employed by the ATO, and the sufficiency of the number of tax agents to carry out supervisory arrangements. Before considering each of them individually, I would say that I consider that the most relevant background matter is the decision in the Federal Circuit Court, in the Kaur matter, and the findings that I set out before.

  1. With respect to the Kaur decision, the Applicants note that it is a decision at first instance that may be appealed to a Full Court, and that the time to commence the appeal has not yet occurred, because there are penalty and compensation matters that need still to be considered. The Applicants say that the decision is not as clear cut as it suggests. In particular, because Judge Forbes referred to the two competing and largely irreconcilable narratives, and said that the facts are complex, and that he did not accept that either side gave a full account of what really transpired between them.

  1. I do not really accept that the decisions and the reliance that should be placed upon it is not clear cut. Yes, there were differing versions of events given, and issues relating to credibility on all sides. But at the end of the day, the findings were in fact clear cut, in particular those that I referred to earlier in my reasons. I do not accept that the Kaur decision should not be given weight on either of the grounds suggested by the Applicants, namely the fact of the potential for an appeal, and that the Applicant in the Kaur decision in the Federal Circuit Court was judged as not giving a full account of what had really transpired.

  1. The relevant parts of the decision in the Federal Circuit Court are not with respect to the Applicant, Ms Kaur, but with respect to Mr Rajput, and his companies. I consider that the declarations in that Federal Circuit Court are statements by a court pronouncing upon the existence of a legal state of affairs. The statements made in that Federal Circuit Court decision concerned failures to meet basic employer obligations and serious contraventions of workplace laws, in a registered tax agent business. The Applicants’ reference to the possibility of an appeal is not a basis to doubt the correctness of that decision.

  1. The next matter that the Applicants raised was the failure to notify of the Federal Circuit Court decision. The Applicants provided some background, namely that the Respondent had sent a letter dated 17 February 2025 to the Applicant, entitled, ‘Preliminary Inquiries by the Tax Practitioners Board regarding your conduct as a registered tax practitioner’. The Applicants’ legal representative formally replied to that letter in writing, on 10 April 2025, and did not notify the Respondent in writing until 29 April 2025, with respect to the Federal Circuit Court decision, which it accepts was 66 days late.

  1. The Applicant said in the written submission, that if he had his time again, he would have disclosed the outcome in the Kaur decision earlier than 29 April 2025, but explains that for the period from 17 February 2025, his attention was focused on satisfying the Respondent’s preliminary inquiries, which were numerous and detailed in nature. That may be true, but nevertheless, the fact is that the Applicant failed to notify the Respondent, with respect to that decision, a matter that required notification under section 30-35(1)(c). Mr Rajput judgment. Therefore, it remains a matter of concern that he failed to do it, and that failure represented a breach of the statutory regime.

  1. The next matter was the Khokar statement which was provided as part of proceedings commenced by Ms Khokar in the Supreme Court on 3 November 2025. The Applicants say that unsurprisingly, the allegations that Ms Khokar relied upon were similar to those in the Kaur matter. The Applicants do not deny that the Respondent was entitled to rely on evidence in the witness statement, but say that very little weight should be given to it. I do not consider this to be a matter of great significance. It is relevant that Ms Khokar has made allegations of a similar nature to those made by Ms Kaur. If those allegations were substantiated, then that would be a matter of concern. But the Supreme Court matter was resolved by way of a settlement agreement, and on the basis of no admission, as to liability.

Therefore, the Khokar statement does not add much. The allegations are matters that will have to be considered by the Tribunal at the substantive hearing. And the weight to be given to it would be determined at that point, but for the purpose of the stay application, I do not give any weight to the allegations made in the Khokar statement.

  1. The next matter relates to the covert ATO audit. The Commissioner commenced an audit without undertaking what the Applicants say is the usual course of first advising the taxpayer that a review of their financial affairs will occur before progressing towards audit. They say that there are corresponding procedural fairness issues, and that there should not be any weight given to the findings of the audit. I accept that it is difficult to assess the outcome of that audit on the material currently before me. The Applicants say that the methodology was flawed in that it inferred that all deposits to Mr Rajput’s bank accounts were assessable income, unless the transaction narrations indicated that the deposits were not income. They say that the alleged impugned transactions were never put to Mr Rajput, and that there are numerous entities within the group of the Applicants, each having their own bank accounts, and there would be various transfers between those entities, which would necessarily result in assessable income to the extent that was found in the audit. The Applicants note that they have lodged objections against each of the assessments or the amended assessments that have been issued by the Commissioner.

  1. It is not for me, on a stay application, to indicate one way or the other, about whether or not those findings will remain after those challenges have gone through. Both parties agreed that, on a stay application, one does not conduct a mini trial and make findings of fact where there appears to be disputes. And this is one area where that applies. Nevertheless, I think it is fair to say that some weight should be given to those findings, because they were assessments which were extensive and detailed, and because of the large sum of money which was found to be owed. But I do not give that much weight to that factor.

  1. The final matter raised by the Applicants was related to the insufficient number of tax agents. The Applicants say that not much weight that should be placed on this, and that there are indications that it has good prospects of overturning any findings about this. I am not really in a position to say one way or the other. I note that the Respondent’s letter dated 17 February 2025, asserted there were 23,091 returns lodged, and that the Applicant says there were really only 11,565 active clients, therefore less than half than what the Respondent acted upon. The Applicant say that in February, or following February 2025, it did provide a very comprehensive response to the Respondent on the question of the sufficiency and suitability of supervisory arrangements in place by the Applicants. And therefore says that very little weight should be given to that matter. I am prepared to not give any significant weight to those findings. But nevertheless, they are findings made after a comprehensive investigation, and therefore some weight should be given to them.

  1. In conclusion with respect to the prospects of success, in my view, the Applicants have not demonstrated that they have any real prospects of success. One can reach that view relying upon the Kaur decision in the Federal Circuit Court alone, and the findings made therein. But I come to that conclusion based on that, and the other matters that I have been taken to. In my view, the available material does not provide a sufficient foundation for the Tribunal to be satisfied that the applications for review are likely to result in an outcome other than termination, that would justify a stay.

  1. With respect to the other matters, besides prospects of success that were set out in Scott, the Applicant makes some general submissions that the stay should be granted, because the Applicants’ clients will be inconvenienced and need to find suitable tax agents at very short notice, to continue providing tax agent services. I accept the submission that there will be inconvenience and I am required to take that into account when considering whether or not this is a stay. There is, obviously, also significant inconvenience and potentially financial harm to staff members, who may find themselves not employed, and therefore have to find alternative work if the Applicants are not able to continue to provide their services.

  1. The Applicants say that if a stay is granted, it would allow the Applicant to salvage his business to the point where he will be able to continue to provide tax agent services, which will have a benefit for the staff and the clients, alike.

  1. The Applicants say that if the state is not granted, there is every likelihood that the Applicants will be adversely affected financially, because it is unlikely that by the time the substantive hearing occurs, all of the clients, of their own volition, will have moved on to find new tax agents. I accept that there is no chance of an expedited hearing in this matter, and so therefore there will be a significant delay until this matter can be heard. And that is likely to have a financial impact on all of the Applicants. But these are matters that need to be considered in the mix.

  1. I am concerned that the Applicants have not provided much probative evidence about the nature, or extent of adverse consequences beyond bare assertions from Mr Rajput that they would be adversely affected financially, and that the client base is unlikely to remain with him unless he is permitted to continue practising. But as I have said, those matters are not particularly controversial and are likely consequences. The refusal to stay the decisions will have some negative reputational and financial consequences for the Applicants, there is no doubt about that. But in the circumstances, I would give that little weight, because financial impacts are an inevitable consequence in circumstances where the person is prevented from engaging in their chosen profession, and the prospect of economic loss to the Applicants consequent upon regulatory action is inherent in the regulatory regime in which the decisions were made. There are numerous authorities to that effect. Whilst there are adverse consequences for the Applicants, I consider they are outweighed by other considerations, particularly the public interest.

  1. I also note that in terms of damage to reputation that the Applicant’s reputation has already been significantly damaged by the media attention, and so, that is going to take place whether or not there is a stay or not. I accept the Respondent’s submissions with respect to the public interest in that it does favour the refusal of the stay. The Respondent is the statutory body tasked with administering the TASA. And the purpose of the TASA is to ensure that taxation services are provided to the community in accordance with appropriate standards of professional and ethical conduct. There is a public interest in maintaining these standards. And there is, accordingly, a public interest in allowing the decisions under review to take effect without further and indeterminate delay.

  1. It is noteworthy that the public and taxpayers in particular are entitled to protection from persons who are considered not fit and proper to provide tax agent services, which, in my view, flows from the decision in Kaur. The public is entitled to the protection offered by those decisions. I note further that the decisions were made after a very comprehensive process of investigation, and a process which enabled the Applicants to be heard, and to make submissions. I note that the Applicants participated in the process leading up to the decisions being made, and that they had legal representation and made significant written submissions. And so it is not as if the decisions that were made are in any way arbitrary or without foundation. Quite to the contrary, the decisions were made by the regulatory authority, taking into account all relevant matters and as I said, after affording the Applicants the opportunity to be heard, and then making the decisions at that point. So they should be given significant weight, and it is important for the public interest that those decisions are adhered to, and that the Tribunal does not interfere.

  1. This is, of course, just one factor. There are, obviously, examples where decisions are made by the regulator, which are stayed, but in this case, the other factors favour refusing the stay, and one of those factors is public interest. Another Scott factor is the consequences for the Respondent, and I think there is significant overlap with the public interest factor there. So I do not say much more about that.

  1. I do not consider that the applications for review would be rendered nugatory if stays were refused. And I also do not believe that the stays being sought are necessary to secure the effectiveness of the hearing and determination of the applications for review, in the sense that I am not satisfied on the evidence that if a stay is not granted, that the applications for review would not proceed.

  1. There is a very significant financial impact on the Applicants, but the evidence does not satisfy me that that will prevent the review proceeding. And therefore, that is a factor that favours not granting the stay.

  1. I conclude that the Applicant has been found to engage in conduct that is relevant to the issues that will be further determined at this hearing. There is a risk to the tax system and the tax profession, if the Applicants are entitled to continue to provide services. The public is entitled to the protection of the decisions, pending the determination of the applications for review.

  1. And as I said, the public interest weighs overwhelmingly against the grant of the stays. I have considered whether or not there should be a stay with conditions, but I am not satisfied that allowing the Applicants to continue to provide services, even if limited to their current set of clients, and even if those clients are properly notified of the decisions that have been made, I still do not think that is sufficient to protect the public’s interest. And so I do not consider the factors with respect to granting a stay can even be satisfied, with those conditions that are being suggested.

  1. The reality is that the Applicants have exhibited behaviour based, as I said, primarily on those findings made in the Kaur decision, that fall significantly short of the standard of professional and ethical conduct expected of a tax professional. A further matter was relied upon, which is not determinative of the matter, but it is of concern, that the P&G entity appears to have breached the conditions attaching to the interim stays, by taking on new clients. There was an explanation for that, and it does appear that it was inadvertent, and the explanation is sufficient. But it does just highlight the difficulty of imposing conditions on a stay. But as I said, that matter is not determinative of the issues.

  1. The decision of the Tribunal is to refuse the stay application and to refuse any application for a stay with conditions. That applies with respect to all of the Applicants.

END OF ORAL DECISION

I certify that these 50 (fifty) paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of Deputy President P Britten-Jones.
