Robertson and Commissioner for Superannuation [1980] AATA 70 (27 November 1980)

Last Updated: 13 March 2007

Superannuation - determination of retirement benefits -benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force -physical or mental conditions specified - whether incapacity caused by or connected with condition specified in certificate - necessity for medical evidence as to causal relationship

Superannuation Act 1976 s66(2), s184(2), s184(7)

Re: G.E. ROBERTSON

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member)

Sir E. Coates (Member)

Dr A. Marsh (Member)

V80/39

AAT Decision No 433

27 November 1980

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) V80/39 GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re : G.E. ROBERTSON

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal: Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member)

Sir E. Coates (Member)

Dr A. Marsh (Member)

Date : 27 November 1980

1.The Tribunal sets aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation confirming the decision of his delegate made on 18 October 1979, to issue in respect of Mrs G.E. Roberton a benefit classification certificate pursuant to sub-section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 specifying the conditions "marked personality disorder", "depression" and "history of bronchitis".
2.The Tribunal further sets aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation confirming the decision of his delegate that the benefit entitlement of Mrs Robertson, upon ceasing to be an eligible employee on 5 July 1979 by reason of her retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining her maximum retiring age of 65 years and having completed less than 20 years contributory service, falls to be determined in accordance with sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act.

(Sgd.) A.N. Hall

Presiding Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) V80/39 GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re : G.E. ROBERTSON

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

In this application for review, the applicant sought review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation confirming the following decisions of his delegate:-

(i) a decision made on 18 October 1979 to issue in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate pursuant to s184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act") specifying the conditions "marked personality disorder", "depression" and "history of bronchitis"; and

(ii) a decision that the applicant's benefit entitlement on ceasing to be an eligible employee on 5 July 1979 by reason of her retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining her maximum retiring age of 65 years and having completed less than 20 years contributory service, falls to be determined in accordance with s66(2) of the 1976 Act.

The effect of these decisions was to reduce the benefit entitlement payable to the applicant on her retirement. The applicant claimed that the decisions were wrong and that the incapacity which was the ground for her retirement was not causally related to any physical or mental condition which existed at the time of her acceptance on 3 February 1975 as a contributor to the Provident Account under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act").

At the conclusion of the hearing, the reasons for decision of the Tribunal, and the formal decision, were given orally by Mr AN Hall, the Senior Member who presided at the hearing. Those reasons, which were concurred in by Sir Ernest Coates and Dr A. Marsh, the other two members of the Tribunal, are restated hereunder.

REASONS FOR DECISION

We are taking the unusual course of delivering a decision in this matter immediately because we are concerned to bring to an end without delay what appears to us to have been a clear injustice caused to the applicant who has already suffered considerably from the effects of the mastectomy which she had to undergo and we would seek to avoid any further hardship to her.

The facts in this matter are conveniently summarised in the statement of reasons for decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation which was before us as part of Exhibit Tl. We will adopt the facts as set out in that statement with appropriate comments based on the evidence before us as we proceed. In paragraph 3 of the statement, the Commissioner indicated that Mrs Robertson was a contributor to the Provident Account under the superseded Act immediately before the commencement of the 1976 Act on 1 July 1976; that on ceasing to be an eligible employee on 5 July 1979 by reason of her retirement on the ground of invalidity Mrs Robertson's period of contributory service was less than 20 years; and that she had not then attained her maximum retiring age. The applicant, therefore, is a person to whom S184(2) of the 1976 Act applies.

The question originally before the Commissioner and now before this Tribunal is whether in terms of s184(2)(c) of the 1976 Act the Tribunal is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of Mrs Robertson was caused or was substantially contributed to:-

(i) by the physical or mental condition of Mrs Robertson that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of the conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with the physical or mental condition of Mrs Robertson that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions.

The reference in that section to "the physical or mental condition that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act" takes us to s184(7)(c) of the 1976 Act which provides that:-

"(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which -

(i)the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the fund; or
(ii)(ii) the person was not, by virtue of a provision of the superseded Act (other than sub-section 5(1) or 79(2) of the superseded Act), accepted as a contributor to the Fund,

other than -

(iii) a physical or mental condition that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, did not exist -

(A) at the time the person became a contributor to the Fund or a contributor to the Provident Account; or

(B) where the person so became a contributor on more than one occasion - at the time he last so became a contributor and immediately before which he was not a contributor to the Provident Account and was not in receipt of pension in accordance with section 45 of the superseded Act; or

(iv) ..."

In paragraph 4 of the Commissioner's reasons for decision he referred to the fact that Mrs Robertson underwent a medical examination for the purposes of the superseded Act on 15 August 1972 when the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended deferrment for 6 months for the following reasons:

"History of bronchitis and for observation of sick leave." (see Exhibit T4, p.23)

We note in passing that there was no mention in that medical history nor in any notes recorded by the Commonwealth Medical Officer at the time of any history of depression or other psychiatric disorder in relation to the applicant.

On 28 March 1973 by way of follow-up to that earlier examination, Mrs Robertson was again medically examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer. Although he was not called to give evidence we do have before us a file note apparently made by him to the following effect:-

"Under psychiatric treatment at present. Today she is moderately depressed. Recommend defer for max. period to assess effects of treatment.

Defer max. period.

N.B.: At this stage she would appear a doubtful acceptance." (See Exhibit T4, p.28)

At the time when this note was recorded it will appear from the evidence to which we will later refer that the applicant was, in fact, attending the Ernest Jones Clinic in Preston, Victoria. The circumstances surrounding this episode in the life of the applicant are of the greatest importance and we will return to them later.

A further medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer was carried out on 17 April 1974. Again we did not have the benefit of hearing from the Commonwealth Medical Officer, but the note of his examination was as follows:-

"Re-exam. and the attached report from the Ernest Jones Clinic indicate an uncertainty as to whether she can be recommended.

In the circumstances special consideration might be given to the possibility of accepting her with Provident Account benefits." (Exhibit T4, p.30)

A report dated 9 April 1974 signed by Dr Rosemary West (psychiatrist) of the Ernest Jones Clinic was in evidence before us (Exhibit T4, p 36) . That report, to which we will refer in greater detail later, stated that "it was considered that she (i.e., the applicant) had a marked personality disorder" and that "she also became depressed during her therapy and was started on anti-depressant medication".

Notwithstanding the provisions of the Commonwealth Medical Officers Handbook, extracts from which were before us as part of Exhibit T4, which require that where there is either a single episode of a nervous, emotional or mental illness or a case of a more complex nature the matter should be thoroughly investigated, it appears that no steps were taken in 1974 when this episode at the Ernest Jones Clinic first came under notice to investigate it fully or to ascertain the background to the comments that were made by Dr West in her letter.

As it was, the comments were apparently accepted at face value and were apparently acted on by the delegate of the Superannuation Board who on 3 February 1975 accepted the applicant into the Provident Account. The record of that acceptance (Exhibit T4, p.20), contains no indication of the reasons which, in fact, actuated the delegate. However, the matter has proceeded on the assumption, and for the purposes of our decision we adopt the assumption, that the reasons which actuated the delegate of the Superannuation Board to accept the applicant into the Provident Account were the three conditions that were mentioned in the papers before us, namely bronchitis, depression and marked personality disorder (two of which conditions were referred to in Dr West's letter). That was the view taken by the Commissioner for Superannuation as recorded in paragraph 4(f) of his statement of reasons. The Commissioner formed the opinion, therefore, that those three conditions were the physical or mental conditions that were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act [see s184(7)(c)(i)].

However, because the Ernest Jones Clinic episode had never been investigated, the Commissioner did not apply his mind to the possible application of the provisions of s184(7)(c)(iii) to which we earlier referred. In particular he did not address himself to the question whether the "personality disorder" and the "depression" referred to in that letter still existed or had any relevance for the purposes of the superseded Act at the time when the applicant was admitted to the Provident Account some two years later on 3 February 1975.

The subsequent history of the matter was that in early April 1975 the applicant discovered a lump in her left breast and on 17 April 1975 she underwent a radical mastectomy of her left breast, an event which befell her when she was still only 27 years of age. Not unexpectedly, the effects of this operation on a young married woman of child bearing age were quite severe. These effects were compounded by her husband's inability to accept the operation, leading to his ultimate rejection of the applicant as his wife. Nevertheless, after a period of recuperation the applicant returned to work in June 1975. So far as the evidence before us discloses, she continued to discharge efficiently and pleasantly her quite exacting duties in connection with the preparation of the Australian Science Index. This is a monthly and annual publication for the typing of which, according to quite precise specifications, she was responsible.

The evidence of her superiors who were called to give evidence was that she coped well with the stress of her work. She had a pleasant personality with no characteristics suggesting any abnormality in that regard. There was no suggestion that she was a moody or depressed person.

However, subsequent to the operation, she was subjected to quite intensive radiotherapy in May and June 1975 and had regular reviews with X-rays and bone scans being taken. For the purposes of this treatment she was required to make frequent visits to the Cancer Institute at the Peter McCallum Hospital, a place which, with its terminally ill patients, she described as having a depressing effect on her. The applicant in the meantime continued to feel lethargic and lacking in energy. Part of the purpose of the intensive investigations to which she was subjected was to ascertain whether there was any organic cause for these continuing problems. However, as a letter before us dated 5 February 1980 from Dr LD Gilsenan, the Assistant Director of Medical Services at the Cancer Institute disclosed (Exhibit T4, p.58):-

"Extensive investigations have failed to reveal any organic cause for her troubles and there seems to be no doubt that they are due to anxiety/depression associated with the malignant disease."

The applicant made continuing efforts to remain at work - a job which she says, and which we accept, that she enjoyed. She told us that, during 1978, her health was still variable. She had three months off work in order to try to get herself back on her feet. She saw Dr Anderson, a consulting psychiatrist, at Peter McCallum Institute during this period. She said that she had lost considerable weight. During 1978 the applicant's problems were added to by the fact that her father, to whom she was apparently very close, died.

When the applicant went back to work, after the three months break, she felt very nauseous and tired; she was weak on her legs and had poor balance. She had difficulty doing the shopping. She said that she had no back-up energy. Ultimately, she found that she could no longer enjoy her work. She had difficulty getting out of bed; her legs were unsteady; she found that she was spilling things, bumping into things and acting in such a way that people would think - as she described it - that she was on drugs. Overall, she said that she felt very sick. Dr Etheridge, a consulting physician,.at Peter McCallum Institute, advised her to give up work.

On 5 July 1979, she saw Dr Wong, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, who considered that she was unfit for continued employment and that she should be retired on the grounds of invalidity. Dr Wong's medical report indicated that the medical conditions from which the applicant was suffering were "carcinoma left breast (1975); post-radical mastectomy syndrome". In the way the report was written, Dr Wong entered the figure "100%" against the words "carcinoma left breast". But it appears to us that in the circumstances, he was referring to the combined effects of these two obviously related events, which cumulatively had rendered the applicant totally incapable of carrying on her work.

In the section of his report containing a brief history of the condition, Dr Wong commented:-

"This 31 year old married woman underwent a radical mastectomy in 1975, and has never enjoyed the same state of health ever since. She complains of chronic tiredness, loss of memory, general lack of interest in life and overall debility. Her specialist physician has recommended her retirement." (Exhibit T4, p.43)

Dr Wong recorded the applicant's prognosis as "uncertain" but he did indicate the possibility that she may, in due course, partially or completely recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment. He suggested a review of her case in two years time.

The applicant was retired on the ground of invalidity on 5 July 1979. The Commissioner, being of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Robertson's retirement was "post-radical mastectomy syndrome", the symptoms of which included "depression", concluded that there was a causal relationship between that incapacity and the "marked personality disorder" and "depression" which, in his view, were physical or mental conditions relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act. In pursuance of S184(2) of the 1976 Act he therefore issued a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the three conditions earlier referred to, namely "marked personality disorder, depression and history of bronchitis". As a consequence of the issue of this certificate, the applicant's invalidity benefit fell to be determined in accordance with s66(2) of the 1976 Act. As the applicant's period of contributory service was less than 8 years, she became entitled on retirement to a lump sum benefit in accordance with s73(2) of the 976 Act.

The reasons for the opinion formed by the Commissioner for Superannuation as to the causal relationship between the "marked personality disorder" and "depression" referred to in the letter from the Ernest Jones Clinic and the post-radical mastectomy syndrome were set out in paragraph 6 of his statement (Exhibit Tl) as follows:-

"6(a) I am satisfied that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Robertson's retirement was post radical mastectomy syndrome and that, on the basis of Dr Gilsenan's report, Mrs Robertson at the time of her retirement had suffered from depression, loss of weight, lack of energy, loss of concentration and other symptoms.

(b)In the absence of any medical evidence from Mrs Robertson that post-operative treatment has caused or substantially contributed to her incapacity, and having regard to Dr Gilsenan's report, I am satisfied on the evidence before me that Mrs Robertson's incapacity was due to anxiety and depression, the immediate cause of which was the malignant disease that had been successfully treated by a mastectomy.
(c)I am aware that mastectomy can have traumatic effects, including a normal reaction of anxiety and depression. I understand also that a pre-disposition to suffer from depression can exacerbate any normal reaction causing a more severe anxiety and depression state. Mrs Robertson's conditions of depression and marked personality disorder evident at her entry to the Provident Account indicate such a pre-disposition.
(d)(d) Accordingly, I am of the opinion that the incapacity referred to in paragraph (a) above which was the ground of Mrs Robertson's retirement was caused by anxiety and depression, conditions connected with depression and marked personality disorder, which were conditions relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and specified in the benefit classification certificate the issue of which is herein confirmed."

The Commissioner formed this opinion, notwithstanding the following advice given by Dr Wong, the examing Commonwealth Medical Officer, in a letter dated 20 February 1980 (Exhibit T4, p.68) written in response to a specific enquiry from the Commissioner by letter dated 11 February 1980 (Exhibit T.4, p.62) whether the conditions "carcinoma left breast and post-radical mastectomy syndrome" were connected with the conditions "marked personality disorder, depression and history of bronchitis":-

"In my opinion the disabilities that necessitated the retirement of the abovenamed, i.e., "carcinoma left breast" and "post-radical mastectomy syndrome" have no connection whatsoever with the conditions specified in the abovenamed's Benefit Classification Certificate." (emphasis added)

The report from Dr Gilsenan mentioned in paragraph 6 (b) of the Commissioner's statement was the letter dated 5 February 1980 (Exhibit T4, p.58) to which we earlier referred. The contents of Dr Gilsenan's report were not made known to Dr Wong nor was he asked whether Dr Gilsenan's report would have altered his opinion as set out above.

Following the issue of the benefit classification certificate the applicant wrote to the Commissioner on or about 7 December 1979 (Exhibit T4, p.54) disputing the correctness of the conditions specified in the certificate and expressing the view that all of these conditions were totally irrelevant to the reason for her retirement in July of that year. We do not propose to refer to her letter in detail except to note that she recounted the extensive radiotherapy and other treatment that she had received after the mastectomy and that she attributed much of her continuing disability to the effects of that treatment.

The Tribunal has had the benefit of hearing evidence from the applicant herself - a young woman who impressed the Tribunal as being honest and hard working - and who would, but for her disabilities, still be working. She appears to have made a determined effort to adjust to the series of emotional traumas which have afflicted her young life. We also heard from five officers from the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation, under whom she has worked. We heard evidence from Mr Neil Johnson, a consulting surgeon; from Dr Etheridge, a consulting physician; from Dr Syme, a practising psychiatrist; and from Dr Bergman, who was the family physician who has known the applicant over a period of some 10 years. For the Commissioner, evidence was called from Dr West, the psychiatrist who wrote the letter dated 9 April 1974 from the Ernest Jones Clinic (Exhibit T4, p.36) and we had tendered as Exhibit 1 an article entitled "Psychological and Social Adjustment to Mastectomy" which was a two-year follow-up study of 160 women admitted to hospital for breast tumours reported in the "Cancer" Journal Vol. 40 (November 1977) pp.2381-2387.

The case for the Commissioner, as the reasons for decision indicated, was that there was a causal connection between the conditions of personality disorder and depression referred to in Dr West's report and the post radical mastectomy syndrome which was the incapacity that was the ground of the applicant's retirement. To a substantial extent, in the way in which the case developed, that alleged connection depended on the evidence of Dr West. However, she said in evidence that she could not recall the applicant and, more importantly, that she had not seen the applicant since the applicant terminated her treatment at the Ernest Jones Clinic at 26 April 1973.

In order to place the episode of treatment in the Ernest Jones Clinic in its correct perspective, we think that it is appropriate to observe that on the evidence of the applicant she would, at the end of 1972, have faced an intensive period of 6 weeks work completing the annual index for the Australian Science Index. She told us that she had lost some weight at the end of that year and that she had been advised by her mother-in-law to consult a herbalist. This she did and, after a brief examination, she was advised that she suffered from six different conditions which were likely to prove fatal unless treated by the taking of drops prescribed by the herbalist. The applicant told us that she embarked on this course of treatment for 3 days and on the fourth day she collapsed in tears.

She then consulted a general practitioner - Dr AD Faulks - who arranged for her to undergo various tests in order to ascertain if there was any substance in the claims made by the herbalist. Apparently these tests proved negative. Subsequently, Dr Faulks referred the applicant to the Ernest Jones Clinic with a covering letter indicating that she was depressed and referring to other symptoms which Dr Faulks had recorded in the note.

Dr West told us that in the initial group consultation, which the applicant underwent at the clinic, the applicant complained of sexual frigidity, but made no mention of depression or any of the other matters referred to in the letter of referral from Dr Faulks. The clinic apparently commenced a course of treatment designed to deal with the one condition which the applicant herself was said to have indicated during this initial interview. Dr West, however, had no personal recollection of the case which was now some 7 years ago and relied entirely upon the notes which had been made at the time to support her recollections of the matter.

Those notes, as became apparent in evidence, were obviously deficient in terms of their completeness and accordingly we prefer the evidence of the applicant as to the events which occurred following her arrival at the clinic. The applicant told us that it was suggested to her at the clinic that she had a sexual problem - a fact of which she said she was not previously aware and that she was subjected to intensive questioning on quite intimate details of her sexual life which she found most distressing.

It was during this period of treatment that she was placed on an anti-depressant drug, the precise nature of which she was unaware but which appears to have been either valium or imipramine. This drug, she said, had quite a serious effect upon her. It was suggested to her at the clinic that her husband should join in the therapy but the applicant did not consider that it was appropriate to take that course and in fact she terminated the treatment on 26 April 1973. She returned to work, gave up taking the medication and, according to her evidence which we accept, her condition improved quite considerably once she terminated the treatment at the clinic and abandoned the medication.

Dr West gave evidence that the personality disorder from which she considered the applicant suffered was classifiable as an hysterical personality disorder. She said that a common feature of this type of disorder in women was sexual frigidity. She explained that the word "hysterical" was applied to the condition because of the way in which the person suffering from the condition reacted to stressful situations of a sexual nature.

However, she said that the stress was only likely to arise in relationships of an inter-personal nature and would not necessarily have any effect upon the person in a normal work situation. She explained that the form of depression from which the applicant had suffered during the period of therapy, was properly characterised as reactive depression and that this should be differentiated from endogenous or psychotic depression which indicated that a person was out of touch with reality. She agreed that, as an isolated occasion of reactive depression, it may have no significance for the future. Under cross-examination Dr West adhered to the correctness of her diagnosis of marked personality disorder although she agreed that she might have been mistaken if there were relevant evidence of satisfactory personal relationships subsequent to the date of treatment.

There was, of course, a considerable lapse of time between the events which we have just described which occurred in early 1973 and the applicant's acceptance as a contributor into the Provident Account on 3 February 1975. The absence of any psychiatric examination closer to the last mentioned date has meant that there is no direct evidence as to the applicant's psychiatric condition at that time. However, we do have before us some evidence on this issue from Dr Syme and Dr Bergman.

Dr Syme, a practising psychiatrist, said that from his examination of the applicant there was no evidence of a personality disorder. As personality disorders by their nature persist throughout the lifetime of a person, the absence of any present evidence of such a disorder negates the possibility of such a disorder having existed either in 1973 or - more particularly - in 1975, at the time when the applicant was admitted to the Provident Account. This evidence was reinforced by the evidence of Dr Bergman, the family physician who had known the applicant over a period of some 10 years. He expressed the opinion, based on that long knowledge of the applicant, that she was not suffering from any personality disorder.

As the evidence stands in relation to that alleged disability, we are not satisfied that the applicant was suffering from a personality disorder at the time she was admitted to the Provident Account. If we were wrong in that conclusion, we are not satisfied, having regard to the evidence of Dr West, that the personality disorder would be likely to have had any effect upon the applicant's work performance. We think that the failure to properly investigate the 1973 episode led to the alleged personality disorder being given an importance which was not justified.

So far as the state of depression referred to in the report by Dr West is concerned, that depression appears to have been a natural sequel to the disturbing advice given to the applicant by the herbalist and to the distressing experience which she underwent during the period of therapy at the clinic. It appears to us, therefore, to have been, at most, an episode of reactive depression for which, on the evidence, there was a satisfactory explanation. There is the further problem however, that the failure to investigate the 1973 episode at the time has meant that there is again no direct psychiatric evidence available as to the nature of, or as to the extent of any continuing depressive condition from which the applicant may have been suffering in 1975. But, the evidence of the family physician and of the other expert medical witnesses called on behalf of the applicant tends to negate the continuing existence of any such condition in 1975.

The only disability disclosed by the papers which would appear to have been a relevant disability for the purposes of the superseded Act was the condition of bronchitis which was the original cause for deferral of a decision as to the applicant's acceptance either into the Superannuation Fund or Provident Account. There was no suggestion, of course, that bronchitis was relevant to the applicant's incapacity on retirement.

Turning then to the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement, the evidence establishes that that incapacity was the disability caused by the sequelae of the mastectomy operation in April 1975. As the evidence disclosed, mastectomy is a life threatening and mutilating operation, which in its nature was likely to produce, as a normal reaction, feelings of grief, anxiety and depression - this to a more marked degree in a young woman of the age of the applicant. Both Mr Johnson, a surgeon of long experience in dealing with cases of mastectomy and Dr Etheridge a consulting physician of equally long experience in that field, expressed the opinion that the applicant's reaction to the mastectomy was within the bounds of normality. Dr Etheridge agreed that her adjustment to the operation had taken a bit longer than usual but he was quite adamant that her feelings of depression were not symptomatic of any psychiatric illness.

Dr Etheridge explained that the effects of a mastectomy on an older woman were not quite as severe as they would be on a younger woman because firstly she was at an age when she was more accustomed to coping with unpredicted and unpleasant events in her life whereas a younger woman in the 20 to 30 year age group would not envisage that mastectomy was an event which might occur to her. Dr Etheridge said that the reaction to cosmetic disfiguration was greater in a younger woman and that, in any age group, grief at the loss of a part of the body was a natural reaction and that this varies in different persons. Dr Etheridge said that he saw no reason to believe that the applicant was suffering from a personality disorder and explained that he understood the reference to post radical mastectomy syndrome as referring to the reaction through grief or bereavement at losing part of oneself.

The history given by the applicant to Dr Johnson was entirely consistent with the evidence which she gave on oath and confirmed a sequence of events which, in our view, explained her present condition as falling within the bounds of a normal reaction. Both of the doctors expressed the view - a view which was shared by Dr Syme, Dr Bergman and it should be noted, by Dr Wong, the Commonwealth Medical Officer, that there was no relationship between the applicant's present disability and the episode in 1973.

In fact, it would appear to us that the Commissioner based his decision on the mistaken belief that Dr Gilsenan's letter was evidence of a clinical diagnosis of a depressive anxiety state which was in some way connected with the episode of depression referred to in the letter from the Ernest Jones Clinic. However, that is not what the letter says and in particular, Dr Gilsenan does not purport to relate the post operative depression and anxiety state with the episode of 1973.

Before the Commissioner rejected Dr Wong's opinion we would have thought that he should have referred Dr Gilsenan's letter to Dr Wong to seek confirmation whether Dr Gilsenan's letter might, in fact, throw any new light upon the matter. Alternatively some expert psychiatric evidence should have been obtained to support the view which the Commissioner formed for himself (against Dr Wong's advice) that there was a connection between the two conditions.

Although during the hearing the witnesses called on behalf of the applicant were questioned by Mr Wilde, who appeared for the Commissioner, with the object of eliciting some supportive evidence to establish a connection between the applicant's present condition and the events of 1973, that attempt has, in our view, been quite unsuccessful. The evidence shows no more than that if the applicant was depresssed in March 1973, it was more a consequence of the therapy and medication, which she found objectionable and unnecessary, than other circumstances.

The 1973 incident, we think, was aptly described by her counsel as "bizarre". In our view had that incident been properly investigated it might well have been dismissed as an unfortunate, unnecessary and, in the long term, irrelevant matter in the applicant's past medical history.

In conclusion, we would add that in cases involving allegations of psychiatric disorder it is imperative, we think, that, before a decision is made, proper expert evidence should be available to the Commissioner to indicate the necessary causal connection between some alleged pre-existing psychiatric condition and the disability which has caused an applicant to be retired on medical grounds. In the present case, the Commissioner had no such evidence. The Tribunal should not be left in the position, which the Commissioner took upon himself, of having to draw its own conclusions as to whether or not an alleged pre-existing psychiatric condition is connected with the incapacity which was the ground for retirement.

In the result we are of the opinion that there was no subsisting condition of depression nor was there any personality disorder from which the applicant was suffering which was likely to interfere with her employment in the public service at the time when she was admitted to the Provident Account. We would consider, therefore, that this was a case in which the provisions of s184(7)(c)(iii) should be invoked.

If we were wrong in that conclusion, we find that there is no expert medical evidence that the episode of depression in 1973 or the alleged personality disorder can fairly be related to the applicant's present condition. All the medical evidence is to the contrary - not only the evidence called by the applicant's doctors but also the written report of the Commonwealth Medical Officer (Exhibit T4, p.68). We find, therefore, that s184(2)(c) of the 1976 Act does not apply in relation to the applicant. As the only condition which would have warranted the issue of a benefit classification certificate was bronchitis and as that was a not a ground for retirement, we do not consider that a proper case arose for the issue of a benefit classification certificate with respect to Mrs Robertson.

We therefore set aside the Commissioner's decision that a benefit classification certificate be issued and we also set aside his decision confirming that Mrs Robertson's benefit entitlement on ceasing to be an eligible employee falls to be determined in accordance with s66(2) of the 1976 Act.
