Re Wayne Thomas Schulz and Commissioner of the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation of Commonwealth Employees [1990] AATA 272 (9 November 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: WAYNE THOMAS SCHULZ

And: COMMISSIONER FOR THE SAFETY, REHABILITATION AND

COMPENSATION OF

COMMONWEALTH EMPLOYEES

No. Q89/312

AAT No. 6360

Compensation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Miss S.A. Forgie(1) (Deputy President), Dr G.S. Urquhart(1)

(Member) and M.B. Hogan(1) (Member)

HEARING

BRISBANE

9:11:1990

ORDER

Sets aside the decision of 27 July, 1989 and substitutes a decision that Mr. Schulz is entitled to be paid compensation in respect of lumbar disc degeneration and intervertebral disc prolapse from 13 September, 1976; and

Adjourns further consideration.

DECISION

The applicant, Mr. Wayne Thomas Schulz, hurt his back on 13 September, 1976. He claimed compensation for it on 6 October, 1978 and, on 21 May 1980, a delegate of the Commissioner for Employees' Compensation ("the Commissioner") decided he was entitled to compensation in respect of lumbar back sprain from 13 September, 1976 but that the effects of the injury to his back had ceased to exist not later than 8 January, 1980. This decision was affirmed on 27 July, 1989 by a delegate of the Commissioner for the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation of Commonwealth Employees ("Comcare"). It is from this decision that Mr. Schulz now applies to this Tribunal for review. This application, lodged on 8 August, 1989 was within the time allowed by section 29 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, 1975 ("the AAT Act") as modified by sub-section 65(4) of the Commonwealth Employees' Rehabilitation Act, 1988 ("the 1988 Act").

2. Mr. O'Gorman of counsel represented Mr. Schulz, while Mr. McKay from the Australian Government Solicitor's Office, represented the Commissioner. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the AAT Act were admitted in evidence ("the T documents") as was a report from Dr. Maguire dated 6 December, 1989 and a report from Dr. Eklund dated 8 February, 1990. Oral evidence was given by Mr. Schulz and oral evidence as to his condition was given by Dr. Maguire and Dr. Eklund.

3. The parties submitted that the central issue in this case was whether Mr. Schulz's employment was "a contributing factor to the contraction of a disease or to its aggravation, acceleration or recurrence within the meaning of section 29 of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act, 1971 ("the 1971 Act"). Both Mr. O'Gorman and Mr. McKay addressed the issues in this case on the basis that the 1971 Act was the applicable Act rather than the 1988 Act which came into effect on 1 December, 1988. The decision under review was made pursuant to section 62 of the 1988 Act. Part X of that 1988 Act sets out the transitional provisions. These provisions have been considered in various cases in this Tribunal. Many were considered at some length in the Tribunal's decision in Re Willis and Australian Telecommunications Commission (1989) 10 AAR 382 (Hartigan J., President, S.A Forgie, Deputy President, and K.J. Lynch, then Member). In that case, the Tribunal determined that the applicant, who claimed compensation for injuries alleged to have occurred while the Commonwealth Employees' Compensation Act, 1930 ("The 1930 Act") was in force, was entitled to claim compensation. The Tribunal also decided that sub-section 124(2) of the 1988 Act requires that the question whether or not the applicant was entitled to compensation was to be determined according to the 1930 Act. Without setting out section 124(2) in full, we note that there it does not differentiate between the 1930 Act and the 1971 Act. Consequently, the reasoning used in that case in relation to the 1930 Act is equally applicable to the 1971 Act.

4. In more recent times, and since the hearing of this case, there have been two decisions in the Federal Court in which the issue has been mentioned. In the first, Cox v. Snowy Mountains Hydro-Electric Authority (unreported, No. G847 of 1989, 5 June 1990, Lockhart, Morling and Beaumont JJ.), the Full Court said that the Tribunal had, in considering the case,

"stated, and it is apparently agreed by the parties, that

sub-para. 6(1)(b)(iii) of the 1988 Act provided the

appellant with a right to compensation under the Act in the

circumstances of the case before the Tribunal subject to the

appellant having been entitled to compensation under the

1971 Act."

While stating that this had been the Tribunal's approach, the Federal Court did not comment upon whether or not this was the correct approach and it would seem from its judgment that it was not addressed in argument.

5. A few days later, in Benham v. Comcare (unreported, No.G18 of 1990, 15 June 1990), Von Doussa J. stated that: "It is common ground that the applicant's entitlement to compensation is governed by the provisions of the Act,even though the Act has now been repealed and replaced by the Commonwealth Employees Rehabilitation and Compensation Act, 1988; see sub-s.129(1), para. 124(2)(c) and sub-s.129(2) of that Act, and Willis v. Australian Telecommunications Commissioner and Anor., a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal dated 23 February 1990, Judgment No. 5220B."

6. Again, it would seem that the issue of whether the 1971 or the 1988 Act applied was not argued in any detail and that neither the Full Court nor Von Doussa J. has made any ruling which is binding on the Tribunal on the issue. Without setting out all of the differing decisions but taking them all into account, we adopt the reasoning set out and the conclusion reached in the Willis' case and will proceed to determine this case on that basis. Consequently, we have accepted the parties' submissions and have determined the case on the basis that section 29 of the 1971 Act is the applicable section. That section, in so far as it is applicable, provides:

"29. (1) Where-

(a) an employee contracts a disease or suffers

an aggravation, acceleration or recurrence of a disease; and

(b) any employment of the employee by the

Commonwealth was a contributing factor to the contraction of

the disease or to the aggravation, acceleration or

recurrence, as the case may be, whether or not the disease

was contracted or the aggravation, acceleration or

recurrence was suffered in the course of that employment,

the succeeding provisions of this section have effect.

(2) ...

(3) ..."

7. There was no dispute between the parties as to the events leading to Mr. Schulz's hurting his back on 13 September, 1976 and the symptoms which he suffered after that date. He gave evidence and, on the basis of that evidence, which was not contradicted, we make a number of findings in relation to those issues.

8. Mr. Schulz joined the Navy when he was 18 years of age. Before doing so, he had spent some time after leaving school, working in an orchard. There, he drove the tractor and picked fruit. Each load of fruit that he picked would weigh about 15 kilogrammes. He had also helped his father look after a property on which lucern was grown as well as at a service station pumping petrol. For a time, he worked in packing shed putting cartons through a gluing machine. Only occasionally did any of these jobs require him to lift weights above 12 kilogrammes.

9. On 13 September, 1976 Mr. Schulz was a member of the Navy and was called with others to take stores on board. The crew members did this by forming a human chain with each member standing about three feet apart. About 1,000 cartons of beer weighing about 12 kilogrammes each were passed from one member to the next. The cartons were passed from side to side and required Mr. Schulz to bend to lower them into a hatch. Mr. Schulz's back hurt him after this but he returned to his normal duties the next day. His normal duties at that time required him to paint. At various times, Mr. Schulz performed light duties such as picking up coffee cups and washing dishes. He then qualified for his watchkeeper's certificate.

10. Mr. Schulz was discharged from the Navy in 1979 and was given a full discharge. He joined Century Batteries where he lifted empty batteries only. They weighed no more than 2 kilogrammes as they had no liquid in them. He did have low back pain on occasions but not necessarily after lifting objects. It could occur when he was filling the batteries with acid or when he got to work. From August 1983 until 1986 he worked for the Webster Biscuit Company. Originally, he was employed as a trades assistant lubricating all machinery and cleaning and greasing it. That job involved a lot of twisting, bending, stooping and leaning. He lifted heavy objects only when he had to move lengths of steel and then he would call on three or four people to help him.

11. While working for the Webster Biscuit Company, Mr. Schulz was greasing a hopper when he felt severe pain and spent the most of that day and the following day on the floor at home. He had eleven days away from work and had physiotherapy. During that time, he spent a further four days on the floor. After that time, Mr. Schulz undertook his routine work but stayed away from machinery that he had to bend into. The level of his pain increased from that time. His back started to affect him when he lifted or ran from that time. He was given Workers' Compensation from the Webster Biscuit Company on the basis that his employment had aggravated a pre-existing condition by 5%. Mr. Schulz then did a "trial morning" at Lloyds Hardware but his back would not allow him to continue. He spent a month as an inspector with Bayside Bus Company but was again prevented from continuing because of the pain in his back.

12. We turn now to the medical evidence before this Tribunal. It consists of:

(1) a report by Dr. Scarf dated 10 August, 1979;

(2) three reports from Dr. Ehrlich dated 8 January, 1980,

19 December, 1988 and 19 April, 1989 together with his oral

testimony by telephone on 26 July, 1990;

(3) three reports from Dr. A. Eklund dated 9 June, 1988,

28 June, 1989 and 8 February, 1990 together with his oral

evidence given before the Tribunal on 26 July, 1990; and

(4) one report from Dr. W.B. Maguire dated 6 December,

1989 together with oral evidence given before the Tribunal

on 26 July, 1990.

13. There is a diversity of opinion amongst the doctors. Some of the difficulty arises from the fact that two of the examinations and reports (ie those of Dr. Scarf on 10 October, 1979 and of Dr. Ehrlich dated 8 January, 1980) were carried out nine years before the others. We shall deal with each in turn.

14. Dr. Scarf saw Mr. Schulz shortly after an incident in August 1979 when "he got up out of a chair with a twisting motion and felt something go `clunk' in his back and all the pain ceased" (T22, page 62). Over the previous few days, however, before he saw Dr. Scarf and whilst stacking batteries once again he had some low back discomfort. He regarded Mr. Schulz as a genuine and well-motivated young man who was anxious to keep on working. He gave the following diagnosis and opinion in his report:

"This lad has suffered intermittent low back pain following

an incident of straining at work in the Navy some 2-1/2

years ago. Intermittently over the periods of time he has

had exacerbations of low back pain when lifting. Examination

today failed to reveal any signs but based on the history

and the incidents which have produced the pain it would

appear that he incurred a lumbar straining injury whilst at

work in the Navy. This has been essentially of a

musculo-ligamentous nature and may have involved the ligaments

around the vertebral bodies and sacroiliac joints and with

lifting or bending he exacerbates this strain. There is

little doubt that it commenced in the Navy and has been

exacerbated by incidents since" (T3, page 63).

15. We note that Dr. Scarf saw the patient on a referral from Mr. Schulz's general practitioner and that his report is addressed to that practitioner. We also note that Dr. Scarf examined x-rays taken the day prior to his examination of the applicant and reported that they-

"...showed a lumbar scoliosis convex to the left but no

other bony or joint abnormality in the lumbar spine or

sacro-iliac joints" (T2, page 63).

16. Dr. Ehrlich first saw the applicant on 8 January, 1980 when he was referred by the Department of Health for an opinion in regard to the compensation claim. His report dated 8 January, 1980 is at T20, pages 43-49. This was just three months after the examination by Dr. Scarf. Dr. Ehrlich made no mention of any complaint of pain by Mr. Schulz at the time of the examination. He did not comment on the incident in August 1979 quoted by Dr. Scarf even though he had a copy of Dr. Scarf's report. Like Dr. Scarf, his clinical examination did not detect any abnormality and he noted that the "x-rays show postural unevenness but no other gross abnormality." At the same time, however, Dr. Ehrlich reported that Mr. Schulz "should avoid heavy lifting or frequent stooping." Quite clearly, Dr. Ehrlich stated that the injury has resulted from "the incident in September 1976 whilst moving stores." He went on to say that: "Though the incident in 1976 might well be regarded as having perhaps caused a certain amount of predisposition or weakness of his back, it cannot be regarded as the cause of all his subsequent back troubles which might well last for a life time" (T20, page 44).

17. Dr. Ehrlich also reported on Mr. Schulz's condition some nine years later in a report dated 19 December, 1988 (T8, pages 25-26). In that report he dealt largely with his response to a report of Dr. Eklund of 9 June, 1988 (T5, pages 15-16). During oral testimony, Dr. Ehrlich referred to what he sees as an obvious conflict of opinions and the Tribunal has taken note of this. In this report Dr. Ehrlich repeats his opinion that "the 1976 incident might well have created a certain amount of weakness but should by no means be regarded as the cause of all his subsequent problems" (page 26). It is in this report that Dr. Ehrlich introduces "the notion that this man has a vulnerable spine." In the view of the Tribunal, the notion of a "vulnerable spine" as a notion, is one which is highly speculative and is not, and cannot be, based on any clinical or radiological evidence before us.

18. Dr. Ehrlich's third report of 19 April, 1989 (T7, pages 18-23) was made after the benefit of a further clinical examination and the examination of certain x-rays and CT scans. He did not specify what x-rays he examined. We note that the x-rays on which he commented in his report of 19 December, 1988 (1977 x-rays) do not seem to be the same x-rays as those to which he commented in his report of 1980 (x-rays of 1979). In his third report, Dr. Ehrlich refers to an incident in August 1979 when Mr. Schulz experienced a "clunk". He noted a number of other incidents of pain. In this report Dr. Ehrlich states "his low back pain must be regarded as well established." It is also noted that this report states "he must be regarded as not suited for heavy work or any activities involving much stooping or lifting." This is to be compared with the statement of nine years earlier viz "is fit to undertake his usual employment" and "should avoid heavy lifting or frequent stooping." This report also states "whilst, when I saw him in 1980, there was no specific reason to suspect disc pathology, it may well be true that he did indeed have this" (page 20). The final sentence of the opinion is:

"The opinion, therefore that the beer carton incident merely

brought to notice an underlying constitutional back problem

should be regarded as quite defensible."

19. Dr. Eklund submitted three reports and gave oral testimony. While, again, much of the reports is devoted to commenting on the opinion of others, the essence of Dr. Eklund's evidence is:

(1) Mr. Schulz's activity of 13 September, 1976 was of

such a nature as to cause injury. In the report of 9 June,

1988 (T5, page 15) Dr. Eklund describes the injury in this way:

"The initial incident, I believed caused a small

rupture in the annulus fibrosis of one disc."

In his report of 28 June, 1989 Dr. Eklund adds that:

"...this would have been quite sufficient to damage

an intervertebral disc annulus, without necessarily

rupturing it. Dr. Ehrlich seems to assume that all disc

injuries must be acute. I emphatically disagree, as in my

long experience, subacute lesions, with no initial prolapse,

often occur, and precede by varying periods, with or without

major symptoms, all major prolapses. X-ray evidence of disc

narrowing usually means an injury or injuries at least two

years before. Most of these cases are treated, for want of

a correct diagnosis, as `lumbar strains'.

I have already stated in my earlier report that the

damage to the disc could be observed in the series of x-rays

taken a little later. This, combined with the classical

episodic history, offered a definite diagnosis, not the very

equivocal `lumbar strain.' The subsequent chain of events

cannot be challenged."

(2) The clinical history is of one continuous process.

Dr. Eklund (exhibit 2) states that:

"this catalogue of events indicates that the initial

incident in the Navy `triggered' the disc into degeneration

and the final protrusion."

Dr. Eklund combines the history of episodes of pain

as the result of activity with the progressive nature of

radiological abnormality.

(3) Dr. Eklund considered the incident of the `clunk' in

August 1979 as:

"...a very significant statement and not one to be

overlooked, as it can be a characteristic of disc lesions

and not of acute or chronic strains."

20. In his oral evidence, Dr. Eklund said that he had examined Mr. Schulz on three occasions and that, given the state of knowledge in 1979, he would have reached the same diagnosis and prognosis as Dr. Scarf. He also agreed with Dr. Maguire that Mr. Schulz suffered from a pre-existing condition but that this was not inconsistent with a minor disc injury occurring in November 1976. He said that a disc injury is not fully understood but that it is believed that a disc injury leads to irritation of the nerves to the disc itself and that this causes muscles near the disc to go into spasm. This, in turn, leads to slight instability. What sort of injury to the disc leads to this result is subject to some debate but he believed that, unlike damage to ligaments, a tear to a disc can not be repaired. It takes approximately two years for disc injuries to become apparent on x-rays. Had he seen the x-rays in 1976 which showed no disc damage, he would have reached the same conclusion as Dr. Scarf that he had suffered a damage of a musculo- ligamentous nature.

21. Dr. Maguire provided a report dated 6 December, 1989 (exhibit 3) and gave oral evidence. Dr. Maguire describes the history of the condition as "classical of gradually developing lumbar disc degeneration." Dr. Maguire postulates that over a period of a year or two prior to 1976 the applicant developed changes of degeneration in his nucleus pulposus of his L4-5 and perhaps his L5-6 discs. Dr. Maguire stated:

"...this was not causing any symptoms but such a lesion

produces some back instability so that during a period of

activity he would be likely to develop some pain coming from

the ligamentous structures of his lower discs."

He went on to say:

"I do not consider that this activity would have resulted in

rupture of the annulus of a normal disc and had a tear of

the annulus occurred it would not have settled within one week."

22. Dr. Maguire saw the series of episodes from 1976 as being episodes, each of which lasted only for a short period (i.e. a week or so) and did not see them as producing permanent effects on the progress of disc degeneration. He gave figures that in one study 28 of 578 cases of prolapsed disc occurred in the 12 to 19 years of age group. He stated that trauma played a more significant role in disc degeneration of younger people.

23. Both Mr. O'Gorman and Mr. McKay made submissions regarding the onus of proof in this case. Mr. O'Gorman, relying on Vulic v. Capital Territory Health Commission (1982) 5 ALD 35 (Morling J.), Phillips v. Commonwealth of Australia [1964] HCA 22; (1964) 110 CLR 347 (Kitto, Taylor and Owen JJ.) and Commonwealth v. Muratore [1978] HCA 47; (1978) 141 CLR 296 (Gibbs, Stephen, Jacobs, Murphy and Aickin JJ.), argued that, where a determination to cease payment is under review, the task of establishing that payments should be ceased falls on Comcare. Mr. McKay relied on McDonald v. Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; 1984 6 ALD 6 (Woodward, Northrop and Jenkinson JJ.). He argued that there is no legal onus.

24. In Muratore's case, Jacobs J., with whom Gibbs, Stephen and Aickin JJ. argued, said at pages 301-302:

"... the Commonwealth, when it alleges that the employee is

physically able to earn, in some suitable employment or

business, a weekly amount which is not less than his weekly

pay at the date of injury is alleging nothing relevantly

different from an allegation that he has no physical

incapacity for work producing an incapacity to earn those

wages. The position would be no different if it were that

his degree of incapacity for work had diminished so that his

compensation should be reduced. In both situations the

Commonwealth is, or would be, alleging that the

circumstances of the employee had changed; and the onus lies

upon the party alleging the change of circumstances to prove

it. The position is quite different if there has been no

previous finding of partial incapacity with a consequent

assessment of the compensation payable. It is established

that the employee then bears the onus of proving the partial

incapacity for work and its reflection in the degree of his

loss of ability to earn: J and H Timbers Pty. Ltd. v. Nelson

[1972] HCA 12; (1972) 126 CLR 625".

25. In Vulic's case, Morling J. considered a case in which the delegate of the Commissioner determined that Mrs Vulic had suffered an aggravation of a disease but that the aggravation had since ceased and, consequently, that she was no longer entitled to compensation. Although not expressly stated, it would seem implicit in the reasons of Morling J. that the determination of liability and cessation were made at the same time. He referred to Phillips' case and quoted a passage from it, part of which read (page 39):

"... the application of the ordinary principles relating to

the determination of disputed questions of fact by judicial

tribunals requires the conclusion that if a claim for

compensation be rejected by the Commissioner or his delegate

the onus of proving the necessary facts to entitle the

applicant to what is virtually an award of compensation will

be upon the claimant in later proceedings before the County

Court. Likewise, the application of the same principles may

well mean that in some cases the onus of proving critical

facts may rest upon the Commonwealth. Such a case would be

where the Commissioner has purported to terminate an

employee's right to compensation under an antecedently

existing determination by reason of a material change of

circumstances."

26. Applying this passage, Morling J. concluded that there had never been "an antecedently existing determination" as there had been no final determination of the appellant's right to compensation in that it was still subject to reconsideration pursuant to section 63 of the 1971 Act.

27. The issue of whether or not an onus of proof lay on either party on issues arising under the Social Security Act 1947 was dealt with in McDonald's case where, dealing with a decision to cease an invalid pension, Woodward J. said at p 10:

"There is certainly no legal onus of proof arising from the

fact that this is an "appeals" tribunal, because the AAT is

required, in effect, by s43 of the AAT Act, to put itself in

the position of the administrator in carrying out its review

and, in the light of the material before the AAT, (not the

material before the administrator, Drake v. Minister for

Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 24 ALR 577 at 589)

make its own decision in place of the administrator's. The

AAT itself, in a series of cases beginning with Re Ladybird

Children's Wear Pty. Ltd. (1976) 1 ALD 1, has taken the view

that there is no presumption that the administrator's

decision is correct. This is clearly the right approach to

the matter."

28. Again, at page 10 he said:

"The next question which arises is whether, once an invalid

pension has been granted, there is an evidential onus on the

Director-General to satisfy himself, or on appeal the AAT,

of changed circumstances before cancelling the pension. This

was the main issue argued before us under the first question

of law."

29. McDonald's case has been considered by the Federal Court in the context of the compensation jurisdiction in cases such as Australian Postal Commission v. Burgazoff (unreported, No.G879 of 1988, Fed No.200, Davies J.), Elleissy v. Australian Telecommunications Commission (unreported, No.G836 of 1988, Hill J.), Barker v. Australian Telecommunications Commission (unreported, No.G1181 of 1988, Fed No.136, Einfeld J.) and Casarotto v. Australian Postal Commission [1989] FCA 116; (1989) 10 AAR 191, Hill J.). In Casarotto, Hill J. said at page 205

"It may be that what was said by Woodward J. in McDonald

should be confined to the context of Social Security

legislation. Thus in Minister for Health v. Thomson (1985)

[1985] FCA 208; 8 FCR 213 at 223-224, Beaumont J referring to proceedings

before the Medical Services Committee established under the

Health Insurance Act 1973 (Cth) said:

"Generally speaking, concepts of onus of proof used in

adversary proceedings are inapplicable in administrative

proceedings in the social security area: see McDonald v.

Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; (1984) 1 FCR 354.

However, where, as here, a breach of discipline, or

something analogous, is alleged, the onus of proving such a

breach lies upon the accuser. The general position is

explained by Professor Enid Campbell in Principles of

evidence and Administrative Tribunals, published in Campbell

and Waller (ed) `Well and Truly Tried', Monash Studies in

Law (1982), p53:

`There may be legal burdens of proof to be discharged in

administrative proceedings just as much as there are legal

burdens of proof in purely judicial proceedings. Sometimes

the incidence of the burden of proof is spelled out by

legislation, but more often than not it is simply implied in

the nature of the proceedings. If, for example, entitlement

to grant of a licence or benefit depends on proof that

certain qualifications have been met, the burden of proving

the relevant facts going to qualifications must fall upon

the applicant. Similarly, where the issue to be decided is

whether circumstances have arisen which would justify

cancellation or suspension of a licence, or a finding that a

breach of discipline had occurred, the onus of proving that

these circumstances have arisen would devolve on the

accuser. This would be so, notwithstanding that the accuser

was also, of necessity, the person or body having authority

to adjudicate.'"

Nevertheless as a practical matter an applicant for review

in the Tribunal in a case such as the present is asserting a

claim for a right to compensation (cf Vulic v. Capital

Territory Health Commission (1982) 5 ALD 35 at 38, per

Morling J.) and ultimately the Tribunal in considering the

claim can only act on the evidence before it; to do

otherwise would be to commit an error of law. Thus in a

practical sense, if not in a strict legal sense, it will be

the responsibility of an applicant for review to ensure that

there is laid before the Tribunal all material which it will

be necessary for the Tribunal to have before it to enable it

to come to a decision."

30. Hill J. referred to these views again in Elleissy's case. Similar views were expressed by Davies J. in Burgazoff's case and they seem consistent with those expressed in Barker's case. Applying those principles to Mr. Schulz's case, then, there is no legal onus on either party. As a matter of practicality, however, the fact that Mr. Schulz is seeking to establish that he is entitled to receive compensation, means that he must adduce the evidence to establish that entitlement. We do not accept Mr. O'Gorman's argument as we are of the view that the determination in this case cannot be properly characterised as a determination to cease payment. It was a determination that Mr. Schulz was entitled to compensation for a fixed period and it is his task to ensure that there is evidence to justify a finding that he is entitled to compensation for a longer period whether or not that period may now be defined. In this, it may be compared with the determination considered in Vulic's case. It should be distinguished from a case in which the delegate has made a determination and then, some time later, has decided that the recipient of the compensation was no longer entitled to compensation or that there has been a variation in his or her circumstances. On the authorities referred to above, the Tribunal would then, as a matter of common sense, look to the delegate to adduce the evidence to establish his case that there has been such a variation.

31. Bearing this in mind, we turn to the evidence. We have no doubt that Mr. Schulz suffers from lumbar disc degeneration and intervertebral disc prolapse in a particularly debilitating form and that he is unable to work despite being willing to do so. Beyond this, we have had some difficulty in considering the medical evidence. We have found it difficult to reconcile the evidence of the medical practitioners in this case. Some of this difficulty has arisen from the fact that Dr. Ehrlich has used in his oral evidence, terms such as "vulnerable spine", "constitutional back problem" and "pre-disposition or weakness of his back." His use of these terms is even more confusing when he has specifically ruled out these conditions as representing the equivalent of gradually developing lumbar disc degeneration. Some of our difficulty also arises from the fact that none of the doctors, from whom evidence was obtained, saw Mr. Schulz until three years after the incident in 1976.

32. Only Dr. Scarf and Dr. Ehrlich saw Mr. Schulz in the first three or so years after the incident involving the beer cartons. Dr. Scarf was already of the opinion that his condition commenced in the navy and that it had been exacerbated by events since. Dr. Ehrlich's reports are not unequivocal. He has seen Mr. Schulz on two occasions in ten years and has not treated him. His reports do not reflect his independent assessment of Mr. Schulz so much as his views on the reports submitted by other specialists while Dr. Eklund's reports reflect a similar approach, we did have the benefit of hearing his oral evidence. Dr. Eklund has seen Mr. Schulz on approximately three occasions and Dr. Maguire on one occasion only in 1989.

33. Having considered all of the evidence, we prefer the evidence of Dr. Eklund to that of Dr. Maguire. Dr. Maguire only had the advantage of seeing Mr. Schulz on one occasion whereas Dr. Eklund had the advantage of seeing him on three occasions. His evidence is consistent with that of Dr. Scarf who saw him in 1978 in that Dr. Scarf's view is explicable on Dr. Eklund's diagnosis of Mr. Schulz's condition. We also prefer Dr. Eklund's evidence to that of Dr. Ehrlich whose opinions we have, on this occasion, found to be inconclusive. Therefore, we accept Dr. Eklund's evidence and find that Mr. Schulz suffered an injury to his back in 1976 and that the injury contributed to the development of lumbar disc degeneration and intervertebral disc prolapse.

34. The Tribunal

1. sets aside the decision of 27 July, 1989 and substitutes a decision that Mr. Schulz is entitled to be paid compensation in respect of lumbar disc degeneration and intervertebral disc prolapse from 13 September, 1976; and

2. adjourns further consideration.
