Hugh Jenner Wily As Trustee of the Bankrupt Estate of Jose Molina and Inspector-General In Bankruptcy [1997] AATA 321 (5 September 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

HUGH JENNER WILY as Trustee of the Bankrupt Estate of JOSE MOLINA v.

INSPECTOR-GENERAL IN BANKRUPTCY

No. N96/1611

AAT No. 12192

Number

of pages - 11

Bankruptcy



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.J. McMAHON (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - discharge - objection to discharge - allegation of disposition of property - allegation of non disclosure of beneficial interest in property - whether ownership of property - whether beneficial interest - whether disposition of property - whether failure to adequately account for proceeds of disposition - whether failure to disclose beneficial interest

HEARING

SYDNEY, 26 August 1997 (hearing), 5 September 1997 (decision)

5:9:1997

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr J Chippendall

Solicitor for the Applicant: MD Nikolaidis & Co, Solicitors

Solicitor for the First Respondent: Gordon & Johnstone, Solicitors

Solicitor for the Second Respondent: Brendan Piggott Solicitor

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

B.J. McMAHON

1. This is an application made pursuant to section 149Q of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 to review a decision of the Inspector-General. Mr Molina became a bankrupt on 23 October 1993. The earliest available act of bankruptcy was on 6 June 1992 and accordingly, by virtue of section 115, the bankruptcy commenced on that date. He filed his statement of affairs on 29 October 1993. By virtue of section 149(2)(c) he would have been discharged from bankruptcy on 29 October 1996, being a date three years from the date on which he filed his statement of affairs. On 2 October 1996, however, the applicant filed a notice of objection to discharge pursuant to section 149B. At the request of Mr Molina, the notice of objection was reviewed by the Inspector-General pursuant to section 149N. As a result of that review, he cancelled all four grounds of the objection. This application is brought by the trustee to review that decision.

2. At the hearing before me, the first two grounds were withdrawn. The remaining two grounds were in these terms:

"3. Pursuant to Section 149D(1)(g)(ii) of the Bankruptcy Act, the Bankrupt disposed of property within the five years immediately before or after the commencement of the Bankruptcy but failed to explain adequately to the Trustee why no money was received as a result of the disposal.

Particulars

Prior to the bankruptcy, the bankrupt operated a tax agency business and had approximately 300 clients. These clients were taken over by Mr Denis Pearson of 20/265-283 Illawarra Road, Marrickville from 1991 (except for a short period when the bankrupt worked for ITP). The bankrupt alleges that Mr Pearson paid no money for the clients but Mr Pearson continues to lodge tax returns each year for the bulk of the clients and employs the bankrupt to service the clients. The clients are mainly Spanish speaking and the bankrupt is fluent in Spanish, while Mr Pearson cannot speak Spanish. I do not consider that adequate explanation has been given as to the disposal of the client base.

Mr Pearson still lodges returns for eighty individuals who were clients of the Bankrupt in 1991. I consider he has gained the benefit of the income from the clients without paying any consideration for same. I also consider that the bankrupt has failed to explain why no money was received as a result of the disposal.

REASONS FOR OBJECTION

I believe that inadequate consideration has been paid for the clients of the tax agency business formerly operated by the bankrupt. I have received inadequate explanation of the agreement between the bankrupt and his employer and former employer ITP for commissions to be paid on the clients. I am pursuing the matter and intending to conduct examinations under section 80 of the Bankruptcy Act. I believe that the consideration which should have been paid for the clients is an asset of the bankruptcy.

4. Pursuant to Section 149D(1)(n) of the Bankruptcy Act, the bankrupt failed, whether intentionally or not, to disclose to the Trustee the bankrupt"s beneficial interest in any property.

Particulars

During the years leading up to the bankruptcy, the bankrupts (sic) taxable income was as follows :

1991 $120,100 1992 $150,500 1993 $29,505

During the bankruptcy, his declared income has been as follows :

1994 $14,810 1995 $14,268 1996 $16,700 Estimated 1997 $20,000

During the years 1991 and 1992, the bankrupt operated a profitable tax agency business and had approx. 300 clients. The bankrupt lodged the returns for his clients using a computerised electronic lodgement system owned by Mr Dennis Pearson of 20/265-283 Illawarra Road, Marrickville, during the 1991 and 1992 years but retained ownership of the clients. When the sequestration order was granted on 13 October 1993, the bankrupt advised me he began working for ITP at Summer Hill, Sydney. He took his clients with him to ITP and lodged their returns through ITP"s system. He then returned to work for Mr Pearson on 1 July 1994 and recommended lodging the returns through Mr Pearson"s system. He has worked for Mr Pearson since that time.

Mr Pearson has advised me that he lodged returns prepared by the bankrupt as follows :

up to 13 October 1991 224 returns lodged 1992 15 1993 15 1994 133 1995 487

He advises that he considers the clients to be his and that no funds were paid to the bankrupt in consideration for the clients.

I consider that the bankrupt has a beneficial interest in the clients as he was able to take them from his employment at ITP to his employment with Mr Pearson. The bankrupt has not disclosed such beneficial interest to me as his Trustee.

REASONS FOR OBJECTION

I believe that inadequate consideration has been paid for the clients of the tax agency business formerly operated by the bankrupt. I have received inadequate explanation of the agreement between the bankrupt and his employer and former employer ITP for commissions to be paid on the clients. I am pursuing the matter and intending (sic) to conduct examinations under section 80 of the Bankruptcy Act. I believe that the consideration which should have been paid for the clients is an asset of the bankruptcy".

3. The Inspector-General dealt both with the form and the substance of the grounds of objection. Section 149C sets out the form which a notice of objection must take. It must set out each of the grounds of objection, being one of those specified in section 149D(1), it must refer to the evidence or other material that, in the opinion of the trustee, establishes each of those grounds and it must state the reasons of the trustee for objecting to the discharge on each of those grounds. Subsection (2) goes on to provide that a notice of objection is not invalid merely because it does not state the grounds of objection precisely, as set out in subsection 149D(1), provided that the grounds can reasonably be identified from the terms of the notice.

4. Grounds 1 and 2 were cancelled by the Inspector-General almost solely for failure to comply with this section. The same objections, however, were raised at the hearing before me in relation to the remaining two grounds. In each case it was said that the trustee had failed to state the evidence upon which he relied to support each ground. It was said that this was self evident because at the hearing before me, much more evidence than appears in the notice of objection was brought forward.

5. In my view, there cannot be realistically an obligation on a trustee to set out in a notice of objection, all the evidence relied upon in the sense that no further evidence than that considered at first instance may be brought forward on review. This would be a negation of the basic principles of administrative review. If all the evidence were to be set out in the notice of objection and there were to be no scope for the introduction of further evidence before the reviewing office holder or Tribunal, then the function of the reviewer would be no more than to look at the decision, only on the basis of the evidence that was before the decision maker. This is so clearly contrary to the general principles of administrative review that I would need to see clear and concise statutory provisions to this effect before I would hold that failure to include all evidence presented at a Tribunal hearing in reviewing a notice of objection was a failure to comply with the requirements of section 149C. In fact, the requirements of that section have been judicially considered on a number of occasions.

6. In Re Hall (1994) 14 ACSR 488 at 491 Branson J considered the requirements of the section and said -

"It may also be the case, although it is not necessary for me to reach a firm conclusion in this regard, that if review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal of a decision to file a notice of objection is sought, the notice of objection itself is intended to supply the reasons for decision referred to in s 28 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The setting out in the notice of objection of the ground or grounds of objection may well be capable of being regarded as the equivalent of the "findings on material questions of fact" referred to in s 28 of that Act. As was pointed out by the Full Court in Van Reesema v Official Receiver in Bankruptcy [1983] FCA 202; (1983) 50 ALR 253 at 258 the discharge of a person from bankruptcy by operation of statute confers on that person a right of great value. The power to object to such discharge is correspondingly great.

It is in the light of the above context that the requirements of s 149C of the Act are to be construed.

Section 149C(1)(b) requires the notice of objection to "refer to the evidence or other material that, in the opinion of the trustee . . . establishes" the ground or each of the grounds of objection. It may be noted that there is no requirement to "set out" such evidence or other material.

The Full Tribunal of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal said in Re Palmer and Minister for the Capital Territory (1978) 23 ALR 196 at 206 as to the provision of reasons for a decision in respect of which an application could be made to that Tribunal :

. . . the citizen"s entitlement to be fully informed was not merely an incident arising in the course of and for the purpose of a review by this Tribunal. It is a right which arises consequent upon a decision being made which is capable of being so reviewed, and the reasons, when properly given, ensure that the citizen is sufficiently informed to determine whether he wishes to take the matter further, and if so whether to make representations to the Minister, proceed in the appropriate court of law or to seek a review by this Tribunal.

I consider that s 149C is intended to give a bankrupt a comparable entitlement to be fully informed by a notice of objection to discharge from bankruptcy as to the bases upon which such notice has been filed. He or she must determine whether to take the matter further, and if so whether to make representations to the trustee or Official Receiver, seek an internal review by the Inspector-General or an external review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal or proceed in an appropriate court of law"."

7. This decision was followed in Re Ansett; Ex parte Ansett v Pattison (1995) 56 FCR 526 at 530 where Olney J said -

"The legislative policy seems to be clear enough. Section 149D(1) sets out some 14 grounds upon which an objection may be based. The mere existence of an available ground does not automatically give rise to an extension of the bankruptcy. To achieve that end the trustee must give notice setting out the ground he relies upon, the evidence which establishes that ground and the reason why he objects to the discharge on that ground. The latter requirement suggests that the trustee must address the relevance of the bankrupt"s conduct in relation to the ground of objection in the context of the administration of the estate and to make a judgment as to whether that conduct provides a basis or reason for the bankruptcy to be extended. Further, the trustee is required to expose his reasoning in the notice".

8. As Branson J pointed out, the requirements of section 149C are not peculiar to the Bankruptcy Act. Similar provisions are to be found in a number of other statutes, including section 28 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and section 13 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977. Observations made in relation to those sections have equal application in relation to section 149C. In Hatfield v Health Insurance Commission (1987) 77 ALR 103, Davies J at 105-106 observed that a statement of this nature should be -

" . . . sufficiently explicit to enable the recipient to determine whether "the making of the decision was an improper exercise of the power conferred by the enactment", "the decision involved an error of law", the decision-maker took into account an irrelevant consideration or failed to take into account a relevant consideration and like matters referred to in ss 5 and 6 of the [ADJR] Act".

9. Similarly, in Ansett Transport Industries (Operations) Ltd v Secretary, Department of Aviation (1987) 73 ALR 193 Lockhart J said at 197 -

"The purpose of s 13 is to enable persons whose property or whose interests are affected by an administrative decision to be fully informed of the basis on which the decision was made and the reasons for it. Hence the section requires that the statement set out the findings on material questions of fact with reference to the material on which the findings were based and that it give the reasons for the decision.

Whether reasons are sufficient or not must depend upon the circumstances of the particular case. The section does not require that the findings on all questions of fact be set out; it is sufficient if the statement sets out the findings on material questions of fact. Nor is it necessary that the evidence or other material be set out in the statement; it is sufficient if it is referred to in it. Nor should the statement be interpreted by the courts narrowly or technically. Section 13 is remedial in character. I agree with the following statement by Burchett J in ARM Constructions Pty Ltd v DCT(1986) 65 ALR 343 at 349: "It would be wrong for courts to construe reasons in any overly critical spirit, forgetful that they are the reasons of an administrator, not of the draftsman of an Act. But it would be as bad to betray the aims of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act, by ignoring what has been required by the Parliament to be disclosed in the interests of just and lawful (and not merely unassailable) administration."

Section 13 seeks to strike a balance between the requirement that persons affected by an administrative decision know the basis upon which it was made and the necessity that the administration of this country be carried on effectively without undue intervention by the courts in the administrative process. The citizen must have, when he receives the statement, sufficient information to decide whether to accept the decision or to pursue the matter further within the administrative process itself or to challenge the decision in the courts".

10. His Honour adopted the above observation that one should not construe reasons "in any overly critical spirit". A similar approach was adopted in Van Reesema v Official Trustee and Bankruptcy [1983] FCA 202; (1983) 69 FLR 424 when reviewing similar provisions in the former Bankruptcy Act. In the view of Sheppard J at 438-439, it is sufficient that there be "a substantial compliance" with the then equivalent of section 149C, provided an objector could show that he had addressed his mind to the right question. Beaumont J at 443 referred to the "limited, suspensory nature of the notice of objection", distinguishing it from other notices, and concluding that so long as a notice gave a sufficient indication that it was grounded upon any of the statutory available circumstances, it should be read accordingly. Some support for this view today can also be found in the words of subsection 149C(2) quoted above.

11. In the present circumstances, the language used in the objections is, in my view, sufficient to inform the bankrupt of the factual basis for the grounds relied upon to a sufficient extent, to enable him to take further steps. The fact that he applied to have the notice reviewed by the Inspector-General and was successful in having the grounds cancelled, is in itself, an indication that he had sufficient notice of the allegations made in the notice of objection.

12. The real issue in this case is whether the facts support the grounds relied upon. Shortly, it may be said that Mr Molina had a flourishing practice as a Certified Public Accountant and a Registered Tax Agent before his troubles began. He is now employed by a chartered accountant, Mr Pearson. Some of the clients of Mr Pearson"s practice were previously clients of Mr Molina. It is the trustee"s contention that Mr Molina disposed of these clients (or perhaps more accurately disposed of a practice which was dependent upon patronage by the clients) to Mr Pearson, without payment, and that he has not sufficiently accounted for this. The fourth ground of objection goes hand in hand with the third.

13. Section 149N obliges the Inspector-General to cancel an objection if "there is insufficient evidence to support the existence of the ground or grounds of objection". In this present re-hearing I am under a similar obligation. I have to consider whether there is sufficient evidence to support the existence of grounds 3 and 4. In doing so, I consider that a normal standard of balance of probability applicable in most administrative reviews is appropriate in this case. The formula used does not permit one to support the existence of the grounds on the basis of suspicion or inferences that are not reasonably open.

14. Dixon CJ in Jones v Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298 at 304 said that a permissible inference is one which -

" . . . arises as an affirmative conclusion from the circumstances proved in evidence and one which they establish to the reasonable satisfaction of a judicial mind. It is true that "you need only circumstances raising a more probable inference in favour of what is alleged". But "they must do more than give rise to conflicting inferences of equal degree of probability so that the choice between them is mere matter of conjecture". These phrases are taken from an unreported judgement of this Court in Bradshaw v McEwans Pty Ltd (Unreported, delivered 27 April, 1951) which is referred to in Holloway v McFeeters [1956] HCA 25; (1956) 94 CLR 470 by Williams, Webb and Taylor JJ. The passage continues : "All that is necessary is that according to the course of common experience the more probable inference from the circumstances that sufficiently appear by evidence or admission, left unexplained, should be that the injury arose from the defendant"s negligence. By more probable is meant no more than that upon a balance of probabilities such an inference might reasonably be considered to have some greater degree of likelihood" (1956) 94 CLR at 480, 481. But the law which this passage attempts to explain does not authorise a court to choose between guesses, where the possibilities are not unlimited, on the ground that one guess seems more likely than another or the others. The facts proved must form a reasonable basis for a definite conclusion affirmatively drawn of the truth of which the tribunal of fact may reasonably be satisfied".

These observations are just as apposite for proceedings in this Tribunal. A formula such as "insufficient evidence to support" will be given the same meaning in this Tribunal as in the courts as part of the Tribunal"s duty to review administrative decisions against the background of applicable law.

15. Five matters fall for consideration. The first question is whether the bankrupt owned "property" in the form of a tax return practice at the relevant time, which was at the commencement of the bankruptcy on 6 June 1992. If this is answered in the affirmative, the second matter falling for consideration is to identify that property. The third matter is to determine whether the bankrupt disclosed a beneficial interest in that property. The fourth matter is whether the property was disposed of. If it was, then the fifth matter for consideration is to determine whether there was a failure to explain adequately to the trustee why no money was received as a result of the disposal.

16. If the third ground of objection is sustained, the applicant"s bankruptcy is extended for a further eight years. The fourth ground, if established, would extend it for five years.

17. The applicant agrees that he had a flourishing practice, prior to 1990. He is a member of the Spanish community, speaks the language and has a special attraction for Spanish-speaking clients. The income figures referred to in objection 4 are not disputed. At an examination held under section 81, Mr Molina estimated that of the amounts shown, between one fifth and one third, would have been income derived from his tax practice.

18. In November 1990, he was arrested and charged with a crime unrelated to the present proceedings. He was refused bail and was held on remand until May or June of 1991. His trial took place in September 1993, when he was acquitted. Mr Molina did not give evidence before me. However, the evidence he gave before a Deputy Registrar in Bankruptcy on 23 October 1996, was tendered without objection and was relied upon by all parties. In that evidence, he described how his practice had collapsed while he was in prison. His sister came out from Spain to help and assisted in contacting many of the Spanish clients. Nevertheless, the number of taxpayers requiring his services dropped considerably during this period. Not only was he physically unable to service their needs, but his reputation also suffered from the fact that he was held in prison. He estimated that before he was charged, he had between 1500 and 1800 clients. Most of these were lost while he was in prison, although as he said, "some continued and eventually they started coming back", after he was released from prison and after 1993.

19. Approximately one month after his trial, he became a bankrupt and the applicant was appointed as his trustee. He was told by an employee of Mr Wily that he was no longer entitled to carry on business on his own account. Section 251K(3C) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 also required the Tax Agents Board to cancel his registration as a tax agent upon his becoming an undischarged bankrupt. He therefore sought employment in the only field with which he was familiar.

20. In October 1993, he took up employment with a local franchisee of Income Tax Professionals (ITP). He continued with this firm until 30 June 1994. Whilst so employed a number of his old clients consulted him and had him prepare their tax return. The fees for preparation of returns of this nature were, of course, the property of ITP. There was no evidence at all of any arrangement amounting to a disposal of all or part of Mr Molina"s former practice to ITP, nor has any such disposal been alleged, notwithstanding that some of the old clients followed him to that firm.

21. In 1991, after he had been released from prison, he was introduced by another accountant friend, Mr Sevdalis, to Mr Pearson. At that time, Mr Molina was, of course, still carrying on what remained of his practice. Mr Pearson had a facility to allow him to lodge his returns electronically with the Australian Taxation Office. Their association began by Mr Pearson making that facility available on the basis of $15 per tax return.

22. In June 1994, after Mr Molina had left ITP, he again approached Mr Pearson and said that he was looking for employment. At the time, Mr Pearson did not know that he had become a bankrupt and did not find out until late in 1994 or early 1995 when he attended a meeting at Mr Wily"s office. According to evidence given by Mr Pearson at the section 81 examination, Mr Molina simply wanted a job. He said of Mr Molina, "I knew he was a well-qualified tax accountant and I needed additional staff." He appreciated that Mr Molina had no experience outside of individual tax returns. He was also aware that Mr Molina had some contacts in the Spanish community. It was arranged that he should be paid a salary. Because of the varying intensity of tax work, he was to be paid more for the first six months than for the second six months of a year. Mr Molina said in his evidence, and Mr Pearson agreed, that there was some talk of paying a commission to Mr Molina, but both agree that no amount has in fact been paid. Mr Molina still lives in hope of receiving it. Mr Pearson says he has been so involved in Mr Molina"s bankruptcy investigations and he has lost so much time as a consequence, that he does not feel inclined to pay any commission. The matter remains unresolved. There is no firm evidence as to whether there is an enforceable agreement to pay commission to Mr Molina, in respect to any returns prepared by him, either for any of his old clients, or for any of Mr Pearson"s clients. Mr Molina carries out income tax return preparations, as directed, for clients which both he and Mr Pearson consider to be Mr Pearson"s clients. This is so, whether or not they are Spanish speaking and whether or not they were formerly clients of Mr Molina. In fact, only a relatively small number of people fall into the latter category.

23. Despite the public examinations of Mr Molina and Mr Pearson, and despite notices given by Mr Wily and Mr Caddy, the official receiver, this is a summary of all the available evidence, directly bearing on the grounds raised in the notice of objection.

24. I turn now to the question whether Mr Molina owned "property", within the meaning of section 149D(1)(g)(ii), in the form of his tax practice. Property is defined in section 5(1) to mean, "real or personal property of every description, whether situate in Australia or elsewhere, and includes any estate, interest or profit, whether present or future, vested or contingent, arising out of or incident to any such real or personal property". To define property to mean property is not particularly helpful. Macquarie Dictionary offers as an appropriate definition, "4. Ownership; right of possession, enjoyment, or disposal of anything, especially of something tangible". To view Mr Molina"s tax practice as an accumulation of rights of disposal is also not particularly helpful when considering whether or not he disposed of property.

25. Mr Wily gave evidence as an experienced chartered accountant that tax practices of the kind carried on by Mr Molina before his imprisonment, are commercially valuable items and are disposable. The basis upon which he makes these allegations, however, appears to be that of general knowledge. He said that, in his opinion, the value of the practice could be fixed in capital terms, on a dollar for dollar basis, by reference to the fees received. He said that he has been unable to value the practice because he has not been able to obtain full details of those fees. It has to be said, however, that Mr Wily"s experience in valuing practices, notwithstanding his long experience as a chartered accountant and trustee in bankruptcy, is very limited. His opinion evidence is not particularly persuasive, having regard both to his lack of experience as a valuer and to the fact that he is in fact the applicant in these proceedings. I am more persuaded by evidence that was given, not in the abstract, but with reference to the particular characteristics of Mr Molina"s practice.

26. At the public examination, Mr Molina said that at his first meeting in Mr Wily"s office, he offered the practice for what it was worth to the applicant, for sale. He said -

" . . . the first meeting I ever had with them, I told them they could have whatever they want and sell it if they think it"s valuable, and nothing"s happened as far as I know".

This evidence remains uncontradicted. The offer was not accepted.

27. Mr Molina then went on to recount his vain endeavours to sell the practice before he became bankrupt. He said -

"Yes, in 1990 when all this happened I had 1800 clients. I was desperately in need of money for legal purposes, things more important, the criminal matters, things more important than bankruptcy and because I needed money urgently. I made many efforts to sell it. This is when I had 1800 clients and nobody would take it because it"s a very specific market and nobody - the only people that would be interested were put so many restrictions that it wasn"t practically a sale. Nobody would want it. No Spanish-speaking accountant would want it. Nobody would touch it because it"s like me buying a Greek business from a Greek agent".

28. This uncontradicted evidence leads me to conclude that there was nothing tangible or disposable which could properly be termed property. Certainly, the evidence does not support a valuation of any property, if such did exist, for any appreciable amount. By the time of the commencement of bankruptcy, on any view of the evidence, the number of returns regularly being serviced had shrunk to such a small number that even applying Mr Wily"s formula, a negligible value would result. It is true that Mr Molina had a "following". On the evidence before me, however, this was not a marketable commodity, except in special, narrow circumstances which were not available at the appropriate time.

29. The special nature of the practice prior to bankruptcy illustrates the difficulty in attempting to identify it as "property". A rare combination of talents, personal to Mr Molina, when applied to a greatly diminished client base, makes it virtually impossible to give content to any notion of property residing in his practice.

30. I am also of the view that even if there was property in existence at the relevant time, there is insufficient evidence to support an allegation that the bankrupt disposed of that property. Clearly, there is no explicit disposition. All available books and records are said to have been delivered to the trustee by both Mr Molina and Mr Pearson. A number of investigatory mechanisms have been activated to ensure that full disclosure was made. None of these books and records disclose any agreement, arrangement or understanding, in writing or otherwise, relating to the sale, transfer or other disposition of Mr Molina"s practice to Mr Pearson. The only basis upon which I am invited to infer that there was such a disposition is that Mr Molina now works for Mr Pearson, services some of his former clients, who are now Mr Pearson"s clients (as all agree), and is paid a relatively small amount (with which description Mr Pearson does not agree). The evidence does not allow me to make such an inference in such an important matter.

31. On the other hand, there is definite evidence that there was no such disposal and I see no reason why it should be rejected. Mr Molina, categorically denied it (transcript of examination, page 24) as did Mr Pearson, (transcript of examination, page 37). When asked why he had not paid Mr Molina for clients he introduced, Mr Pearson said : "Why would I? I don"t buy clients". This evidence remains unchallenged. In any event, as Mr Pearson put it in a letter to Mr Wily on 14 August 1995 : "As far as I am concerned the tax practice of Mr Molina was defunct around 1991, prior to my even knowing him, and I had no dealings with this". Even if there were an agreement to pay commission to Mr Molina, I would not accept this as evidence that the client base still belonged to Mr Molina. Indeed, if an inference were to be drawn from that alleged fact, one would come to the opposite conclusion.

32. In relation to the fourth objection, I am satisfied that the bankrupt made full disclosure to the trustee of his beneficial interest in the practice. Whether this amounted to a beneficial interest in property is, as I have said, most unlikely. The fact that the practice was offered to the trustee for sale at the first meeting, and the fact that he disclosed the nature of his practice in answer to question 38 of his statement of affairs, in themselves would be sufficient to negate the allegations made in objection 4.

33. As to objection 3, I have concluded that Mr Molina did not own any relevant property at the commencement of his bankruptcy, or in the alternative, if there was any property, it was not disposed of and consequently, a question of failing adequately to explain to the trustee why no money was received as a result of the disposal, does not arise for consideration.

34. I have therefore concluded, that there is insufficient evidence to support the existence of grounds 3 and 4 and accordingly the decision under review is affirmed.
