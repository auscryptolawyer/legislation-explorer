Re Taxation Appeals [1989] AATA 229; 5471 Taxation 89 ATC 964 (2 November 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT87/2725

AAT No. 5471

Taxation

89 ATC 964

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - Sole Parent Rebate - Sole Care - applicant living apart from his wife and children - contributed moneys to his wife's account for the welfare of his children - wife also received a pension, part of which was specifically referable to the children - wife expended moneys from the account - applicant did not have sole care - Income Tax Ruling 2337 criticized

Words and phrases : Sole care

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s 159K(1), (3)

Case M78 80 ATC 549

Case T45 86 ATC 370

Case T55 86 ATC 423

Case U40 87 ATC 307

Case V119 88 ATC 749

Sharma v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4260

Income Tax Ruling No 2243

Income Tax Ruling No 2337

HEARING

SYDNEY

2:11:1989

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the respondent's objection decision in relation to the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1986.

DECISION

The applicant claims the benefit of a "sole parent" rebate, pursuant to s 159K of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for the year ended 30 June 1986.

2. Shortly prior to the commencement of that year, the applicant separated from his wife. He gave evidence that he was concerned about the welfare of his two children, aged 11 and 8. In order that he could still have close contact with them, he purchased a house in which his wife and children were to live during the whole of that fiscal year. He lived in a flat, which he said was some 5 minutes away.

3. Although the mother looked after the day-to-day needs of the children, the applicant gave evidence that he took a close interest in their welfare. He visited the children twice a week. He jogged to their house regularly. They visited him on weekends and on other occasions. He took time to go through school work with them. He took them to athletics. He generally saw a good deal of them. Photographs were tendered in evidence, illustrating their close relationship. The purpose of living nearby, he said, was so that he could provide care and support for his children.

4. He provided financial assistance by paying $225 per fortnight, which was transferred directly from his bank account to the mother's bank account. The payments on the house were $640 per month. Although he gave evidence that the fortnightly payments were intended to cover food, clothing, school fees and sporting activities, as well as mortgage repayments, clearly they would not be sufficient for that purpose. His wife was in receipt of social security benefits in the form of a pension, commonly known as a single mother's pension. The applicant agreed that the amount of her pension would have been calculated by reference to her responsibility for the 2 children. He estimated that $10 or $20 per week per child would have been included in her pension because of this.

5. In his view, the fact that he provided additional financial assistance enabled him to determine where his children lived, which school they went to and whether they were being well cared for. He agreed that it would have been impossible for him to work and look after the children on a day-to-day basis at the same time. He was, in fact, in full-time employment throughout the year. The sole parent rebate was not claimed by his wife for that year.

6. Sub-section 159K(1) provides that where during the whole of the whole of the year of income a taxpayer has the sole care of a dependant of a certain class then he is entitled to a rebate of tax. It was agreed that the children of the applicant fell within the class prescribed in the sub-section. The question to be decided was whether, during the whole of the year of income, the applicant had their sole care. Because the applicant was married during that year it was also necessary, pursuant to sub-section (3), to demonstrate special circumstances which would justify a rebate.

7. The applicant's claim was rejected and the following reason was given in a letter from the respondent to the applicant -

"A sole parent rebate is granted to the person who

has the sole responsibility for the actual looking

after, caring, upbringing etc of a child, and who

expends the money on such maintenance, irrespective

of its source. Therefore in order that a person

may be entitled to a sole parent rebate he/she must

have actual responsibility for the dependent child."

This was, in part, a reference to Income Tax Ruling 2337.

8. In Case M78 80 ATC 549 a Board of Review considered the meaning of the expression "sole care". At page 552, the Board observed -

"In other words, we consider that "sole care" vests

in that person who has the full and unshared

responsibility for making decisions on a day-to-day

basis as to the child's upbringing and welfare,

whether it be the food that he eats, the clothes

that he wears or the school that he attends."

This view was specifically rejected by Rogers J in Sharma v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4260, a decision of the NSW Supreme Court. At page 4264, His Honour said -

"So to approach the problem is, in my respectful

view, to disregard the ultimate objective of giving

effect to the intention of the Act, accurately

described as purposive interpretation. What is

under consideration is a taxing Act. The self

evident intention of the provision is to grant

relief to those within the prescribed category who

are involved in a financial outgoing. The focus of

attention at all times remains on financial

obligations whether to the revenue or to the

dependant. It is certainly right to recognise that

it is not every outgoing expended on a dependant

which permits of a deduction being had. There is

the further qualification that the dependant has to

stand in a particular relationship to the taxpayer.

However, once again, that relationship is a

relationship predicated on financial responsibility

and expenditure. In my view, what the legislature

intended to achieve by inserting the qualification

"sole care" was to ensure that the deduction was

obtained by one taxpayer only and not by a

multiplicity of taxpayers each of whom may expend

money on the welfare and maintenance of the

dependant. In order to ensure that only the one

taxpayer benefited he or she was specified as being

the person who had the sole care in the sense of

having the sole financial responsibility for the dependant."

9. This view was apparently accepted by the Commissioner and was reflected in Ruling No 2243. For some reason which was not immediately apparent that Ruling was repealed and replaced by the current Ruling 2337. The former ruling is no longer in print. The new ruling appears to reject both the decision and the reasoning in Sharma absolutely. For example, in paragraph 8 the Commissioner says -

"To say, however, that sole financial responsibility

is the one test that determines eligibility for a

sole parent rebate is not, in the view of this

office, in accord with the legislative intent

behind the enactment of s 159K."

The terms of the Ruling may have been based on the view expressed by Senior Member Roach in Case T45 86 ATC 370 and reiterated by him in Case U40 87 ATC 307, although no reference is made to his reasons in the ruling. The Commissioner arrives at his conclusions by reference to an analysis of the Treasurer's speech when the legislative provision was introduced and by reference to analogous provisions in the Act with particular reference to concessional rebates.

10. In my view the basis upon which the Commissioner is now apparently proceeding is not in accordance with law. It is not open to the Commissioner, or to this Tribunal, to select the reasons of a Board of Review as offering a more rational or convenient approach to the interpretation of a section than those of a judge of a Supreme Court. There is, so far as I am aware, no other case in which the meaning of the section has been judicially considered. So long as the decision of Rogers J in Sharma is not over-ruled or otherwise distinguished by an appropriate authority, I do not consider that either this Tribunal or the Commissioner is entitled to the luxury of substituting its or his own interpretation of the section. In Case V119 88 ATC 749, Deputy President Todd appeared to assume that a choice was available between the day-to-day care and the financial support tests. In my view, there is no such choice available, at any rate at the level of this Tribunal. Until a judge otherwise decides, sole care must, in my view, reside in the person who has sole financial responsibility for the dependant.

11. The question, then, is whether the present applicant has that sole responsibility. It seems to me that for 2 reasons he fails to meet this test. Firstly, he is not alone in the financial support of the children. His wife earns, or receives, a pension, part of which is particularly referable to her responsibilities and is designed for the upkeep of the children. Even if it could be said that the applicant contributed, or was even a substantial contributor, to the financial support of the dependent children, it could not be said that he is the sole person responsible. This aspect of Sharma puzzled Mr Stevens, Chairman of the No 1 Board of Review in Case T45 86 ATC 370. In his view, the reasoning of His Honour in Sharma did not resolve the situation where there was an admixture of funds. Where both parents undertook financial responsibility, Mr Stevens pointed out that neither of them might thereupon become entitled to the rebate. He asked rhetorically at paragraph 10 "Someone must be entitled surely?". In my view, that need not be the case. The section is designed, in its own terms, to provide assistance for persons who have sole care. If a person does not have sole care then he is not entitled to the rebate, either literally or in accordance with the intention of the legislature.

12. The second reason why the applicant does not qualify as a person with sole financial responsibility arises from the manner in which his regular payments were made. They were simply transferred from his bank account to his wife's bank account. From there, she determined the disposition of the money and actually outlayed it. I agree with the observations of Mr HP Stevens in Case T45 where he said at paragraph 11 (86 ATC pp 372-3) -

"In the circumstances I would find (without being

entirely convinced of its correctness) that the

section grants a rebate to the person who has the

responsibility for the maintenance of a child and

who expends money on such maintenance, irrespective

of its source. That way here, although both mother

and father each "expend money on the welfare and

maintenance of the dependant", it is only the

mother who qualifies. It may be said such

conclusion is contrary to Sharma's case but the

present position is to say the least unclear

and requires clarification by legislation ideally

to set out clearly what constitutes sole care in

all the varying situations that can exist."

13. I should add that I do not consider a conclusion that the rebate is available only to the ultimate disburser of financial support is, in any way, contrary to the reasoning or decision in Sharma's case.

14. The question of special circumstances need not be considered as the applicant does not comply with the pre-requisites of sub-section (1). However, I will offer my views in support of some observations that have been made in other cases. In Case T55 86 ATC 423 Dr Beck found that there were no special circumstances and at page 427 said -

"The test I put on the words used in s 159K(3)(a) is

to decide whether the allowance of a rebate is

appropriate because the taxpayer is bearing costs

of caring for a dependant that are caused, not by a

decision that the taxpayer has made of his or her

own volition, but because some onerous outside

event or "special circumstance" has made that

situation unavoidable . . . The rationale behind the

sub-section seems to me to be to assist a parent

more or less left in the lurch by intentional (eg

desertion) or unintentional (eg accident or

illness) action by the spouse."

15. I respectfully agree with those views. They were also quoted with approval by Deputy President Todd in Case V119 88 ATC 749 at 752. The section is designed to allow the rebate to a person who is married but whose partner has, through no fault of the taxpayer, taken no part in the upbringing of the dependent child. I would add to the examples given by Dr Beck a gaol sentence which would deprive the parent at liberty from any assistance of any kind from his or her partner. To use the phrase in Case M78 at page 553, there would then be "absence of the human supportive role and financial assistance normally forthcoming from the other partner".

16. In the present case, the children were in the care of their mother who attended to their every day needs. There is no evidence that the applicant in any way was deprived of the supportive role usually forthcoming from his partner. The separation of husband and wife in this case is voluntary. There is therefore, in my view, no room for a finding of special circumstances, even if the applicant had complied with the pre-requisites of sub-section (1).

17. The objection decision under review is therefore affirmed.
