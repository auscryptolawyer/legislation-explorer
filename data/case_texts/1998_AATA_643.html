REASONS FOR DECISION

18 August 1998 Deputy President G.L. McDonald

Associate Professor S.D. Hotop, Senior Member

Associate Professor R.D. Fayle, Senior Member

  1. In September 1994 The Western Australian Turf Club (“the Club”), by its agent Coopers and Lybrand, wrote to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (“the Commissioner”) to request a refund of Sales Tax paid in respect of goods purchased for the three year period to 1 September 1994. The Commissioner replied on 9 March 1995, refusing the request on the ground that the Club is not a public authority. The Club, on 5 November 1996, lodged an objection against that decision but extended the refund period to goods purchased from 1 September 1991 up to and including 8 March 1995. Although out of time, the Commissioner accepted the objection as being within time but disallowed it. Curiously, the notice of disallowance, dated 1 July 1997, refers to the period from 1 September 1991 to 11 June 1997 as the refund claim period in question[1]. That decision was referred to this Tribunal for review.
  2. At the hearing the Club was represented by Mr J. Pickering, solicitor, of Freehill Hollingdale and Page. The Commissioner was represented by Mr J. Allanson of counsel. Mr P. Neck gave evidence on affidavit and orally on behalf of the Club. Affidavits by Messrs Barry Andrew Sargeant and Reginal Frederick Dawson were taken into evidence. The Tribunal had before it documents filed by the Commissioner pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the T documents”).

THE RELEVANT LAW

  1. The period for which the refunds were claimed is covered by separate instruments of Sales Tax legislation. The period to 31 December 1992 falls for consideration under subsection 26(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (N° 1) 1930 (Cth) (“the 1930 Act”) and the period since then is under CR1 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 (Cth) (“the 1992 Act”).
  2. The Club contends that the amounts have been overpaid because the goods were exempt from tax under Item 78(ii) of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 (Cth) (“the Exemptions Act 1935”) and Item 127(1)(b) of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992 (Cth) (“the Exemptions Act 1992”) respectively. Those provisions are set out below.
  3. By s.5 of the Exemptions Act 1935 sales tax is not payable upon the sale value of any goods, in this context, covered by Division XI, Goods for use by Governments, Representatives of Governments and Public Bodies, Items 78(i) and (ii) which state:
“Item 78 Goods for use (whether as goods or in some other form) and not for sale by -
(i) a municipal, shire or district council constituted for the general purposes of local government under any law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory;

(ii) a public authority constituted under any law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory for the purpose of carrying out any or all of the functions ordinarily carried out by a council or the kind specified in (i);
. . . .” [emphasis added]
  1. Under the Exemptions Act 1992, in broad terms, if all the requirements of an exemption Item are satisfied at or before the time of an assessable dealing, the dealing is not taxable[2]. Item 127 of the Exemptions Act 1992 is found in Schedule 1, Chapter 14 (Goods for use by Governments, Hospitals etc), Sub-Chapter 14.1 (Australian governments and government representatives). It states relevantly:
“ITEM 127: [Local government bodies etc.]
  1. 127 (1) Goods for use by any of the following bodies that is constituted by a law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory:
    • (a) a municipal, shire or district council that is constituted for general purposes of local government;
    • (b) a public authority that is constituted for the purpose of carrying out some or all of the functions that are ordinarily carried out by a council of the kind described in paragraph (a);
    • (c) [not relevant]” [emphasis added]

It is noted that the phrase “public authority” is not defined in either statute nor in either Exemption Act.

THE ISSUES

  1. If the Club satisfies all the requirements of the exemption Items above, then it would not be liable for sales tax on purchases of goods for its own use and not for resale. In that event it would be entitled to a refund of tax overpaid. It was agreed from the outset that the Tribunal would only concern itself with the question of whether the Club satisfied the requirements of the two exemption items. In the event that the Tribunal decided that it did, the matter of whether the Club was entitled to refunds and their quantum would be remitted to the Commissioner for determination.
  2. The issues therefore are whether the Club satisfies the requirements of exemption Item 78(ii) for goods purchased up to 1 January 1993 and exemption Item 127(1) in respect of goods purchased from that date.

THE FACTS

  1. There was no disagreement as to the facts. The Club was established in 1852 as a members’ club for the purpose of conducting horse racing. Although the Club is not incorporated the chairman and his successors act as if the Club was a corporation sole.
  2. A private Act of the colonial parliament of Western Australia, assented to on 18 March 1892, and known as The Western Australian Turf Club Act 1892 (WA)[3] (“the 1892 Act”) formalised a previous grant of lease to the Club[4] of the land now occupied by it at Ascot in Western Australia. The term of the lease was for 999 years for an annual consideration of “one peppercorn”. The Act “expressly provided that the said piece or parcel of land should during the said term be used as a place of public enjoyment and especially for the training and running of Race Horses and other purposes connected therewith and in accordance with the Rules and Regulation of The Western Australian Turf Club for the time being.”[5]
  3. The 1892 Act established wide powers for the committee of the Club. It states in ss.13 and 14:
“13 THE committee or an absolute majority in number of such committee may from time to time subject to the special provisions of this Act make such by-laws as they think fit for regulating the election or admission of members into the Club and the expulsion of members therefrom for providing for the due management of the affairs of the Club for regulating all matters concerning or connected with the said lands by this Act vested or which may hereafter be vested in the chairman and the admission thereto and expulsion therefrom of members of the Club and the public respectively (and the rates or charges to be paid for such admission) and for the general management of the said racecourse and all races and race meetings and for the working and management of any Totalisator or other betting machine and may from time to time by any other by-laws alter or repeal any such by-laws Provided that no such by-laws be repugnant to the laws for the time being in force in Western Australia. And every by-law shall be reduced into writing and shall be signed by the chairman.
  1. NO by-law shall be of any force or effect until the expiration of one month after the same or a copy thereof signed by the chairman shall have been sent to the Colonial Secretary of Western Australia for the time being and until publication as hereinafter mentioned and at any time within the said period of one month the Governor-in-Council may disallow such by-law and after such disallowance such by-law shall not come into operation.”
  2. The By-laws empower the committee to control Club membership, management and administration of its two metropolitan racecourses and improvements, regulation and conduct of race meetings including the adoption of the Australian Rules of Racing[6] and any local rules adopted including the rules of betting. They also provide for the registration and licencing of course bookmakers and the fixing of the race calendar.
  3. By operation of the By-laws and its adoption of the Rules of Racing for the Western Australian Turf Club, the Club, in effect, has an exclusive right to register and licence within the State of Western Australia all racing clubs and meetings; race horses; trainers; jockeys; stable hands and apprentices; bookmakers and their clerks; stewards and the delegated powers of stewards; the totalisator and the rules of betting.[7]
  4. In 1994 the Club amended its By-laws to prohibit it from distributing any assets to members or former members and limiting such distributions to any similar bodies with similar objects or if none exists, to one or more charitable organisations as recommended by the committee or, in the absence of any such recommendation, as the Minister for Racing, Gaming and Liquor determines. That amendment was not disallowed.[8]
  5. By the Totalisator Act Amendment Act 1899 (WA) the government delegated to the Club the power to authorise the use of a totalisator at race meetings.
  6. The Racing Restriction Act 1917 (WA) was assented to in March 1917. That Act is concerned with the administration of horse racing in Western Australia. It imposed certain obligations on the Club and granted it certain powers in that respect. Essentially, the Act provided that no horse or pony race (as distinct from trotting races) for any stake or prize shall be held in Western Australia without first obtaining a licence in writing from the Club. It also limited the number of race meetings which may be held in the metropolitan area each year. And since the Western Australian Turf Club (Property) Act 1944 (WA), a private Act, the Club has had exclusive right to conduct metropolitan race meetings at its leased Ascot and freehold Belmont courses.
  7. The Club is required to have its annual financial accounts audited.
  8. Under the Totalisator Agency Board Betting Act 1960 (WA) the Totalisator Agency Board (“the TAB”) makes a distribution of its profits each year, some to the Club which, in turn, is required to distribute 28.09 percent of that to country racing clubs conditional upon them having presented audited financial accounts to the Club. This arrangement is by agreement with the Minister. Under the arrangement the Club voluntarily furnishes the Minister with a copy of its audited annual financial accounts (which, amongst other things, discloses the receipt and distribution of TAB profits) and a certificated statement regarding the use of those funds each year. In this way the Club is accountable to the Minister for its TAB receipts.[9]
  9. Mr Neck, the Chief Executive officer of the Club gave evidence of the Club’s financial affairs, attaching to his affidavit copies of the Club’s annual reports incorporating its audited financial accounts for the period ended 31 July 1991 and the years ended 31 July 1992 to 31 July 1997 inclusively.[10] These consistently show that the major source of the Club’s income has been from TAB distributions of which 28.09 percent is paid out to the WA Provincial Thoroughbred Racing Association and the Country Racing Association. The other significant sources of its income are from bookmakers’ fees and betting tax receipts, totalisator profit, membership subscriptions, race meeting fees levied on owners, sponsorship, admissions and food and beverage trading. His evidence was that income from the By-law regulation responsibilities of the Club is relatively insignificant, however that does not reflect the degree and scope of administrative effort which those activities require. His evidence was that the Club devotes considerable resources to regulating race meetings in Western Australia and all the attendant responsibilities which this entails - for example, the Club, at its own expense, provides stewards for all provincial and country race meetings. He said about 30 percent of staff devote at least some of their time to these activities. The most significant expenditures of the Club are related to the provision of stakes, the conduct of race meetings, including providing financial subsidies to provincial and country meetings and general administration. The accounts show that the Club outlays a substantial amount for the maintenance and upkeep of the two race courses and improvements.
  10. The membership of the Club is limited to 3,000 and is currently about 2,350.
  11. The affidavit of Mr Sargeant, the Executive Director of the Western Australian Office of Racing, Gaming and Liquor (“the office”) states that the office is responsible for the general administration of racing, gaming and liquor activities in the State and that he is responsible to the Minister. These responsibilities include the approval of racing dates allocated by the Club for metropolitan and country races - the Racing Calendar. The office has a responsibility to ensure the overall integrity and promotion of the racing industry, that its self-regulation is appropriate and that it makes appropriate contributions to the government’s revenue. His evidence is that the office has no direct control over the racing industry or the controlling authorities, which includes the Club, which it regards as independent by force of the 1892 Act.
  12. The Club is not required by law to report to either the Parliament or the Minister, nor are its affairs subject to review by the Public Accounts and Expenditure Review Committee. Nevertheless, in fact the Club provides its annual audited accounts and report on activities for tabling by the Minister and it maintains close communications with the Minister through the office which provides an informal but significant overview and monitoring of the Club’s activities, especially in relation to its handling of the TAB distribution.
  13. Mr Dawson, whose credentials are extensive and include many years experience in local government administration and management said that the care and control of community recreational facilities is an ordinary function of local government. However, that whilst he had no personal experience that that responsibility included the maintenance of race courses, in his opinion councils could make by-laws to undertake such a task.

THE 1978 HIGH COURT JUDGMENT

  1. The applicant in this matter, the Club, was also the appellant in The Western Australian Turf Club and The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1978] HCA 13; (1978) 139 CLR 288 a decision of the Full High Court (Barwick CJ, Stephen, Jacobs and Aickin JJ, Murphy J dissenting). The CLR headnote provides a convenient overview of the case:
“Section 23(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) exempts from tax income which is the revenue of a municipal corporation or other local governing body or of a public authority constituted under any Act or State Act, or under any law in force in a Territory being part of the Commonwealth.

The taxpayer was an unincorporated club founded in 1852. The Western Australian Turf Club Act, 1892 (WA) vested the club’s lands in its chairman and his successors in office as if a corporation sole. Those lands had previously been held by trustees for the club as lessees under a lease which contained covenants requiring the lands to be used for public race meetings. That requirement was continued by the Act but the club was otherwise free to deal with its assets as it thought fit. The Act also gave the club power to make by-laws the breach of which was an offence. The Racing Restriction Act, 1917 (WA) make the club the sole licensing authority for horseracing in Western Australia and provided that no horse-race for prize money should be held without the club’s written licence.

Held by Barwick CJ, Stephen, Jacobs and Aickin JJ, Murphy J dissenting, that the taxpayer was not a public authority within the meaning of s23(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936; by Barwick CJ, Stephen and Jacob JJ on the ground that it was inconsistent with that status for a body to have entire control over the disposition of its profits and for its members to be entitled to share in the division of its assets on dissolution; and by Aickin J on the ground that the public functions of the club were merely incidental to its private functions.”
  1. The relevant part of s.23 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) is as follows:
“23 The following income shall be exempt from income tax:

. . .
(d) the revenue of a municipal corporation or other local governing body or of a public authority constituted under any Act or State Act, or under any law in force in a Territory being part of the Commonwealth.”
  1. As this decision was referred to by both parties, we will return to it in some detail when considering those submissions below.
  2. As an observation on the facts at this juncture, in terms of the constitution of the Club it has since amended its By-laws by the inclusion of By-laws 94A, 94B and 94C. The first two are complementary and provide that the Club may be dissolved by a resolution of three-quarters of members present and voting at a duly convened meeting, and the distribution of any funds, property or other assets remaining after payment of all debts to a body with similar objectives (subject to the approval of the Minister), or if no such body exists to charity with the approval of the Minister. By-law 94C prohibits the distribution of profits to members.

CONSIDERATION OF SUBMISSIONS AND REASONS

  1. Mr Pickering submitted that there is ample authority to support the proposition that an unincorporated body can be a public authority, citing in general Rich J in Renmark Hotel Inc v FCT [1949] HCA 7; (1949) 79 CLR 10 at 19, Stephen J in The Western Australian Turf Club and The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1978] HCA 13; (1978) 139 CLR 288 (“WATC case”) and Hill J in FCT v Bank of WA Ltd; FCT v State Bank of NSW Ltd (1995) 32 ATR 380 at 397 (“the Bank of WA case”). The Tribunal is of the opinion, consistent with those judicial views, that it is conceivable that an unincorporated body may be a public authority. That is, being unincorporated is not sufficient of itself to make an adverse finding in that respect.
  2. In his judgment in the WATC case Stephen J (with whom the Chief Justice and Jacobs J agreed), made the following observation:
“It is clear from these cases[11]that the entity need not from its origin have possessed those qualities which at the relevant date make it a public authority; it may acquire the necessary attributes subsequently and if it does so as a result of legislation it will thereupon have become a public authority. In determining whether the Club now possesses those attributes its history must be examined.” (293)
  1. His Honour observes that the club has, since 1917, had two distinct roles, that of a conventional members’ turf club and that of an agency of government, controlling horse-racing within the State. His Honour, after making particular comment that a public authority must provide a public benefit to be derived from the body’s activities, and said:
“It is rather the general approach of the courts to the question of status as a public authority that is useful; that approach has involved a weighing of all relevant circumstances before deciding in particular cases upon the status of the body in question ... the decision whether a body was a public authority for the purposes of the Act was often found to depend upon a ‘nice balance of considerations and the scale may, therefore, be easily turned’[12].” (296)
  1. His Honour then proceeds, at 297 and 298, to canvass the various features of the Club which would assist it in a determination that it was a public authority and those which would lead to the opposite view. In respect of the latter his Honour adopts, with approval, what Burnside J said in the Full Court decision (the decision then on appeal to the High Court):
“Nor is the club precluded from owning real or personal property in like manner as any other club, and I take it that in the event of a dissolution, its assets would be divisible among its members. The controlling body of the club is elected by the members of the club, and within the limits of the authority conferred by the by-laws they are vested with a discretion in its management, unfettered by public interference.” (298)
  1. Following which Stephen J concludes:
“It follows that the members of the Club have a real interest in the assets of the Club, assets which have been built up by the investment of the revenue of the Club. This is not, of course, to suggest that it is any purpose of the Club that it should earn profits for distribution to its members. It has not done so in the past and will no doubt continue in the future, as is the way with members’ clubs, to devote its profits to the improvement of its facilities. However this does not dispose of the incongruity of applying the description of public authority to a club having entire control over the disposition of its profits and the members of which would, on dissolution, be entitled to share in the division of its assets.

The result of a consideration of all these factors satisfies me that in its context in s23(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act the Club is not a public authority for the purpose of that paragraph.” (298-299)
  1. In agreeing with Stephen J’s judgment, the Chief Justice said in relation to the above context:
“I agree with [my brother Stephen’s] conclusion that the appellant is not ‘a public authority constituted under any Act or State Act’ within the meaning and operation of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 . . .

Apart from its function of licensing horse races under the Racing Restrictions Act 1917 (WA) . . . the appellant clearly, in my opinion, had no function of a public or governmental nature. My brother Stephen’s analysis of the powers and functions of the appellant, apart from the licensing of horse races, amply demonstrates that conclusion. The case thus resolves itself in to one in which an unincorporated body operating for the benefit of its members, though with some obligation to admit the public to its racecourses, has been given by statute the function of granting licences for the holding of race meetings, a function unproductive of revenue or profit to the unincorporated body.” (290)
  1. Mr Pickering submitted that the Tribunal should exercise caution in applying the High Court’s remarks and findings to the present case which is about different statutory provisions. He submitted that it is clear from the judgment of Stephen J and the further remarks, above, by the Chief Justice in agreeing with his fellow justice, that there was concern that the income of the Club, for which exemption from income tax was sought under the relevant revenue statue, had little to do with the Club’s role as a public authority. He submitted that both justices (and by inference Jacobs J who concurred) identified a public authority function carried out by the Club and vested in it by the Racing Restriction Act 1917 (WA). In this context Mr Pickering submitted, that a body such as the Club cannot be seen to be partly a statutory authority and partly not, depending on its relative functions. The decision must be made by reference to the actual statutory provision in question and, as Stephen J observed, by a nice balance of considerations of fact, the scale may be easily turned. He submitted that a significant consideration in this respect is that the exemption sought is from Sales Tax on goods purchased for the Club’s own use. And the evidence supports that a significant part of the Club’s expenditure relates to its functions imposed on it by operation of the Racing Restriction Act and in maintaining the two metropolitan race courses. Those functions, he submitted, give the Club the necessary public authority character and satisfy the additional requirement for exemption from Sales Tax, that the Club was constituted for the purpose of carrying out some of the functions that are ordinarily carried out by a local council.
  2. Mr Pickering further submitted that as the Club is now precluded from making any distribution of profit or assets to its members, that impediment to its claim to be a public authority as recognised by the High Court in 1978, has been removed.
  3. In a contrary submission Mr Allanson, for the Commissioner, directed the Tribunal to, among other matters, the judgment of Aickin J in the WATC case. Aickin J reached the same conclusion as did Barwick CJ, Stephen and Jacobs J but by a distinctly different process. The Tribunal finds it necessary, in this respect, to cite a rather lengthy passage from Aickin J’s judgment to reflect Mr Allanson’s point:
“I would not however question the general proposition that the classification of a body as falling inside or outside some statutory definition or description must turn upon a consideration of all its aspects and a balance of conflicting or possibly conflicting considerations and tendencies, and in some cases may be a question of degree.

In agreement with the views expressed in the cases referred to above[13], I regard the basic requirement [of a public authority] as being that the relevant Act under which the relevant body is said to be “constituted” should confer powers of an exceptional nature not possessed by private individuals or by companies formed by private individuals under the provisions of the Companies Acts. . . .

The power or authority to conduct a racecourse on land demised by the Crown, though it may be said to be exceptional in some respects is not I think such a power as the Court had in mind in the Renmark Hotel Case, notwithstanding that it probably was not ‘otherwise authorised’. An examination of the [1892] Act, and in particular s13 demonstrates that the Club was authorised to conduct a racecourse at which betting might be authorised by the by-laws of the Club - see the reference to ‘any totalisator or other betting machine’. In that sense the Club was authorized to conduct or permit gaming which would otherwise be illegal. In addition the committee was given by ss 20, 21 the powers and functions referred to above.

It is thus apparent that the Club does have certain exceptional powers to do what an ordinary private individual, or group of individuals, may not do, some of which at least, if not all may properly be described as statutory duties and the exercise of public functions. It does however have other powers and it exercises other functions, in that it is a members’ club and is an unincorporated association such as is commonly the mode of organization of members’ clubs. It is true that the Act makes a number of special provisions not often found in the case of a club. Thus the chairman of its committee is in the same position as if he were a corporation sole, providing a convenient mode for holding the Club’s property and for the Club to sue and to be sued. This avoids the long standing inconveniences arising with respect to an unincorporated association, both for the association and for those who deal with it. It is however expressly provided that the Club is not an incorporated body and that the members of the Club are to remain subject to such liabilities as they might otherwise have been subjected to as members of the Club. These provisions would not of themselves warrant the conclusion that the body was a public authority or that it was constituted as such under a State Act.

The situation is accordingly one in which the Club has a number of functions and activities which are properly described as being those of a public authority and it acts as such in exercising those powers performing those functions. In addition it is an ordinary members’ club established as such and carrying on what may be properly described as the ordinary function and activities of a racing club and in that capacity the Club and its individual members are not preforming any function appropriate to a public authority.

It appears from the material placed before the trial judge that the Club derives no income from its licensing activities nor from the powers which it exercises as a by-law making authority. Its income is primarily derived from gate receipts, totalisator turnover commissions, bookmakers’ fees and commissions, other fees, the sale of refreshments and drinks and to an extent from members’ subscriptions. The Club is entitled to sixty per cent of the profits of the Totalisator Agency Board. In addition it has certain investments which yield income. There would seem to be no reason to doubt that if the Club were to be disbanded any surplus assets would be divisible amongst the members, though the land itself would revert to the Crown upon the basis that the Club would then no longer be using it as a racecourse, though compensation would be paid in respect of buildings on the land.

If an association of persons has dual powers and functions, some of a private character and some of an exceptional character such that they are not enjoyed by ordinary individuals, neither set being plainly incidental to the other, it may be that it is possible to regard it as a public authority for some purposes and as a private association of persons for others. Even if this be so, it would not assist the Club in the present case because the income upon which it has been assessed is not derived from any operation conducted as a public authority.

Insofar as the Club has these public powers and functions, they are conferred upon it by statute and, if it is a public authority either in relation to all of its activities or some of them, then it seems to me that it is constituted as such under these two Acts[14]. On the other hand it cannot be said that it is wholly or exclusively a public authority because it has substantial other activities which cannot be regarded as those of a public authority. There does not appear to be any decided case which deals with the situation where there is a body in existence which powers and functions of a purely private character, such as the Club was prior to 1892, and which has then conferred upon it powers, authority and functions of a public character. No doubt bodies properly described as public authorities may have ancillary powers and functions which, if viewed alone, would be appropriate to and properly described as private functions or non-public functions, but the latter may properly be treated as powers or function of the public authority because of their association with the principal or primary public functions of the authority. Income derived from such ancillary functions would properly be described as income of a public authority.

In the present case however, the public functions of the Club are neither the principal nor substantially the principal functions of the Club. They are incidental to the private functions rather than the reverse. For that reason the Club itself cannot be regarded as a public authority, and the fact that it derived no income from the exercise of its public powers and duties assists that conclusion.” (311-313)
  1. The relevant facts of this case are materially different from those obtaining when the above matter was considered by the High Court only by reason that the Club is now prohibited from distributing its surplus assets or income to members and the subsequent voluntary agreement to submit annual reports to the Minister. Other than that the material facts are not different. The other relevant differences are found in the law. This case concerns the Sales Tax provisions outlined above whereas the former case concerned Income Tax legislation. And there is a difference between the two relevant Sales Tax statutes in question. The Exemptions Act 1992 is different to its predecessor inasmuch as Item 127 of the Exemptions Act 1992 requires the body to be constituted “by” a law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory, whereas Item 78 of the predecessor Exemptions Act 1935 requires the body to be constituted “under” any law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory.
  2. It is noted that the relevant Income Tax statutory provision with which the High Court was dealing in the WATC case uses the words “. . . a public authority constituted under any Act or State Act, or under any law in force in a Territory being part of the Commonwealth.”[15] The fact is that the Club was first formed in 1852[16] some considerable time before the 1892 Act. And indeed, the Club did not take on any of the attributes of a public authority until the 1892 Act and later the Racing Restriction Act 1917 (WA). However, the High Court did not consider this to stand in the way of the Club pursuing its argument that it was a public authority for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act (WA).[17] That is, it was no impediment that the Club was formed well before the relevant legislation giving it characteristics of a public authority as discussed in detail by Aickin J above.
  3. Mr Allanson submitted that insofar as Item 127 is concerned the Club cannot be said to be constituted by a law of the State. That would require the Club to be established and constituted by a relevant Act, although it was not, he submitted, necessary that the relevant Act be exclusively for the Club. The Club has at all times remained a private members’ Club with its own membership rules and governing structure, in a sense, formalised by the 1892 Act many years after its formation.
  4. The Tribunal takes a fairly generous view of the law in this respect. No assistance was given to it in relation to why the legislature saw fit to alter the provision in the particular way. The High Court in both the WATC and Renmark Hotel cases focussed its respective judgments on the question of whether the facts supported the contention that the entity was a public authority, which issue is central to this matter. In the Tribunal’s opinion the separate judgments of both Stephen J and Aickin J are concerned with whether the Club was then a “public authority” in a generic sense, and not just for the exclusive purpose of s23(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.
  5. The Tribunal is of the opinion that the WATC judgment, so far as it relates to what is a “public authority” for the purposes of paragraph 23(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act can be relied upon in this case despite the different legislative provisions and purposes. The judgment of Hill J (with whom Wilcox and Drummond JJ agreed) in the Bank of WA case lends support to the notion that the term “public authority” in this context is generic rather than statute specific. His Honour makes several references to the WATC judgment - at 396[18]. After discussing the judgment of Gibbs J in Committee of Direction of Fruit Marketing v Australian Postal Commission [1980] HCA 23; (1980) 144 CLR 577 in relation to the meaning of “authority”, he observes:
”It is likewise generally true to say, with respect, that the outcome of most of the decided cases was fairly predictable.

Thus, a community-based public hotel (Renmark Hotel Inc ...); a corporation established to operate a private railway (FCT v Silverton Tramway Co Ltd [1953] HCA 79; (1935) 88 CLR 559); a members racing club (albeit that it had power by statue to license horse racing) (Western Australian Turf Club v FCT (1978) ...) and a body incorporated by statute for the publication of law reports(Incorporated Council of Law Reporting of Queensland v FCT [1924] HCA 23; (1924) 34 CLR 580) have been held not to be authorities (or public authorities). . . .

In Renmark Hotel Rich J gave some useful examples of bodies which his Honour considered would be ‘public authorities’ or which are called such in normal speech. The list is diverse and includes municipal authorities, a highway authority, a sanitary authority, a water supply authority, a lighting authority, a harbour authority, a tramway authority, a transport authority, a railway authority and a construction authority. In addition there are, as his Honour observes by reference to the English cases, numerous other examples, such as boards of guardians, the Mersey Docks and Harbour Board, public elementary schools and the like.

A number of propositions can be derived from the cases.
  1. A question whether a particular entity is an authority will be a question of fact and degree dependent upon all the circumstances of the case: Western Australian Turf Club per Stephen J with whom Barwick CJ agreed at CLR 290. No one factor will be determinative, rather there will be a ‘range of considerations’: the Fruit Marketing case at CLR 580.
  2. Incorporation by legislation is not necessary before a body may be classified as an authority: Renmark Hotel at CLR 19 per Rich J, Western Australian Turf Club at CLR 293.
. . .
  1. At least where the question is whether a body is a ‘public authority’ the body must exercise control power or command for the public advantage or execute a function in the public interest: Silverton Tramway at CLR 565 and 567 per Dixon CJ. The central concept is the ability to exercise power or command: the Fruit Marketing case at CLR 580 per Gibbs J."
  2. In the Tribunal’s opinion however, the majority in the WATC case took into account the significance (or lack of significance) of the income which the Club derived from carrying out its obligations pursuant to the Racing Restriction Act 1917 (WA)[19], as but one of the many considerations entertained in reaching their Honours’ respective conclusions. However, Stephen J (with whom Barwick CJ and Jacobs J concurred) found, on balance that the Club, in the context in s23(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (WA), was not a public authority. The Tribunal can see no point of distinction in the determination of what is a public authority by reference to that revenue statute and the Sales Tax legislation here in question. Essentially, as Hill J points out in the Bank of WA case, the determination is founded on fact. A controlling factor in Stephen J’s judgment in the WATC case is what he described as “the incongruity of applying the description of public authority to a club having entire control over the disposition of its profits and the members of which would, on dissolution, be entitled to share in the division of its assets" (p.299).
  3. Aickin J, on the other hand, in concluding that the Club was not a public authority, did not seem to place much store in the fact that the Club was technically able to make such a distribution of assets to its members in the event of it being dissolved. His Honour’s primary focus was on the relevant characteristics of the Club as is apparent from the passages cited above. In this regard the Tribunal merely repeats what it considers to be the most telling passage to that effect:
“In the present case however, the public functions of the Club are neither the principal nor substantially the principal functions of the Club. They are incidental to the private functions rather than the reverse. For that reason the Club itself cannot be regarded as a public authority, and the fact that it derived no income from the exercise of its public powers and duties assists that conclusion.” (313)
  1. The amendments to the Club’s By-laws made on 10 May 1994 and published in the Gazette on 5 July 1994 were, it seems, intended to cure the perceived problem alluded to by Stephen J in his judgment at pages 298 and 299. However, the committee of the Club is empowered by clause 13 of its By-laws to make such by-laws as they think fit as are consistent with the By-laws, for, amongst other matters, the due management of the affairs of the Club, presumably the provision relied upon to amend the By-laws as mentioned. The only impediment to the Club effectively rescinding those amended clauses[20] is that the Parliament[21] has one month to disallow such amendments. Stephen J observed in his judgment in the WATC case that the Club had not distributed profits to its members and he had no doubts that it would (not) do so in the future[22]. However that did not alter his conclusion that it could do so. For similar reasons it is open to this Tribunal to conclude that whilst it is highly unlikely that the committee would rescind clauses 94A, 94B and 94C of its By-laws, there is a mechanism in place whereby, at the instigation of the committee that is a possibility.
  2. For the above reasons, the Tribunal does not accept that the Club is able to distinguish sufficiently the facts and reasoning of the High Court in the WATC case from those now before the Tribunal[23]. This obtains notwithstanding that the two cases concern different statutory provisions, however, as discussed above, the relevant differences are not significant since both concern the question of whether the Club was or is a public authority, that term having been attributed a generic meaning (by the cases cited) rather than a peculiar meaning specific to either statute.
  3. In concluding the Tribunal, having found that the Club is not a “public authority” for the purposes of the relevant statutory provisions, finds it unnecessary to consider whether the Club was established for the purpose of carrying out some or all of the functions of local government. However, it does find on the evidence, that the Club’s functions of maintaining the two race courses, their surrounds and improvements would meet that criterion and that that is a significant purpose of the Club. This finding however does not alter the previous conclusion that the Club is not a relevant “public authority”.

DECISION

  1. For the above reasons, the decision under review is affirmed.
