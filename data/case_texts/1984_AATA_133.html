Re David Harold Eastman and Commissioner of Superannuation [1984] AATA 133 (1 May 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: DAVID HAROLD EASTMAN

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Nos. A83/56, A83/75

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr A.N. Hall (Deputy President)

Mr. I. Prowse (Member)

Dr. H.W. Garlick (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - deemed invalidity retirement - paranoia - request to cancel election for lump sum benefit in lieu of pension - request denied - application for review - relevant considerations - whether applicant possessed of all relevant information - whether applicant understood nature and consequences of election - invalidity retirement condition affecting capacity for rational judgment - whether prejudice to superannuation scheme by cancelling election - applicant not responsible for delay since request first made - direction that election be cancelled

Superannuation - deemed invalidity retirement - Benefit Classification Certificate specifying nervous disorders - refusal to extend time to request reconsideration of decisions (a) to issue certificate and (b) that condition causing retirement substantially contributed to by certified condition - applicant aware of significance of medical history - aware of availability of reconsideration procedure - no basis for exercise of discretion to extend time

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.66(2), 72(2), 154(2), 157(3), 184(2)

Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978

Superannuation (Cancellation of Elections) Regulations

Re Eastman and Commissioner for Superannuation (1982) 4 ALN N110

Re Schmack and the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1981) 3 ALN N77

HEARING

CANBERRA

1:5:1984

ORDER

For the reasons set out herein the Tribunal decides as follows:

The Tribunal sets aside the Commissioner's decision not to direct that the applicant's election for a lump sum benefit in lieu of a pension be cancelled and in substitution for that decision the Tribunal directs that the election be cancelled.

The Tribunal affirms the decisions of the Commissioner not to extend the time within which the applicant may request a reconsideration by the Commissioner of the decisions taken on 23 June 1977 and 25 January 1978 respectively (a) to issue a Benefit Classification Certificate specifying a history of nervous disorders and (b) deciding that the condition causing invalidity retirement was substantially contributed to by the certified condition.

DECISION

1. These two applications for review which by consent were heard concurrently, relate to separate decisions taken by the Commissioner for Superannuation affecting either directly or indirectly the applicant's entitlements under the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") following his 'deemed' retirement on the grounds of invalidity. The first application (A83/56) relates to a decision made on 16 May 1983 refusing to direct that the election which Mr Eastman made on 31 January 1978 for a lump sum benefite in lieu of a pension be cancelled (see s. 157(3) of the Act). The second application (A83/75) relates to a decision made on 17 June 1983 refusing to extend the prescribed period in which Mr. Eastman may request reconsideration of decisions taken by the Commissioner under ss.184(2) and 66(2) of the Act on 23 June 1977 and 25 January 1978, respectively. It was as a consequence of these decisions that the applicant's benefit entitlements (whether for a pension or a lump sum payment) were more limited than they might otherwise have been.

2. The applicant, who is an unmarried man now aged 38, joined the Public Service on 1 February 1966. He was recruited to the Service in London towards the end of the previous year following completion of a University degree in England. He was medically examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 1 March 1966, following which a decision as to his acceptance into the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account established pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act") was deferred pending further medical assessment. In fact it was not until 27 April 1967 that a decision was made to accept Mr Eastman into the Provident Account.

3. Mr Eastman said that he had endeavoured in September 1971 to ascertain through his treating doctor, Dr Lionel Henderson, the reasons why the Superannuation Board had decided that he should be accepted into the Provident Account rather than the Superannuation Fund. By letter dated 11 October 1971 the Board had declined to provide its reasons stating that that information was privy to the Board.

4. On 17 February 1977, following the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976, Mr Eastman wrote to the Commissioner through the Inspector (Personnel) seeking the revocation of the benefit classification certificate that by virtue of s.184(2) of the 1976 Act (as it then stood) was deemed to be in force in relation to him. Mr Eastman was no doubt aware that because he had been a contributor to the Provident Account under the superseded Act there was a benefit classification certificate that was deemed to be in force in respect of him specifying the physical or mental conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board had decided that his health and physical fitness were not such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established under the superseded Act. By letter 23 June 1977, which was recorded as having been sent by certified mail (Exhibit T4/34), the Commissioner replied to Mr Eastman's request indicating that it had been decided that the certificate should remain in force in his case. A copy of the 'certificate' was enclosed specifying 'history of nervous disorders'. Mr Eastman, however, had no recollection of having received this letter or the attached certificate.

5. Mr Eastman claimed that it was not until the end of January 1978 that he had learned the reason for this non-acceptance into the Superannuation Fund. He said that he had understood the reference to 'history of nervous disorders' to mean a family history of nervous disorders because 'I personally had no history of nervous disorders proir to joining the Public Service' (Transcript p36). However, we are unable to accept Mr Eastman's evidence in this regard.

6. There is evidence before us that at the medical examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 1 March 1966 Mr Eastman had referred to an acute episode of depression in November 1965. Although the evidence before us in respect of this episode is less than satisfactory, the probabilities are that it was the disclosure of that episode of depression that led to the deferral of any decision in respect to Mr Eastman's entry into the Superannuation Fund or Provident Account. The decision as to deferral was clearly justified. In December 1966 Mr Eastman commenced consulting Dr W.A. McIlrath, a consultant psychiatrist then practising in Canberra - treatment that was necessitated, he said, by the 'pressures' of working in the Public Service. He has been receiving psychiatric treatment more or less continuously since that date.

7. As earlier mentioned, it was on 27 April 1967, following a further medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 29 March 1967, that a decision was finally taken to accept Mr Eastman into the Provident Account. In our view Mr Eastman must have realised from the outset and certainly by no later than the end of January 1978, that his acceptance into the Provident Account was primarily due to his own history of nervous disorder. He knew that he was on probation for an initial period of six months and that the probationary period had been extended. He claims not to have been notified of the eventual decision to accept him into the Provident Account, but whether that be so or not, he could not have been unaware of the significance of his having commenced psychiatric treatment in December 1966, some three to four months before the date of his final entry medical examination on 29 March 1967.

8. Mr Eastman said that despite the fact that he was receiving psychiatric treatment, he was able to continue working and to achieve a normal promotion rate (Transcript p53). Apart from a fortnight in 1973, he claimed not to have been off work for any period of time due to illness. However, on 30 June 1977 having completed just over 11 years service, Mr Eastman resigned from the Public Service. The precise circumstances surrounding Mr Eastman's retirement do not need to be explored in detail. Suffice it to say that the applicant felt that he had been victimised after he had been reprimanded by a senior officer for his conduct in relation to the performance of his duties.

9. On 30 August 1977, after what his psychiatrist Dr R.C. McDonald described as 'more sober reflection', the applicant wrote to the Commissioner for Superannuation requesting that his resignation be treated as retirement on the grounds of invalidity and that he be granted retirement benefits accordingly.

10. On 25 January 1978 a delegate of the Commissioner, being satisfied that at the time Mr Eastman ceased to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason of anxiety, depression and personality disorder, unfit to perform his duties, made the requested direction. However, pursuant to s.66(2) of the 1976 Act, the delegate of the Commissioner also formed the opinion that:

'... the conditions (namely Anxiety Depression and Personality Disorder) that have been accepted as the reason for Mr Eastman's being deemed to have been retired on 30 June 1977 on the ground of physical or mental incapacity were substantially contributed to by the condition (namely, History of Nervous Disorders) specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate deemed by virtue of the operation of section 184 of the Act to have been in force on the commencing day in respect of Mr Eastman and which was still in force immediately before Mr Eastman's deemed retirement on 30 June 1977.' (see Exhibit T29)

These decisions which were made on 25 January 1978 were notified to Mr Eastman under cover of a letter of that date.

11. Pausing there for a moment, it is appropriate to observe that the decisions that Mr Eastman now wishes the Commissioner to reconsider and which are the subject of the second application for review (A83/75) before the Tribunal are:

(i) the decision notified on 23 June 1977 refusing to revoke the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant; and

(ii) the decision made on 25 January 1978 pursuant to s.66(2) of the 1976 Act that the conditions (namely anxiety, depression and personality disorder) that had been accepted as the reason for Mr Eastman's being deemed to have retired on 30 June 1977 on the ground of physical or mental incapacity were substantially contributed to by the condition (namely history of nervous disorders) specified in the benefit classification certificate deemed by virture of the operation of s.184 of the Act to have been in force on the commencing day in respect of Mr Eastman and which was still in force immediately before Mr Eastman's deemed retirement on 30 June 1977.

12. Neither decision was challenged at the time (although, as earlier noted, Mr Eastman claims not to have received notice of the first decision until the end of January 1978). Pursuant to s.154(2) of the 1976 Act a person affected by a reviewable decision who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by notice in writing given to the Commissioner within the period of thirty days after the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision. It was not until 8 October 1982, that Mr Eastman first wrote to the Commissioner requesting reconsideration of the decision made on 25 January 1978 pursuant to s.66(2) of the 1976 Act. The Commissioner appears to have treated this request as extending as well to the decision taken on 23 June 1977 not to revoke the benefit classification certificate deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant. However, the Commissioner declined to extend the time in which to request reconsideration of either decision. We will return to these decisions later (see paragraph 43).

Cancellation of the Election for Lump Sum Benefit (A83/56)

13. Following the decision by a delegate of the Commissioner to deem Mr Eastman to have retired on the grounds of invalidity, the Commissioner wrote to Mr Eastman by letter dated 27 January 1978 explaining the options available to him as follows:

'The following are your options and the amounts of pension and lump sum involved:

(a) a pension of $6,628.55 per annum based on 70% of final salary reduced by 5% of pension for each year or part of a year that contributory service falls short of 20 years (for this option to apply you would need to refund the resignation benefit of $7,511.28 already paid to you); or

(b) a pension of $4,734.68 per annum based on 50% of final salary reduced by 5% of pension for each year or part of a year that contributory service falls short of 20 years plus a lump sum of $7,511.28 (an amount equal to the lump sum has already been paid to you); or

(c) a lump sum of $26,289.48 representing 3 1/2 times your accumulated basic contributions. This benefit would be reduced to $18,778.20 because of the $7,511.28 resignation benefit already paid to you.

2. In addition to the details regarding the options you asked me in your recent telephone conversation for information concerning the effect on your pension or the lump sum of your becoming employed in the Public Service or elsewhere.

3. Section 74 of the Superannuation Act, a copy of which is attached, provides for the review of invalidity pensions by the Commissioner. The review is generally in two parts:

(i) a review in relation to employment, and, if considered appropriate,

(ii) by a medical examination.

4. In the event that you should choose to have your benefit as a pension

under the options (a) or (b) above your pension would be subject to review from time to time under section 74. If you were to select option (c) above to receive a lump sum of 3> times your accumulated basic contributions the lump sum would be paid to you and there would be no future review.

5. If you select a pension and you were to become employed as a permanent employee with the Commonwealth or an approved authority of the Commonwealth, your pension would be cancelled and you would again be required to contribute to the superannuation scheme. If the level of salary were to be lower than the updated salary on which you were 'retired', a partial invalidity pension would be payable. If you had selected the option for a pension related to 50% of final salary plus a lump sum under (b) above, you would not be required to repay the accumulated contributions previously paid to you, but if you were to be subsequently retired on invalidity grounds, your entitlement would be a pension related to 50% of final salary plus a refund of accumulated contributions based on the contributions paid during the period following your re-employment.

6. If you were to become employed on a full-time basis as a temporary employee and were to be directed as an eligible employee by the Commissioner, the effect, as far as superannuation is concerned, would be as though you had been permanently appointed with results similar to those outlined in paragraph 5 above.

7. If you select the option for a lump sum of 3 1/2 times accumulated basic contributions as in (c) above, on appointment as a permanent employee you would be required to commence contributing as a new contributor. If you commenced as a temporary employee, you would be able to contribute as a new contributor after you had completed one year's service and provided you had three years' prospective service. In either case past service would not count as contributory service in assessing benefit.

8. If you select a pension and you become employed otherwise than with the Commonwealth or an Approved Authority of the Commonwealth or become employed in any part-time employment, such employment would be sufficient reason to initiate a review under the provisions of section 74 of the Act.

14. On 1 February 1978 Mr Eastman telephoned Mr Lorenz concerning this letter and asked, amongst other things, what period was allowed for him to make his election in the matter. Mr Lorenz informed Mr Eastman that, in the circumstances, a period of one month from the date of the letter (27 January 1978) would be allowed. By letter that was in fact dated 31 January 1978 but which was not received by the Commissioner until 2 February 1978 Mr Eastman wrote to the Commisioner as follows:

'I refer to your letters dated 25.1.78 and 27.1.78 (reference PA329266) advising me of the Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation' decision, and details of the options available to me.

I have decided to select the option '(c)' referred to in your letter of 27.1.78, that is to say a lump sum of 3 1/2 times accumulated basic contributions.'

15. The election to accept a lump sum benefit was made pursuant to s.72(2) of the 1976 Act. At the date when it was made the election was irrevocable. There was no power in the Act either for a person to request cancellation of an election or for the Commissioner to accede to such a request. However, by s.64 of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 ("the 1978 Act") which came into operation on 24 April 1978, s.157 of the 1976 Act was amended by adding the following sub-section:

'(3) Where -

(a) a person makes an election (whether before or after the commencement of this sub-section) under section 62,,64, 68, 69, 71, 72, 83, 84, 86, 87, 92 or 137;

(b) the person who made the election, or another person (being a person who is prescribed or included in a prescribed class of persons) makes an application to the Commissioner not later than 3 months after the day on which the election is made or the day on which this sub-section comes into operation, whichever is the later, or within such further period as the Commissioner, in special circumstances, allows, requesting that the Commisioner cancel the election; and

(c) the Commissioner, having regard to such matters (if any) as are prescribed and such other matters as he considers relevant, is satisfied that the election should be cancelled,

the Commissioner may direct that the election shall be cancelled and, if he so directs, this Act has effect as if the election had not been made.'

On 10 September 1980 Mr Eastman requested an extension of time within which to request that the Commissioner cancel his election. The Commissioner refused that request but on appeal to the Tribunal the Tribunal was satisfied that there were special circumstances justifying the extension of time and extended the time accordingly (see Re Eastman and Commissioner for Superannuation (1982) 4 ALN N110).

16. Following the Tribunal's decision, a delegate of the Commissioner considered whether to direct that the applicant's election be cancelled and on or about 15 September 1982 decided not to do so. Advice of that decision was conveyed to the applicant's then solicitors by letter dated 15 September 1982. On 8 October 1982 the applicant requested reconsideration of the decision, but on reconsideration the Commissioner decided to affirm the decision of his delegate. This is the decision the subject of the first application for review (A83/56).

17. Pursuant to the Superannuation (Cancellation of Elections) Regulations which are deemed to have taken effect from and including 24 April 1978 (see regulation 2) there are two prescribed matters for the purposes of paragraph 157(3)(c) of the Act. Regulation 5 provides as follows:

'5. For the purposes of paragraph 157(3)(c) of the Act, the following matters are matters to which the Commissioner shall have regard in determining whether an election referred to in paragraph 157(3)(a) of the Act should be cancelled:

(a) the availability of all information relevant to the making of the election to the person who made the election (including a person who, by virtue of sub-section 157(2) of the Act, is deemed to have made the election) and, if the election was made under sub-section 157(2) of the Act, also to the person whom the Commissioner, in pursuance of that sub-section, allowed to make the election; and

(b) the understanding that the person who made the election (including a person who, by virtue of sub-section 157(2) of the Act, is deemed to have made the election) and, if the election was made under sub-section 157(2) of the Act, also the person whom the Commissioner, in pursuance of that sub-section, allowed to make the election, had of the nature of the election and the consequences of making the election.'

18. With respect to the matters referred to in regulation 5(a), it was virtually conceded by Mr Johnstone, who appeared on behalf of the applicant, that the applicant did have available to him all information relevant to the making of his election on 31 January 1978. It was suggested that it should have been made clearer to the applicant that his prospects of re-employment in the Commonwealth Public Service may have been less if he accepted a lump sum payment rather than a pension, but we do not regard this allegation as substantiated. In our view, the information provided to the applicant by the letters from the Commissioner's Office dated 25 January 1978 and more particularly by the letter dated 27 January 1978 coupled with the very careful explanations provided by Mr Lorenz to the applicant during the course of telephone conversations between 26 January and 1 February 1978, satisfied the requirements of regulation 5(a).

19. With respect to the matters referred to in regulation 5(b), namely the understanding that Mr Eastman had 'of the nature of the election and of the consequences of making the election', the position is more complicated. If Mr Eastman can be judged by the standards of a normal, rational person, we again have no doubt from the documentary evidence before us, including the records of telephone conversations between Mr Lorenz and Mr Eastman, that Mr Eastman had a very clear understanding of the nature of the election that he in fact made and of the consequences of making that election. The evidence establishes beyond question that Mr Eastman chose option (c) very deliberately after careful consideration of the alternatives. His evidence was that, at the time that he retired he was not giving any thought to returning to the Public Service. He thought that he had excellent prospects of obtaining a job in private enterprise. He was concerned that if he had opted for a pension and was successful in obtaining work outside the Service, his pension would be cancelled. By taking the lump sum he could not be required to repay in the event (as he thought likely) that he found another job (Transcript pp30-31).

20. In his evidence before the Tribunal, Mr Eastman acknowledged that by August 1977 when he requested that his retirement be treated as an invalidity retirement, he had developed some doubts about his prospects of re-employment. He added, however (Transcript pp43-4):

'But the doubts were very slight at that stage. I did not accept and for that matter do not accept still that I was suffering from any real mental illness and therefore I thought that my employment prospects were basically good.' (emphasis added)

21. The fact is that Mr Eastman has not been able to obtain permanent employment since the date of his deemed retirement from the Public Service. He attempted, unsuccessfully, to work as a real estate salesman but had difficulty in his relationships with people. He was employed for a few weeks as a temporary research assistant at the ANU but was dismissed, he said, because of a clash with a work colleague. He was employed for four months (his longest period of employment) in the Queensland Public Service. Again, there was a personality clash that led to his resignation. Mr Eastman described the situation as follows:

'The problem was that this person was difficult and aggressive and argumentative but the way everyone else in the office saw it it was my fault and that was the reason why I would have been dismissed if I had not resigned.'

In addition, Mr Eastman had obtained some temporary employment of a clerical assistant type in the Public Service in Canberra. There had been three such jobs, he said. The first one ran its normal course. The second was for a month and he resigned after a fortnight following an argument with a work colleague. The third one was for a month and it terminated two days before the end of the month for similar reasons.

22. The difficulties that Mr Eastman has experienced in finding permanent employment are exactly as predicted by Dr R.C. McDonald who was the applicant's treating psychiatrist for many years but who is now unfortunately deceased. The transcript of Dr McDonald's evidence at the earlier hearing before the Tribunal on 17 November 1981 (in connection with the application by Mr Eastman for an extension of time within which to request cancellation of his election) was tendered by consent as an Exhibit in the present case. The main thrust of Dr McDonald's evidence is conveniently summarised in paragraphs 21 and 22 in the report of the decision in that case (4 ALN N110 at 111-2):

'21. ... According to Dr McDonald the applicant suffers from an unusual condition of paranoia, the characteristic manifestations of which include delusions of grandeur, a persecution complex, and a diminished capacity for successful inter-personal relationships. Such a person has a false view of himself; and of himself in relation to the world, and of his capacity to make decisions. Whilst Dr McDonald considered the applicant to be of well above average intelligence and well above average capability, he said that the logical process in an intelligent paranoid mind is apt to fail. If he is dealing with impersonal material, he can reason rationally. Once the material to which he is applying his reasoning process involves inter-personal relationships or material that relates to his personal security, then the irrationality moves in (transcript 45). Whilst such a person can, in calmer moments, see the irrationality of what he has done in the past, he cannot structure his own future in a rational way, based on rational predictions of possible outcomes. With such a person it is extremely difficult to fault his logic until one goes back to the basic postulate on which the logical process is founded.

22. In the present case, Dr McDonald said that the basic postulate from which the applicant appears to have reasoned that it would be in his best interests to accept a lump sum payment, was that he would have no difficulty in finding alternative employment whether in private enterprise or in the Public Service. In Dr McDonald's view, that basic postulate was, and was always in his view likely to be, tragically wrong as borne out by the applicant's subsequent unsuccessful efforts to obtain employment. In Dr McDonald's view, the applicant's paranoia is such that he will not be able to hold down a long term job in the future. For this reason he had strongly advised the applicant against accepting the option of a lump sum payment at the time. It was typical of his paranoia that the applicant was not prepared to accept that advice. Asked if he could say whether, in late January 1978 when the applicant made his election, he was then capable of making a calmly reasoned decision, Dr McDonald frankly acknowledged the difficulty of the question. The substance of his answer was as follows:

'I cannot really say specifically yes or no, I can only say on the balance of probability, knowing the make up of the man, that his judgment can go askew at the drop of a hat but the odds were that it was not really a rationally thought out decision on his part.' (transcript 46)"

23. Reference to the transcript establishes that Mr Eastman first consulted Dr McDonald on 3 January 1974. He took over the management of Mr Eastman's treatment from Dr McIlrath. Dr McDonald said that he had seen Mr Eastman in the intervening years 'regularly over certain periods, then spasmodically, then regularly practically every year since 1974'. Paranoia, Dr McDonald said, is a very difficult condition to explain. The important thing about Mr Eastman, he said, was that he had a well-educated mind. When he is in a quiet state he is well above average intelligence and well above average capability and quite a pleasant man. 'However, when anything touches on the area of his paranoid preoccupation he becomes quite irrational or very aggressive, very unpleasant and this has been the cause of him losing many jobs ... One only has to say something in a critical tone of voice to trigger off his paranoid hostility, irritability, and especially if it comes from a woman'.

24. Dr McDonald referred to the job Mr Eastman had obtained at the ANU. It was a temporary job, he said, which was part-time, suited his academic qualifications very well and a lot of the work could be done at home. One day, however, Mr Eastman had overheard two female employees talking. He immediately jumped to the conclusion that they were talking about him and became extremely hostile. The women became frightened and Mr Eastman was dismissed. Dr McDonald also referred to the unsuccessful attempts Mr Eastman had made to work in the real estate industry. He added 'this is really the tragedy of Mr Eastman. He is capable of working and producing work of good quality, he just cannot get along with people'.

25. Dr McDonald had said that when Mr Eastman was attempting to gain re-entry to the Public Service he had approached him (Dr McDonald) to support his application. Dr McDonald had refused whereupon Mr Eastman left in a state of acute agitation and had visited every other psychiatrist in town to obtain a report. Dr McDonald added that:

'they found him abrasive, they would not have anything to do with him. The appeal for re-entry failed and eventually he came back to see me. The one thing about the paranoid is that they do to a certain extent have some insight into the fact that there is something wrong. But this is usually an insight in retrospect. They act on impulse and it is uncontrollable impulse '.

26. Dr McDonald described the breakdown of Mr Eastman's relationship with his parents as follows:

'The mother was terrified, eventually I had to advise Mr Eastman that he must discontinue his contacts with his family altogether because they were always afraid of actual violence. And I can understand the women in the offices the places where he was employed. He can be a very aggressive man and a very terrifying man, not that I think he would really do any damage to anybody but he is very threatening. Even my secretary ... was quite terrified, she would not stay in the office for his visit. She would make some excuse to go out to the powder room and he would just sit and wait.'

27. Dr McDonald described the situation after Mr Eastman's retirement as follows:

'After he had settled down and had been accepted for retirement he found that he had nothing to do. He went after job after job after job. He wrote to places in Sydney, I think Melbourne, Adelaide. He felt it would be better if he got out of Canberra because nobody really wanted to know him - this is the way he saw it. But he always believed that he would work again, that he could work again, that he had put all these years into the studying and getting his degree. I tried to convince him that it was highly unlikely that he would ever hold down a job and I went over the difficulties he has in personal relationships. Anyway, despite what I said he was quite convinced that he would work again and the whole thing about it was that by taking the lump sum that he could have a period off, he could establish his security and then he would go back to work. Now, I tried to persuade him that this would not happen, that he might have short term jobs, he might have temporary positions, but that frankly he would not last because friction would develop, the people would not want him around, they would make this clear, he would become more hostile and eventually either he would have to be fired or he would have to leave. But all the way along the line he went on believing that he would work again, that he would utilise -

Is that a feature of a paranoid personality that he would have such a false view of himself and himself in relation to the world and his own capacity to make decisions?-Yes, yes that is quite characteristic. The whole basis of paranoia at a phenomenological level is the fear - the fear of encroachment by everything and anybody around one, and that the only person one can rely on is oneself. So one makes a judgment and then if anybody opposes this then they must rigidly stick to that because the other person is persecuting them in some way. There is also the sort of - the two main features of paranoia are the ideas and illusions of persecution and grandiosity. The two go together. And it is this grandiosity which would make him believe that - well, which has led him to pursue all these intricate things for which I have had to write reports about. He is still convinced in his own mind that he will get back into the public service and without wishing to be facetious he is likely to do it, even against my opinion and I think it would be tragic - I think it would be tragic for him and it would be extremely difficult for whatever department he went into because at interview he would be perfectly calm, self-possessed, etcetera. But within a couple of weeks he will have got these ideas about people, especially women."

28. When asked to explain what difference it would have made to Mr Eastman if he had opted for the regular pension rather than the lump sum payment, Dr McDonald replied:

'It would not do anything for his mental condition. Nothing is going to change that. He would just have the basic security. I am still not convinced that he believes that he will not get back into the public service and that he will not get a job. He is a very presentable young man and he will get a job but he will not keep a job. It is my opinion that he will never earn a living wage over a sustained period of time. It sounds probably an extreme statement to make when you saw the man who was sitting there and walked out there, he is neatly groomed, he is well-spoken, he is intelligent and you would think that there is nothing the matter with him, that he should be able to get a job and work.h He can get a job all right and he can work, but he cannot hold the job and it is because of the paranoia ... There is another paranoid element too. He sat down with a pencil and paper and worked out that if he had had regular income by now he would have received in that regular income as much as he got in the lump sum but if he had taken the regular income it would still be going on. So he feels that, not only did he make the wrong decision but virtually he was forced into making the wrong decision - despite the fact that I had advised him not to take the lump sum.'

Dr McDonald's evidence continued (Transcript p35):

'He has no capacity for judgment as concerns the projection of some decision into the future and with its future consequences and that is the important thing about this particular situation.'

29. Dr McDonald acknowledged that by January 1978 the applicant was thinking more clearly and was no longer under stress. Asked whether he would expect Mr Eastman to have been able to make a reasonable decision at that point of time, Dr McDonald replied (Transcript p40):

'I know that that is the central point of this whole issue and it is a very difficult question to answer. ... I would like to know what he is thinking even now about what is happening in here and I would say that the paranoid strain is there and could be provoked at any time. So that really I cannot say specifically yes or no, my guess would be something along these lines. This is what they want me to do, this is how it is set out. From my pragmatic point of view this is the best thing to do but I can go on and get another job. I will have all this money, I will have the security, I will still be able to do the job etc. That sort of train can go on and this is why it is always difficult to explain to anybody what actually happens in the mind of the paranoid. It is a split, it functions at two levels and it functions at one level which can appear completely normal.'

Later in evidence Dr McDonald said (Transcript p41) 'I cannot really know what actually goes on in the second level of this man's mind'.

30. Dr McDonald said that with an intelligent paranoid it is extremely difficult to fault his logic until one goes back to the basic postulate on which that logical process is founded. The whole thing about paranoia, he said, is that the function of the mind is not fragmented. Whilst Mr Eastman was properly characterised as 'psychotic', a condition which in his opinion would never change, the difference between his psychosis, namely paranoia and other psychoses, is that the mind does not fragment. However, in his opinion, despite the applicant's intelligence, 'he really cannot structure his own future in a rational way, based on rational prediction of possible outcomes' (Transcript p36).

31. Evidence was also given at the hearing by Dr B.J.W. Hughson who was called on behalf of the respondent. Dr Hughson had been consulted by Mr Eastman on two occasions, the first being on 25 January 1979 and the second on 10 April 1980. The first consultation was on referral by Mr Eastman's treating general practitioner at Mr Eastman's request with a view to Mr Eastman obtaining a medical certificate certifying that he was fit for re-employment in the Public Service. Mr Eastman had told Dr Hughson of his past history but had also said that he had been free of symptoms for some months. This information was confirmed in a covering letter from Mr Eastman's general practitioner. Dr Hughson said that although the history given by the applicant indicated difficulties related to his personality, he had nevertheless given him a certificate saying that he 'seemed to be as fit for employment as he had been when initially employed' or words to that effect (Transcript p11). The second consultation in April 1980 had been at the request of the Deputy Crown Solicitor for the purposes of Mr Eastman's claim for employee's compensation against the Commonwealth.

32. Dr Hughson said that in January 1979 Mr Eastman gave no account of active symptoms at that time. He acknowledged a personal propensity to be mistrustful of others, a tendency to have a chip of his shoulder and a tendency to respond if he felt that he had been mistreated. Dr Hughson had regarded this as a personality problem of long standing. Asked whether he would characterise Mr Eastman's personality disorder as a paranoid personality Dr Hughson replied (Transcript p12):

'A paranoid personality is somebody who is mistrustful and tends to see the responsibility for things that go wrong as resting with other people and to that extent 'Yes'.'

33. Dr Hughson said that in major psychotic illness the intellectual process is disordered, fragmented and often bizarre but in a paranoid personality disorder that does not apply. The capacity of a person with a paranoid personality to think logically is not impaired, but he acknowledged that the capacity for judgment as to the future consequences of a decision may be affected. Dr Hughson said that Mr Eastman's fixed personality traits led him into frequent situations of interpersonal conflict producing anxiety or depression. If a person is mistrustful and frequently tends to blame others, that type of conduct leads to rejection and to hostility on the part of others which escalates into personal conflicts.

34. Dr Hughson did not agree with Dr McDonald's opinion that Mr Eastman was a paranoid psychotic. He said that on neither of the occasions that he had seen Mr Eastman was he psychotic. Mr Eastman had not given any history that suggested that he had ever been psychotic nor did the medication prescribed by Dr McDonald suggest a diagnosis of psychosis.

35. However, Dr Hughson did not have the advantage of knowing the full extent of the evidence that Dr McDonald had given at the Tribunal hearing on 17 November 1981 - in particular, Dr McDonald's opinion as to the unusual nature of the paranoia from which Mr Eastman suffers (see paragraph 22). It is no disrespect to Dr Hughson to observe that, in contrast with Dr McDonald, he has not at any stage had the responsibility for Mr Eastman's treatment and that both consultations were for rather specialised and limited purposes. In the first consultation, in particular, Mr Eastman was no doubt presenting himself in the best possible light. To the extent, therefore, that there is any inconsistency between the evidence of Dr Hughson and that of Dr McDonald we prefer the evidence of Dr McDonald who has had the opportunity to observe Mr Eastman over a period of many years as his treating psychiatrist and whose evidence appears to us to exhibit a remarkable degree of insight into and understanding of the workings of Mr Eastman's complex mind.

36. Dr McDonald's evidence persuades us that, despite the appearance of rational judgment presented by Mr Eastman at the time when he made his election for a lump sum benefit on 31 January 1978, Mr Eastman's judgment was affected by the condition of paranoia which had precipitated the circumstances leading to his invalidity retirement. In our view the probabilities are, as Dr McDonald said, that Mr Eastman's whole process of reasoning as to why he should opt for a lump sum payment rather than a pension was based on the completely false premise that there was nothing wrong with him mentally and that, having regard to his qualifications and experience, he would have no difficulty whatsoever in finding and holding secure employment in the private sector. Dr McDonald's opinion that the applicant would be unable, by reason of his mental state, to hold down a job for more than a short time has, we think, been completely vindicated by the difficulties that Mr Eastman has since experienced in that regard. The probabilities are, as Dr McDonald said, that whilst Mr Eastman may be able to find employment from time to time in the future, he will have great difficulty in holding down a permanent job.

37. The Commissioner for Superannuation had not been prepared to judge Mr Eastman's actions otherwise than against normal mental standards. He did not fully accept Dr McDonald's opinions and was critical of his evidence in a number of respects. The Commissioner placed considerable reliance on Dr McDonald's statements to the effect that in calmer moments when there is no pressure on him, Mr Eastman understands things more clearly than when under pressure. The Commissioner took the view that by January 1978 Mr Eastman was no longer under the stress of his work situation and that in contrast to the circumstances in which he resigned, somewhat impetuously, under a situation of personal stress, Mr Eastman was exhibiting all the appearances of rationality and a capacity to make a carefully reasoned judgment by January 1978.

38. In our view however, for the reasons we have given, we do not consider that Mr Eastman can be judged by the standards of a rational, normal person. We accept Dr McDonald's opinion that the applicant lacks the capacity to structure his own future in a rational way, based on rational predictions of possible outcomes. We do not agree with the Commissioner that this is a case of a man who has made a carefully considered choice which turns out to be the wrong choice in retrospect. It is a case of a man whose mental condition had a critical influence on his making a choice against the advice of his treating psychiatrist - a choice that, on a proper understanding of Mr Eastman's medical condition in January 1978, was clearly not appropriate for a man who was never likely to find and hold secure employment in the future. Although he had a month in which to make his election, Mr Eastman appears to have acted under self-generated pressures to reach a decision as quickly as possible after receipt of the Commissioner's letter dated 27 January 1978. That is precisely the type of environment in which, according to Dr McDonald, Mr Eastman's judgment was likely to go astray.

39. Thus, notwithstanding that Mr Eastman had available to him all information relevant to the making of his election and notwithstanding that he appeared to have a proper understanding of the nature of the election and the consequences of making that election, we have concluded that by reason of his mental condition his capacity to make a proper judgment as to the consequences of electing to take a lump sum was seriously impaired by reason of the very mental condition that had led to his early retirement.

40. In our view these very unusual medical circumstances are a relevant consideration when it comes to exercising the discretion given by s.157(3) of the Act as to whether or not to direct that Mr Eastman's election should be cancelled. However they are not the only considerations. The fact is that Mr Eastman has, by reason of the election that he in fact made, had access to a substantial lump sum which, according to his evidence in the earlier proceedings, was invested initially in debentures and other interest bearing deposits and ultimately in a home unit. Mr Eastman has had the benefit over the past six years of that lump sum payment whereas, based on the figures advised to him on 27 January 1978, his maximum entitlement in terms of pension would have been initially $6,628.55 per annum. Undoubtedly, this would place him in a preferred position to that which he would have enjoyed had he elected at the outset for one of the pension options. However, it was not submitted to us on behalf of the Commissioner that the problems of making financial adjustments at this stage would be insuperable.

41. We have also had regard to the fact that it was not until 10 September 1980 that Mr Eastman made his request that the Commissioner cancel the election that he had made on 31 January 1978. However the reasons for the delays in this regard are adequately canvassed in the Tribunal's decision on the Extension of Time application (4 ALN N110, see paragraph 23 of the Reasons for Decision), and in the very unusual circumstances of this case we do not consider that the delay in making the request for cancellation to be of overriding significance. The fact that it is now some 3 1/2 7years since Mr Eastman first requested to have his election cancelled is no fault of his and we therefore put that consideration aside.

42. This case undoubtedly presents considerable difficulty. The critical difference between the view that we have formed and that of the Commissioner is in the assessment of the ongoing impairment of the applicant's capacity for judgment by reason of his mental condition. However, given the beneficial purpose for which the superannatuion scheme was established and having paid due regard to the prescribed matters and to the other matters that we consider to be relevant, we are satisfied, for the reasons we have given, that the election should be cancelled and we propose to direct therefor that the election be cancelled accordingly.

The Request to Extend the Time for Reconsideration (A83/75)

43. We are not prepared however to accede to Mr Eastman's application for an extension of time within which to request the Commissioner to reconsider the decisions taken on 23 June 1977 and 25 January 1978 respectively relating to the applicant's benefit classification certificate and limited retirement benefits. Mr Johnstone submitted on behalf of the applicant that all the grounds which had influenced the Tribunal in the earlier case to extend the time within which a request for cancellation of the applicant's election could be made were equally applicable to the present case. In our view, however, the circumstances in the present case are very different.

44. When the applicant made his election for a lump sum benefit under s.72 of the 1976 Act, the law as it then stood made the election irrevocable. Section 157(3), which allowed for cancellation of an election, was not introduced until some months later and it was a considerable time thereafter before the applicant became aware of the amendment. By contrast, the decisions now sought to be reconsidered are decisions to which s.154 of the Act has always applied. There is no evidence in the present case that the applicant was unaware of those provisions. On the contrary, he had applied for reconsideration of another decision of the Commissioner in November 1980.

45. In our view the real reason for the applicant not having sought review of either decision was that in his moments of greater insight he has recognised that he does have a psychiatric problem. It is significant in this regard that during the hearing before the Tribunal on 17 November 1981 there was no question at all that the decisions with respect to invalidity retirement were other than correct.

46. Given his medical history prior to the date on which he was accepted to the Provident Account, the psychiatric treatment that he was receiving throughout his employment in the Public Service and since his retirement, and the grounds upon which he was ultimately accepted as having been deemed to retire on the grounds of invalidity, we do not believe that it occurred to Mr Eastman to challenge the correctness of those decisions. As earlier indicated, we do not accept that Mr Eastman could have been unaware of the significance of his own history of psychiatric treatment in the decision that was ultimately taken to accept him into the Provident Account. His attempt to suggest that he had misunderstood 'History of nervous disorder' as meaning 'Family history of nervous disorder' can be understood, having regard to the mental condition from which he suffers, but we cannot accept the suggestion as seriously arguable. There is nothing in either the documentary evidence or in the evidence given by Dr McDonald or Dr Hughson to give any grounds for believing that the decisions taken by the Commissioner in respect of invalidity retirement were wrong.

47. In Re Schmack and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1981) 3 ALN N77, the Tribunal said, in respect of a provision similar to s.154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976, that the power to extend the time for reconsideration of a decision allowed a wide discretion. The Tribunal referred in its decision to some of the factors which ought to be taken into account when exercising the discretion as to extension of time. We have already canvassed some of those issues in what we have said. In our view, however, there is no proper basis for exercising the discretion in favour of the applicant in respect of decisions so long past and for so long accepted by Mr Eastman as unchallengeable. Critical evidence from Dr McDonald may now be unavailable by reason of his death. Dr McIlrath no longer has his records with respect to Mr Eastman and it is far from clear whether those records would now be available. Most importantly, however, the justice and merits of the case do not, in our view, require that the earlier decisions should now be re-opened.

48. In the circumstances we would propose therefore to affirm the Commissioner's decision refusing to reconsider the decisions with respect to the benefit classification certificate and the invalidity benefits payable on retirement.
