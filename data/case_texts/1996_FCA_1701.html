Peta Anne Fraser v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and Official Trustee [1996] FCA 1701 (14 August 1996)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

PETA ANNE FRASER v. DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION AND

OFFICIAL TRUSTEE

No. G051 of 1996

FED No. 708/96

Number of pages - 22

Bankruptcy - Precedents

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

BLACK CJ(1), BEAUMONT(2) AND TAMBERLIN(3) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - prohibition upon creditors enforcing remedies against bankrupts under s.58(3)(a) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 - application by Deputy Commissioner of Taxation under s.79A of the Family Law Act 1975 to set aside property settlement orders made by consent in the Family Court - whether leave of the Federal Court to commence proceedings in Family Court required under s.58(3)(b) of the Bankruptcy Act - bar established by s.58(3)(a) of the Bankruptcy Act extends only to "enforcement" of legal proceedings - institution and maintenance of legal proceedings up to the point of recovery of judgment unaffected by s.58(3)(a) - application under s.79A of the Family Law Act regarded as a legal proceeding

Re McMaster; Ex parte McMaster [1991] FCA 598; (1991) 105 ALR 156 considered

Re White (1960) 20 ABC 11 distinguished

Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 44; (1984) 154 CLR 589 considered

Re Stewart and Secretary, Department of Social Security (1985) 4 AAR 95
considered

Bankruptcy - whether legal proceedings against bankrupt commenced "in respect of a provable debt" under s.58(3)(b) of the Bankruptcy Act - indirect connection between proceedings and debt may be sufficient - Deputy Commissioner possesses unsatisfied debt - objective of bankruptcy legislation is equitable distribution of property amongst all unsecured creditors - paragraph should be interpreted so as to give practical effect to legislative purpose - proceedings commenced in respect of the debt

Bankruptcy - leave to commence proceedings against bankrupt under s.58(3)(b) of the Bankruptcy Act - application under s.79A of the Family Law Act instituted prior to sequestration and part-heard - possible prejudice to Deputy Commissioner and creditors if proceedings stayed - premature for Federal Court to address issue of standing under s.79A of the Family Law Act until all evidence is in - issue of standing properly left to be determined by Family Court

Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Spanjich 88 ATC 4985 followed

Precedents - Federal Court should act consistently with authority of the Family Court on the approach to be taken to the question of standing under s.79A of the Family Law Act - intermediate appellate courts should ordinarily follow the authority of other intermediate appellate courts on the interpretation of Commonwealth legislation - specialist nature of the Family Court's jurisdiction an added reason for Federal Court not acting inconsistently with Family Court authority on s.79A of the Family Law Act

Australian Securities Commission v Marlborough Gold Mines Ltd [1993] HCA 15; (1993) 177 CLR 485 2.

Words and Phrases - "enforcement" - does not refer to the institution of legal proceedings - Deputy Commissioner of Taxation to set aside property settlement orders made by consent in the Family Court

R v Bates (1982) 2 NSWLR 894 considered

Words and Phrases - "in respect of" - connection to subject matter which is rational and discernible may be indirect

Technical Products Pty. Ltd. v State Government Insurance (Queensland) [1989] HCA 24; (1989) 167 CLR 45 considered

Re Lattouf [1994] FCA 1342; (1994) 52 FCR 147 considered

HEARING

SYDNEY

14:8:1996

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

The appeal be dismissed with costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

BLACK CJ I agree with Beaumont J that this appeal should be dismissed with costs.

2. I agree generally with his Honour's reasons but would add some observations about the appellant's submission that Davies J ought not to have granted leave pursuant to s58(3)(b) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 to commence proceedings because, as was submitted, the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation did not have standing to continue his proceeding in the Family Court. Davies J considered that questions of standing were matters for the Family Court rather than for this Court.

3. In Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Spanjich (1988) 88 ATC 4985 a Full Court of the Family Court gave a broad construction to the expression "a person affected" in s79A(1) of the Family Law Act 1975 and one that, at least ordinarily, has the result that questions of standing should be determined at the same time as the other issues in a proceeding under s79A. It would have been quite inconsistent with the approach taken by the Full Court of the Family Court to this important provision in the Family Law Act if Davies J had determined the question of standing on an application for leave to proceed under s58(3)(b) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966.

4. It would be wrong of this Court to act inconsistently with the approach taken by the Family Court to such a question. It is of course well established that an intermediate appellate court should ordinarily follow the decision of another intermediate appellate court on the interpretation of Commonwealth legislation: see, for example: Zibillari v The Queen (1980) 50 FLR 274 at 285 per Brinsden J; The Queen v Daher (1981) 57 FLR 467 at 471 per Street CJ; Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Access Finance Corporation Pty Ltd (1987) 8 NSWLR 557 at 558 per Samuels JA. Similarly, the importance of uniformity of decision in the interpretation of uniform national legislation was stressed recently by the High Court in Australian Securities Commission v Marlborough Gold Mines Ltd [1993] HCA 15; (1993) 177 CLR 485. In that case, Mason CJ, Brennan, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ said, at 492:

"Although the considerations applying are somewhat different from

those applying in the case of Commonwealth legislation, uniformity

of decision in the interpretation of uniform national legislation

such as the (Corporations Law) is a sufficiently important

consideration to require that an intermediate appellate court -

and all the more so a single judge - should not depart from an

interpretation placed on such legislation by another Australian

intermediate appellate court unless convinced that interpretation

is plainly wrong."

See also Trans Pacific Investment Corporation Pty Ltd v Rusty Rees Pty Ltd [1995] FCA 1223; (1995) 57 FCR 210 at 214.

5. The specialist jurisdiction of the Family Court in family law matters provides an added reason why Davies J was correct in not acting inconsistently with the approach taken by the Family Court to the question of standing in a proceeding under s79A of the Family Law Act.

6. In any case, as a matter of general principle, a court has a discretion whether it should, or should not, determine whether an applicant has a sufficient interest to bring proceedings before it proceeds to determine the merits of a case: see Onus v Alcoa of Australia Ltd [1981] HCA 50; (1981) 149 CLR 27 at 38 per Gibbs CJ and Robinson v Western Australian Museum (1977) 138 CLR 283 at 302-303 per Gibbs CJ.

INTRODUCTION

BEAUMONT J This is an appeal from orders made by a Judge of the Court (Davies J) in proceedings in the bankruptcy jurisdiction in which orders, including declaratory orders, were sought by the appellant, Peta Anne Fraser, the former wife of Bruce Michael McCauley, now a bankrupt, with a view to the termination of certain litigation in the Family Court. It will be necessary, later, to describe the background to the litigation more fully, but in the Family Court, the first respondent, the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, sought to have set aside certain orders made, by consent of the parties to the marriage, in the Family Court.

2. In her application in this Court, Mrs. Fraser contended, inter alia, that the Deputy Commissioner lacked standing in the Family Court; and that his application in that Court could not be maintained without the leave of this Court under s.58(3) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 ("the Act"). She sought, inter alia, an injunction under s.60(1)(b) of the Act restraining the Deputy Commissioner from prosecuting his Family Court application. For his part, the Deputy Commissioner sought from this Court a declaration that he could maintain his application in the Family Court without seeking the leave of this Court under s.58(3) of the Act; and alternatively if, contrary to his contention, it be held that leave were required, that this Court then grant that leave.

3. By s.58(1) of the Act, it is provided relevantly that, subject to the Act, upon bankruptcy, the property of the bankrupt (not being after-acquired property) vests forthwith in the Official Trustee; and that after-acquired property of the bankrupt vests (as soon as it is acquired by, or devolves on, the bankrupt) in the Official Trustee.

4. Section 58(3) states:

"58(3). Except as provided by this Act, after a debtor has become

a bankrupt, it is not competent for a creditor:

(a) to enforce any remedy against the person or the property of

the bankrupt in respect of a provable debt; or

(b) except with the leave of the Court and on such terms as the

Court thinks fit, to commence any legal proceeding in respect of a

provable debt or take any fresh step in such a proceeding."

5. By s.60(1)(b) of the Act, it is provided that the Court may, at any time after the presentation of a petition, upon such terms and conditions as it thinks fit, stay any legal process, civil or criminal, against the person or property of the debtor.

6. Although Davies J doubted whether it was necessary, his Honour ordered that conditional leave to proceed under s.58(3)(b) be granted and declined to order a stay under s.60(1). Mrs. Fraser now appeals from that order.

7. In order to understand the issues on the appeal, reference should be made to some of the background circumstances.

BACKGROUND

(a) The alleged understatement by Mr. McCauley of his income during the period 1980-1985 for tax purposes

8. The Deputy Commissioner alleges that in respect of the years 1980-1985, Mr. McCauley substantially understated his income for tax purposes. In February 1992, the Deputy Commissioner issued amended Notices of Assessment which assessed tax in respect of those six years. In July 1992, in proceedings instituted in the Supreme Court of New South Wales, the Deputy Commissioner recovered judgment against Mr. McCauley in the sum of $8,301,358.78, with costs. The judgment has not been satisfied.

(b) The orders made by consent in the Family Court in 1990 under s.79(1) of the Family Law Act

9. On 3 April 1990, Mr. McCauley and Mrs. Fraser (then, of course, Mrs. McCauley) applied jointly to the Family Court for the making of orders under s.79(1) of the Family Law Act 1975. Under that provision (which is contained in Part VIII of the Act, dealing with property, spousal maintenance and maintenance agreements), in proceedings with respect to the property of the parties to a marriage, or either of them, the Court may make such order as it considers appropriate altering the interests of the parties in the property, including an order for a settlement of property in substitution for any interest in the property, and including an order requiring either or both of the parties to make, for the benefit of either or both of the parties or a child of the marriage, such settlement or transfer of property as the Court determines. By s.79(2), it is provided that the Court shall not make an order under s.79 unless it is satisfied that, in all the circumstances, it is just and equitable to make the order.

10. The parties' joint application under s.79(1) was supported by "minutes of notation and consent orders" dated 30 March 1990, signed by the parties to the marriage. The minutes recited details of the marriage, of the children of the marriage, and of the history of the acquisition of several assets by the parties to the marriage, or by family companies, including Logan Park Investments Pty. Ltd. ("Logan"). It was recited that Mr. McCauley (as to 80%) and Mrs. Fraser (as to 20%) held the shares in the capital of Logan, and that Logan had acquired several properties, including five terrace houses in South Dowling Street, South Sydney, and the matrimonial home, apparently a valuable property in Bellevue Hill. Details were also recited of the acquisition of other properties by Logan, and by other family companies. The minutes made provision, inter alia, for orders as follows:

"12. That the Husband the Wife and Logan Park on or before 31st

May, 1990 do all acts and things and execute such deeds,

documents, instruments and writings necessary to assign to the

Wife all right title and interest in the South Dowling Street

Properties and the Bellevue Hill Property."

11. On 3 April 1990, the Family Court made orders in accordance with these minutes. It is common ground that, accordingly, Logan then transferred these assets to Mrs. Fraser.

(c) The application to the Family Court under s.79A of the Family Law Act made in December 1992 by the Deputy Commissioner seeking to set aside the consent orders

12. Section 79A(1) of the Family Law Act provides:

"79A. (1) Where, on application by a person affected by an order

made by a court under section 79 in proceedings with respect to

the property of the parties to a marriage or either of them, the

court is satisfied that:

(a) there has been a miscarriage of justice by reason of fraud,

duress, suppression of evidence, the giving of false evidence or

any other circumstance;

(b) in the circumstances that have arisen since the order was

made it is impracticable for the order to be carried out or

impracticable for a part of the order to be carried out;

(c) a person has defaulted in carrying out an obligation imposed

on him by the order and, in the circumstances that have arisen as

a result of that default, it is just and equitable to vary the

order or to set the order aside and make another order in

substitution for the order; or

(d) in the circumstances that have arisen since the making of

the order, being circumstances of an exceptional nature relating

to the welfare of a child of the marriage, the child or, where the

applicant has the custody of the child, the applicant, will suffer

hardship if the court does not vary the order or set the order

aside and make another order in substitution for the order;

the court may, in its discretion, vary the order or set the order

aside and, if it considers appropriate, make another order under

section 79 in substitution for the order so set aside."

13. By s.79A(2), it is provided that in the exercise of the powers under (inter alia) s.79A(1), the Court shall have regard to the interests of, and shall make any order proper for the protection of, a bona fide purchaser or other person interested.

14. By his amended initiating application in the Family Court dated 17 March 1993, the Deputy Commissioner sought final orders as follows:

"(1) That order 1 made by the court on 3 April 1990, (being the

order contemplated by the minutes) be vacated.

(2) Such further or other orders as the court deems fit. ..."

The orders were sought on the grounds that in the circumstances then pleaded by the Deputy Commissioner -

"27.1 the Husband and the Wife were guilty of 'fraud' and

'suppression of evidence' (within the meaning of Section 79A of

the Family Court Act), in obtaining the subject orders and

27.2 there has been a mis-carriage of justice in the making of the

said orders, within the meaning of Section 79A, by reason of the

said fraud and suppression of evidence."

The Deputy Commissioner's application first came before Cohen J in the Family Court in December 1994. The hearing continued in May 1995, although in the meantime, as will be seen, Mr. McCauley's estate was sequestrated.

(d) Mr. McCauley made bankrupt in March 1995

15. On 9 March 1995, Mr. McCauley was made bankrupt on the petition of the Deputy Commissioner.

(e) The ruling by Cohen J in May 1995 that the leave of this Court under s.58(3) was required

16. On 30 May 1995, Cohen J adjourned the further hearing of the Deputy Commissioner's application, holding that s.58(3)(b) applied and that the leave of this Court was required before the Family Court proceedings could continue further. In so holding, Cohen J referred particularly to the decision of Hill J in this area in Re McMaster; Ex parte McMaster [1991] FCA 598; (1991) 105 ALR 156, which will be discussed later.

THE PROCEEDINGS BEFORE DAVIES J

17. As has been said, there were several applications before the learned primary Judge, as follows:

(1) The Deputy Commissioner sought a declaration that leave under

s.58(3)(b) was not necessary, or, if it was required, that leave

be granted.

(2) Mrs. Fraser applied for (a) a declaration that neither the

Deputy Commissioner nor the Official Trustee was competent to

continue the proceedings under s.79A; (b) an injunction

restraining the Deputy Commissioner and the Official Trustee from

continuing those proceedings; and (c) a stay of those proceedings

pursuant to s.60(1)(b) of the Act.

18. The Official Trustee appeared in the proceedings by counsel as an intervening party.

19. Davies J held that the absolute bar imposed by s.58(3)(a) was not applicable; and that, assuming that leave to proceed was required by virtue of the provisions of s.58(3)(b), it ought to be granted, but on conditions; and that no stay under s.60(1) should be granted, for the following reasons.

THE REASONING AT FIRST INSTANCE

20. The learned primary Judge referred to Cohen J's ruling that the Family Court application was a proceeding "in respect of a provable debt" within the meaning of s.58(3)(b), and said:

"I am content to apply his Honour's ruling. I doubt, however, that

leave is required. The proceedings in the Family Court are not

concerned with debt or damages. The Deputy Commissioner already

has judgment for the debt. In the proceedings, the Deputy

Commissioner does not seek and cannot obtain any order for the

payment to him of money or for the transfer to him of property.

The sole issue is whether the consent orders should be set aside

for fraud and, if so, what consequential orders should be made.

...

As any property the subject of an order for setting aside will

vest not in or to the benefit of Mr McCauley, but in or to the

benefit of the Official Trustee, it seems to me that the Family

Court proceedings are not proceedings 'in respect of a provable

debt.' The debt for which judgment has been obtained is

significant only as a fact relevant to the standing of the Deputy

Commissioner as a 'person affected' and as a fact relevant to the

proof of fraud."

After a discussion of some of the authorities (Rickard v Caldwell [1911] NSWStRp 34; (1911) 12 SR (NSW) 1; Re Brennan; Ex parte Gordon (1930) 2 ABC 120; and Re McMaster; Ex parte McMaster, above, to which I shall return) Davies J went on to say:

"Nevertheless, as Cohen J has ruled that the leave of this Court

is necessary, I think that I should deal with the matter on that

footing."

His Honour next addressed a submission made by Mr. Douglas QC on behalf of Mrs. Fraser that the only entitlement of the Deputy Commissioner was to lodge a proof of debt; and that transactions with respect to the property of a bankrupt could only be avoided at the instance of the trustee in bankruptcy acting under provisions of the bankruptcy legislation such as ss.115, 120, 121 or 122. Rejecting the argument, Davies J said:

"As the Bankruptcy Act does not purport to cover the whole field,

the principle suggested by Mr Douglas cannot be drawn from its

provisions. Transactions which are subject to avoidance for

reasons other than those with which the Bankruptcy Act is

concerned remain susceptible to attack, except insofar as s.58(3)

precludes that attack. See Ganas v Ganas (1971) 18 FLR 298."

On the question of the standing of the Deputy Commissioner in the Family Court, his Honour said:

"Questions of standing seem to me to be matters for the Family

Court rather than for this Court. I therefore do not attempt to

determine the issue. It is sufficient to say that, as presently

advised, it seems to me that a claim to have orders set aside by

reason of fraud on the Commonwealth is a matter in which the

Commonwealth is a proper party and that, when the fraud involves

non-payment of tax, the Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner is

an appropriate person to represent the interests of the

Commonwealth: James v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1957) 97

CLR 23 at 35. The Commonwealth, and the Deputy Commissioner, as

representing the Commonwealth's interests, would be persons

'affected by' the consent orders."

Davies J next addressed the submission by Mr. Douglas that s.58(3)(a) applied, with the consequence that the Court had no power to grant leave to proceed. The learned primary Judge said:

"... the Family Court proceedings are not proceedings against the

person or property of the bankrupt. They are proceedings against

what is now the property of Mrs McCauley and they are taken with a

view to restoring property to the Official Trustee who stands in

the shoes of the bankrupt. Moreover, the orders sought, the

setting aside of the consent orders and orders ancillary thereto,

are not a remedy 'in respect of a provable debt'. As s.58(3)(a)

provides a statutory embargo and makes no provision for leave to

be given by the Court, it should not be given the wide meaning

which similar words appearing in s.60(1) of the Bankruptcy Act

1924 (Cth) were considered to bear by Clyne J in Re White (1960)

20 ABC 11. In the context, the provision refers to such matters

as enforcing payment of a debt by means of seizing property or by

execution or by proceedings for the imprisonment of fraudulent

debtors. The proceedings in the Family Court are not such a

remedy."

In deciding to grant conditional leave under s.58(3)(b), his Honour said:

"As I consider that the proceedings in the Family Court will

benefit the estate of the bankrupt if successful, I am of the view

that leave to continue the proceedings should be granted. Leave

should be conditional, however, on the Deputy Commissioner's

undertaking not to oppose any application which the Official

Trustee may make to be joined in the proceedings and also on the

undertaking that the Deputy Commissioner keep the Official Trustee

advised of the orders which are proposed to be sought. I should

add that, as the property of Mr McCauley is vested in the Trustee,

the latter would appear to be a proper party to the Family Court

proceedings, whether as an applicant or as a respondent. The

bankrupt estate has an interest in the proceedings. Indeed, it

may be appropriate for the Trustee to commence proceedings under

s.120 or s.121 of the Bankruptcy Act and to have those proceedings

transferred to the Family Court and heard together with the

present proceedings."

In then declining to grant a stay under s.60(1)(b) of the bankruptcy legislation, Davies J said that this provision did not empower the Court to make an order "where the issue is not a bankruptcy issue and the decision making power can be exercised only by another court." His Honour added that, in any event, as the Family Court proceedings were potentially beneficial to the bankrupt estate, no stay should be granted.

MRS. FRASER'S GROUNDS OF APPEAL

21. Mrs. Fraser now appeals from the order granting leave under s.58(3)(b) and from his Honour's refusal to make declaratory and consequential orders under s.58(3)(a) and s.60(1)(b) on the following grounds:

(1) The Family Court proceedings were within s.58(3)(a).

(2) The transactions in question could only be attacked under the bankruptcy legislation, and could not be challenged under other legislation and, specifically, not under s.79A of the Family Law Act.

(3) Leave under s.58(3)(b) should, in any event, have been refused on discretionary grounds, including the circumstance that the Deputy Commissioner lacked standing in the Family Court.

(4) A stay should have been granted under s.60(1)(b).

CONCLUSIONS ON THE APPEAL

22. It will be convenient to consider the grounds of appeal in turn.

(a) Did s.58(3)(a) apply?

23. It will be recalled that, under this provision, except as provided by the Act, once a debtor has become a bankrupt, it is not competent for a creditor "to enforce any remedy against the person or property of the bankrupt in respect of a provable debt". There is an important structural difference from s.58(3)(b), namely, that there is no exception provided (i.e., the grant of leave to proceed). The bar imposed by s.58(3)(a) is absolute if the circumstances specified are shown to exist. The place of s.58(3)(a) in the general statutory scheme is explained in the recent decision of the High Court in Cummings v Claremont Petroleum N.L, (1996) 137 ALR 1 per Brennan CJ, Gaudron and McHugh JJ at 4,7; per Dawson and Toohey JJ at 13-16.

24. It is common ground that the (judgment) debt, which is owed to the Commonwealth (see James v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 36; (1957) 97 CLR 23 (at 35)), but payable to the Deputy Commissioner, was "a provable debt". It was submitted by Mr. Douglas for Mrs. Fraser that the other operative provisions of s.58(3)(a) were also applicable here. I have difficulty accepting the submission.

25. It will be remembered that Davies J rejected the wide interpretation given to the precursor of s.58(3), viz. s.60(2) of the Bankruptcy Act 1924, which was propounded by Clyne J in White, above. Section 60(2) of the earlier Act provided that, after sequestration, except as directed by the statute, no creditor to whom the bankrupt was indebted in respect of any debt provable in bankruptcy, should have any remedy against the property or person of the bankrupt in respect of the debt, or should commence or take any fresh step in any action or other legal proceeding, unless with the leave of the Court and on such terms as the Court imposed. In White, two sequestration orders had been made and an application for rescission of the second order succeeded on the ground that (Slater's) the (second) petitioning creditor's only right was to share in the distribution of the bankrupt's estate under the first order. Clyne J said (at 12-13):

"It is well established that when a person is made bankrupt his

property becomes vested in the official receiver of his estate and

the remedies which his creditors had against his property can no

longer be enforced. In substitution for these remedies the

creditors acquire a right to share equally and proportionately in

the distribution of the bankrupt's estate. ... .

This is clearly the effect of s.60(1) of the Bankruptcy Act. When

White became bankrupt on 23rd March, 1959, Slater's remedy against

White or his property ceased to exist, and he had then merely a

right of proof against White's bankrupt estate. It was contended

that the words in s.60(2) of the Act, 'any remedy against the

property or person of the bankrupt in respect of the debt', i.e.,

a debt provable in bankruptcy, must mean a remedy in the way of

enforcing payment of the debt, not by way of bringing action or

suit, but a proceeding such as a proceeding under an Act dealing

with the imprisonment of fraudulent debtors. This contention, in

my opinion, cannot be accepted. The word 'remedy' has a well

defined meaning and this meaning cannot be restricted in the

manner suggested by counsel for Slater."

26. The present corresponding provision, s.58(3), uses some of the same language as the earlier version, but with the important structural difference that leave is now only contemplated in the circumstances described in s.58(3)(b), but not in those specified in s.58(3)(a).

27. The decision of the High Court in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 44; (1984) 154 CLR 589 dealt with the question whether the Court had the power to make a sequestration order on a pending creditor's petition after the presentation, in the meantime, of a debtor's petition. Gibbs CJ, Murphy, Brennan and Dawson JJ said (at 594):

"Before the court can make a sequestration order, it must be

satisfied that 'the debt or debts on which the petitioning

creditor relies is or are still owing': s.52(1)(c). But since the

debtor was already bankrupt when the petition came to be heard,

the remedies against the person and property formerly available to

the Deputy Commissioner had been taken away and there was

substituted a right to prove against the estate which had become

vested in Mr. Andrew as trustee... . At that time the Deputy

Commissioner 'was not a mere creditor. (He) was a creditor whose

claim was in proof. (His) claim was no longer a mere right of

action for a debt. (He) could no longer have maintained an action

as for a debt. The debt had been, at any rate provisionally,

merged in an equitable execution... ."

Their Honours went on to say (at 595):

"The judgment of Clyne J in In re White in part rested on the

view that the word 'remedy' in s.60(2) of the Bankruptcy Act 1924

(Cth), as amended, (which in substance corresponded with s.58(3)

of the present Act) includes a remedy by way of an action or suit

- a construction which is open to doubt having regard to the

express mention in the sub-section of a legal proceeding. However

the true ground of the decisions is that the earlier bankruptcy

had converted the creditor's right to be paid by the debtor into a

right of proof, and the debt was not still 'owing' within the

meaning of the statute." (Emphasis added)

Apart from the expression of these doubts by the majority of the High Court, itself, of course, a matter of considerable weight for our purposes, there are, according to the submissions made by Mr. Gleeson on behalf of the Official Trustee, other reasons why s.58(3)(a) did not apply here.

28. In the first place, the Official Trustee relied upon the decision of Jenkinson J in Re Stewart and Secretary, Department of Social Security (1985) 4 AAR 95 for the proposition that a "remedy" is "against the person" only if it involves physical restraint. In Stewart, review was sought of a decision under s.140(2) of the Social Security Act to recover an amount overpaid to a bankrupt by deductions from his current unemployment benefits. It was held that the deductions were not made unlawful by s.58(3). Jenkinson J said (at 97):

"It was submitted that to make a deduction, pursuant to s.140(2),

from the unemployment benefit the applicant is receiving is 'to

enforce ... (a)... remedy against the person ... of the bankrupt'.

I cannot accept that submission. A remedy is against the person

only if it involves physical restraint."

With respect, I agree; no such restraint could be suggested here.

29. Jenkinson J then turned to the question whether the making of the deduction was the enforcement of a "remedy against ... the property of the bankrupt". It was there conceded, or assumed, that "property of the bankrupt" comprehended only such property as had vested in the trustee. Jenkinson J went on to say (at 98):

"But, in any event, nothing that is done pursuant to s.140(2) of

the Social Security Act, or in exercise of power conferred by that

section, is the enforcement of a remedy by a creditor. The

Secretary is the person by whose determination deduction from

pension, allowance or benefit may be authorised. The Secretary

does not make such a determination at the instance, or on the

application, or at the direction, of the creditor, the

Commonwealth: he exercises a statutory discretion committed to

him by s.140(2), free of any influence by the creditor. Section

58(3) of the Bankruptcy Act speaks not of him, nor addresses any

command to him, in my opinion."

Stewart was followed by Lockhart J in Taylor v Secretary to the Department of Social Security (1988) 18 FCR 322 (at 334-5), a case of pension overpayment and deduction from later payments to compensate, which was held to stand outside s.58(3).

30. It may be said that the special context in both Stewart and Taylor requires them to be distinguished for our purposes. In any event, it should be noted, as Lockhart J observed in Taylor (at 334-5), that there is now a special definition of "property of the bankrupt" for the purposes of s.58(3) which was inserted by an amendment made to s.5(1) in 1987 as follows:

"'the property of the bankrupt', in relation to a bankrupt, means:

(a) except in subsections 58(3) and (4):

(i) the property divisible among the bankrupt's creditors; and

(ii) any rights and powers in relation to that property that would

have been exercisable by the bankrupt if he or she had not become

a bankrupt; and

(b) in subsections 58(3) and (4):

(i) the property, rights and powers referred to in paragraph (a)

of this definition; and

(ii) any other property of the bankrupt;"

31. The present definition of "property" thus being extremely wide, a question arises whether the Family Court proceedings concerned that property, directly or indirectly. But I need not decide this, having regard to my conclusion on other aspects of the matter, which will now be explained.

32. As the High Court observed in Clyne (at 595) a distinction should be drawn, for the purposes of s.58(3), between the "enforcement" of a remedy against the property of the bankrupt, on the one hand, and the commencement or the taking of a fresh step in a "legal proceeding", on the other. An obvious example of the former would be to levy execution against the lands or goods of the bankrupt. In this connection, the words "enforcement" of a "remedy" should, I think, be interpreted as having their settled meaning. In R v Bates (1982) 2 NSWLR 894, Samuels JA. said, of "enforce" (at 895):

"... I would harbour considerable doubt, as a matter of grammar or

syntax, whether the word 'enforced' extends to cover the

institution of proceedings for breach of a provision of a statute.

The relevant meaning assigned in the Shorter Oxford English

Dictionary is that to enforce means 'to compel observance of'.

That is, I think, its ordinary meaning..."

With respect, I agree. Moreover, it will be recalled that the notion of "enforcement" was not used in s.60(2) when considered by Clyne J in White. This may be a basis for distinguishing White. But, in any event, the doubts cast in Clyne upon the approach taken in White, when considered in the context of the settled meaning of the phrase "enforce a remedy", lead, in my opinion, to the conclusion that the absolute bar imposed by s.58(3)(a) should be construed so as to apply only to the enforcement of remedies, including extra-curial remedies, as distinct from the institution of legal proceedings and their maintenance up to the point of recovery of judgment. The institution and maintenance of such proceedings will, of course, be subject to the different constraints imposed by s.58(3)(b), unless leave be granted under that provision.

33. It follows, in my view, that the application under s.79A of the Family Law Act should be characterised as a "legal proceeding" rather than the "enforcement" of a "remedy"; so that s.58(3)(a) has no application in the present circumstances.

(b) Did s.58(3)(b) apply?

34. Since the Family Court proceeding is a "legal proceeding" for the purposes of s.58(3)(b), the question for resolution is whether an application to set aside the consent orders is such a proceeding "in respect of a provable debt".

35. It will be recalled that, in this connection, Davies J considered three authorities, Rickard v Caldwell, Re Brennan and McMaster. The first two of these cases arose in a conveyancing context under the general law which may, I think, be distinguished for our purposes. But, in my opinion, McMaster is on point in the present statutory context.

36. In McMaster, after a property settlement order was made by the Family Court in favour of the wife in the sum of $18,358, the husband became bankrupt. The wife became an unsecured creditor in the bankrupt estate for that sum. The size of the Family Court order was related to the amount of the husband's superannuation entitlement. The wife applied to this Court for leave under s.58(3)(b) to commence proceedings under s.79A of the Family Law Act to set aside the Family Court order, and in lieu thereof, to obtain an order that the wife be entitled to a proportion of the husband's superannuation entitlement when vested, or $12,600, to be paid to her upon that vesting. Hill J held: (1) The proceedings contemplated to be brought by the wife in the Family Court were proceedings "in respect of" the provable debt of the wife. (2) The words "in respect of" in s.58 of the Act should be given a wide meaning in order to promote the policy of the Act, which was to assist in ensuring that the assets of a bankrupt were distributed in the interests of creditors generally and to prevent one creditor obtaining an undue advantage over the others. (3) In the circumstances, the Court should not exercise its discretion to give leave to the wife to commence the proceedings proposed.

37. Hill J said (at 159-160):

"The modern bankruptcy law serves three purposes. The first is to

ensure that the assets of the bankrupt are distributed rateably

among creditors. The second, which is interrelated to the first,

is to ensure that one creditor does not obtain an undue advantage

over other creditors. The third is to bring about the discharge

of the debtor from future liability for his existing debts, so

that the debtor may start afresh... .

A grant of leave in the present case would scarcely advance the

third purpose of the legislation. What the wife seeks to do here

is to seek the order of a court which will effectively defer the

liability and take it out of the category of provable debts, so

that the bankrupt, upon discharge, will continue to be liable for

an equivalent amount.

For the wife it was argued that no detriment could accrue to the

estate because the proceeds of the superannuation benefit do not

form part of the estate of the bankrupt. That is certainly true

at the present time. The entitlement to benefit does not

represent property that belonged to, or was vested in, the

bankrupt at the commencement of the bankruptcy, nor, it may be

said, does the bankrupt have a capacity to exercise power over it

for his benefit within s.116(1)(b) of the Act. However, it cannot

be said that the proceeds could never become property divisible

among creditors."

As has been seen, Davies J was minded to distinguish McMaster upon the basis that the wife there sought orders for the payment of money or for the vesting in her of property, whereas no such relief was sought here. But, with respect, I am not persuaded that the approach taken in McMaster is so far removed from the present context.

38. Of the words "in respect of", Brennan, Deane and Gaudron JJ said, admittedly in another context, in Technical Products Pty. Ltd. v State Government Insurance (Q) [1989] HCA 24; (1989) 167 CLR 45 (at 47):

"The words 'in respect of' have a very wide meaning. Indeed, they

have a chameleon-like quality in that they commonly reflect the

context in which they appear. The nexus between legal liability

and motor vehicle which their use introduces in s.3(1) is a broad

one which is not susceptible of precise definition. That nexus

will not, however, exist unless there be some discernible and

rational link between the basis of legal liability and the

particular motor vehicle."

In an analogous bankruptcy context, in considering the meaning and operation of the related provision s.60(1), a Full Federal Court has held (Re Lattouf [1994] FCA 1342; (1994) 52 FCR 147) that the reporting of the breach of a recognisance condition concerning monthly payments on an acknowledged debt constituted "legal process in respect of the non-payment of a provable debt" within the meaning of s.60(1)(b). Wilcox, Einfeld and Carr JJ said (at 152):

"In this case the nexus between the non-payment of the debt and

the alleged breach of the compensation payment condition is

demonstrated by the fact that each compensation payment

automatically had the legal effect of reducing the amount of the

probable debt. The probable debt is identical to the compensation

sum. Accordingly, the legal process whereby Mr Lattouf is exposed

to the possibility of being sentenced in respect of his conviction

for fraudulent conversion because he stopped making the

compensation payments cannot, in our opinion, be viewed as being

independent of the non-payment of the provable debt. There is no

independence of the one from the other to prevent one from being

'in respect of' the other in the manner recently referred to by

the High Court of Australia in Nintendo Co Ltd v Centronics

Systems Pty Ltd... . In our view the legal process which it is

agreed will occur before Judge Gibson QC constitutes 'legal

process, whether civil or criminal' within the meaning of

s.60(1)(b) of the Act. We have therefore come to the conclusion

that there is sufficient nexus between the foreshadowed

proceedings before Judge Gibson QC (being 'legal process, whether

civil or criminal') and the non-payment of the provable debt for

the former to be 'in respect of' the latter."

Thus, a connection which is rational and discernible may be indirect.

39. The High Court's decision in Storey v Lane [1981] HCA 47; (1981) 147 CLR 549 also demonstrates that, in order to prevent the avoidance of the statutory scheme, an indirect connection may be sufficient. There, Gibbs CJ said (at 557):

"It is equally clear that any system of bankruptcy law 'may

frequently require various ancillary provisions for the purpose of

preventing the scheme of the Act from being defeated':

Attorney-General (Ontario) v Attorney-General (Canada)... . For

example, it may be necessary to frame provisions to stop

individual action by creditors for the purpose of obtaining

payment of the debts due to them when the aim of the law is to

secure administration of the debtor's assets in the interest of

the creditors generally... . As I have attempted to show, the

provisions of s.60(1)(b) are designed to assist in ensuring that

the assets of the insolvent debtor are distributed in the

interests of creditors generally, to prevent one creditor

obtaining an undue advantage over the others, and to prevent the

scheme of the Bankruptcy Act from being defeated."

In Re Keogh (1995) 133 ALR 681, another s.60 case, Burchett J said (at 685):

"Imprisonment for debt has a long history, but it was recognised

very early that, if a debtor yielded up all his property to his

creditors, imprisonment might be more harsh than just. Section 60

applies this broad principle to the complexities of the modern

law, by which a debtor is faced, not only with obligations

directly imposed by orders of civil and criminal courts, but also,

for example, with those imposed indirectly by the conditions of

bonds. There is no reason why the principle should not apply

equally in each of these situations."

40. Although these decisions were concerned with s.60, rather than s.58(3), they do, I think, provide a proper analogy here.

41. Moreover, in my view, two further factors should be accorded significant weight in addressing the question whether s.58(3)(b) applies. First, the foundation of the Deputy Commissioner's claim before the Family Court is his unsatisfied debt. For the purposes of s.79A of the Family Law Act, the existence of his debt is the reason why he says he is a "person affected" by the consent orders, with the consequence, so he claims, that he has standing in the Family Court. This factor suggests at least some connection between the s.79A application and the Deputy Commissioner's debt.

42. Secondly, there is a more general background consideration serving as an aid to interpretation, namely, a central feature of the established scheme of bankruptcy is an equitable or rateable distribution of property amongst all unsecured creditors. Any mechanism to secure this objective can allow for the recovery of assets by one person only and that is the trustee, acting on behalf of the general body of creditors. In exercising that function, the trustee will need to make administrative decisions, or judgments, from time to time. But it is for the trustee alone to make those judgments, in the interests of creditors as a whole; even if, where appropriate, the trustee may take into account their expressed wishes. The point is that there is no scope here for any role to be played by individual creditors acting on their own initiative; and if litigation is to be instituted with a view to the recovery of assets, it is the trustee's function, and responsibility, to be the dominus litis and thus entirely in charge of the litigation to the exclusion of individual creditors. In other words, the relevant scheme of the legislation, specifically that of s.58(3), is that individual creditors have no right to decide to pursue, or not pursue, the assets of the bankrupt with a view to the satisfaction of individual debts. This is a matter, in terms of authority, as well as responsibility, for the trustee exclusively (see, e.g., Morris v Murjani (1996) 1 WLR 848 at 852-3). Section 58(3) should be interpreted in a way that gives practical effect to this legislative purpose.

43. It follows, I think, that what the Deputy Commissioner proposes to seek from the Family Court under s.79A is to augment the assets available to all creditors who prove in the estate, including himself, and so the proceeding can be said to be "in respect of a provable debt". Therefore, unless this Court grants leave, such proceeding by an individual creditor (as distinct from that of a trustee who is not bound by s.58(3)), is prohibited by s.58(3)(b).

44. In my opinion, the Deputy Commissioner's application under s.79A is a step taken towards the satisfaction of his individual debt and is thus "in respect of" that debt within the meaning of s.58(3)(b). Accordingly, he required leave to continue that proceeding.

(c) Should leave have been granted under s.58(3)(b)?

45. It will be recalled that Davies J granted leave, but only on conditions having as their objective the protection of the position of the Official Trustee, in effect as dominus litis, in the interests of the general body of creditors. In my view, that was a proper exercise of the Court's discretion in this area. If the matter were de novo, that is, if the Deputy Commissioner had not applied under s.79A until after the bankruptcy, there would have been much to be said for the view that leave to commence the proceeding should now have been refused, at least if the trustee were willing to apply instead of the Deputy Commissioner. But the matter is not de novo. Prejudice, at least in terms of the presentation of evidence, to the Deputy Commissioner and to the general body of creditors could result if the Deputy Commissioner's part-heard proceedings, instituted some time ago, were now, in effect, to be permanently interrupted and stayed by the operation of s.58(3))b).

46. At the same time, the position of the trustee as dominus litis should be preserved. The conditions imposed by Davies J achieve this in a practical way. In my view, his Honour's conditional order granting leave should not be disturbed.

47. On behalf of Mrs. Fraser, an attempt was made before Davies J, and before us, to have this Court rule that the Deputy Commissioner lacks standing in the Family Court because, it was contended, he was not, within the meaning of s.79A(1), a "person affected" by the consent orders, so that his Family Court application was bound to fail.

48. I agree with Davies J that this is a matter for the Family Court, rather than this Court, to decide. In any event, it appears that the argument is contradicted, at least in terms of any suggestion that there should be a summary dismissal of the Deputy Commissioner's claim, by the decision of a Full Family Court in D.F.C. of T v Spanjich (88 ATC 4985). In Spanjich it was held that the question of standing should be considered upon the whole of the evidence. As a matter of comity between courts, we should follow this decision and hold that it is premature for any court to address any issue of standing until all the evidence is in (cf. Onus v Alcoa of Australia Ltd. [1981] HCA 50; (1981) 149 CLR 27 per Gibbs J at 38).

49. There will be other adjectival matters for the Family Court to deal with; for instance, whether there is a need to join the family companies (specifically Logan) as parties, if, as appears to be the contention, it is to be suggested that assets of these companies were misapplied under the corporate and family rearrangement that occurred under the minutes, for instance cl.12 above (see, e.g. McPherson's Law of Company Liquidation, 3rd ed. (J. O'Donovan) at 355, Fn (4)). It is settled that the Family Court must take the property of a party to the marriage as it finds that property, yet as Gibbs J pointed out in Ascot Investments Pty. Limited v Harper [1981] HCA 1; (1981) 148 CLR 337 (at 355), this general rule is subject to exception "in the case of shams, and companies that are mere puppets of a party to a marriage". (See also s.79A(2), previously mentioned; cf. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Swain [1988] FCA 238; (1988) 20 FCR 507 at 513-7; Re Sabri, (1995) 60 FCR 131 (at 134); Marriage of Reed [1989] FamCA 62; (1989) 13 Fam LR 566; cf. also Marriage of Audet (1995) 19 Fam LR 291 (at 296)). These will also be matters for the Family Court to deal with. Although it may be accepted for present purposes that, provided the trustee is dominus litis, the Family Court rather than this Court (as a court of bankruptcy), is the appropriate forum for the determination of this dispute, there is no hard and fast rule as to the suitability of a particular forum for the present kind of case (see, e.g. In re Hutton (1969) 2 Ch 201 per Goff J at 205). Reference was made, in the course of argument, to an observation by Einfeld J in Re Woods, 22 February 1995, unreported, at 23-4, as to the effect, in the instant type of context, of consent orders made under s.79. Whilst, with respect, I agree with his Honour's observation that such orders are binding until set aside "by order of a court", I would not read his Honour's reasons as deciding that courts other than the Family Court could not intervene, at least indirectly, in this area in a bankruptcy matter. It may, for instance, be open to a court of bankruptcy to order that parties to Family Court proceedings take, or refrain from taking, steps in those proceedings. But it is not necessary to pursue the point here in the absence of argument on the issue.

(d) Should the power to stay under s.60 have been exercised?

50. For present purposes, I am prepared to accept, but without deciding, that the power given to this Court by s.60(1) is available here. Its exercise is clearly discretionary. In my view, similar considerations apply to its exercise as applied in the case of s.58(3)(b). Those considerations, already mentioned, indicate that Davies J was right in deciding both to grant conditional leave to continue the proceedings, and, consequentially, to refuse a stay under s.60(1).

ORDERS PROPOSED

51. It follows that I would propose that the appeal be dismissed, with costs. Each respondent was separately represented on the appeal and in the special circumstances of this appeal, each should, in my view, receive his own costs. The position in the Family Court in respect of the costs of future separate representation, if any, of the respondents may be different; this will be a matter for that Court to decide.

TAMBERLIN J I have had the opportunity of reading the reasons for judgment of Beaumont J. I agree with the reasons and conclusions of his Honour.
