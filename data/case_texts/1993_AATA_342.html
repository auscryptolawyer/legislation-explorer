Stephen Edward Sheridan and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 342 (12 November 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

STEPHEN EDWARD SHERIDAN v. COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

No. Q92/85

AAT No. 9126

Number of pages - 18

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

S A FORGIE (Senior Member), K L BEDDOE (Senior Member) AND G S URQUHART

(Member)

CATCHWORDS

HEARING

BRISBANE, 19-20 April 1993; SYDNEY, 21-23 April 1993 and BRISBANE, 3 September 1993

12:11:1993, BRISBANE

Solicitors for the Applicant: Mr J Stannard

Welfare Rights Centre

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr R Whithear

ORDER

The Tribunal:

1. varies the decision dated 29 January, 1992 in respect of the benefit

classification certificate by:

(1) setting aside that part of the decision to issue a new benefit

classification certificate in relation to Mr Sheridan and specifying

pursuant to sub-section 6(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 the

conditions of obsessive compulsive neurosis and undifferentiated

personality disorder with obsessive features and undifferentiated

personality disorder together with bilateral sensori-neural hearing

loss;

(2) substituting for that part a decision that there be issued a new

benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions of severe

anxiety and varying depression in the context of severe compulsive

obsessive neurosis, undifferentiated personality disorder with obsessive

features and bilateral sensori-neural hearing loss; and

(3) otherwise affirming the decision; and

2. affirms the decision of the Commissioner under sub-section 66(2) of

the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

S A FORGIE, K L BEDDOE AND G S URQUHART When the applicant, Mr Stephen Edward Sheridan, who joined the Overseas Telecommunications Commission ("OTC") in September, 1981, became an eligible employee within the meaning of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), he underwent a medical examination. That medical examination was required by sub-section 16(2) of that Act. After the medical examination on 1 July, 1982 and after referring Mr Sheridan to an audiologist, the Commonwealth Medical Officer ("the CMO") reported to the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") on 26 August, 1982 that Mr Sheridan was not likely to continue to be an eligible employee until attaining the maximum retiring age by reason of, or for a reason connected with, his having a condition of a very mild bilateral sensorineural hearing loss.

2. The CMO's report, together with other material, was considered by a delegate of the Commissioner. As a result, the delegate issued a Benefit Classification Certificate ("a BCC") specifying bilateral sensorineural hearing loss under sub-section 16(4) of the Act. That BCC was issued on 4 August, 1983.

3. On 8 December, 1987, Mr Sheridan was retired from his employment with OTC on the ground of invalidity. After gathering further information, a delegate of the Commissioner exercised his power under sub-section 16(11)(c) of the Act as it was in force on 15 August, 1988 and revoked the BCC in force in respect of Mr Sheridan and issued a new BCC specifying the conditions of bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, obsessive compulsive condition and chronic anxiety state. Pursuant to sub-section 16(11B), the new BCC was deemed to have been issued on 4 August, 1983 and the first was deemed never to have been issued.

4. On the same day, the delegate also determined under sub-section 66(2) that the incapacity which was the ground for Mr Sheridan's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a condition or conditions specified in the BCC or by a condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions. The consequence of this decision was that Mr Sheridan was paid a lump sum rather than a pension under the Act.

5. Mr Sheridan requested that the Commissioner reconsider the decisions. On 29 January, 1992, a delegate of the Commissioner re-considered the decisions. In respect of the decision to issue a new BCC, he decided to vary the decision by substituting a new decision under section 16AC of the Act to revoke the BCC in force immediately before Mr Sheridan's retirement and to issue in substitution for it a new BCC specifying the conditions of obsessive compulsive neurosis and undifferentiated personality disorder with obsessive features together with bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. The delegate confirmed the previous determination under sub-section 66(2).

6. By an application lodged on 26 February, 1992, the applicant, Mr Stephen Edward Sheridan, applied for review of the Commissioner's decision in relation to the BCC as varied and his decision under sub-section 66(2). At the hearing of his application, held over four days in Brisbane and Sydney, Mr Sheridan was represented at different stages by either Mr Major, of Counsel or Mr Stannard, a Solicitor with the Welfare Rights Centre. The Commissioner was represented by Mr Whithear of his office.

7. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the Act") were admitted in evidence ("the T documents") together with a bundle of documents from the Wolston Park Hospital, a bundle of documents from the Rosslyn Private Hospital, a bundle of documents from the Royal Brisbane Hospital, a bundle of documents from the Prince Henry Hospital, a letter to Mr Sheridan from Dr Byrne dated 3 March, 1981, an extract from Chapters 7 and 13 of the Handbook for Guidance of Medical Officers and a Medical Report Form signed on 26 August, 1982.

8. Oral evidence was given by Mr Sheridan in support of his case together with Dr Phillips. In support of the Commissioner's case, oral evidence was given by Dr Mulholland, Dr MacMillan, Dr Bartle and Dr Smith.

THE LEGISLATION

9. Since the initial decision to substitute a new BCC was made, the Act has been amended by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1990 ("the amending Act"). The amending Act repealed sub-section 16(11) and substituted section 16AC. Both parties have approached this case on the basis that it should be considered in light of the law as it now stands. In so far as the application of section 16AC itself is concerned, we agree that this is the proper approach to adopt and consider that the repealed sub-section 16(11) has no application in this case. In doing so, we rely upon the case of Meilak v Commissioner for Superannuation ((1991) [1991] FCA 96; 99 ALR 559, Sheppard, Jenkinson and Ryan JJ).

10. As Mr Sheridan, as an eligible employee, retired before his maximum retiring age and with less than 20 years' contributory service, he is a "relevant person" within the meaning of paragraph 16AC(1)(a). Sub-section 16AC(2) provides that:

"Subsections (4) to (8), inclusive, apply where the Commissioner is

satisfied, in respect of a person who is a relevant person:

(a) that:

(i) at or in connection with a medical examination that the person who

was required under subsection 16(2) or (6) or 16AB(3) to undergo; or

(ii) in connection with a request under subsection 16(6) by the person;

the person failed to answer properly a question asked of him or her or

gave false or misleading information; and

(b) that, if the person had answered the question properly or had not

given that false or misleading information:

(i) where there is in force, or there was in force immediately before

the person's retirement or death, a benefit classification certificate

in respect of the person - a condition or conditions of the person not

specified in the certificate would be or would have been specified; or

(ii) where subparagraph (i) does not apply - there would be in force, or

there would have been in force immediately before the person's

retirement or death, a benefit classification certificate in respect of

the person specifying a condition or conditions of the person."

11. What amounts to a person's answering a question properly is dealt with in sub-section 16AC(9) but we do not need to consider that as Mr Major conceded on Mr Sheridan's behalf that he had failed to answer properly a question asked of him at the time he completed a statement of his personal medical history on 26 August, 1982 (T documents, pages 98-99).

12. As a BCC was issued, the first issue in this case turns upon the application of sub-paragraph 16AC(2)(b)(i). If that sub-paragraph applies, sub-section 16AC(4) provides that the Commissioner must revoke the original BCC and issue a new BCC which specifies the conditions, in substitution. The first is taken never to have been issued (sub-section 16AC(6)) and is taken to have been issued on the date on which the revoked BCC was issued (sub-section 16AC(7)).

13. In considering whether a condition or conditions would have been specified in the BCC, regard must be had to sub-section 16(4). At the time of the hearing, that provided that:

"The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other

matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is

of the opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by reason of

or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or

conditions referred to in the report or reports, will not continue to be

an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age,

the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to

that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or

conditions is or are specified."

14. At the time that Mr Sheridan's BCC was issued in 1983, sub-section 16(4) required the Commissioner to consider not whether there "is a real risk that the person ... will not continue to be an eligible employee" but whether "the person is not likely ... to continue" to be an eligible employee. Mr Whithear submitted that we should consider sub-section 16(4) in its present form but also very properly referred us to the case of Kelly and Commissioner for Superannuation (unreported, 6 May, 1988, Decision No. 4319, Deputy President Todd and Dr Howell and Mr Attwood, Members), which does not support his submission.

15. In Kelly's case, the Tribunal said:

"The proposition put by Mr. Whithear was that as this provision had been

amended with effect from 1 July 1976, the 'real risk' test as

legislatively ordained should be applied in the present case, in which

the decision was made on 15 July 1986. We do not see how this can be

so. The question under s.16(11) is whether a BCC 'would have been in

force', which is a question of fact. How an amendment made

retrospectively could be held to overlay a prior factual situation of

some 8 years standing escapes us." (paragraph 24)

16. While we have considerable sympathy for the views expressed in Kelly's case, we have concluded that we must apply section 16(4) in its amended, rather than its unamended, form. Sub-section 2(1) of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 provides that sub-section 6(1), which amends section 16, "shall be deemed to have come into operation on 1 July, 1976." It seems to us that we must assess the factual situation as if the law in force at the time the decision to issue the BCC was made was the law which it is now deemed to be.

17. This interpretation is confirmed by reference to both the Second Reading Speech of Mr Hurford, the then Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs and Minister Assisting the Treasurer in relation to the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Bill 1986, and the Explanatory Memorandum. The Minister said:

"The provisions relating to the issue, revocation and variation of

benefit classification certificates are being amended following the

recent decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to set aside the

decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation to issue a benefit

classification certificate in relation to Mr P.J. Bewley. The

amendments are in accord with the intended purpose of the provisions and

clarify that the provisions are to have the meaning attributed to them

prior to the Tribunal's decision rather than the meaning adopted by the

Tribunal. The amendments are to have effect from 1 July 1976, the date

of commencement of the principal Act, but will not affect the Tribunal's

decision in relation to Mr Bewley." (House of Representatives, Hansard,

pages 1615-1616 and see also the Explanatory Memorandum in relation to

sub-clause 6(2) of the Bill)

18. Once we have decided the condition or conditions which would have been specified in the BCC had Mr Sheridan answered the questions properly, the second issue arises for our consideration. That turns upon the application of section 66 which specifies a person's entitlement to an invalidity pension and provides for certain entitlements where

"(2) Subject to subsections (3), (3A) and (4) of this section and

section 79, ...:

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement

on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the

person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the

ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed

to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the

certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected

with such a condition or such conditions."

19. There was no dispute between the parties that Mr Sheridan comes within paragraphs 66(2)(a) and (b) and the only issue turns upon the application of paragraph 66(2)(c).

BACKGROUND

20. There were certain factual aspects of this case which were not in dispute between the parties and on that basis, together with the evidence before us, we have made certain findings of fact which we will now set out.

21. Mr Sheridan was born on 3 December, 1953. His parents divorced when he was two years of age and re-married when he was thirteen. Between the ages of 2 and 13, he was raised by his mother and maternal grandparents. His father, paternal grandparent and a paternal aunt have all received treatment for obsessional illnesses. Mr Sheridan first exhibited ritualistic behaviour when he was approximately 9 years of age and the nature of the behaviour changed over the years. He became anxious if he did not perform the rituals he devised for himself. He first sought treatment for his condition in approximately 1971 or 1972 and did so at the Mary Street Clinic in Brisbane. He was prescribed medication but Mr Sheridan considered the treatment totally ineffective. He was then referred to a specialist who, in turn, referred him to Dr Mulholland. Dr Mulholland prescribed various treatment but Mr Sheridan still exhibited the ritualistic behaviour.

22. Mr Sheridan had started to work for Telecom in 1970. He found that work made it very difficult for him as his ritualistic behavioural patterns added to the pressure of work. Despite that, he felt that he was able to conceal them from his colleagues and only resigned from Telecom in 1975 when he was transferred to Emerald. Had he moved to Emerald, he would have been unable to continue his treatment with Dr Mulholland.

23. On 1 December, 1977, Mr Sheridan was admitted to Wolston Park Hospital ("Wolston Park") and remained there for approximately a year. From Wolston Park, he was referred to the Prince Henry Hospital in Sydney ("Prince Henry") for assessment as to whether he should undergo a leucotomy. He underwent the leucotomy on 30 March, 1979. After the operation, Mr Sheridan was reported to be overtly less anxious and tense and, although his obsessionality remained, he was more amenable to the idea of behaviour modification (Exhibit 5, Discharge Summary dated 4 July, 1979). Mr Sheridan then underwent behaviour modification therapy at the Prince of Wales Hospital ("the Prince of Wales") which is also in Sydney. That therapy lasted for some six to eight weeks and he was then transferred back to the Prince Henry where it was felt that Mr Sheridan had improved dramatically. It was reported on his discharge summary from that hospital on 4 July, 1979 that he was less anxious and rigid and that he was more able to cope with residual ritualistic behaviour.

24. Mr Sheridan was then referred back to the Mary Street Clinic for further behaviour modification. At this time, he was still receiving medication and was still exhibiting some ritualistic behaviour patterns. He continued to undergo therapy of the same type as he had undergone at the Prince of Wales and he completed his treatment in late 1979 or early 1980. On 19 July, 1981, he was again admitted to the Prince of Wales for behaviour modification of his paedophilic sexual pre-occupations and received behaviour therapy for one week. Treatment then followed in a day programme. (Discharge Summary, Exhibit 5).

25. Mr Sheridan commenced employment with OTC in September, 1981. He lived in a variety of houses before moving to Kings Cross. At this time he was not exhibiting any ritualistic behaviour patterns and did not experience the symptoms from which he had suffered prior to his leucotomy. In February, 1985, Mr Sheridan was attacked by approximately six youths, one of whom he thought had a knife wrapped in a cloth. He warded them off by swinging his brief case. After this incident, he held a fear that attackers could come through the ceiling, walls and floor of his unit as well as through the doors and windows. As a precaution, he would pick up a small coffee table and use it as a weapon while he checked the unit each time he returned home.

26. After this incident, Mr Sheridan became extremely distrustful of people to the extent that he refused to help an old lady on the basis that she might be a man, dressed as a woman, and carrying a gun. He had a great fear that other people wished to take his life. This was quite a different fear, Mr Sheridan felt, from that he had associated with the obsessive compulsive disorder. The anxiety from the obsessive compulsive disorder came from within and from a fear of not having things properly organised but the other was a fear of being attacked.

27. In February, 1986, Mr Sheridan was threatened by a man at the hotel. The man told him that he would shoot him with a gun and stab him with a knife. Mr Sheridan picked up a bar stool to protect himself and both were evicted from the bar. Mr Sheridan took the threat very seriously and was frightened to go out and so minimised his time on the streets. Again, his fears were different from those he had held before the leucotomy. They mirrored those he had experienced after the mugging.

28. Mr Sheridan moved to Randwick after complaining about the noise made by his neighbours in Kings Cross. The people who lived next door to him at his new flat made a considerable amount of noise one night after Mr Sheridan had returned from the hotel. When the noise continued for some time, he became very frightened of them. He knocked on their door and stabbed the man when he came to answer it. Again, Mr Sheridan did not equate the fear he held that night with the feelings he had experienced prior to his leucotomy operation.

29. As a result of the stabbing, Mr Sheridan was convicted, fined and placed on a five year good behaviour bond. He found that his contact with the probation officers developed a fear of such people and he began to fear that they would follow him to observe his behaviour. He spent the next year under treatment with Dr Beckett as well as in the Ashfield Hospital, East Sydney Clinic and the Prince Henry Hospital.

30. Up to the time of the stabbing and while in Sydney, Mr Sheridan had frequently gone to a hotel in the evening. He would usually drink six schooners of beer. He began to drink more and spent most of 1988 in and out of alcoholic rehabilitation hospitals as well as the Rosslyn Hospital.

EVIDENCE

Medical evidence to 4 August, 1983 when the BCC was issued

31. Dr Mulholland, who now practises as a consultant psychiatrist, treated Mr Sheridan from 1973 to about 1974 or 1975. There is no report or documentation dating from the time of his treatment but Dr Mulholland had a very clear recollection of Mr Sheridan. The earliest report of Dr Mulholland's dates from 13 April, 1988 when he wrote that, in 1974 or 1975:

"The problem at the time was mostly that of severe anxiety and varying

depression in the context of a severe obsessive compulsive neurosis.

Stephen also had an undifferentiated personality disorder with obsessive

features." (T documents, page 112)

32. At the same time, Dr Curtis, the Deputy Psychiatrist in Charge, and Dr Feros, the Psychiatry Registrar at the Royal Brisbane, wrote on his admission to the Royal Brisbane after Mr Sheridan had attempted suicide:

"The patient had a long history of obsessional personality with probable

superimposed psychotic features over the previous twelve months. He had

been prone to excessive alcohol consumption often followed by outbursts

of violent behaviour. On the day of admission he had been drinking

excessively and had reacted violently to his parents. He had had a

fairly strict upbringing including tuition at catholic schools and had

always been a quiet lonely boy and the previous two years he had

developed marked obsessional symptoms and had developed neologisms to

describe his obsessionality." (Summary of Admission dated 14 November,

1974, Exhibit 4, page 2)

33. In his oral evidence, Dr Mulholland said that he did not think that a psychotic condition was in issue. Mr Sheridan's severe obsessive compulsive neurosis fluctuated in severity and would have been close to incapacitating in so far as work and normal activities of living were concerned. At the same time, he had a high level of anxiety even when his life was proceeding reasonably satisfactorarily. Mr Sheridan's depression was a secondary reaction to his obsessive compulsive disorder and so fluctuated. Dr Mulholland said that Mr Sheridan also had a mixed personality disorder. That could also be described as an undifferentiated personality disorder in which the obsessive feature is prominent and in which schizoid avoidant features would be prominent also. He was reluctant to place a single label on Mr Sheridan's personality problems as there was a range including very low self esteem, inability to establish satisfactory interpersonal relationships and a number of issues to do with sexual identity. Dr Mulholland said that Mr Sheridan would drink heavily from time to time as this gave him some relief from his obsessional behaviour but, on the other hand, made him more emotional and suicidal. Heavy drinking was not a regular feature at the time he treated him.

34. Dr Mulholland described Mr Sheridan's appearance and behaviour before the leucotomy in the following way:

"Well, the Stephen that I saw, he looked rather unusual, he was sort of

noticeable from a distance. He looked like a person who was extremely

anxious and agitated and angry, that is what he looked like. And he was

given to frequent sort of strange movements, sitting in peculiar

postures; touching various parts of himself; frequently jumping up and

down and muttering to himself words which you wouldn't - which most of

the time you often couldn't understand and occasionally shouting out and

swearing. Plus or minus doing things like, you know, punching the wall,

or punching the desk. These sorts of things. And I found that this

behaviour was so sort of off putting, if I could say, that I used to see

him after hours because to sort of minimise the effects of his behaviour

upon my staff and other patients and so on."

(transcript B page 61, 4-15)

35. Dr Mulholland stated that, in the 1970's a leucotomy would not have been considered for a person whose main problem was a personality disorder. This followed from the fact that the results of a leucotomy in such a situation were unpredictable in that some people recovered and some worsened. Where a person has an obsessive compulsive neurosis as well as a personality disorder, the best results from surgery are in those patients who are the least disturbed. In cross-examination, Dr Mulholland said that, in a relatively small number of cases, a leucotomy lessens the obsessive compulsive disorder. In a small number of people, they are cured in the sense that a long period of time passes without symptoms. Others are totally unaffected and yet others are improved to a greater or lesser degree.

36. Mr Sheridan's admission to Wolston Park followed his treatment in the Royal Brisbane Hospital ("the Royal Brisbane") where he had been diagnosed as having a severe obsessive/compulsive neurosis and noted as showing violence if his complex rituals were disturbed (Exhibit 2, pages 3-9).

37. In Wolston Park, he received behaviour therapy, individual psychotherapy, group therapy and medication but none had caused any prolonged remission. It was noted that violent episodes had occurred during the course of all treatments and that the violence was related to fear and anger at the staff who were trying to prevent his obsessional behaviour. At that time, the diagnosis was that of obsessional-compulsive neurosis (Exhibit 2, pages 1-2).

38. The Discharge Summary from the Prince Henry on 30 March, 1979 noted that Mr Sheridan did not display any thought disorder, hallucinations or delusions. Behaviour modification attempted prior to the leucotomy had not succeeded due to Mr Sheridan's high level of anxiety. After the operation, Mr Sheridan was observed by Dr Smith, the Director, and Dr Thomas, the Psychiatric Registrar, to be overtly less anxious and tense. His obsessionality remained but he was more amenable to the idea of behaviour modification. Several small rituals were eliminated and the duration of the more complex was decreased. It was felt that he was more able to cope with his residual ritualistic behaviour. Treatment continued at the Prince of Wales (Exhibit 5, pages 5-6).

39. At the time that he was seeking employment in early 1980, Mr Sheridan wrote to the Prince Henry on 4 March, 1980 asking for a written opinion as to the success of his leucotomy and therapy in treating his obsessional ritualistic behaviour. He wrote that he "... most urgently need(ed) this opinion to obtain employment, as most employers seriously doubt the effectiveness of treatment for a psychiatric ailment." (Exhibit 5) In response, Dr J. Sydney Smith, the Director of the Neuropsychiatric Institute at the Prince Henry wrote:

"As you know you received a stereotactic leucotomy at Prince Henry

Hospital on 30/3/79. The leucotomy was performed because you had been

suffering obsessive behaviour since the age of nine years but a severe

obsessive compulsive neurosis in the six years prior to the operation.

From our observation the leucotomy resulted in immediate and dramatic

improvement in your level of anxiety and whilst your obsessional

behaviour persisted at that time, it was then quite responsive to

behaviour modification so that by the time of your discharge from

hospital on 13/6/79, it was considered that you had undergone a very

significant improvement, being less anxious and rigid and more able to

cope with your problems. It was recommended that behaviour modification

continue on your return to Brisbane.

When we saw you in follow-up, six months after the operation, we were

most pleased with your further improvement, so that few obsessional

features were then evident.

Based on our observations, it is my impression that you would be fully

capable of coping physically, intellectually and emotionally with a full

time job. If a potential employer had any queries about your capacity

to work, I would only be too happy, with your permission, to provide

them with any further reassurance." (Exhibit 5)

40. On 8 August, 1980, Dr Smith wrote to the Bureau of Meteorology on behalf of Mr Sheridan and reported that he had been free from symptoms for some 18 months and it was "most unlikely that he will ever relapse". In addition, he considered Mr Sheridan "from a psychiatric point of view ... to be perfectly normal ... (and) would expect him to remain so" (Exhibit 5).

41. Some time after 29 December, 1980, Mr Sheridan wrote to Dr Jane Byrne, the Staff Specialist at the Neuropsychiatric Institute and asked for "as much information as possible indicating that the leucotomy was 100% successful. This information particularly needs to indicate that there is no possibility of my suffering a relapse and that no side effects were incurred in the surgery (change of personality, epilepsy (sic), loss of I.Q., loss of memory and whatever else people can possibly dream up)". Mr Sheridan indicated that he wanted this information to take to the CMO. Dr Byrne wrote on 3 March, 1981 that she concurred with Dr Smith's comments and said that there had been no symptoms of recurrence of depressive illness when she had last seen him on 29 December, 1980. Furthermore, there was no indication that he had lost any intellectual function at all (Exhibit 5, page 36).

42. On 19 July, 1981, Mr Sheridan was again admitted to the Prince of Wales and remained there for only a short period until 24 July, 1981. The Discharge Summary signed by Professor McConaghy and Dr Lajqie, the Psychiatry Registrar, said that he had been admitted on that occasion for behaviour modification of his paedophilic sexual pre-occupations. He received one week's therapy as an inpatient and was continuing to participate in a day programme and to see Ricki Sharpe (Exhibit 5, page 7).

43. The following year, Dr Byrne confirmed her earlier opinion and did so in a letter written to Mr Sheridan's then solicitors on 10 February, 1982 (Exhibit 5). She also added that

"During the period of his illness, Mr Sheridan committed several

offences. He had used alcohol to attempt to relieve his symptoms and

certainly some of these offences arose whilst he was under the influence

of alcohol. In that respect the offences might be said to be related to

his condition.

Since his original illness has responded to surgery, the main reason for

his occasional abuse of alcohol has been removed. It is impossible to

state categorically that he will not abuse alcohol again but one can say

that it is less likely." (Exhibit 5)

Medical evidence relating to the period after 4 August, 1983

44. Dr Beckett treated Mr Sheridan regularly for some two years after the stabbing incident in 1986. He wrote a report on 7 March, 1986 for the purposes of the court hearing. Dr Beckett noted that Mr Sheridan had suffered from obsessive compulsive disorder for which he had received treatment. He went on to say:

"He appears to have few phobias and obsessions at the present time and

only some residual rituals. The thinking that he brings to bear upon

unresolved problems is essentially normal thinking. He is not deluded

or paranoid, nor is he experiencing hallucinations. He does not present

as having an anti-social personality nor is he suffering from a

psychosis or depressive illness." (Exhibit 3, page 16)

45. Later, on 25 February, 1988, Dr Beckett reported that in approximately March, 1987, it became apparent that Mr Sheridan was not coping at all well. His alcohol consumption had increased and his abuse of prescribed drugs escalated dramatically. Dr Beckett noted that "Stephen finally realised that it was not external events that were pressuring him so much as his own internal tension." (Exhibit 3, page 13)

46. Dr Beckett, who was not available for cross-examination, reported on 12 April, 1988. He noted that

"Mr Sheridan's psychiatric problems centre upon his severe and

long-standing anxiety symptoms. These symptoms, both directly and

indirectly, influenced his personality development, his social

adjustment, his social skills and, finally, his ability to successfully

hold down paid employment in an ordinary working situation, even though

the technical requirements of his former job were well within his

range." (T documents, page 109)

47. Later, in the same report, Dr Beckett wrote:

"At the time I first saw him, he presented as an honest, conscientious,

somewhat inflexible man of above average intelligence. He had some

insight and was trying hard to use it. He was noticeably tense. He

appeared to have three sorts of problems. First, there was a high level

of continuing anxiety symptoms. Secondly, possibly as a result of

having spent many years in psychiatric institutions, he appeared to lack

the sort of common sense and practical grasp of his environment that

most of us take for granted. Thirdly, he appeared to be functioning by

means of a number of systems that he had artificially devised as a way

of coping with everyday life in an environment that he only poorly

understood. These systems worked reasonably well for him, so long as

his daily life fitted in with his anticipations. They let him down

badly, however, when he was faced with anything unexpected and

spontaneous. Anything unexpected had a catastrophic effect on his

anxiety level." (T documents, page 110)

48. In conclusion, Dr Beckett said:

"Mr Stephen Sheridan has suffered for many years from a severe and

chronic anxiety state, with obsessive-compulsive symptoms. He tried

hard to hold down an ordinary job in spite of his severe and intractable

condition. In my opinion, he would probably be the best judge of when

he could do so no longer." (T documents, page 111)

Further evidence relevant to any conditions that would have been endorsed on the BCC had Mr Sheridan disclosed all relevant details of his medical history in 1982

49. When asked to comment on whether, in 1982, how he would have assessed the likelihood of Mr Sheridan's working until he was 65 years of age, Dr Mulholland said that he would have felt unable to do so as he had not seen him for sometime. He would have recommended that he be assessed but would have thought him more likely to develop mental problems than a person who did not have Mr Sheridan's history.

50. Dr MacMillan was the CMO who conducted the medical examination of Mr Sheridan on 26 August, 1982. He gave oral evidence in which he said that "there was nothing to alert me whatever to suspect an abnormal medical condition" (transcript page 89). Had he been alerted, he would have sought opinions from as many of his previous treating psychiatrists as he could. In addition, he would have referred him to one of the independent psychiatrists used by the Department of Health as referees.

Further evidence relevant to whether Mr Sheridan developed a separate condition after 1982

51. Dr Mulholland observed that the fact that a person no longer exhibits ritualistic behaviour is a pointer that he may be cured from obsessive compulsive disorder but only a pointer. To say that he is cured, he must also not be involved in excessive ruminations. In addition, to say that a person is cured means not only that there are no symptoms now but that there will not be any in the future. He would be guarded about the future.

52. Dr Bartle, who is also a psychiatrist, reviewed the reports of Drs Mulholland, Beckett, Smith and Phillips but did not examine Mr Sheridan. In concluding his report, Dr Bartle stated that

"In my opinion there is sufficient evidence to suggest that Mr Sheridan

benefited from the leucotomy, but some symptoms persisted, at a

considerably reduced level of intensity, and other symptoms were

controlled by his artificially devised defensive systems.

It is my further opinion that between 1981 and 1985, at the very best,

Mr Sheridan's symptoms would have been controlled or subclinical, but

not cured by any of the preceding treatment approaches. Nothing is

documented about his lifestyle during this period. He worked, and

probably his lifestyle improved.

In my opinion in the post February 1985 period Mr Sheridan did not

experience a totally new psychiatric illness, but a recrudescence of a

latent process that existed throughout his life." (T documents, page

148)

Dr Bartle did not resile from his opinion in his oral evidence.

53. Dr Jonathan Phillips, who is a psychiatrist based at the Northside Clinic, saw Mr Sheridan on one occasion on 1 December, 1988 and also perused notes of the Northside Clinic Greenwich relating to Mr Sheridan's admissions to the drug and alcohol service of the centre. He took a detailed history from Mr Sheridan and acknowledged in his report of 8 December, 1988 that his comments could only be based on the history given to him by Mr Sheridan (T documents, page 124).

54. Dr Phillips went on to say in his report:

"If the material is correct then it is improbable that Mr Sheridan's

obsessive/compulsive disorder interfered with his day to day life during

the period of his employment by the Overseas Telecommunications

Commission. It seems that the earlier years of his employment were

relatively satisfactory and it was only from 1985 that he began to be

troubled by symptoms of anxiety and to abuse alcohol and drugs. Not

surprisingly his work performance deteriorated rapidly and he reached

the point where he was apparently unemployable.

Mr Sheridan joined his superannuation fund in 1982 or 1983. This

appears to have been at a time when he was functioning rather better

than he had done before. Specifically, there was no residual problem of

obsessive/compulsive disorder of any importance and the major anxiety

symptoms had not come to the fore. ...

...

Finally you have asked three specific questions in your letter and I

will attempt to answer them.

1. It is quite probable that leucotomy led to a virtual cure of Mr

Sheridan's previous obsessive/compulsive disorder. Improvements

following leucotomy is not unexpected and in Mr Sheridan's case it was

reinforced by behaviour therapy provided at the Prince of Wales

Hospital.

2. It is more likely than not that Mr Sheridan retired as a consequence

of his anxiety state which had become disabling. However, this is not

an adequate picture on its own and the history of alcohol and drug abuse

must also be taken into consideration.

3. It is hard to make a link between the anxiety state of recent years

and the earlier problem of an obsessive/compulsive disorder. On the

basis of the history, there appears to be a period of several years when

Mr Sheridan was performing well and was apparently symptom free."

I am not suggesting that there is absolutely no link between one

disorder and the other but a better explanation is that Mr Sheridan

suffers a predisposition to emotional illness and has suffered two

distinct and unrelated disorders at various times in his life." (T

documents, pages 124-125)

55. In his oral evidence, Dr Phillips expresses the same view in this way:

"Sheridan, in my view, at two points in his life has had emotional

illnesses which appear unrelated to each other. What I'm talking about

there is a general predisposition. It's a genetic vulnerability to the

development of psychological symptoms presumably under stress at times

of stress." (transcript page 126)

56. Later, Dr Phillips was asked whether the vulnerability to which he referred was Mr Sheridan's predisposition and he answered:

"In broad terms, yes. I'm just a little careful in answering this

question, because I don't believe that I had enough time with Mr

Sheridan to really determine his underlying personality style. This is

something which is not always easy to determine at the first interview.

It appears subsequently and certainly in the hospital setting, and often

much comment relating to personality style will be based on

corroborative evidence from registrars, from nurses, and so on. Dr

Smith has obviously come to the conclusion, and I don't know on what

basis, but he's come to the conclusion that this man, Sheridan, had an

obsessive personality and as such, was vulnerable. What I had said was

not as focussed as this. I had, on the basis of my material, said that

I believed him to be vulnerable. I wouldn't have been surprised if I

had seen him two, three or four times, if I had come to the conclusion

that this man has a particular personality style of obsessional type

which is now evolving. But I say that as in a theoretical fashion, not

because I know, and I cannot take it further than that." (transcript

page 128)

57. Dr Phillips readily agreed that Dr Beckett and Dr Smith would have been in a better position to assess Mr Sheridan's ritualistic behaviour as both had far greater contact with Mr Sheridan than he. Furthermore, Dr Phillips acknowledged that his views had been based on the history he took from Mr Sheridan and that this proved to be inadequate. When he had regard to the reports of Dr Mulholland, Dr Beckett and Dr Smith, all of whom had treated Mr Sheridan, Dr Phillips thought that "we can assume that there is an underlying personality style or personality disorder and the features that my colleagues are suggesting is principally in the domain of obsessionality" (transcript page 133)

58. In conclusion, Dr Phillips said that he considered that, on the balance of probabilities, Mr Sheridan did not suffer a psychosis. Rather, as a result of the muggings and other stressful events, and again on the balance of probabilities, he considered that Mr Sheridan developed a severe anxiety state.

59. Dr Helen Borgman, who is a psychiatrist, wrote two reports. She had seen Mr Sheridan on three occasions after his voluntary admission to the Rosslyn Private Hospital. In her first report, she noted that Mr Sheridan had been "in the full sense cured by the leucotomy" (1 December, 1988, T documents, page 151). Dr Borgman went on to say that she was

"... firmly of the opinion that Mr Sh. is suffering from a Paranoid

Psychosis, with an onset in Jan 1985, precipitated by a Kings Cross

Mugging, a slow progression reaching a climax with an Acute Paranoid

Reaction in 1986 when he attacked a neighbour with a knife and was

arrested. This Psychosis was the cause of his retirement from his

permanent Job. He remains still with Paranoid Psychosis and I firmly

believe that he is entitled to Superannuation having retired from a

non-pre-existing Condition." (T documents, page 151)

Dr Borgman repeated this view in her second report of 25 July, 1990 (T documents, pages 152-153).

CONSIDERATION

60. We must consider what is meant by "real risk". Mr Whithear drew our attention to the recent judgement of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Minister for Immigration, Local Government and Ethnic Affairs v Batey ((1993) [1993] FCA 75; 112 ALR 198, Spender, Foster and Cooper JJ) in which the words "real risk" were considered in the light of the Minister's decision to order Mr Batey's deportation. In considering the level of risk that Mr Batey would commit further offences, the Minister had said:

"I have not given much weight to the statistical possibilities of

recidivism offered by Mr Lannen as a result of studies of the SOTP

program. Statistics are of little use when one considers an individual

case. The reality is that there is a real risk, however minimal, of

recidivism. My conclusion on recidivism, canvassing the factors

mentioned above, though higher than the AAT's conclusion on recidivism,

does not depart greatly from the AAT. I consider that it is low." (page

202)

61. The Full Court had to consider the meaning that the Minister had ascribed to the words "real risk" and noted that it was to be determined by reference to the language he had used in his reasons and the process of reasoning which was apparent from those reasons. It went on to say that "it was not to be determined by the use of the same or similar words in statutes or in other context." (page 206) The Minister, the Full Court found, had acknowledged that the risk of Mr Batey's recidivism was quite minimal and was "assessing the risk at the bottom end of the range of possibilities." (page 208) In doing so, the Minister was making a quantitative assessment of the risk.

62. The Full Court then went on to consider the way in which the Minister had used the words "real risk" quoted in paragraph 60 above. It said:

"The phrase, in context, is used by the minister in contradistinction to

the use by Mr Lannen of statistics and statistical possibilities, viz

hypothetical possibilities. The word 'real' which is then used to

describe the risk extends the notion of existence as an actuality in

contrast to something that is unrealistic, far-fetched or fanciful. In

this respect the minister is dealing with risk in a qualitative sense.

In our view, the minister is undertaking a course of reasoning and using

the word 'real' in a context similar to that undertaken and

demonstratable in the extract from Wyong Shire Council v Shirt ((1980)29

ALR 217, Stephen, Mason, Murphy, Aickin and Wilson JJ), cited above.

The minister is saying no more than there is a risk of recidivism which

is not far-fetched or fanciful, notwithstanding that the possibility of

recidivism may be minimal or, in the minister's judgment low. There is

no inconsistency between the minister and the tribunal on this issue

because the tribunal, to come to the view which it did, must have found

that there was a risk which was not far-fetched or fanciful before it

assessed that risk as minimal." (page 208)

63. As the Full Court said, we must consider the meaning of "real risk" in the context of the Act. In the context it was considering, the Federal Court ascribed a qualitative sense to the words "real risk". It is inherent, however, in the decision that the words could be understood in a quantitative sense. In view of the two different meanings, we have had regard to the Explanatory Memorandum which sets out quite comprehensively what Parliament intended. It was stated at page 6 that

"The purpose of the medical classification provisions of the Act is to

protect the Commonwealth against the risk of premature retirement or

death caused by a physical or mental condition that existed at the time

the person became a contributor. The Commissioner has, therefore,

administered the provisions of section 16 on the basis that 'likely'

means 'such as might well happen' or that 'there is a real risk'. This

approach was supported by a 1979 decision (Re Linden) of the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal. In a 1985 decision (Re Bewley),

however, the Tribunal adopted the approach that 'likely' means 'more

probable than not'.

The approach adopted in the recent Tribunal decision imposes a much more

severe test for the issue of benefit classification certificates than is

appropriate to the purpose of the medical classification provisions.

Also, it has a serious implications for the administration of those

provisions and would render them almost unworkable in view of the

difficulty in establishing a particular degree of probability as opposed

to that of identifying the existence of a real risk."

64. In Re Linden and the Commissioner for Superannuation ((1979) 2 ALN 659, Mr Hall, then Senior Member and Dr Glick and Mr Hutchinson, Members), the Tribunal considered sub-section 16(4) in its unamended form said:

"When, in sub-ss (4) and (8) of s16, parliament has referred to a person

who is not 'likely', by reason of or for a reason connected with some

physical or mental condition, to continue to be an eligible employee

until attaining maximum retiring age, it seems to us that, in the

context, parliament must be referring to conditions which give rise to a

'real risk' and not merely to conditions which create a 'probability' or

'better than even chance' that the person will not continue until

attaining maximum retiring age.

There is no question in our minds that where the medical evidence

establishes a probability or 'better than even chance' that a particular

condition will prove disabling before a person attains maximum retiring

age, the Commissioner is entitled, if not obliged, to issue a

certificate. But we are unable to accept that the section limits the

Commissioner to issuing a certificate only where such a degree of

certainty is established. The general medical standards developed for

the guidance of Commonwealth medical officers, which were designed at

establishing acceptable levels of risk, are as we understand them,

consistent with that broader view. Failure to meet the general medical

standards may not render it'probable' that a person will not continue in

employment until maximum retiring age. But such a failure does not

suggest that, if the condition persists, early death or retirement on

grounds of invalidity is an event which might well happen."

(pages 660-661)

65. It is clear from the case of Re Linden that it is the qualitative meaning which should be attributed to the words "real risk" and we propose to do so.

66. Turning to the evidence, which we have set out, we find that, had Mr Sheridan revealed in 1982 that he had undergone previous psychiatric treatment, the CMO would have made further enquiries. Those further enquiries would have been made of those who had treated him. In particular, they would have been made of Dr Mulholland and Dr Smith as well as from the Mary Street Clinic, the Prince of Wales, the Prince Henry and Wolston Park. In addition, he would have had regard to the opinions expressed by Dr Smith in his letters (see paragraphs 39 and 40) and would have sought an opinion from Dr Mulholland. Had he presented all of that evidence to the Commissioner, we find that the Commissioner would have found that, based on the evidence of Dr Mulholland who was the treating psychiatrist before the leucotomy, that Mr Sheridan had been suffering from severe anxiety and varying depression in the context of a severe obsessive compulsive neurosis as well as an undifferentiated personality disorder with obsessive features. Dr Mulholland's diagnosis was consistent with the notes of the Prince Henry, Prince of Wales and Wolston Park. It is also consistent with the views expressed by Dr Smith although he concentrates upon the obsessive features rather than upon an undifferentiated personality disorder.

67. The Commissioner would then have taken the leucotomy into account. Dr Mulholland's evidence, while given much later than 1983 when the Commissioner first considered the issue, does not lose credibility because of that. He has doubts about the long term effects of a leucotomy. His evidence is to the effect that he has reservations whether a total cure can be obtained. Dr Smith's evidence is relied upon to support the view that there was indeed a long term cure. When Dr Smith's correspondence is examined closely, however, it reveals that the leucotomy and the subsequent behaviour modification therapy rendered a significant improvement but had not cured Mr Sheridan of all obsessional features even six months after the operation. While Dr Smith's letter of 8 August, 1980 speaks of its being "most unlikely that he will ever relapse" and that he would "expect" Mr Sheridan to remain "psychiatrically normal", unlikelihood and expectation were not the tests which the Commissioner had to consider. He had to consider whether he was of the opinion that there was a real risk (i.e. a risk that was not fanciful, unrealistic or far-fetched) that Mr Sheridan would not continue to be an eligible employee until his maximum retiring age by reason of any condition.

68. In view of all of the evidence, we consider that the Commissioner would have formed that opinion. The condition in respect of which we would have formed the opinion would have been the condition diagnosed by Dr Mulholland. Consequently, we find that the Commissioner would have specified in the BCC the condition of severe anxiety and varying depression in the context of severe compulsive obsessive neurosis as well as the condition of undifferentiated personality disorder with obsessive features.

69. That brings us to the condition which ultimately led to Mr Sheridan's early retirement. Unfortunately, Dr Beckett was not available to give oral evidence but, despite that, we feel that, as he was the treating psychiatrist at the relevant time, his views must be given considerable weight. Dr Phillips, who had formed a different opinion based on the information he had been given, did not in fact have the full range of material available to us. He readily agreed that he did not and that he had not had the benefit of seeing Mr Sheridan on more than one occasion. Dr Borgman died before the hearing and we could have regard only to her reports. In view of Dr Phillips' concessions, Dr Borgman stood alone in her views that Mr Sheridan was suffering from a psychosis which was unrelated to his pre-1983 condition. Dr Bartle's evidence supports that of Dr Beckett. Taken on its own, we would not necessarily place great wight on Dr Bartle's opinion as he had only reviewed the reports of others. When taken with Dr Beckett's evidence, however, it does add weight to it. We have formed the opinion that the weight of evidence is that of Dr Beckett supported as it is by that of Dr Bartle and certainly not contradicted by that of Dr Phillips. We find, therefore, that Mr Sheridan retired because of a severe and chronic anxiety state with obsessive compulsive symptoms. This was not a new condition brought about by the events described in paragraphs 25-30 but was connected with, if not the same as that which Dr Mulholland had diagnosed. Based on Dr Beckett's evidence, we find that he was incapacitated for work because of that condition.

70. For these reasons, we

1. vary the decision dated 29 January, 1992 in respect of the benefit

classification certificate by:

(1) setting aside that part of the decision to issue a new benefit

classification certificate in relation to Mr Sheridan and specifying

pursuant to sub-section 6(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 the

conditions of obsessive compulsive neurosis and undifferentiated

personality disorder with obsessive features and undifferentiated

personality disorder together with bilateral sensori-neural hearing

loss;

(2) substituting for that part a decision that there be issued a new

benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions of severe

anxiety and varying depression in the context of severe compulsive

obsessive neurosis, undifferentiated personality disorder with obsessive

features and bilateral sensori-neural hearing loss; and

(3) otherwise affirming the decision; and

2. affirm the decision of the Commissioner under sub-section 66(2) of

the Superannuation Act 1976.
