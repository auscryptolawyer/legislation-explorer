Richard Walter Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1995] FCA 1491; (1995) 95 Atc 4554 (25 August 1995)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

RICHARD WALTER PTY LTD v COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. NG 974, 975 and 978 of 1992

FED No. 668/95

Number of pages - 8

Income Tax - Costs

(1995) 95 ATC 4554

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

TAMBERLIN J

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - whether Commissioner entitled to assess applicant on the basis of s 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) in circumstances where it has been found that certain transactions were caught by s 260 and, in the alternative, that they were also a "sham" - effect of potential for double recovery of tax.

Costs - whether costs should be awarded on an indemnity basis where a major issue raised was abandoned six days before hearing - principles discussed.

Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth) s 43

Colgate Palmolive Co v Cussons Pty Ltd [1993] FCA 536; (1993) 118 ALR 248

Davids Holdings Pty Ltd v Coles Myer Ltd (1995) ATPR 41-383

Trade Practices Commission v Nicholas Enterprises Pty Ltd (1979) 28 ALR 201

Ragata Developments Pty Ltd v Westpac Banking Corporation (FCA, 5 March
1993, unreported)

Cachia v Hanes (1991) 23 NSWLR 304

Huntsman Chemical Company Australia Ltd v International Pools Australia Pty
Ltd (1995) ATPR 41-403

Hancock v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258

HEARING

SYDNEY, 11 August 1995

25:8:1995

Counsel for Applicant: Mr R F Edmonds

Solicitors for Applicant: Mallesons Stephen Jaques

Counsel for Respondent: Ms M Gordon

Solicitor for Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The applications be dismissed.

2. The applicant pay the costs of the respondent.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in accordance with Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules

DECISION

TAMBERLIN J My Reasons for Judgment in matters Nos NG 973-978 of 1992 inclusive were delivered on 11 July 1995.

2. On that occasion I directed the respondent to bring in draft Short Minutes to give effect to the reasons for decision and stated that I would hear the parties on the question of costs when settling the Minutes of Order.

3. In relation to matters Nos NG 973, NG 976 and NG 977 I made orders by consent on Friday 11 August 1995.

4. Having made those orders there remains a dispute between the parties in relation to the appropriate orders to be made in matters NG 974, NG 975 and NG 978.

5. The first issue relates to matters NG 975 and NG 978 and it is whether the Commissioner is entitled to assess the applicant on the basis of s 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) ("the Act") in circumstances where I have found, in the alternative, that the transactions were caught by s 260, and that they were also a "sham" insofar as the payment of "loan" moneys was concerned with the consequence that moneys paid to Richard Walter Pty Ltd ("Richard Walter") were assessable income in its hands. In the judgment I found that the "loans" were payments made in reality with no obligation to repay the moneys advanced. The issue is important because assessment on the basis that s 260 applies to the whole transaction results in a substantially greater amount of tax payable, than that which would result from the striking down of the "loans" as a sham.

6. The second issue relates to matter No NG 974 and it turns on the question whether costs should be awarded on an indemnity basis in that matter in relation to a major issue raised in that matter by the applicant with respect to interest payments made to Linwood Finance Limited ("Linwood") in circumstances where that issue was abandoned only six days before the matters were due to be heard.

7. I will deal with the question of indemnity costs first.

Indemnity Costs - Relevant Principles

8. The relevant principles in relation to an application for indemnity costs are set out and discussed by Sheppard J in Colgate Palmolive Co v Cussons Pty Ltd [1993] FCA 536; (1993) 118 ALR 248. That case was recently applied by Drummond J in Davids Holdings Pty Ltd v Coles Myer Limited (1995) ATPR 41-383 at 40,298-9.

9. Section 43 of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Federal Court Act") confers power on the Court or a judge to award costs in all proceedings other than proceedings in respect of which any other Act provides that costs shall not be awarded. There is no dispute that the Court has jurisdiction and power to make orders for costs on an indemnity basis. This stems from both s 43 of the Federal Court Act and also because this Court is a superior court of record. The power of this Court to direct that costs be taxed on any particular basis of taxation other than the party and party basis is not constrained by rules of this Court.

10. The discretion to award costs on a particular basis is unfettered as to matters which must be taken into account but this discretion must be exercised judicially. See Trade Practices Commission v Nicholas Enterprises Pty Ltd (1979) 28 ALR 201 at 207.

11. Generally, the courts have taken the view that solicitor client costs or indemnity costs can properly be awarded in appropriate cases where there is some special or unusual feature in the case to justify the Court exercising its discretion. See Preston v Preston (1982) 1 All ER 41 at 58.

12. As Davies J pointed out in Ragata Developments Pty Ltd v Westpac Banking Corporation (FCA, 5 March 1993, unreported):

".... An award of costs on an indemnity basis

may be made only in a special case, where the

circumstances justify departure from the

ordinary principle. The circumstances must be

such as to justify an award indemnifying the

successful party in respect of all of the costs

incurred, save only as to those costs which are

unreasonable in amount.

The very nature of the award of costs on an

indemnity basis gives a guide to the type of

case in which such an award is appropriate.

Thus, indemnity costs may be awarded where

unsuccessful proceedings have been brought and

prosecuted, not for the bona fide purpose of

protecting and enforcing a legal right, but to

achieve an ulterior or extraneous purpose."

13. The categories in which the discretion may be exercised are not closed.

14. In Colgate (supra) at 257, Sheppard J made the following observations in which he outlined some examples of special circumstances:

"... it is useful to note some of the

circumstances which have been thought to warrant

the exercise of the discretion. I instance the

making of allegations of fraud knowing them to

be false and the making of irrelevant

allegations of fraud ....; evidence of

particular misconduct that causes loss of time

to the court and to other parties ...; the fact

that the proceedings were commenced or continued

for some ulterior motive ...; or in wilful

disregard of known facts or clearly established

law ...; the making of allegations which ought

never to have been made or the undue

prolongation of a case by groundless contentions

...; an imprudent refusal of an offer to

compromise ... and an award of costs on an

indemnity basis against a contemnor ... The

question must always be whether the particular

facts and circumstances of the case in question

warrant the making of an order for payment of

costs other than on a party and party basis.

... It remains to say that the existence of

particular facts and circumstances capable of

warranting the making of an order for payment of

costs, for instance, on the indemnity basis,

does not mean that judges are necessarily

obliged to exercise their discretion to make

such an order. The costs are always in the

discretion of the trial judge. Provided that

discretion is exercised having regard to the

applicable principles and the particular

circumstances of the instant case its exercise

will not be found to have miscarried unless it

appears that the order which has been made

involves a manifest error or injustice."

(Emphasis added)

15. Speaking of party and party costs Handley JA said in Cachia v Hanes (1991) 23 NSWLR 304 at 318:

" ... Litigation is already very expensive. The

limited indemnity provided to a successful

represented litigant for expense incurred and

time lost reflects a compromise between the

interests of successful and unsuccessful

litigants. It is also an important spur to

settlement. The rule that a litigant in person

can only recover out of pocket expenses also

represents a compromise between the interests of

successful and unsuccessful litigants. In my

opinion if the existing law is to be changed

this must be done either by the High Court or by

Parliament." (Emphasis added)

16. It is also instructive to consider the decision of the New South Wales Court of Appeal in Huntsman Chemical Company Australia Limited v International Pools Australia Pty Limited (1995) ATPR 41-403. In that case the Court declined to award costs on an indemnity basis even though the appeal before the Court was abandoned on the morning of the hearing.

Orders Sought

17. The order sought by the Commissioner in proceeding No NG 974 is that:

"The Applicant to pay the costs of the

Respondent on an indemnity basis which the

Respondent incurred in respect of whether the

Applicant was entitled to a deduction for

interest paid or accrued to Linwood Finance

Limited except insofar as they are of an

unreasonable amount or have been unreasonably

incurred."

18. The Linwood issue was abandoned by the taxpayer only six days before the trial commenced without any prior indication that the issue was proposed to be abandoned. No doubt a great deal of work was carried out by the Commissioner at interlocutory stages and in the course of preparation of the case to meet the applicant's case on this issue.

19. To justify an award of indemnity costs, the Commissioner relies, inter alia, on the following considerations:

"(1) the proceedings were commenced in December 1992;

(2) the Respondent filed its detailed Statement of Facts

and Contentions on 15 February 1993 and its principal

Affidavit relating to the Linwood Finance Arrangement

in April 1994;

(3) the Respondent's Affidavit is comprised primarily of

the Applicant's own documents;

(4) the Applicant has at all times been legally

represented by a major law firm, Mallesons Stephen

Jaques and by Counsel;

(5) by reason of section 14ZZO of the Tax Administration

Act, the Applicant bears the onus of proof in these

proceedings;

(6) despite bearing the onus of proof as referred to

above, the Applicant only intended to call Mr Holden

when, at the very least, Mr Lear was the main

architect of the loan transaction the subject of the

appeal and, it is submitted, only Mr Lear could have

answered the various matters relating to the

transaction. Accordingly, it is submitted that the

Applicant could not (and knew that it could not)

succeed in any appeal and in particular, could not and

knew that it could not discharge the onus of proof;

(7) the Applicant's own documents reveal that the Linwood

transaction was "contrived" from the outset."

20. The Commissioner's case in summary is that it is clear that Richard Walter knew or ought to have known that its case was hopeless. There was a lengthy delay in discontinuing that part of the appeal which was unwarranted notwithstanding that the taxpayer had legal representation from the outset. It is said that it was clear that the Linwood transaction was a "sham" or at least a blatant attempt to avoid payment of tax and that if an indemnity order is not made, the Australian taxpayers will have to bear the costs of preparation to meet a case which has been abandoned as hopeless.

Conclusions on Costs

21. While there is force in the matters pointed to by the Commissioner I am not satisfied they justify departure from the settled principle that costs should be awarded on a party and party basis.

22. The operation and consequences of the Linwood Finance arrangement were complex in a factual sense and involved difficult questions as to the application of the law to those facts. From the evidence in the other applications which did proceed to hearing before me I do not consider it can properly be said that the question of the Linwood arrangement was hopeless or not reasonably arguable or was doomed to failure. Settlements, particularly in complex litigation, should be encouraged and to impose costs on other than a party and party basis militates against this desirable result.

23. Moreover, one important feature of the evidence in the present case was that a body of material was referred to and addressed on by the Commissioner in relation to the Linwood arrangement in order to set the background context against which the overall arrangements made by the Richard Walter group ("the group") were implemented, in order to impress those arrangements in the period 1980-1984 with the character of tax avoidance. Such evidence, it was submitted, went to support the inference that the group and its advisers were committed to contrive tax avoidance schemes of a highly artificial nature over almost a decade. I am therefore by no means satisfied that the preparation undertaken in relation to the Linwood arrangement was thrown away as the result of that issue having been abandoned.

24. Having regard to the foregoing, I am not persuaded that there should be any order as to costs on an indemnity basis, but rather my view is that the costs awarded in favour of the Commissioner in relation to the Linwood issue should be on a party and party basis.

Basis for Assessment - s 260 or "sham"

25. The other issue relates to the appropriate basis of assessment.

26. I have decided that the loan transactions were a sham and that the moneys paid to Richard Walter purportedly as "loans" were assessable income in its hands.

27. Also, in the alternative, I have found that the loan transactions come within s 260 of the Act and that the moneys received constituted assessable income to it as set out above.

28. Thirdly, again in the alternative, I have decided that the arrangement involving the 5 steps conceded by Richard Walter to be caught by s 260 resulted in Richard Walter being assessable to income tax, after the operation of s 260.

29. Counsel for Richard Walter in substance submits that because the loan arrangement was struck down as a sham it is not open to the Commissioner to assess Richard Walter on the basis of the higher amount which would result if the s 260 assessment were upheld. It is submitted that s 260 only operates with respect to transactions that are otherwise valid and legally effective. In support of this, reference is made to the comments of Windeyer J in Hancock v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258 at 301 where his Honour said:

"In a case under s. 260 one starts with the

position that the arrangement that is void as

against the Commissioner is valid as between the

parties - that is to say that it is a legal

reality and not a sham; because, if it were a

sham, it would fall in any event, and without

the aid of s. 260. But subordinate steps taken

in the carrying out of an arrangement that is

within s. 260 may, it seems to me, be shams.

Indeed one of the indications that an

arrangement is vulnerable under the section may

be that it was to be carried into effect by

fictitious procedures. Those who devise methods

for enabling taxpayers to order their affairs so

as to attract the least possible amount of tax

do not always appreciate that, quite apart from

s. 260, the taxpayer must enter into a real not

a pretended transaction to effect his purpose

..." (Emphasis added).

30. In my view the "loans" in the present case can properly be described as a "subordinate step" taken in the course of carrying out a tax avoidance arrangement.

31. Whilst it must be accepted that s 260 can only apply to a transaction which actually exists and is a real transaction, nevertheless, in the present case, the only matters which were attacked as "shams" were the so-called "loans" to Richard Walter. This was the sixth step in the scheme particularised by the Commissioner. The effect of this is that no loans, in my opinion, were made and that the "loan" moneys were received by Richard Walter and were assessable in its hands.

32. The factual situation which remained after the application of the "sham" principle was simply that moneys were paid to Richard Walter and were properly characterised as income.

33. The finding in relation to sham does not mean that s 260 cannot apply in relation to the overall arrangement. Cf Hancock (supra) at 301.

34. I went on to hold that the effect of the first five steps after the application of s 260 was that the funds must be treated as having flowed through to Richard Walter as a beneficiary under the Aborda Trust. This is a separate question from that relating to the "loans". I have not found that the transactions comprised in the first five steps which were designed to avoid tax were "shams" but only the "loans".

35. In relation to the first five particularised steps the main debate between the parties was as to the effect of the striking down and whether income distributed after the elimination of those steps was assessable income to Richard Walter and as to the quantum. The holding was that after the striking down of those steps the funds were channelled through to Richard Walter and therefore assessable to it because it had a present entitlement to these funds. Whether the funds were given away in fact or were used as loans or were otherwise spent at the direction of Richard Walter is not to the point. Accordingly, the alternative assessment on the basis of s 260 should be sustained. It is in my view open to the Commissioner to assess Richard Walter on the basis of s 260 notwithstanding that one step in the arrangement was a sham.

36. It is also submitted that, if recovery is allowed on the basis of s 260, there is a potential double recovery and it is said that there is no mechanism in the Act for adjusting the rights of the parties in such a situation. I do not find this submission persuasive.

37. I have been assured by Counsel for the Commissioner that the Commissioner would not double tax or seek double recovery. The Commissioner takes the position that he is entitled to assess on the higher amount on the basis of the application of s 260. In these circumstances I am satisfied that there is no likelihood of double recovery and I consider that the Commissioner is entitled to assess on the higher amount resulting from the application of s 260 to the arrangement in question.

Conclusion

38. Accordingly, I conclude in matters No NG 975 and NG 978 that the Commissioner is entitled to assess on the higher of the alternative bases, namely on the basis that s 260 applies to the transaction.

39. I also conclude that in matter No NG 974 the costs of the Commissioner in respect of the Linwood issue should be paid by Richard Walter on a party and party basis.

40. Since each party has to some extent been successful in relation to the arguments as to the orders I make no order as to the costs of and relating to the drawing up and settling of these short minutes of orders.
