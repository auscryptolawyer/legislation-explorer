QT86/1732 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 902; 87 ATC 183 (2 January 1987)

QT86/1732 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 902 (2 January 1987); 87 ATC 183

Last Updated: 8 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Rebates - Concessional Expenditure - medical expenses - fees paid to chiropractor and naturopath, whether payments to a legally qualified medical practitioner or payments for theraputic treatment by direction of a legally qualified medical practitioner.

FC of T v. Wade 11951) [1951] HCA 66; 84 CLR 105, 9 ATD 337, 5 AITR 214

Case F50 74 ATC 276

Case 96 1 TBRD 392

Case A53 69 ATC 313

Case S2 85 ATC 102

Case S8 17 TBRD 31

DECISION AND REASONS

Re: QT86/1732

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

Nos : QT86/1732

QT86/2164

AAT Decision No: 3147

Date: 2 January 1987

Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) NOS. QT86/1732

) QT86/2164

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: QT86/1732

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS

The question at issue in this reference is whether the applicant is entitled to a rebate in respect of fees paid to two persons in respect of chiropractic treatment and in respect of naturopathy. In respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1983 the claim amounts to $1,190 and in the 1984 year of income the claim amounts to $117.

  1. The relevant provision of the Income Tax Assessment Act is s159P. That section provides for a rebate under s159N in respect of medical expenses paid by the taxpayer in respect of himself, or in respect of dependents subject to certain qualifications as to reimbursement.
  2. The particular provision in issue before the Tribunal is the definition of medical expenses under sub-section 159P (4). So far as is relevant "medical expenses" is defined to mean:-
    • (a) payments to a legally qualified medical practitioner, nurse or chemist, in respect of an illness or operation; or
    • (b) payments for theraputic treatment administered by direction of a legally qualified medical practitioner.
  3. The hearing of the application was conducted on the basis that the applicant could not succeed unless he brought the expenditure claimed within either of the limbs of the definition of medical expenses. I agree with the basis upon which both parties conducted their case as there is no other provision in the Income Tax Assessment Act which could provide a rebate, or a deduction, in respect of such expenses. To succeed the applicant must bring those expenses within defined meanings of medical expenses.
  4. The Tribunal has not been made aware of how the amounts in dispute have been calculated or as to the nature of the services provided and to whom those services were provided. The applicant stated in evidence that he had destroyed the receipts in question given by the chiropractor and the naturopath and furthermore that he had been unable to obtain duplicates of those receipts. The Commissioner's representative did not cross examine the applicant and I must assume was content to accept the evidence in chief given by the applicant in respect of the claim. The applicant gave evidence that the amounts claimed had in fact been expended but was unable to provide any dissection as to how the amounts were made up.
  5. It became apparent during the course of evidence that the amounts claimed were in respect of treatment given to the taxpayer himself and also in respect of treatment given to members of his family. No evidence was lead as to the nature of the treatment given to the family members. However the evidence given by the applicant explained in some detail the nature of the treatment given to him and I infer that a substantial proportion of the amounts claimed was in respect of the applicant taxpayer.

7. It seems that in June 1980 the applicant suffered what is commonly, although sometimes euphemistically, described as a slipped disc. This put him in severe pain and as a consequence he consulted a medical practitioner. The medical practitioner prescribed pain killers and arranged for x-rays to be taken of the applicant's back. Other medicines were also prescribed. The applicant had an adverse reaction from prescribed drugs and none of this treatment succeeded in improving the taxpayer's health generally, or his back in particular. After a considerable amount of treatment by the medical practitioner the applicant requested the medical practitioner to refer him to a chiropractor or physiotherapist for theraputic treatment.

  1. Apparently this request was not favourably received by the medical practitioner who, according to the applicant, got upset by the suggestion, describing chiropractors as charlatans. The medical practitioner also responded to the suggestion of chiropractic treatment by suggesting in turn that the nerves of the applicant's spine should be cut. The applicant did not agree to this proposed course of treatment.
  2. The applicant then sought advice from another medical practitioner who also refused to recommend chiropractic treatment. Finally, on the recommendation of a friend who is not a medical practitioner, the applicant went to a chiropractor without any reference from a medical practitioner. As a consequence of this chiropractic treatment there was a substantial improvement in the applicant's health. It must be understood that the above recitation of facts is taken entirely from the applicant's evidence before this Tribunal.

10. Unfortunately for the applicant the back problem re-occurred in 1982, hence the present claim. Again the applicant first went to a medical practitioner who also was not prepared to recommend chiropractic treatment and as a consequence the applicant returned to the chiropractor who had successfully treated him as a consequence of the 1980 incident. On this occasion, the chiropractor not only administered chiropractic treatment but also prescribed treatment which is generally known as naturopathy. There is no dissection available to the Tribunal as to what proportion of the treatment was naturopathy.

11. The applicant stated in evidence, and as I have already stated the Commissioner's representative chose not to cross examine the applicant, that claims for the fees paid to the chiropractor were allowed by the Commissioner as rebates for the years ended 30 June 1981, 1982, and 1985. The applicant also stated that he had been encouraged to make these claims for the rebate by the chiropractor who apparently stated to the applicant that another of his patients was a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation who had advised the chiropractor that claims for fees in respect of chiropractic treatment should be made for taxation purposes by the chiropractor's clients. That was the basis of the applicant's evidence to the Tribunal. It was not asserted that the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation had stated that fees paid to chiropractors came within the definition of medical expenses. The subtle difference would not be readily apparent to a layman and I am quite satisfied that the applicant thought he was justified in making the claims.

  1. For the year of income ended 30 June 1984 the claim is in respect of naturopathy only and was paid to another person because the chiropractor referred to above retired from business in 1983. The applicant did not pursue the claim for these naturopathy fees before the Tribunal although he did not formally concede that the Commissioner's decision on the objection should be affirmed.
  2. The applicant's case was based upon the undoubted fact that chiropractic treatment is theraputic treatment and therefore his claim should be allowed under sub-section 159P(4). He sought to rely upon a ruling of the Commissioner of Taxation, being Taxation Ruling IT2146 dated 21 March 1985. However, he was unable to produce a copy of that ruling although clearly he had had access to it through his taxation agent. A copy of the ruling is not in evidence before the Tribunal.
  3. The Commissioner's representative did not lead any evidence other than to provide the Tribunal with copies of extracts of the Medical Act (Qld) and the Chiropractic Manipulative Therapists Act (Qld). The Commissioner's representative also tendered certified extracts from the Register of Medical Practitioners maintained by the Medical Board of Queensland. The extracts tendered proved that neither of the persons to whom the applicant paid fees is a registered medical practitioner in the State of Queensland. The applicant did not seek to persuade the Tribunal that either of these persons was a registered medical practitioner.

15. In summary then the applicant's claim is for fees paid to a chiropractor and a naturopath, neither of whom is a registered medical practitioner. Nor was the applicant referred to these persons by a registered medical practitioner so there is no basis for finding that the fees were in respect of theraputic treatment administered by direction of a medically qualified medical practitioner. The evidence is to the contrary - the applicant went to the chiropractor against the advice of the medical practitioners whom he consulted. There is therefore simply no basis in the evidence upon which the Tribunal can find that the treatment was administered in terms of paragraph (d) of the definition of medical expenses in sub-section 159P(4).

  1. The Medical Act of Queensland defines a medical practitioner as a person registered as a medical practitioner and whose name remains upon the Register of Medical Practitioners, Queensland.
  2. By section 19 of the Medical Act the Medical Board appointed under that Act is required to maintain a register of medical practitioners. I do not need to consider section 19 in detail as it is beyond doubt that the chiropractor and the naturopath in question are not registered as medical practitioners.

18. However the chiropractor is, according to the applicant registered under the Chiropractic Manipulative Theraputists Act. Under that Act a Chiropractic Manipulative Theraputists Board is required to maintain a register of chiropractors and as I understand the Act it is only such registered chiropractors who are entitled by law to practice chiropractic in the State of Queensland. I am prepared to accept, although it was not really established, that the chiropractor in question was a registered chiropractor and therefore legally qualified.

  1. No claim was made and in fact the applicant asserted to the contrary, that the naturopath was registered under any Act of Parliament be it Queensland or any other part of Australia.
  2. The question then is whether the chiropractor was a legally qualified medical practitioner, nurse or chemist. Clearly he is not a chemist and I think just as clearly he is not a nurse. I am also of the view and I so find that he is not a legally qualified medical practitioner because he is not legally qualified in terms of the Medical Act to practise medicine. I agree with the view of Taxation Board of Review No 2 in Case F50, 74 ATC 276 as to the meaning of the words "legally qualified".
  3. I have also considered the decision of Taxation Board of Review No 3 in Case 96 1 TBRD 392 where the Board found that an osteopath was not a legally qualified medical practitioner according to the law in Australia although he was legally qualified to practise as an osteopath overseas and the decision of Taxation Board of Review No 2 in Case A53 69 ATC 313 where the Board had to consider whether treatment provided by two chiropractors was theraputic treatment administered by direction of a legally qualified medical practitioner. The Board held that the cost of the treatment was not within the definition of medical expenses, in that case because although the treatment had been obtained by the taxpayer at the suggestion of his medical practitioner the treatment had not been given by direction and therefore was not administered by direction of a medical practitioner.
  4. The applicant relied on a decision of Taxation Board of Review No 3 reported as Case S2 85 ATC 102. In that case the family physician had decided to substitute goats milk for cows milk in the diet of the taxpayer's son. The change in diet was successful but the taxpayer's claim for the cost of goats milk as a medical expense was unsuccessful before the Board. There is nothing in the Board's reasons which provided any assistance to the applicant in this case.
  5. A somewhat similar situation gave rise to a claim for medical expenses in Case S8 17 TBRD 31 which was also heard by Taxation Board of Review No 3 as then constituted. There the claim was for the expenses in connection with the maintenance of a goat used as an alternative supply of milk. The Board held that the costs of maintaining the goat were not medical expenses. Although the applicant quoted this case in support of his claim I cannot see how he derives any support from it, not only because of the different factual basis for the Board's decision but also because the claim failed before the Board anyway.

24. I have considered the applicant's claim that certain advice was given to the chiropractor by a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation which was in turn conveyed by the chiropractor to the applicant in relation to claiming fees paid to a registered chiropractor as medical expenses. I have also considered the applicant's claim that the Commissioner allowed rebates for fees paid to chiropractors in respect of three years of income. One might be excused upon being confronted with both of these pieces of evidence for thinking that fees paid to chiropractors, without more, are medical expenses as defined and therefore rebatable. However, the correct position is, as I see it, that fees paid to a chiropractor are payments for theraputic treatment but more is required before they become medical expenses as defined because the theraputic treatment must be administered by direction of a legally qualified medical practitioner. Therefore I must assume that the Deputy Commissioner's informal advice was either misunderstood or incomplete. I also must assume that either the facts in the earlier and later years of income, when claims for chiropractic fees were accepted by the Commissioner, were different or the Commissioner made a mistake in treating the claims for fees in those years as medical expenses. It is well established that no conduct on the part of the Commissioner can operate as a estoppel against the operation of the Act (per Kitto J in FC of T v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105, 9 ATD 337, 5 AITR 214).

25. The Commissioner's decisions on the objections were correct and will be affirmed.

This and the preceding ten (10) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matters of QT86/1732 and QT86/2164.
