QT94/116 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 144; 95 ATC 313; (1995) 30 ATR 1335 (30 May 1995)

Last Updated: 11 February 2009



TAXATION : Income Tax - allowable deduction - interest on loan - executor of deceased estate - testator's family maintenance - use of funds considered - not connected to income producing activities



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : S.51



Begg v FCT (1937) 4 ATD 257

Public Trustee v Commissioner of Taxes (1938) NZLR 436

Williams v CIR (1988) 12 TRNZ 16

Yeung v FCT 88 ATC 4193

FCT v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153

FCT v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd 798 ATC 4279.

Ure v FCT [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 11 ATR 484, 81 ATC 4100

Fletcher and ors v FCT [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1

FCT v Roberts and Smith 92 ATC 4380

Date : 30 May 1995

Tribunal : Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



DECISION



The Tribunal decides that the objection decision under review be affirmed.



(Sgd) K L Beddoe

(SENIOR MEMBER)



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

Applicant

Respondent



Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 30 May 1995

Place: Brisbane



REASONS FOR DECISION



By a notice dated 11 April 1994 the respondent notified the applicant that an objection against the assessment of taxable income for the year of income ended 30 June 1988 had been disallowed. By an application filed in this Tribunal on 8 June 1994 the applicant Trustee seeks review of that objection decision.



2. The issue involves a claim for a deduction for interest said to have been paid by the Trustee during the year of income. The only statutory provision which is suggested to be relevant is subsection 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). Subsection 51(1) provides that losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions. That provision is subject to exceptions which are expressed in the subsection but which are not said to be relevant in the proceedings before the Tribunal. The issue is confined to whether or not the claim comes within the first or second limbs of the section and has not been argued on the basis that one of the excluding provisions in subsection 51(1) would apply to exclude the claim for deduction.



3. At the hearing of this matter Mr Hack of counsel appeared for the applicant trustee and Mr McGill of Senior Counsel appeared for the respondent Commissioner. The documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were placed before the Tribunal as the T documents and a number of other documents were placed into evidence and marked as exhibits. Oral evidence was given to the Tribunal by the applicant and the applicant's accountant.



4. The facts relating to this matter are essentially contained in Exhibits A, D and F. It is not necessary that I set out in detail the facts as set out in those affidavits because they were effectively not in dispute before the Tribunal and to do so would be to risk breaching section 14ZZJ of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. I formally find the facts as set out in the affidavits which are marked as Exhibit A, Exhibit D and also the valuation report which is marked as Exhibit F.



5. Exhibit E is a statutory declaration by a solicitor, Brian Richard Galvin, which establishes that an amount of $150,000 was paid by the applicant as the result of a Supreme Court decision in relation to a testator's family maintenance application. The statutory declaration also proves that the payment was made on 24 March 1988, that is during the subject year of income.



6. The applicant is the executor of a deceased estate. The deceased died on 15 November 1984 and probate in the estate was granted on 29 May 1985. Subsequent to the grant of probate an application was made to the Supreme Court of Queensland under the testator family maintenance provisions of the Succession Act. The result of that application to the Supreme Court was that the Court made an order that provision be made out of the estate in favour of the applicant for testator family maintenance to the extent of $150,000 and that such amount was to be paid on or before 24 March 1988. The Court further ordered that the executor be at liberty to encumber the assets of the estate in an amount not exceeding $150,000 for the purpose of making the said payment awarded by the Court, costs of the proceedings in the Supreme Court to be taxed and paid out of the said estate.



7. The applicant took advice from various people including the estate's solicitor and the estate's accountant and decided in all the circumstances that the estate should borrow the amount of $150,000 as provided for in the Supreme Court's order. As it transpired that money was borrowed from the applicant and her husband with interest payable by the estate at two points above the Westpac indicator rate. Before deciding to borrow the $150,000 from herself and her husband the applicant made enquiries of Westpac Bank and taking all factors into account, including certain advantages to the estate by borrowing from herself and her husband, she decided to take that course of action. In particular, the applicant envisaged advantages to the estate by such a borrowing because to do so would be to avoid bank establishment and administration fees and also avoid account reviews and valuation fees. It would also appear that by the informal borrowing, rather than the formal borrowing through a bank, the applicant avoided the need to encumber the assets of the estate.



8. At 28 January 1988 the executor had in hand an amount of $79,991.00 as cash held in various bank accounts. There were also at that time other assets of the estate which could have been liquidated, however, the applicant was concerned as to various outstanding debts of the estate, including legal costs awarded against the estate by the Supreme Court, which would become payable although, as it transpired, some of those costs did not become payable in the immediate future. In all, the legal costs added up to an amount of $36,998.



9. Accepting as I do that the executor considered it was necessary to borrow the $150,000 to satisfy the order of the Supreme Court, the question I have to decide is whether the interest paid in respect of those moneys borrowed is an allowable deduction within the terms of subsection 51(1).



10. The essence of the applicant's case is that the $150,000 was borrowed because the executors were required by the terms of the will to maintain the corpus for the benefit of the residual beneficiary which had been devised the whole of the estate subject to life interests and legacies. The residual beneficiary is a religious charitable organisation. The terms of the Will became subject to the order of the Supreme Court but I accept the executor's evidence that she was concerned to preserve the estate as a viable venture for the benefit of the residual beneficiary. I also accept her evidence that the funds were borrowed from the executors for the advantage of the estate as compared with an armslength borrowing from a lender such as a bank. The moneys were borrowed in accordance with and to satisfy the order of the Court so as to maintain the income producing assets of the estate.



11. Mr Hack submitted that the interest charges incurred on the borrowed moneys come within the positive limbs of subsection 51(1) and, in particular, were incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing the assessable income of the business. Mr McGill SC submits to the contrary.



12. In particular, Mr Hack relied upon the decisions in Begg v FCT (1937) 4 ATD 257, Public Trustee v Commissioner of Taxes (1938) NZLR 436, Williams v CIR (1988) 12 TRNZ 16, Yeung v FCT 88 ATC 4193. He also relied upon dicta in FCT v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 and FCT v Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Ltd 79 ATC 4279.



13. In Yeung the relevant issue involved interest incurred by a partnership for taxation purposes on moneys borrowed to allow the partnership to repay moneys previously advanced to the partnership on capital account. In essence a capital contribution was replaced by an interest bearing loan. At page 4204 Davies J said:



"I am prepared to accept that from the partnership's point of view, what occurred was that two of the partners decided to withdrawn funds from the partnership. It does not materially matter whether those funds were loan funds, or capital which the partners had invested in the properties. The notional payment out to Dr and Mrs Yeung and the borrowing of an amount from Ozanu Pty Ltd necessarily effected a change in the capital interests which each of the partners had in the partnership. What the partnership achieved by the borrowing from Ozanu Pty Ltd was the maintenance of the income earning properties. Funds were withdrawn, but were replaced by loan funds and the income-earning properties remained held by the six members of the family.



In an income-earning enterprise, both income and equity capital may be invested in assets directed to the earning of income. In such an event, if equity capital is repaid and loan capital replaces it, interest payable on the loan capital will ordinarily be an allowable deduction from the income derived from the assets. This is because the assets held represent the equity and the loan capital and, if the assets are directed to the earning of income, then both the loan capital and the equity capital which they represent are devoted to the earning of assessable income."



Davies J decided that the loan moneys had been applied for the purpose of investing in the income producing properties of the partnership and the interest incurred on the loan was an allowable deduction to the partnership.



14. By way of contrast is the decision of the High Court in Munro. There income producing property was encumbered by a mortgage to secure a borrowing from a bank. The borrowed money was applied as to £20,000 for payment for 20,000 shares in a company and the balance of £10,000 was advanced to the company without interest. Of the shares, 18,000 were allotted to the taxpayer's sons and the remaining 2,000 were allotted to the taxpayer. The taxpayer contended that the loan was secured by the income producing property and the interest charges were therefore incurred in gaining the assessable income irrespective of the purpose for which the money was borrowed.



15. That proposition was rejected by the High Court. At page 171 Knox CJ found against the taxpayer as follows:



"In this case the assessable income of the taxpayer was in no way referable to the transaction with the bank out of which the liability to pay interest arose, and the loan by the Bank was in no way instrumental in or conducive to the production of the assessable income or that part of it which consisted of the rents of the freehold property. The respondent was, before the mortgage was given, entitled to the whole of these rents, and he did not gain them nor were they produced in consequence of the payment of interest. The interest was paid, not for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income of the taxpayer, but for the purpose of satisfying pro tanto a debt which the taxpayer had incurred with a view, not to the production of his assessable income, but to the production of income by the company for the benefit of its shareholders. The debt having been incurred for a purpose wholly unconnected with the production of assessable income of the respondent, I think it impossible to say that the interest paid on the amount of the debt was money wholly and exclusively laid out or expended for the production of his assessable income."



16. Isaacs J came to the same conclusion at pages 197-8. Higgins J concurred with Knox CJ at page 204 while Rich J at page 210 merely agreed that the appeal be allowed at page 210. At pages 217-8 Starke J expressed his own reasons not dissimilar to the reasons of Knox CJ.



17. A case more closely aligned with the present case is the decision of the Supreme Court of South Australia (Reed AJ) in Begg. Two issues arose in that case. The executors of a deceased estate decided not to dispose of the income producing assets of the estate because of depressed economic conditions. However, the executors were liable to pay and the assets of the estate were charged with liability for payment of Estate Duty and Succession Duty. These duties were paid by the executors through a bank overdraft facility. The executors claimed a deduction for the interest charges on this facility in calculating the net income of the estate. The executors had also decided to defer payment of certain legacies resulting in interest being paid to the legatees.



18. The Court held that the interest incurred on the overdraft facility had a relevant connection with the derivation of assessable income but no such connection was found between the interest paid on the legacies and the derivation of the assessable income.



19. Counsel for the Commissioner in Begg's Case relied on the decision in Munro. However, that decision was distinguished, the Court holding that for the interest outgoing to be an allowable deduction it was not necessary to establish that the outgoing must directly cause the production of income.



20. In its judgment the Court used dicta which might be thought to apply to the present case. At pages 269-70 Reed AJ stated:



"The payments of interest in the present case fall into two groups - one, the interest paid to the banks on the borrowed moneys : the other, the interest paid on the legacies.



As regards the former, I find that the moneys paid in each year were moneys wholly and exclusively laid out and expended for the production of assessable income, and were outgoings actually incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income for the year, within the meaning of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. As I have already pointED out, one effect of the borrowings was to preserve most of the assets in the form in which they were at the testator's death. It is clear from the evidence that, if the executors had sold some of the assets to pay the duties, the income producing value of the estate must have dropped. They were under no obligation to sacrifice the estate ; indeed, the course which they adopted was one which was within their powers, and which prudence dictated. Executors, who have power to postpone the sale of an estate, and have also powers to borrow money to pay duties and meet other outgoings, would be failing in their duty if they were to pursue a course which would involve the estate in heavy losses of capital values, and also deprive it of assets producing income at good rates of interest. To act in such a way as to avoid such an undesirable course is to save portion of the income from extinction. In this case income so saved was certainly being earned when the executors became entitled to the property ; but the very circumstances under which they acquired the estate imposed a liability, the satisfaction of which would necessarily reduce that income. Borrowing money on the security of the estate kept it intact, and the payment of interest on the borrowed money kept the loans alive, and so enabled the estate to continue to produce income."



22. A similar approach was taken by the New Zealand Court of Appeal in Public Trustee v Commissioner of Taxes (1938) NZLR 436. Myers CJ said at page 452:



"What the estate has in fact done is substantially the same thing, and has the same effect. The borrowed money was employed, it has been suggested, not in the production of income, but in the payment of a debt or charge. That, however, is not, to my mind, the true position. I think that the suggestion involves a confusion of ideas : it confuses the purposes of expenditure with the result of such expenditure. As Dixon J said in Herald and Weekly Times, Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113 :



'The question whether money is expended in and for the production of assessable income cannot be determined by considering only the immediate reason for making a payment and ignoring the purpose with which the liability was incurred.' Ibid 118.



If the suggestion made on behalf of the Commissioner is sound, then a merchant who borrows money to enable him to pay off debts incurred by him in his business, and to continue to employ his existing assets in production of income, would not be permitted, in the calculation of his assessable income, to deduct the interest on the moneys so borrowed. The true inference, I think, in the present case is that the money borrowed enabled the trustee to pay out of the estate the amount of the death duties and left the money so borrowed or its equivalent in capital assets in the estate to be employed in the production of income."



And at pages 454-55:



"Here, the death duties were not a voluntary debt. They were a debt of the estate, which was charged upon the estate, and which the trustee was compelled to pay. The Death Duties Act, 1923, authorizes him to borrow money upon the security of the assets of the estate in order to enable him to pay the duties. It was not therefore a voluntary expense incurred by the estate as the Privy Council held the payment in Ward and Co.'s case [1923] A.C. 145 to have been. Here, also, the money was borrowed in order to prevent reduction of the income. The borrowed money was not employed to quote the words of Isaacs J for purposes alien to or independent of the property, and, to use the language of Knox CJ, the loan here was instrumental in or conducive to the production of the assessable income. It cannot be said that the debt was incurred for a purpose wholly unconnected with the production of the assessable income of the estate. On the contrary, it was incurred for the very purpose of maintaining the income of the estate and preventing its reduction."



The reasoning of Myers CJ became the ratio decidendi of the Court of Appeal in deciding that an undetermined proportion of the interest incurred was an allowable deduction.



23. In Williams v CIR (1988) TRNZ 16 the High Court of New Zealand (Barker J) discussed the decision in Public Trustee but did not follow it preferring to follow subsequent decisions which have emphasised the use made of the borrowed moneys rather than the purpose for the borrowing.



24. In Ure v FCT [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 11 ATR 484; 81 ATC 4100, the issue was a claim for deduction of interest payments incurred by the taxpayer for borrowed moneys on lent to associated persons at a nominal rate of interest considerably less than the rate incurred. In a joint judgment Deane and Sheppard JJ formulated the question to be answered as follows at ATR 494:



"The question whether an outgoing should properly be seen as being wholly or in part 'incidental and relevant' to the 'end' of gaining or producing the assessable income and the question whether the outgoing is wholly or in part of a private or domestic nature are both questions of characterization. Where liability to make the outgoing has been voluntarily incurred, those questions of characterization will ordinarily be determined by reference to 'the object' which the taxpayer had in view: Latham CJ W Nevill & Co Ltd v FC of T [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290 at 301; [1937] HCA 9; 1 AITR 67 at 71, the 'result aimed at' by the taxpayer (per McTiernan J, supra at 308; 77) or 'the advantage which the expenditure was intended to gain, directly or indirectly, for the taxpayer' (per Gibbs J FC of T v South Australian Battery Makers Pty Ltd [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 at 660; [1978] HCA 32; 8 ATR 879 at 887) in the context of the relevant facts and circumstances. In the ordinary case where the income which is expected to flow from an outgoing offers an obvious commercial explanation for incurring it the relevant characterization can readily be determined by reference to the gaining or producing of that income. In the more complex case however, where there is no such obvious commercial explanation, the solution of the problem of characterization must be derived from a weighing of the many aspects of the whole set of circumstances including direct and indirect objects and advantages which the taxpayer sought in making the outgoing."



And at page 495:



"In a case such as the present where the outgoing claimed as a deduction is interest paid on borrowed money, one cannot ordinarily look to the direct object or advantage which the outgoing was intended to achieve for the reason that that will ordinarily be the receipt of the borrowed money which is likely to be neutral in character. One must, of necessity, look more to the objects or advantages which the application and use of the borrowed money were intended to gain: FC of T v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 at 197."



25. Whatever the difficulties may have been because of the decisions in Begg and the New Zealand cases those difficulties have now been resolved by the decision of the High Court in Fletcher and ors v FCT [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1. The decision in Munro was applied by the Court at page 19 as follows:



"In the present case, the outgoings of interest in the tax years were incurred in the borrowing of money. The funds borrowed did not constitute assessable income. To the extent that the outgoings of interest incurred in the borrowing can properly be characterized as of a kind referred to in the first limb of s. 51(1), they must draw their character from the use of the borrowed funds (see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153, at p.197; Ure (1981) 50 FLR at pp.223, 232; 34 ALR at pp.241, 249)."



26. In FCT v Roberts and Smith 92 ATC 4380 the Full Court of the Federal Court considered claims for deduction of interest incurred by partners in a professional practice. The partners borrowed moneys to allow a reduction of capital for existing partners in the firm. Smith claimed interest arising from this arrangement. Roberts, on the other hand, claimed interest incurred on a loan the funds from which were used to purchase an interest in the partnership and succeeded with the claim.



27. Hill J delivered the leading judgment. His Honour summarised the law relating to deductibility of interest at pages 4386-4389. At page 4389 his Honour refers to the difficulty of reconciling the decision in Begg with the decision in Munro. Hill J concluded that the borrowing by Smith as an existing partner was for the provision of funds to Smith to which he was not entitled during the currency of the partnership. There was therefore no essential connection with the income producing activities of the partnership. Nor was the borrowing incidental and relevant to the partnership business. However, the taxpayer (Smith) was entitled to succeed to the extent that the capital account included moneys which Smith was entitled to withdraw from the account. As that issue was not raised in the findings of the Tribunal, the matter was remitted to the Tribunal to make the necessary findings.



28. In the context of the present case the apparent conflict between Munro and Begg has now been resolved. Begg is not an exception to Munro but must be treated as a decision of narrow compass depending upon its own particular facts.



29. The applicant borrowed funds from her husband and herself for the purpose of maintaining the capital structure of the income producing activities of the estate, albeit, as Mr McGill argued, those income producing activities were not very successful in producing income. But the funds were not used for that purpose. They were applied to a non-income producing purpose - the satisfaction of the Supreme Court order. The use of the funds was not therefore connected with the income producing activities and the interest outgoing claimed does not fall within either of the positive limbs of section 51 of the Act.



30. For these reasons the objection decision under review must be affirmed.



I hereby certify that this and the preceding nine (9) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matter of QT94/116 and COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION
