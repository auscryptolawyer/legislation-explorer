L M & T M R Quinlivan and the Quinlivan Family Trust and Chief Executive Officer of Customs [1997] AATA 138 (28 April 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

L M & T M R QUINLIVAN and THE QUINLIVAN FAMILY TRUST v. CHIEF

EXECUTIVE OFFICER OF CUSTOMS

No. W96/277

AAT No. 11810

Number of pages - 10

Customs

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

T.E. BARNETT (Deputy President) AND R.D. FAYLE (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Customs - Duties - Diesel Fuel Rebate - Customs Act 1901 - s164(1) and s164(7) definitions of "primary production", "agriculture", "gathering in of crops", and "core agricultural activity" - whether artificial grain drying carried out off-farm after harvesting crop but before delivery into permanent storage part of "gathering in of crops".

Customs Act 1901 s.164(1), (7)

Australian Native Landscapes Pty Ltd v Collector of Customs NG 853 of 1995 Herbert Adams Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 27; (1932) 47 CLR 222 D & R Henderson (MFG) Pty Ltd v Collector of Customs for the State of New South Wales (1974) 48 ALJR 132 Australian Grains, Morescope Publishing, Victoria, Ed. Bob Collins, 1994 Oxford Dictionary; New Shorter Oxford Dictionary; and Macquarie Dictionary; World Book Encyclopedia, World Book International, London, 1996 ed.

HEARING

PERTH, 3 April 1997 28:4:1997

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr H Little

Counsel for the Respondent: Ms K Skultety (Departmental Advocate)

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

T.E. BARNETT AND R.D. FAYLE

INTRODUCTION

1. On 28 June 1996, the applicant claimed diesel fuel rebate in relation to diesel fuel used, between 11 January 1996 and 22 April 1996, in the operation of a grain drier at Esperance in Western Australia. The claim was made pursuant to the Customs Act 1901 and the Excise Act 1901. As the relevant provisions of those Acts are the same then this decision will address only those of the Customs Act 1901 ("the Act").

2. The issue before the Tribunal is whether the purchase of the diesel fuel for use in the grain drier, to dry the applicant's harvested wheat before delivery to the grain receival agent's store in Esperance, qualifies for rebate pursuant to s164 of the Act.

3. Section 164 relevantly provides: 164(1) A rebate is, subject to subsections (2) and (3) and to such conditions and restrictions as are prescribed (being conditions and restrictions that relate to goods generally, to goods included in a class of goods that includes diesel fuel or to diesel fuel only), payable to a person who purchases diesel fuel for use by him: ... in primary production (otherwise than for the purpose of propelling a road vehicle on a public road); ... In this section: "agriculture" means ... the cultivation or gathering in of crops; or ... "primary production" means: agriculture; ...."

4. It was common ground between the parties that no other provision of the Act could apply to the applicant's claim in question.

5. The applicant was represented by Mr H Little, the operator of the grain drier in Esperance. Evidence was given for the applicant by Mr H Little, the operator of the grain drier and Messrs L Quinlivan and G Wilson, both farmers operating in the immediate vicinity of Esperance. The respondent was represented by Ms K Skultety, a departmental advocate, assisted by Mr C Ling.

6. The Tribunal had before it documents filed pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, a statement of agreed facts and it received into evidence eleven exhibits from the applicant and eight from the respondent.

EVIDENCE

7. The applicant is a grain grower in the Esperance region of Western Australia, growing wheat, oats, barley, lupins and field peas. In the 1995/96 season the applicant harvested approximately 5,000 tonnes of grain.

8. The applicant purchased fuel for use in a commercial grain drier, located in Esperance, some 80 kilometres or so from the farm. The drier is adjacent to the Esperance Port Terminal at the Cooperative Bulk Handling ("CBH") grain receival point and storage facility. The grain drier is used to reduce the moisture content of the grain to 12.5% or below, being the level at which the grain receival agent accepts grain. It will not accept grain having a moisture content above that level. The acceptable moisture content level for grain received at Esperance has remained at 12.5% for some years. The maximum acceptable moisture content level of grain is determined by the Australian Wheat Board.

9. Whilst the crop is ripening, chemical desiccation of green material may be carried out, if appropriate. This is done to remove weeds or green heads of grain that have developed too late and have no commercial value. Green material also increases the moisture level of the crop such that the otherwise drier grain absorbs moisture from it, leading to a rejection of the crop by the grain receival agent.

10. Once ripe, the crop is cut, threshed and cleaned by using a machine called a header. The header has an in-built moisture content measurement device but it is not sufficiently accurate to be certain that the grain meets the desired level of 12.5% or less. The moisture content of grain can vary during the course of a day, depending on relative humidity at the time.

11. The header's grain holding capacity is seven tonnes and it unloads, on the move into chaser bins which are towed alongside the moving header for this purpose.

12. The applicant does not have facilities to store its grain permanently on the farm apart from the storage of a small proportion of the crop to be used for both seed grain and stock feed. It does however, have temporary storage capacity, in the form of portable bins, which hold about one day's harvest. Those facilities are filled from the applicant's "chaser bins" and emptied, by the cartage contractor, about fifty times during the course of a harvest.

13. The applicant's portable storage bins are moved, when empty, to various locations during the harvest to facilitate the process. The portable storage bins are emptied into the cartage contractor's trucks for transport to Esperance and delivery to the grain receival agent.

14. Before the grain is accepted into storage by the receival agent its moisture content is measured and should it exceed 12.5% then the carter takes it to the grain drier. The grain is then dried to the required moisture level, the diesel fuel used for this process being measured and subsequently billed by the supplier to the relevant grower. The applicant pays the grain drier a fee based on cost recovery apart from fuel, as the entity which owns it is owned, by and large, by local grain growers. Once dried the grain is delivered into permanent storage after passing over a weigh-bridge. It is common ground that title in the grain passes at this point. The weigh bridge ticket records all the relevant information and becomes the prime document against which future payments are made to the grower by the Australian Wheat Board or Grain Pool of WA

15. Apart from an unacceptable moisture content the most common causes of rejection by the grain receival agent are: the presence of contaminants at a level above the standard; the presence of insects (alive or dead) at a level above the standard; and sub-standard wheat in terms of quality parameters.

16. Grain can achieve the desired moisture level by either leaving it on the stalk after it has reached maturity until weather conditions provide natural drying, or by drying the grain using artificial means such as those available at the Esperance grain drier. Approximately 5% of the applicant's harvest for the 1995/96 season was rejected because of excessive moisture and had to be dried. No grain was rejected for any other reason.

17. Grain intended for trade is generally acquired from growers, after having been received into storage by the grain receival agent, CBH in this instance, by the Australian Wheat Board, for export wheat and the WA Grain Pool, for domestic sale. Grain can be sold directly into market by the grower if granted a permit by the authorities to do so. Apart from that, any grain not delivered to the receival agent is held for seed or stock feed.

18. Mr Wilson gave evidence that before the Esperance grain drier was available he dried his own grain on farm when he believed the moisture content was excessive. He said that this was not a satisfactory process because, apart from cost, the moisture measurement on which he relied was not as accurate as that of the grain receival agent and consequently he tended to over-dry the grain thus reducing its weight and therefore its value, although transport costs were less because it weighed less.

19. Mr Quinlivan described, in evidence, the activities in which he engages in preparation for harvest. When he believes the wheat grain is ready for harvest he ensures that his equipment is in good working order, that is, the header, the chaser bin, the portable field bins and the tractors. He then makes suitable arrangements with the cartage contractor and checks with the grain drier to ensure it is ready, if needed, to dry wet grain. He said it is imperative that all these arrangements are coordinated because he cannot complete the harvest without all of them. When all are coordinated the header can work from early morning till about 10 p.m. because the grain drier remains available to accommodate him in this regard. Delays in the process are costly. The sooner the grain is harvested the better because, on average, the yield falls at the rate of about 1% per day once the grain is ripe and ready for harvest. Also, delays increase the risk of damage caused by rain or storms. For example, if the cartage contractor does not turn up on time then he has to delay the harvest because of the limited on-farm storage. Similarly, if the drier is not available he could be faced with additional costs of having the grain returned to the farm, rendering that delivery uneconomic. He said that so far as he was concerned the harvest was not complete until the grain was safely delivered into the grain receival agent's storage in Esperance, or in the case of grain intended for own use, when stored on-farm. The latter accounts for less than 5% of the harvest.

20. The Tribunal was directed to the following passage from Australian Grains, Morescope Publishing, Victoria, Edited by Bob Coombs, 1994, which states at page 278: "The harvesting capacity required on any given property is a function of area, yield and time available for harvest. Anything which increases time available for harvest, or increases the efficiency with which the harvester is used during that period, will permit a greater area to be harvested using the same capacity. Considerable attention has been given to the role of drying and storage systems in this context over the past few years. In an environment where the first harvesting date is determined by the maximum moisture [at] which grain can be sold, addition of a drier to the system brings forward the earliest harvest date. Harvest system management enabling dryers to advance the first harvest date, and to extend harvester operating hours into the evening, are common in Europe. As the harvesting system gets larger, the cost of delays due to failures in the transport system also increases. Availability of more on-farm grain storage, and particularly of aerated storage in which high moisture grain can be held awaiting drying, can significantly improve the flexibility of the harvesting operation." (Exhibit R2)

21. Both Mr Quinlivan and Mr Wilson gave evidence that, in their experience the word "harvest" is used to describe the process of taking off the grain heads and delivering grain into permanent storage, the drying process being integral to this. The term "gathering in" as a synonym for "harvest", so far as they are aware, is not one used by farmers or in the industry.

22. The Tribunal was also directed to a passage from the World Book Encyclopedia, World Book International, London, 1996 edition, where at page 164 under the topic "Agriculture" it states: "Methods of crop production. Crop farming involves at least four separate operations: (1) preparing the soil, (2) planting and cultivating, (3) harvesting, and (4) processing and storing."

23. When this definition was put to Mr Quinlivan he said that but for the first operation, preparing the soil, it was a fair description of his activities. He said he did not prepare the soil as such as that could result in soil erosion. Mr Quinlivan was not drawn on this point. Be that as it may, the description states harvesting is a separate operation from that of storing.

REASONS

24. By way of background, it is useful to note that the legislation under review is as a result of amendments effected by the Customs and Excise Legislation Amendment Act 1995 (Cth) ("the Amendment Act"). In Australian Native Landscapes Pty Ltd v Collector of Customs (NG 853 of 1995), Sackville J provides a useful historical background to the Amendment Act which we do not propose to repeat here. Suffice it to say that the effect of the Amendment Act, in amending the definition of "agriculture" in s164(7), is to limit the diesel fuel rebate to four specified primary activities, one or which is set out in paragraph (a) as "the cultivation or gathering in of crops;". The amendments went further by repealing what was known as the "sweeper clause", that extended the benefit of the rebate to activities "connected with" the primary activities, and the amendments added 24 new paragraphs (para. (e) - (zb)) identifying specific activities to be included within the definition. As mentioned, it is common ground that none of those activities assist the applicant in these proceedings. Those amendments were described in the Second Reading Speech, by the Minister when introducing the Bill for the Amendment Act, in these terms: "The amendments proposed in Items 4 and 8 of Schedule 1 to the Bill are to remove the subjectivity associated with the 'sweeper clauses', and replace those clauses with an objective list of activities that are eligible for rebate. ... It should be noted that although the proposed amendments will necessarily narrow the range of activities for which rebate is payable, farmers ... will generally be unaffected. The intention of these amendments is to put beyond doubt that the Scheme is not meant to provide rebate eligibility for activities which are not sufficiently connected with ... agriculture; for instance, the provision of a service or utility to a farmer ..., such as electricity through a grid, or the building of a dam which is intended to supply water to, amongst others, farmers, ... It is proposed that diesel fuel purchased for use in the following activities will be eligible to receive rebate: ... storage of the produce of a 'core agricultural activity' on the agricultural property it was produced, (new paragraph (l)); ..." In the amending Act the above appears as paragraph 164(7)(w) and states: "the storage of produce of a core agricultural activity on an agricultural property where a core agricultural activity is carried on;" The term "core agricultural activity" is defined in s164(7) as: "'core agricultural activity' means an activity referred to in paragraph (a), (b), (c) or (d) of the definition of 'agriculture' if that activity is carried out for the purposes of, or for purposes that will directly benefit, a business undertaken to obtain produce for sale."

25. With that in mind, the Tribunal must decide whether the artificial drying of the wheat at the Esperance Drier, before delivery into permanent storage is within the concept of the statutory phrase of "gathering in of crops", the core agricultural activity in question.

26. The following passage was quoted, with apparent approval by Sackville J, in Australian Native Landscapes Pty Ltd v Collector of Customs: " ... a revenue law directed to commerce is usually taken to employ the description and adopt the meanings in use among those who exercise the trade concerned: Herbert Adams Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 27; (1932) 47 CLR 222, at 227, per Dixon J." (p.11) Later, his Honour said, in relation to the trade usage of a term: "In legislation dealing with revenue or rebates, a court is generally ready to conclude that the statutory language is intended to be understood in accordance with common commercial or trade usage. In D & R Henderson (MFG) Pty Ltd v Collector or Customs for the State of New South Wales (1974) 48 ALJR 132, Mason J acknowledged (at 135) this principle. However, his Honour was not satisfied on the evidence before him in that case that the relevant words had a meaning which 'according to commercial and trade usage differs from their natural and ordinary meaning'."

27. The only evidence before the Tribunal in this regard is that of both Mr Quinlivan and Mr Wilson to the effect that the term "gathering in the crop" or "gathering in of crops" is not used by farmers but instead they speak of "harvesting the crop". Adopting therefore, the views of Sackville J and Mason J, mentioned above, the Tribunal is of the opinion that the present case is one in which it must be guided only by the ordinary or natural meaning of the expression used in the statute.

28. The word "gather" has many and varied dictionary meanings. The following selection are confined to the context of gathering in crops.

29. The Oxford dictionary defines "gather" as "To bring (things) together; to collect from different quarters into one mass or place; ... To collect (grain, fruit, etc) as harvest or annual produce; also to gather in. ...". And it defines "gathering" as "The action of the verb GATHER, in various transitive senses."

30. The New Shorter Oxford dictionary's definition of the word "gather", in this sense, is "Bring together, collect, assemble. ... collect or pick (flowers, fruit) from place of growth. Collect (grain etc) as harvest. ..."

31. The Macquarie Dictionary, second edition, defines "gather", in this sense, as "to pick (any crop or natural yield) from its place of growth or formation: to gather grain, fruit, or flowers."

32. That dictionary also defines "harvest" as: "the gathering of crops. ... to gather, as a crop. .. to gather the crop from: to harvest the fields. - to gather a crop; reap."

33. Subparagraph (aa) of s164(1) provides that a rebate is payable to a person, in this context, who purchases diesel fuel for use by him in primary production, that is, in the gathering in of crops. Ms Skultety, for the respondent, pointed out that the diesel fuel must be used in the activity and, since the amendments referred to, it is not sufficient for the use to be in connection with the gathering in of crops. By operation of the preceding preposition in, the activity of gathering in the crops must be read restrictively, State Rail Authority of NSW v Collector of Customs [1991] FCA 610; (1991) 33 FCR 211 at 214 and Robe River Mining Co Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 21 FCR 1 at p.12.

34. It was submitted by the respondent that the drying of the crops occurs too late to be an activity properly described as carried out in (the course of) gathering in the crops. Before the crops can be dried in the artificial manner employed at the Esperance drier, they must have been gathered in. The Tribunal was provided with a description of an alternative method of drying crops often used in Canada and referred to in Australian Grains (supra) (at p.277), called windrow harvesting: "The crop is cut and deposited in a dense windrow as a separate operation from the pickup/threshing tasks carried out be the harvester. ... Cereal crops deposited in a windrow dry out more rapidly and uniformly than they would when standing and are also relatively safe from damage by wind, hail or minor rainfall." Therefore, the drying of the crop using windrows is a natural method of drying and is carried our before the crop is gathered in. That is contrasted with the artificial method of drying used in the present case. It was further submitted that the artificial drying merely removes excess moisture from already gathered in grain in preparation for permanent storage at the grain receival point.

35. Evidence was also given by all three witnesses that the drying of the grain lessens the potential deterioration of the crop once stored. The deterioration occurs as a result of a combination of moisture and heat generated in the storage facility and the relatively colder outside temperatures which cause a fungal growth at the perimeters. This is minimised by pumping outside air through the storage bin. In this light, the drying and the air cooling functions to prevent deterioration are not activities which can be characterised as gathering in the crops but, at best, as activities involved in the storage of the crop for future commercial exploitation. Paragraphs (w) and (x) of the s164(7) definition of "agriculture", extend the rebate beyond the four core agricultural activities. Subparagraph reads (x): (x) the packing or prevention of deterioration, of the produce of a core agricultural activity if: (i) the packing, or the prevention of deterioration, of the produce is carried out on an agricultural property where a core agricultural activity is carried on; and (ii) there is no physical change to the produce; and (iii) the packing, or the prevention of deterioration, of the produce does not constitute a processing of the produce; ... That paragraph specifically refers to "the prevention of deterioration of the produce, that is, in this case, the grain. Therefore, the definition of the core agricultural activity of "gathering in of crops" is extended to both the gathered in crop's storage and prevention of its deterioration, but only insofar as those activities are carried out at the place where the core agricultural activity is carried on. In the present case that would be at the applicant's farm.

36. The Tribunal is of the opinion that the drying operation carried out at the Esperance Drier is not an activity of "gathering in of crops" as that term is used in the definition of "agriculture" discussed above. The artificial drying of the crop occurs after the crop has been gathered in. The Tribunal is of the opinion that that conclusion is self evident from the foregoing discussion and it is not necessary to go further. Nevertheless, it finds additional support for the conclusion reached by reference to the extended definition of "agriculture" as already mentioned. The specific objective tests of paragraphs (w) and (x) of the s164(7) definition of "agriculture" confine eligible activities to those carried out on an agricultural property where [the] core agricultural activity is carried on. That is clearly not the case here.

37. Mr Little submitted that there is an apparent anomaly in the legislation if it is to be construed in this manner. That is, if a farmer carried out grain drying on his farm or pumped air through grain stored on his farm for the purposes of reducing deterioration, the purchase of any diesel fuel used by him in those operations would qualify for rebate. The anomaly, he suggested, is that the location of the activity and not the activity itself determines the eligibility for rebate. The Tribunal agrees with Mr Little on this point. However, having regard to the Amending Act's provisions, the accompanying Explanatory Memorandum and the Second Reading Speech the result does not appear to be unintended.

DECISION

38. For the above reasons the decision under review is affirmed.
