Statement of Reasons

WHAT ARE THESE REASONS ABOUT?

  1. This case concerns whether, and if so, to what extent, the general anti-avoidance provisions in Part IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth)[1] apply to a series of complex arrangements implemented by the applicant, Mr Collie.
  2. The arrangements were the subject of a decision of the former Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT) which upheld the Part IVA determinations apart from certain adjustments to the amounts treated as Mr Collie’s assessable income. At the AAT, and before this Tribunal, Mr Collie did not dispute that the arrangements were “schemes” as relevantly defined, nor did he advance any submission on the dominant purpose of the schemes. Rather, Mr Collie contended that he had not obtained a “tax benefit” as defined or, alternatively, any such benefit was less than that contended by the Commissioner.[2]
  3. The applicant appealed the AAT decision to the Federal Court. A Full Court of the Federal Court allowed the appeal, set aside the AAT decision and remitted the matter to this Tribunal:
    for hearing and determination of whether the applicant obtained a tax benefit pursuant to Part IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) for each of the 1997 to 2001 income years, and any consequential effects on penalties.[3]
  4. Mr Collie has put forward counterfactuals under which he says he would not have derived any of the contested amounts if the schemes had not been entered into or carried out. Aside from whether Mr Collie’s counterfactuals constitute a reliable prediction of what would have occurred but for the schemes, there is an issue regarding whether, as the Commissioner maintains, Mr Collie’s counterfactuals cannot succeed because those postulates would themselves fall foul of Part IVA. A related issue is whether, as Mr Collie maintains, to entertain that submission at this stage of the proceedings would amount to a denial of procedural fairness.

SCOPE OF THE REMITTAL AND DISPUTE

  1. Until his reply submissions on the second day of the hearing, the Commissioner maintained that the Tribunal was confined by the terms of the remittal to whether the applicant obtained a tax benefit in each relevant income year and could not entertain submissions that, if so, the quantum of the tax benefits determined by the AAT should be varied.
  2. The Commissioner has now abandoned that submission. I am satisfied that concession is appropriate.
  3. While the Court’s orders did not expressly deal with the issue, there would be an awkwardness in the Tribunal endeavouring to determine whether Mr Collie obtained a tax benefit in the abstract; that is, without regard to the quantum of the benefit said to have been obtained. It would also be a curious intention to attribute to the Court that the Tribunal could be left in a position where it could determine that the applicant obtained a tax benefit in a particular amount for an income year but would be required to make a decision that left Mr Collie with assessments in different amounts that the Tribunal considered to be incorrect.
  4. Further, the Tribunal is not bound to adopt any of the counterfactuals proposed by the parties; it could come to a conclusion that different counterfactuals would be the most reliable predictions of what would have occurred but for the schemes. In those circumstances, it would be incongruous for the Tribunal to determine the matter on the basis of particular counterfactuals, but for the quantum of the tax benefit to be determined by reference to entirely different counterfactuals that were rejected by the Tribunal.[5]
  5. For these reasons, I consider that the proper application of the Court’s order is that the question of whether the applicant obtained a tax benefit in each relevant income year encompasses the quantum of any such benefit.
  6. Proceeding on that basis, as the parties’ cases finally came to be put, the primary issue is whether Mr Collie obtained a tax benefit. Although that is to be determined for each year, the parties approached the issue on a global basis, their arguments addressed to the whole of the five income years in dispute. STATUTORY FRAMEWORK

Meaning of “tax benefit”

  1. For Part IVA purposes, under s 177C(1)(a) the obtaining of a tax benefit by a taxpayer in connection with a scheme is stated to be a reference to:
    an amount not being included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of a year of income where the amount would have been included, or might reasonably be expected to have been included, in the assessable income of the taxpayer of that year of income if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out . . .

    (Emphasis added.)

  2. The bolded words in s 177C(1)(a) above require a reasonable expectation of events. In that regard, on the highest authority it is established that:
    A reasonable expectation requires more than a possibility. It involves a prediction as to events which would have taken place if the relevant scheme had not been entered into or carried out and the prediction must be sufficiently reliable for it to be regarded as reasonable.[9]

Burden of proof

  1. The applicant bears the burden of proving the assessments are excessive and what amounts should have been assessed.[10]
  2. In that regard, I apply the following principles as previously summarised by this Tribunal’s predecessor:
    (a) Facts may be found based on oral evidence alone. There is no barrier to a fact being found on the uncorroborated evidence of an applicant. There is no requirement that direct evidence by oral testimony or affidavit may only be accepted if corroborated.

    (b) However, self-serving statements should be closely scrutinised.

    (c) Where a taxpayer has received funds or incurred costs that are not clearly or fully explained in contemporaneous records, the taxpayer may face a challenge in satisfying the burden of proof without corroborating evidence.

    (d) However, even in such cases, oral or written evidence of the taxpayer is not to be regarded as prima facie unacceptable. While it will often be prudent to put forward corroborating evidence, taxpayers are not obliged to call all material witnesses or produce all material documents.

    (e) The standard of proof is the balance of probabilities. An applicant that succeeds ‘in weighing down [the] scales ever so slightly’ in the applicant’s favour will discharge the burden of proof it bears.

    [26] . . . the observations of Logan J in Anglo American Investments Pty Ltd (Trustee) v Commissioner of Taxation noting that informality can and often does attend the formation of legal relations in small business. As his Honour noted:

    Even more this is so where the relevant corporate actors are or are represented by the same individual acting in different capacities or by individuals who are close family members or business associates. Sometimes the only documentary manifestation of that legal relationship may be a transaction recorded in a ledger or perhaps just an annually prepared profit and loss account and accompanying annotations. There may then, in a taxation appeal, be related oral evidence of the individual(s) concerned that the transaction was as so recorded informally...

    [27] That is not to say there is any special or lesser standard of proof for a small business. Where informality is present, ‘much can depend on the credibility one affords the accounts given by participants and, where they exist, representations in business records created under their supervision or with their approval’.

    [28] The Tribunal can only make findings of fact based on the evidence put before it and, where necessary, its relevance explained. Mere assertions will not suffice . . .

    [29] Further, the process of finding facts needs to consider the evidence led through the lens of what was, or could be expected to be, capable of being led. As already noted, that does not mean an applicant is obliged to produce every relevant document or potential witness. However, where relevant evidence that could be expected is not produced, that is a relevant factor for the Tribunal to consider.

    (Footnotes omitted.)[11]

  3. In the context of determining whether a taxpayer obtained a tax benefit, it is not sufficient for the taxpayer to show that the Commissioner’s counterfactual is not reasonable. A taxpayer will usually seek to demonstrate the absence of a tax benefit by identifying on the evidence a counterfactual or postulate which shows what the taxpayer might reasonably be expected to have done if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out. The taxpayer’s postulate must be a reasonable one, measured by reference to the substance of the scheme and any result or consequence for the taxpayer achieved by the scheme.[12]
  4. The prediction required must be based on objective inquiry and determination, not subjective intent.[13] That does not mean that evidence of the taxpayer is irrelevant. A taxpayer may give evidence regarding the steps the taxpayer says would have been taken in the absence of the scheme. That evidence may be useful to the extent that it reveals facts or matters that bear upon the objective determination of the reasonableness of the alternative postulate.[14]
  5. However, mere assertion or speculation unsupported by foundation facts will not assist the taxpayer.[15] Production of contemporaneous documentation to evidence the objective circumstances will be far more probative than mere assertions, along with consideration of the logic of the taxpayer’s counterfactual having regard to the surrounding circumstances established by the evidence.[16]
  6. Although a taxpayer will usually seek to discharge the burden of proof by identifying a reasonable postulate, the High Court has recently confirmed that “in unusual cases” a taxpayer may demonstrate excessiveness of an assessment based on a Part IVA determination by establishing that there is no postulate that is a reasonable alternative to entering into or carrying out the scheme that was carried out.[17]
  7. The events in this case occurred many years ago. In taxation cases, that dimension can present particular challenges for the taxpayer upon whom the burden of proof is cast. Generally, it is appropriate to consider the evidence through the lens of what evidence could reasonably be expected to be available to be adduced by the taxpayer having regard to the passage of time. That does not mean any lesser burden is cast on the taxpayer; it merely provides context for consideration of such evidence as may be adduced.
  8. In this case, although many years have passed since the schemes were carried out, the Commissioner first started raising information requests in respect of the relevant transactions in the early 2000s. For a sophisticated taxpayer like Mr Collie, steeped in taxation law and practice; engaged in marketing tax avoidance schemes; and with an acrimonious history with tax officials as discussed below, it must have been obvious by then that he was at risk of a dispute with the Commissioner and that it would be prudent to retain all relevant records until any such dispute was resolved.

What if the applicant’s alternative postulate would also be susceptible to Part IVA?

  1. The applicant submits that the authorities identified by the Commissioner do not support the proposition that a taxpayer’s burden of proof cannot be discharged by establishing a counterfactual which would, itself, attract the operation of Part IVA.
  2. The most recent authority cited by the Commissioner is Mylan Australia Holding Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (No 2).[18] In that case, the Court considered, but rejected, a submission that an alternative postulate would also have attracted the operation of Part IVA. However, that rejection was based on the facts of the case. There is an apparent assumption that in other circumstances an alternative postulate might fail to discharge the burden of proof for that reason, but there is no discussion of relevant principle or authorities in the judgement.
  3. Commentary in three other cases was brought to my attention.
  4. The first is found in Commissioner of Taxation v Trail Brothers Steel & Plastics Pty Ltd,[19] a deductions case concerning an employment arrangement. Referring to a potential alternative postulate under which a relevant deduction would be available, Greenwood J noted:
    That is not to say that an activity which simply involves a variation to employment contracts so as to substitute one contrived mechanism or colourable structure to clothe non-deductible payments with deductibility, with another, forms part of the relevant hypothesis as such a payment would not give rise to an allowable deduction of the kind removed by the scheme.[20]
  5. Although his Honour did not expand on this reasoning, I infer that Greenwood J intended to convey that such a contrived or colourable payment “would not give rise to an allowable deduction” because, on the hypothesis of that postulate, it too would be the subject of a Part IVA determination which would deny the deduction. That seems to have been how Besanko J, in Futuris Corporation Lt v Federal Commissioner of Taxation,[21] understood Greenwood J’s statement. His Honour referenced Greenwood J’s statement in support of the observation that:
    The counterfactual must not itself be a scheme with a dominant tax purpose.[22]
  6. The Full Federal Court in Hart v Commissioner of Taxation posed the question:
    Does [the taxpayer’s] professed intention not to pay tax change the outcome here? Can a taxpayer avoid the reach of s 177C by contending that but for the scheme, implementation of another arrangement of equal ingenuity would have been pursued to avoid the payment of any tax?[23]
  7. The Full Court went on to cite Besanko J’s observation, extracted above, with apparent approval. However, it appears that the issue was not the subject of submissions as the taxpayer accepted the correctness of the proposition. In any case, as discussed below, that matter was decided against the taxpayer on the basis that his evidence was insufficient to discharge the burden of proof.
  8. The upshot is that there is no detailed judicial discussion of this issue in any case brought to my attention. Such discussion as there is in the cases outlined above is consistent with the Commissioner’s position.
  9. Additionally, there is an inherent logic in the Commissioner’s position: a counterfactual that would result in a contested amount being included in a taxpayer’s assessable income by the operation of Part IVA cannot discharge a taxpayer’s burden of proving there is not a reasonable expectation of the income being included in the taxpayer’s assessable income.
  10. Mr Collie submitted that whether his counterfactual would be a Part IVA scheme “has no bearing on the predictive exercised required in determining what is a reasonable counterfactual.”[24] In my view, even if that is correct, it is not an answer to the Commissioner’s submission. That is because the relevance of whether a taxpayer’s counterfactual would fall foul of Part IVA is not in relation to the predictive exercise. It is that, if the predictive exercise results in the taxpayer’s counterfactual being a reasonable expectation, the counterfactual would not meet the taxpayer’s burden of proof. That is because the contested amount would still be included in the taxpayer’s assessable income, under that hypothesis, by the operation of Part IVA.
  11. I proceed on the basis that the applicant cannot succeed by proving his counterfactuals give rise to a reasonable expectation if, under his counterfactuals, the contested income would be included in his assessable income by any provision of the income tax law including those found in Part IVA.

THE PARTIES’ SUBMISSIONS – IN SUMMARY

The parties’ counterfactuals

  1. Mr Collie primarily relies upon his counterfactuals. These are detailed for each relevant income year in his affidavit of 6 May 2025 and in Annexure IDC-2 to his affidavit of 26 May 2025 which is included as Appendix 2 to these reasons.
  2. In his closing submissions, Mr Collie’s counterfactuals are summarised as follows:
    Step 1: part of the Income Earning Trusts' net income would have been distributed to the Canowindra Trust;

    Step 2: the Canowindra Trust would have distributed the net income distributed to it from the IETs to the CGH Trust;

    Step 3: the CGH Trust would have distributed the net income distributed to it from the Canowindra Trust to trusts with losses, namely the Starlights Unit Trust, the Tarragunda Trading Trust and the Woodbury Farm Trust; and

    Step 4: once the trust losses were exhausted, the CGH Trust would have distributed any balance income to companies within Mr Collie’s group, namely Bond Park Pty Ltd, Woodbury Park Pty Ltd, Tarragunda Pastoral Co Pty Ltd, Equipment Hire Systems Pty Ltd.[26]

  3. Further, and in the alternative, Mr Collie contends that there is no reasonable alternative postulate under which his share of the IETs would have been distributed in a manner which would have those amounts forming part of his assessable income.[27]
  4. Mr Collie submits that his counterfactuals are supported by:
    (a) his testimony;

    (b) the pattern of distributions before and after the income years in question;

    (c) the inherent logic of events.

  5. In relation to what he says is the inherent logic of the postulated events, Mr Collie says the objective facts support his contention that for asset protection reasons relating to the history of serious claims against him, he would never have structured his affairs such that he would have received the IET income personally.
  6. Mr Collie gave evidence as to litigation in which he had become embroiled in the period leading up to and during the relevant years. He says this makes it objectively unlikely that he would have arranged his affairs in a way that resulted in him receiving IET income personally. The Commissioner did not dispute Mr Collie’s account of the litigation history but submitted that asset protection objectively might motivate a desire to not accumulate assets in his name but did not make it necessary for Mr Collie to not receive any income in his name.
  7. Under his counterfactuals, it is companies within Mr Collie’s group of entities that would have received any tax benefit, not Mr Collie himself. Alternatively, for the same reasons including those relating to asset protection and historical distribution patterns, Mr Collies submits that any prediction involving IET income being distributed to Mr Collie personally would not be reasonable.
  8. The Commissioner rejects Mr Collie’s assertions and submits that, if not for the schemes, Mr Collie’s share of the IET income could reasonably have been expected to have been distributed to:
    (a) trusts that were related to or associated with Mr Collie; or, alternatively

    (b) a discretionary trust which Mr Collie controlled,

    which then have distributed the whole or part of that income to Mr Collie.

  9. The Commissioner acknowledges that a reasonable expectation could be that the whole or part of Mr Collie’s share of the IET income would have been distributed to him. However, the Commissioner says there is no basis in the evidence on which to determine a part of Mr Collie’s share of the IET income would have been distributed to him. Accordingly, the Commissioner says, the whole of Mr Collie’s share in each relevant year must be treated as a tax benefit that he obtained.
  10. Thus, it is as to the final step in Mr Collie’s counterfactuals and the Commissioner’s – that is, whether income would have been distributed to Mr Collie personally - that the parties are mainly in disagreement. As already noted, the task of the Tribunal is not to choose between the Commissioner’s and Mr Collie’s counterfactual. The Tribunal’s task is to determine whether Mr Collie has tipped the scales, even if ever so slightly, in favour of a conclusion that his counterfactuals are reliable predictions of what would or could be expected to have occurred if the schemes had not been entered into and carried out or, alternatively, that there is no postulate under which it would be reasonable to predict that IET income would have been distributed to Mr Collie personally.
  11. In that regard, the Commissioner submits that it is a premise of Mr Collie’s counterfactuals, but that he has not proved, that he had access to sufficient funds to meet living expenses under his counterfactuals. Unless that is proved, the Commissioner says, the counterfactuals cannot be reliable predictions of what would have occurred but for the schemes. Additionally, the says the availability of tax losses on which the efficacy of the schemes depend has not been proved. Further, in respect of the 1997 and 1998 income years, the Commissioner says that Mr Collie has not proved that tax losses in the amounts asserted by Mr Collie were available in the Tarragunda Trading Trust for offset against amounts that might otherwise have been, on the Commissioner’s postulate, distributed to Mr Collie.

Foundational facts

To what extent, if any, has Mr Collie proved there is a pattern of distributions before and after the relevant income years that supports his counterfactuals?

  1. Mr Collie argues that the first three of the four steps in his counterfactual, outlined above, are a continuation of a pattern of distributions that occurred in the 1994 to 1996 income years. Therefore, Mr Collie says, it is reasonable to predict those steps would have occurred in the years in dispute if the schemes were not carried out.
  2. The Commissioner maintains that the evidence does not reveal a pattern of the kind asserted by Mr Collie.
  3. In respect of the distributions set out at Steps 1, 2 and 3 of his counterfactuals as summarised above, Mr Collie submitted:

76. Steps 1, 2 and 3 above are a continuation of the pattern of distributions in the 1994 to 1996 income years in respect of income earned by Comlaw Consultants (i.e. prior to the operation of Cleary Hoare Corporate and the Income Earning Trusts). In those years, the income earning entities were within the Comlaw Consultants group. The income from the group was distributed to the Annesley Trust and then to Canowindra Trust, and then from the Canowindra Trust ultimately to trusts with tax losses, either directly, or indirectly via the CGH Trust.

The income earning entities (then the IETs) would have distributed income to the Annesley Trust, which would then be distributed to the Canowindra Trust, and then to the CGH Trust, and then to trusts with losses, being the Starlights Unit Trust, Tarragunda Trading Trust and Woodbury Farm Trust.[37]

As Mr Collie’s summary indicates, the 1994 to 1996 distributions relate to income earned prior to the operation of Cleary Hoare Corporate and the IETs. Thus, the description of Steps 1, 2 and 3 as a continuation of the prior pattern of distributions is only accurate in the sense that income from a similar business operation was also distributed to trusts with tax losses. Nor do they evidence income being finally distributed to and taxed in a company.

That, Mr Collie says, is consistent with his counterfactual that would have the IET income ultimately distributed to a company under his control and taxed at the corporate tax rate.
  2. That submission raises several issues.
  3. First, Mr Collie’s summary of his counterfactuals is that the IET income would ultimately be distributed to “a company within Mr Collie’s group”. It is not clear to me that Bond Park Pty Ltd is such a company. It had been acquired by Cleary Hoare Consulting Pty Ltd as trustee for the CHC Services Trust (which owned the business name, “Cleary Hoare Corporate”) and thus appears to have been a Collie/Hart entity.[39] Nevertheless, distribution of income to Bond Part Pty Ltd is included, in the detailed description of Mr Collie's counterfactual for each relevant income year, as one of a range of potential corporate beneficiaries to which it is asserted the income would have been distributed.[40] As such the distribution to Bond Park Pty Ltd in 2002 is consistent with Mr Collie’s counterfactuals, or with distributions to at least one of the potential beneficiaries identified in his counterfactuals.
  4. Secondly, and importantly, the nature and context of the 2002 distribution were markedly different to the circumstances in which it is said distributions in the relevant years would have been made.
  5. The amount of $205,000 distributed to Bond Park Pty Ltd is considerably less than Mr Collie’s shares of IET income in the relevant years, which ranged from $415,000 to $2,920,703.
  6. Additionally, as Mr Collie deposed: “[t]here were changes within the tax planning industry and developments beginning with an access visit without notice by the ATO to Cleary Hoare in August 2001.”[41] Thus, the tax planning landscape and the position in which Mr Collie and Mr Hart found themselves by the time relevant trustee would have resolved to distribute income to Bond Park Pty Ltd can be expected to have given rise to a heightened awareness of the compliance activities of the ATO. They had, after all, been subject to ATO compliance at the upper end of the spectrum of such activities – an access visit without notice. Those developments were, in fact, of such significance that they “resulted in the effective shut down of Cleary Hoare Consulting Pty Ltd and the CCH Services Trust by June 2002.”[42]
  7. Thirdly, as the table above shows, distributions were also made to APCF Ltd and Rosehill Trust. The ultimate destination of the distribution to the Rosehill Trust, was not highlighted in Mr Collie’s submissions relating to the argument that Mr Collie’s counterfactuals reflected a consistent pattern of distributions before and after the relevant years. Mr Collie acknowledged that a counterfactual involving income accumulating in a trust where the trustee would be taxed at the highest marginal rate would be unlikely.[43]
  8. Mr Collie’s affidavit[44] also referred to a distribution, after allowing for carried forward losses, by the Starlights Unit Trust to the Starlights Holding Trust which in turn distributed that amount to Equipment Hire Systems Pty Ltd where it was taxed at the corporate tax rate. However, that distribution was in the amount of $84,244, which is markedly lower than the amounts earned by the IETs in the relevant years. It also occurred some years after the relevant income years and after the IETs ceased to operate. I consider it is not sufficiently proximate in time or circumstances to be regarded as a continuation of a pattern of distributions before or during the relevant years.
  9. Accordingly, I am not persuaded that the distributions identified by Mr Collie provide strong support for the assertion in his counterfactuals that, but for the schemes, Mr Collie’s shares of the IET income would have been distributed to and taxed in a company in Mr Collie’s group rather than being distributed to and taxed in Mr Collie’s hands. However, they are consistent with, although not determinative of, Mr Collie’s submission that he would not have allowed any entity he controlled to distribute income to him in the relevant years.
  10. For completeness, I note that the Commissioner submitted that identification of the necessary foundation facts for a counterfactual requires a taxpayer to show a “proven past history” of events that precede the income years. According to the Commissioner, events that occurred after the relevant income years do not support Mr Collie’s counterfactual. In support of that proposition, the Commissioner referred to a passage from Bromwich J’s first instance judgement in the Hart litigation in which his Honour referenced Mr Hart having “no proven past history” of causing distributions consistent with his counterfactual to be made.
  11. From that comment, the Commissioner appears to leap to a principle that only past distributions, and not distributions made after the relevant income year, may be relevant to the reliability of a counterfactual. Reference to the passage in full does not, in my view, reveal such a principle:
    Mr Hart’s senior counsel rhetorically asked in closing oral submissions why anyone would distribute income to somebody who is going to pay 47 or 48 cents in the dollar [referring to the top marginal tax rate in Australia], when the income could lawfully be distributed to somebody who was going to pay less than that. The ultimate problem with that submission is that Mr Hart had no proven past history of distributing to any member of his family and no other history to rely upon to take that question beyond the rhetorical. The Commissioner’s case was, in substance, that Mr Hart had in fact received the money and in the absence of the NVI Scheme and in the absence of any evidence as to any alternative course of conduct in the past or a factual substratum for a different course of conduct in the future, the only reliable prediction was that he would continue to receive that money, albeit in taxable form. A minor point also to be made is that the implicit reference to marginal tax rates was not entirely apposite given that, with a declared income of $100 for the 1997 income year until the assessment and amended assessment, Mr Hart had not exhausted the tax-free threshold, let alone the lower marginal rates.[45]

    (Emphasis added.)

  12. It seems to me that this passage merely references the absence of any evidence – such as proven past distributions - to support the proposition that Mr Hart would not have caused himself to receive distributions personally in the face of the submission, accepted by the Court, that he had in fact received the relevant monies. The question of the relevance of distributions in subsequent years did not arise.
  13. Accordingly, in my view this passage does not support the Commissioner’s proposition. Further, I can identify no reason in principle why distributions made in the year immediately after disputed income years could not, depending on the surrounding circumstances, be relevant to the reliability of a prediction regarding what would have occurred in the disputed income years, especially the most proximate year, but for a relevant scheme. Such distributions seem capable of potentially informing a prediction of what could be expected to have occurred in immediately prior years. Indeed, Bromwich J’s references the absence of evidence of both past conduct and “a factual substratum for a different course of conduct in the future.)
  14. I have therefore not relied upon the Commissioner’s asserted principle and have taken into account the 2002 distributions in relation to the reliability of Mr Collie’s counterfactuals. I have, though, given them limited weight for the reasons mentioned, especially the context in which they occurred.

Tax losses

  1. The Commissioner also submitted that Mr Collie asserted that there was “a litany of trusts and companies under his control with significant tax losses” but that he “has not proved the existence of those losses. He merely asserts them.”
  2. This led to competing submissions about whether the Commissioner was entitled to put Mr Collie to proof of those losses or to do so would be a denial of procedural fairness because, as Mr Collie submitted, the issue had not been identified before written submissions were exchanged in the lead up to the hearing. However, I need not be concerned with the procedural fairness issue because, on the material before the Tribunal, I am satisfied that it is reasonable to conclude that in the absence of the schemes Mr Collie would have proceeded on the basis that the losses were available.
  3. I address proof of the Tarragunda Trading Trust losses in the 1997 and 1998 income years below in relation to the quantum of any tax losses in respect of those income years. On the basis of the same material that led to my satisfaction that the losses for those years were available, I am satisfied that the Tarragunda Trading Trust losses to which Mr Collie deposed were available in respect of each of the other relevant income years.
  4. In any case, proof of existence of the Tarragunda Trading Trust losses assumes less significance in respect of their relevance to the reliability of Mr Collie’s counterfactuals. That is because I accept – based on the relevant Tarragunda Trading Trust losses having been claimed in tax returns as outlined below – that at the least Mr Collie (and it may be inferred, his accountant who prepared the returns) believed the losses were available. That is implicit in his evidence and a sufficient foundation for those parts of Mr Collie’s counterfactuals that rely upon distributions of IET income to the Tarragunda Trading Trust being offset by losses.
  5. Having concluded based on the evidence that the Tarragunda Trading Trust losses were available, I have no reason to doubt that Mr Collie’s evidence that other significant losses were available is not reliable. Additionally, Mr Collie drew attention to correspondence with the Commissioner in April 2002 setting out substantial losses he asserted were available to the Starlight Restaurant Unit Trust.[46] This assertion occurred at a time relatively proximate to the relevant income years and favours a conclusion that Mr Collie would have acted on the basis that the losses were available.
  6. Mr Collie also drew attention to correspondence dated 9 September 1997 from the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation following settlement of the Supreme Court proceedings, and referencing a deed of settlement dated 10 June 1988, in which the Deputy Commissioner confirmed that the entities listed in the deed were considered to have fully discharged their taxation liabilities. Because the income years the subject of the Supreme Court proceedings pre-date the relevant income years, these materials do not prove the available tax losses for those years.[47] However, to the extent that the losses are said to have arisen out of circumstances arising in those earlier years, they are consistent with Mr Collie’s evidence that substantial tax losses were available.
  7. Having regard to Mr Collie’s affidavit evidence which the Commissioner does not say was untruthful, and the documentary evidence which, although far from complete, is consistent with Mr Collie’s evidence, I am satisfied it would be reasonable to conclude that in the absence of the schemes Mr Collie would have acted on the basis that the tax losses to which he deposed were available.

The significance of Mr Collie’s litigation history

  1. The Full Court’s judgement, extracted above, referenced litigation in which Mr Collie had been embroiled, including with the ATO and ANZ Bank. There were also proceedings involving the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) and defamation proceedings involving the Australian Government Solicitor (AGS) and associated parties.
  2. The litigation environment in which the schemes were implemented is a central plank in Mr Collie’s case. Mr Collie says his counterfactuals are inherently logical because, with the associated risks of such litigation, it is logical that he would seek to avoid receiving income personally that might be at risk in the event of bankruptcy. This is no idle speculation; Mr Collie had in fact been served with bankruptcy notices on two occasions during or leading up to the relevant income years.
  3. There were four tranches of litigation involving respectively the ATO, the AGS and associated parties, the ANZ Bank and ASIC. The following details are drawn from Mr Collie’s evidence in his affidavit of 6 May 2025 which was unchallenged in relation to these matters.

The ATO litigation

  1. Mr Collie had various dealings with the ATO in the 1980s and 1990s which I infer resulted in strained relations - to the extent that, as Mr Collie deposed, he came to believe he could not trust the ATO. Those dealings included:
    (a) Assessments issued in 1988 against Mr Collie for primary tax of $7,236,211.06 and penalties of $9,545,172.00 in respect of the 1978 to 1982 income years.

    (b) The ensuing dispute was settled by the execution of a settlement deed in 1988 under which Mr Collie would pay $250,000 and agreed to associated entities foregoing tax losses.

    (c) The settlement unravelled and in late 1990 the ATO commenced Supreme Court proceedings to recover the full amount of tax and penalties and obtained, after a four-day hearing in November 1995, a judgement for over $23M. Mr Collie appealed that decision.

    (d) The ATO commenced bankruptcy proceedings in the Federal Court in 1995 which Mr Collie resisted.

    (e) In or around 1997, the Supreme Court and Federal Court proceedings were settled on terms involving no further action by either side and payment of their own costs.

The AGS litigation

  1. In April 1996, Mr Collie commenced defamation proceedings against the AGS and associated parties. Those proceedings were settled in or around 1997, also on terms that no party would take any additional action and each would bear their own costs.

The ANZ litigation

  1. Mr Collie was also involved in litigation with the ANZ Bank and a former business partner which commenced in 1991. In 1996, the ANZ sought to recover $6M from Mr Collie pursuant to personal guarantees.
  2. Those proceedings were settled in April 1998 with the Collie parties agreeing to pay $30,000 to the bank. Associated proceedings with a third party ended in 2003, but not before the third party had appealed a first instance judgement to the Victorian Court of Appeal and sought, unsuccessfully, to obtain special leave to appeal to the High Court. The end result was an amount of around $600,000 for principal, interest and costs received by Mr Collie in the early 2000s.

The ASIC litigation

  1. The ASIC litigation commenced with ASIC’s predecessor seeking to have Mr Collie and his wife prohibited from acting as company directors because of failures of companies of which they had been directors. Those failures were, Mr Collie said, the result of the legal actions referred to above.
  2. ASIC’s actions were ultimately resolved in favour of Mr and Mrs Collie. However, the path to that outcome had many twists and turns, which included the Federal Court dismissing as premature an application brought by Mr Collie, and awarding costs against him.
  3. In August 1998, a bankruptcy notice was issued against Mr Collie at the behest of ASIC in relation to the costs order.[48]
  4. Having regard to all these matters, I have little difficulty accepting Mr Collie’s evidence that the litigation in which he was involved meant that:
    for most of the 1990s and into 2002 I was potentially exposed to a significant risk of adverse outcomes both as to results and costs from that litigation for all of that time.
  5. The Commissioner did not contest this evidence. I find accordingly.[49]

The Commissioner’s submissions in relation to the litigation risk

  1. The Commissioner did not dispute the particulars of the litigation. Rather, the Commissioner disputed that the identified risk would support a prediction that, absent the scheme, no part of Mr Collie’s proportionate shares of the IET income would have been distributed to him personally.
  2. As the Commissioner put it:
    [Mr Collie’s] history of litigation with regulators and banks puts him in no different position from any other businessperson in Australia who is at risk of some exposure to liability as a consequence of litigation. Such caution does not require that a taxpayer personally earn no or only nominal income. One may separate business undertakings from personal assets by conducting business ventures through structures involving trusts and corporate trustees in a conventional approach to asset protection. However, holding a concern about litigation risk but does not necessitate conducting one’s affairs in a way that involves never earning income personally. There are other means of protecting assets, albeit ones that involve paying income tax, that Mr Collie could have employed instead of the NVI Schemes.[50]
  3. I accept that, with the pending or recent litigation risk, Mr Collie would have been mindful of protecting his assets in the event of a successful claim against him including the risk of an adverse costs award or awards. I also accept that protection of assets in itself did not necessarily require that Mr Collie not derive any income in his own name. There are countless businesspeople in Australia who face severe financial risk on a daily basis. For example, partners in firms who are unable to limit their exposure to or fully insure against the risk of negligence claims and the many small business owners who have either personally incurred, or guaranteed, borrowings or other current or contingent liabilities. It would not be reasonable to predict such persons would structure their affairs in such a way that they would never receive any personal reward for their efforts or for putting their capital at risk.
  4. Further, based on Mr Collie’s own submissions, and even assuming in his favour the availability of and taking into account the Tarragunda Trading Trust losses, Mr Collie’s share of the IET income over the relevant income years amounted to around $3.5M. Mr Collie would have the Tribunal accept that he invested in the IET businesses and engaged in the activities required of him as a director of the IETs that generated this substantial income, over a period of five years, in return for not a single cent of income. And that in a context in which, according to his tax returns, he received minimal or no income from other sources.[51]
  5. I take into account that this is not a case of alienation of personal income by, for example, a professional person providing services for a fee. Nevertheless, the IET income was derived from the activities of the IETs for which the trusts were compensated. Mr Collie carried out administrative work associated with the IET businesses. Although the IET businesses had the benefit of work undertaken by administrative staff and the use of standard templates, it is clear from Mr Collie’s oral evidence that he was not a mere passive investor in the businesses. He was engaged in completing or directing the completion of documents and other work required for the operation of the IETs and had duties as a director of the various IETs.[52]
  6. I accept that the particular circumstances that prevailed at the time the NVI schemes were carried out set this matter apart to some extent from the common circumstance of businesspersons seeking to protect their assets from potential negligence or other claims of creditors. Although the various disputes were ultimately resolved largely in his favour, in the period leading up to the entry into and carrying out of the schemes, Mr Collie faced:
    (a) a judgement debt of over $23M owed to the ATO (following the Supreme Court hearing in November 1995), which with interest had grown to some $30M before the settlement in 1997;

    (b) a bankruptcy notice issued at the behest of the ATO (in 1995);

    (c) potential for an adverse costs award in the AGS litigation (settled in 1997);

    (d) a writ issued by the ANZ Bank claiming $6M in 1996 (settled in 1998);

    (e) a bankruptcy notice issued at the behest of ASIC in August 1998;

    (f) an environment in which he had come to believe he could not trust ATO officers.[53]

  7. On the other hand, most of this litigation was settled before or early in the period of the relevant income years.
  8. Mr Collie had a long history of acquiring assets and conducting business operations in trusts.[54] He received no income from trusts in any of the relevant years. However, Mr Collie conducting various other business and investment activities in a multitude of trusts over an extended period is not inconsistent with income from such ventures being distributed to Mr Collie personally. That history did not require that no income from any of these ventures would be distributed to Mr Collie. No evidence relating to how those various trusts distributed income over their years of operation was brought to my attention, and certainly no evidence that Mr Collie had any history of causing these various trusts not to distribute income to him.
  9. However, the Commissioner’s submission that income distributed to Mr Collie personally could then have been invested or reinvested in trusts established by Mr Collie is, in my view, unrealistic. Against the background of the risks faced by Mr Collie, if seeking to protect himself from creditors, it is unlikely that Mr Collie would have exposed himself to the risk of payments to a related trustee company being recovered in bankruptcy proceedings.
  10. These factors weigh in favour of it being reasonable to conclude that Mr Collie may have avoided the whole of his share of the IET income being distributed to him personally. The case for a conclusion that none of that income would have been distributed to Mr Collie is less compelling for the reasons I have mentioned.
  11. Mr Collie sought to draw support for his proposition that he would not have received IET income personally from the judgement of the Full Federal Court in Commissioner of Taxation v Mochkin.[55] In that case, the taxpayer had previously entered into a commission-sharing agreement with a stockbroking firm (Bridges) but became embroiled in a dispute regarding whether the taxpayer was liable for defaults by clients. Bridges brought proceedings against the taxpayer. The dispute ended in the taxpayer compromising the proceedings, at considerable cost, after receiving senior counsel’s advice that he was unlikely to be successful in defending the claim.
  12. Thereafter the taxpayer ceased to conduct business in his own name. Instead, he conducted business through a company (Ledger), controlled by the taxpayer and his wife, which acted as the trustee of a family trust. Relevant fees were held to have been derived by the company, but the Commissioner also argued that Part IVA applied such that the fee income should be treated as the taxpayer’s income.
  13. Having regard to the findings at first instance regarding the taxpayer’s litigation history, the Full Court held that the taxpayer did not obtain a tax benefit as a consequence of ceasing to conduct the business in his own name and instead conducting it through his company, Ledger. Mr Collie drew attention to these passages in the judgment of Sackville J with whom Markel and Kenny JJ agreed:
    101 Mr de Wijn pointed out on behalf of the Taxpayer that the tax benefit identified by the Commissioner was the diversion of income from the personal exertion of the Taxpayer to Ledger, thereby reducing the Taxpayer's assessable income. Mr de Wijn submitted that on the findings made by the primary Judge, the commission income derived by Ledger could not reasonably be expected to have been included in the Taxpayer's assessable income, had the Ledger scheme not been entered into or carried out.

    102 In my view, the Taxpayer's submission has considerable force. On the primary Judge's findings, once the Taxpayer found himself personally liable to Bridges, he would not have carried on the stockbroking business in his own right. Given the Taxpayer's unwillingness to accept personal liability for client defaults, it was virtually inevitable, as the Taxpayer submitted, that the business would have been carried on through a limited liability entity. That entity, not the Taxpayer, would have derived the commission income from the brokers.

    103 The Commissioner attempted to meet this formidable difficulty by asserting that, even if a corporate entity had carried on the business, that entity would have paid or distributed to the Taxpayer the income that in fact was distributed to the beneficiaries through the No 2 Trust. However, this submission seems to assume, contrary to the primary Judge's findings, that the commission income was generated solely by the Taxpayer's own efforts and that the so-called Ledger team was (in Mr Maxwell's words) "largely ephemeral".

    104 In view of the findings made by the primary Judge, it is difficult to see why, assuming the Ledger scheme had not been entered into or carried out, the net commission income derived by the hypothetical corporation substituted for Ledger, would have found its way to the Taxpayer. It might have been expected that some of that income would have come to the Taxpayer, in the form of a fair reward for the services he would have provided to the corporation. But the primary Judge was not asked to and did not make a finding as to what a fair salary might have been for the Taxpayer, if his salary was to reflect his contributions to Ledger or to a hypothetical corporation conducting the business. Nor did the Commissioner ask this Court to make any such finding. In short, the Commissioner's case has always been that the tax benefit obtained by the Taxpayer in connection with the Ledger scheme was the total of the net commission income derived by Ledger.

    105 In these circumstances, I think the evidence precludes a finding that there was a reasonable expectation that, had the Ledger scheme not been entered into or carried out, the Taxpayer would have derived the whole of the net commission income that was in fact paid to Ledger.[56]

    (Emphasis added.)

  14. The current case in my view is relevantly distinguishable. In Mochkin, it was the business carried on by the taxpayer that carried the risk of liability. Nor was it a hypothetical risk; the earlier litigation demonstrated that the risk was real and obviously unacceptable. In those circumstances, it is not surprising that the Court characterised the taxpayer’s decision to trade through a company as “virtually inevitable”.
  15. Further, the Commissioner’s case was that, absent the scheme, the whole of Ledger’s net income would have been derived by the taxpayer. By contrast, in this case the Commissioner’s counterfactual is that the whole or part of Mr Collie’s share of the IET income would have been derived by Mr Collie. Indeed, the Full Court in Mochkin acknowledged that it “might have been expected that some of that income would have come to the Taxpayer.”
  16. In short, with its relevantly different factual context from Mr Collie’s circumstances and the Commissioner’s counterfactual, I find Mochkin to be of limited assistance in resolving the current matter.

Has Mr Collie proved on balance that he would have had available funds to meet living expenses and other outgoings under his counterfactuals?

  1. A controversy between the parties is whether Mr Collie has proved that he had access to funds to meet his living expenses during the relevant years.
  2. The Commissioner characterised the assertion by Mr Collie that he did not require the monies that were passed through the schemes because he had access to other monies as essential to Mr Collie’s case. On that basis, the Commissioner submitted that, if the evidence did not establish that premise, Mr Collie would not have shown that his postulates were sufficiently reliable predictions of what would have occurred if the schemes had not been entered into or carried out.
  3. Mr Collie denied that the availability of financial resources to fund living expenses was an essential plank of his case. His submissions indicate his evidence in that regard was adduced primarily to distinguish his circumstances from those in the Hart case where the Court at first instance found that the income from the IETs was deposited to Mr Hart’s personal bank account.
  4. Mr Collie gave evidence by affidavit and was cross-examined both in the AAT hearing and before this Tribunal. His evidence was that he drew down living expenses from bank accounts held by the trustee of the Woodbury Farm Trust and the Tarragunda Trading Trust as and when required to meet expenses.
  5. In the Applicant’s Reply to Respondent’s Written Closing Submissions, Mr Collie submitted that, because such amounts were paid to acquire goods and services for full consideration from third parties in the ordinary course of business, they could not be clawed back under bankruptcy law provisions. I accept that would be so.
  6. However, Mr Collie went on to submit that his actions were therefore consistent with this evidence. Even if that is so, I do not see how it significantly assists Mr Collie. The same could be said if income were received by Mr Collie directly and expended on acquiring goods and services for full consideration from third parties.
  7. The Commissioner submitted that the Tribunal should not accept Mr Collie’s evidence about the availability of funds he could access for living expenses. The Commissioner expressly eschewed a submission that Mr Collie should not be believed. That is to say, the Commissioner did not say Mr Collie was lying. Rather, the Commissioner submitted that his evidence was not reliable in the absence of sufficient objective corroborating evidence and, consistent with the authorities requiring self-serving statements to be closely scrutinised, should not be accepted.
  8. Mr Collie gave specific evidence as to the source of funds he said would have been available to him to meet living expenses. He gave evidence regarding funds he would have been able to access from the Woodbury Farm Trust arising from the 1993 sale of the trust’s stud cattle and plant and equipment for more than $600,000 and the sale of a grazing property – “Willow Grove” – for in excess of $1.4M. He also gave evidence of repayment of loans owed to that trust and the CGH Trust by entities that received Canowindra Trust distributions in excess of $988,150 in the 1994 to 1996 financial years. Mr Collie’s evidence was that these proceeds amounted to more $2,000,000 in after tax funds which provided more than sufficient monies to meet living expenses.
  9. This is not a case where there is, for example, a mere assertion that funds were available. Mr Collie gave quite specific evidence as to the sources of the funds said to be available. However, the Commissioner still maintained that there was insufficient evidence to corroborate Mr Collie’s assertions. For example, that Mr Collie did not provide documentary evidence such as bank statements, trust resolutions, loan agreements, although he did, for example, produce copies of transfers consistent with his evidence regarding the sale of the properties.
  10. Significantly, although some bank statements were in evidence, Mr Collie did not produce bank statements showing balances of the order he indicated would have been available to him to access, or entries evidencing him doing so.
  11. The Tribunal is left in the awkward position that, as previously noted, the Commissioner does not say Mr Collie is not to be believed. The Commissioner does not even say that Mr Collie is mistaken as to recollection of the matters on which he gave quite specific evidence regarding the source of the funds Mr Collie says were available to meet expenses, although that must be implicit in the submission that his evidence is not reliable. Nevertheless, the Commissioner says I should not accept Mr Collie’s evidence because there is insufficient documentary evidence to support Mr Collie’s assertion.
  12. In summary, there are a number of relevant circumstances that I have taken into account in relation to this controversy.
  13. First, as noted, the Commissioner does not attack Mr Collie’s credibility; he does not say Mr Collie is not to be believed.
  14. Secondly, Mr Collie has presented some documents consistent with his evidence, albeit not a complete documentary trail, such as loan agreements and an account of the dispersal of sale proceeds for the relevant properties. The transactions occurred some 30 years ago which makes that less surprising than might otherwise be the case. However, in my view, this matter needs to be considered in the context of Mr Collie’s evidence regarding his history of disputation with the ATO; that he came to believe he could not trust the ATO; and the nature and outcome of the schemes.
  15. The schemes were such that substantial income – more than $3.5M as it turned out - would be received by entities associated with Mr Collie but with no tax at all being paid on that income under the schemes. Having regard to his acrimonious history with the ATO and his state of mind in which, as he asserted and I have accepted, he did not trust the ATO, it would be expected that Mr Collie would have kept meticulous records of his expenditure and the source of that expenditure over the years. It must have been quite predictable that the ATO might seek to apply Part IVA to the arrangements and that the question of how Mr Collie could meet his outgoings, while returning modest or nil taxable income for the whole of the five-year period in which the schemes were carried out, would likely become an issue.
  16. On the other hand, although now in the somewhat distant past, the amounts said to be available – amounting to some $2M – are significant. It is not inherently improbable that, if Mr Collie had access to funds of that order of magnitude, he would have a specific recollection in that regard. The same may be said of the sales of the grazing properties. They were substantial transactions; it is not improbable that Mr Collie would retain some memory of the details of their sales.
  17. However, there is an absence of bank records and documentary evidence that, having regard to the context mentioned, one would expect Mr Collie to have retained. There is, for example, evidence that the property sales took place, but no evidence was brought to my attention that fully accounts for how the proceeds were dispersed.
  18. I acknowledge that the schemes took place many years ago. When a dispute or serious risk of a dispute arises for the first time many years after the relevant transactions, that may justify a particular approach to the question of what evidence might reasonably be expected to have been adduced by the taxpayer in the circumstances especially if, for example, the statutory time periods for retention of records have passed. But that is not this case. Mr Collie had been in dispute with the ATO for an extended period. Investigations into these schemes commenced within a relatively short period after the relevant income years.
  19. Further, and importantly, there is the question of the other side of the equation, so to speak: what was Mr Collie’s lifestyle during this period; what were his expenses and other outgoings; what assets, if any, did he acquire; and where did he source the funds to do so? No significant evidence was brought to my attention to enable me to make an assessment of the outgoings that Mr Collie would have met during the relevant period against which to test his assertions regarding available funds.
  20. Mr Collie asserts that he did not receive any monies from the distributions of IET income in the relevant years. It follows that he must have obtained funds to meet living expenses from elsewhere. It is one thing to say there were funds in a trust or trusts which Mr Collie controlled or exercised influence over. However, trust funds are just that. The trustee must account for trust funds in accordance with the relevant trust deed. That requires the keeping of records.
  21. If trust funds were or could have been lent or otherwise made available to Mr Collie, the trustee would be obliged to keep records regarding the dispersal of the trust funds and the terms on which the funds were released. Accordingly, it is to be expected that records evidencing whatever funds were drawn upon by Mr Collie would have been created and, in view of the history of disputation with and distrust of the ATO, retained by Mr Collie. Against that context, the absence of comprehensive records indicating the extent to which funds were available or drawn upon, and the extent of the funds deployed to meet Mr Collie’s living costs and other outgoings, casts doubt on the reliability of Mr Collie’s recollection of events that occurred many years ago.
  22. My starting point is that Mr Collie has given evidence of the availability of funds to meet living expenses. I do not begin with a default position that such evidence is not to be believed. I start with the position that, since the Commissioner has not challenged Mr Collie’s credit, I should accept the evidence unless there is a good reason for determining that it is not reliable.
  23. I am also mindful of the exhortations in the authorities referenced above that self-serving testimony should be treated with caution and carefully tested. The need to do so does not only arise to guard against making factual findings based on deliberately false evidence which is not alleged in this case. It is also necessary to test whether a taxpayer’s evidence, especially when relating to events that occurred many years ago, represents a reliable recollection of those events.
  24. In that regard, it is also relevant that Mr Collie has been in dispute with the ATO over these issues for many years; the AAT proceeding was commenced in 2010. In those circumstances, there is inevitably a risk, even with the greatest of diligence, that Mr Collie’s recollections will have been inadvertently coloured to a greater or lesser degree by the outcome he hopes to achieve in these proceedings. That factor underlines the importance of considering the extent to which Mr Collie’s testimony is supported by contemporaneous records of the kind it is to be expected that a sophisticated practitioner with a history of disputation with and distrust of the ATO, and embarking upon a scheme that would (subject to Part IVA) shelter some millions of dollars in income from tax, and who returned little or no taxable income over the relevant years, would be expected to be able to produce.
  25. I have found this issue to be finely balanced. The considerations discussed above, particularly the absence of records regarding the availability of trust funds and the extent to which they were drawn upon by Mr Collie, give me pause in accepting Mr Collie’s evidence at face value. On the other hand, as I have mentioned, this is not a case of mere assertions being made by Mr Collie. He backs up his assertions with relatively specific evidence and some documentary support. The Commissioner does not say Mr Collie is not to be believed. It is unlikely Mr Collie would be mistaken in his recollections regarding such a significant matter which are supported to a degree, although by no means fully, by corroborating documents.
  26. Since the Commissioner does not attack Mr Collie’s credit, to reject his evidence would be tantamount to concluding he is mistaken in his recollection of the funds available or as to the detailed bases for that assertion set out in his affidavit. Nothing in Mr Collie’s affidavit evidence – for example, significant internal inconsistencies – suggests the evidence is not an honest account of Mr Collie’s recollection. Likewise, there was nothing in Mr Collie’s responses to cross-examination questions that caused me to doubt the accuracy of his evidence. Mr Collie did not present as tentative or hesitant in the answers he gave, but rather as firm and assured.
  27. On the other hand, basic evidence of the type which would have assisted Mr Collie – such as relevant bank statements – was not adduced in circumstances where, for the reasons I have given, evidence of that kind would be expected to be available. Mr Collie would have the Tribunal accept that he had available in excess of $2M to access over a five-year period from which to meet living expenses and whatever other outgoings he may have incurred. Yet he did not produce a bank statement or other document to corroborate the ready availability of such funds or the extent to which they were drawn upon. There was no evidence drawn to my attention that Mr Collie was unable to obtain such statements. Other bank statements were in evidence.
  28. Further, even if it is accepted that Mr Collie has access to funds in the order of $2M as he asserts, I am unable to be satisfied that Mr Collie would not have required access to any of the IET income. It is not possible to be so satisfied without knowing more about the lifestyle of Mr Collie and his wife and their expenditure over the five-year period in which the schemes operated and in which Mr Collie returned little or no taxable income. While Mr Collie’s evidence is that he had access to sufficient funds to meeting his living expenses, there is an almost endless array of other potential ongoings, for example, holidays, asset acquisitions, loans to family members and so on.
  29. No significant evidence was drawn to my attention on which to base any assessment of Mr Collie’s expenditure over the relevant years. I cannot assess whether the available funds, even on Mr Collie’s own evidence, would have been sufficient to meet such expenditure. Mr Collie was, after all, generating substantial income through the IETs and paying no tax on that income (and little other tax). In that context, it would not be surprising if he did not confine himself to a modest lifestyle. Yet, I have no evidence on which to base any assessment of his outgoings.
  30. For these reasons, I am not satisfied that it would be reasonable to conclude that, absent the schemes, Mr Collie would not have required access to any of the IET income.

Has Mr Collie proved that his counterfactuals each comprise a prediction as to events which would have taken place if the relevant scheme had not been entered into or carried out which is sufficiently reliable for it to be regarded as a reasonable prediction?

  1. The Commissioner attacked Mr Collie’s counterfactuals on several bases.
  2. The first, that Mr Collie had not proved what the Commissioner says is the underlying premise of the counterfactuals: that Mr Collie had access to sufficient funds to meet his outgoings without recourse to IET income. For the reasons I have given, I am not satisfied on the evidence before the Tribunal that Mr Collie could have met his outgoings without access to his share of the IET income.
  3. The second was that Mr Collie had not proved the availability of the tax losses on which the counterfactuals depended. For the reasons I have given, I accept that there is sufficient evidence for a reasonable expectation that Mr Collie would have acted on the basis that the losses to which he deposed would have been available at the relevant times.
  4. The third basis is that Mr Collie’s litigation history did not require him to avoid deriving any income at all. I have already indicated why, to the extent that Mr Collie aimed to achieve asset protection, I consider it to be unrealistic to predict that he would have received income directly and then direct that income (after tax) to the trustee of a trust to acquire assets if he had the option of directing the income directly to a trust.
  5. However, Mr Collie’s asset protection objectives could have been achieved even if he derived some IET income personally – at least sufficient income to meet his living expenses and other outgoings. Such income spent on acquiring goods and services from third parties for arm’s length consideration would not be vulnerable to recovery in the event of bankruptcy.
  6. Expressed at the highest level, the central disputed elements of Mr Collie’s and the Commissioner’s counterfactuals, are the final distributions of the IET income. Mr Collie says income would go to a company where it would be taxed at the lower, corporate tax rate. The Commissioner says would be distributed wholly or partly to Mr Collie. In that regard, the Commissioner submitted that it is reasonable to predict that Mr Collie would have received his share of the IET income from the Canowindra Trust which he controlled.[57]
  7. However, Mr Collie gave evidence that he was not a beneficiary of the Canowindra Trust.[58] If that were correct, the Commissioner’s counterfactual would be inconsistent with the terms of the trust. As already noted, the Tribunal’s task is to determine whether Mr Collie has discharged the burden of proving his counterfactuals constitute a sufficiently reliable prediction to be reasonable. If he fails to do so, the objection decisions must be affirmed (other than as to quantum). There is no burden on the Commissioner to prove the detail of his counterfactuals.
  8. In any case, though, while it may be correct to say that Mr Collie was not a beneficiary of the Canowindra Trust in the sense that Mr Collie did not have a beneficial interest in the trust property, he was a discretionary object of the trust. His son was the “Principal” of the trust and the discretionary objects of the trust included the parents of the Principal.[59] Thus, the terms of the trust deed were not a barrier to distributions to Mr Collie as the Commissioner submitted.
  9. Having regard to all of the circumstances as discussed above, I am not persuaded that it would be reasonable to conclude that, absent the schemes, Mr Collie would have ensured that none of the IET income would be distributed to him. In circumstances where Mr Collie returned minimal or no income in each of the five relevant years, there is, in my view, insufficient evidence regarding Mr Collie’s lifestyle and other outgoings to be persuaded that he could have met all expenditure (the level of which is unable to be determined on the evidence before the Tribunal) without recourse to any of the IET income. His asset protection objectives, even having regard to the extraordinary litigation history, did not require that Mr Collie receive no part of the substantial income from his IET-related activities over a five-year period. On the evidence before the Tribunal, I am unable to make an assessment of what proportion, if any, of the IET income it would be reasonable to predict would not have been distributed to Mr Collie.
  10. It follows that Mr Collie has not, on the basis of his counterfactuals, proved that he has not obtained a tax benefit.

Would Mr Collie’s counterfactuals be subject to Part IVA?

  1. For the reasons already set out, I am not persuaded that Mr Collie’s counterfactuals represent a reliable prediction of what would have occurred in the absence of the schemes. It is therefore not strictly necessary to consider whether, in any case, the counterfactuals would not, if accepted, also fail to establish that Mr Collie did not obtain tax benefits because the counterfactuals would be subject to Part IVA determinations.
  2. However, in view of the submissions made by the parties, and in case I am wrong in respect of my conclusions thus far, I have considered the issues raised by the parties in that regard.
  3. The first matter is Mr Collie’s submission that he would be denied procedural fairness if the Tribunal entertained the issue. The basis of that submission is that the potential application of Part IVA to Mr Collie’s counterfactuals was not raised in any of the Commissioner’s statements of facts, issues and contentions, including the final iteration, the Respondent’s Second Further Amended Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions. Mr Collie submitted that
    if the Respondent seriously wished to rely on this argument he should have referred to, and particularised the alleged scheme in the Respondent's SFIC so that Mr Collie could consider the argument and, if need be, respond with evidence.[60]
  4. I certainly agree that the contention should have been raised in the Commissioner’s final Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions. This dispute has been before the Tribunal since 2010. It is inappropriate for a significant issue of this kind to be raised a matter of weeks before the hearing of the remittal.
  5. However, it does not follow that a matter, which I have concluded is potentially relevant to determination of the correct or preferable decision of the Tribunal, is simply to be ignored. The Tribunal is, after all, tasked with considering whether Mr Collie obtained a tax benefit and, for the reasons I have given, the potential application of Part IVA to Mr Collie’s counterfactuals is relevant to that issue.
  6. Mr Collie was on notice that the Commissioner would be raising this issue from 1 September 2025 when the Respondent’s Outline of Opening Submissions was filed. That was some three weeks before the date set for hearing of the remittal.
  7. Mr Collie could have approached the Tribunal with a view to seeking directions allowing for a response, including with evidence, to be filed, or even for the hearing to be vacated and re-listed at a later time. He could have sought a direction that the Commissioner articulate the basis on which his counterfactuals would reasonably be expected to be subject to a Part IVA determination. He did not take any of those steps. Nor did Mr Collie identify the nature of any evidence might have been adduced in response to the Commissioner’s contention.
  8. Mr Collie relied upon technical submissions regarding the case law concerning this issue and contentions related to procedural fairness. Mr Collie is a lawyer of many years standing and had the benefit of sophisticated legal representation. I infer that he made an informed choice to approach the issue, once it was raised by the Commissioner, in the way that he did.
  9. In those circumstances, I have concluded that it would not be unfair for the Tribunal to consider this issue.
  10. However, there remains an awkwardness in considering the potential application of Part IVA to a statement of circumstances – Mr Collie’s counterfactuals – that are themselves necessarily hypothetical in nature. All the more so if the Tribunal is asked to consider the hypothetical application of Part IVA to a hypothetical scheme without the benefit of detailed submissions regarding why it is said that relevant conclusions as to dominant purpose and tax benefit should be reached.
  11. One aspect of how the Tribunal should go about that task concerns the burden of proof. As noted above, Mr Collie bears the burden of proving the assessments underpinned by the Part IVA determinations are excessive. Insofar as Mr Collie seeks to discharge that burden through his counterfactuals, is it part of his burden to prove the counterfactuals would not attract the operation of Part IVA?
  12. In response to my question during the hearing, Ms Pierce said the Commissioner did not go so far as to submit that Mr Collie must prove his counterfactuals would not be subject to Part IVA. Ms Pierce submitted the Tribunal must determine whether each of Mr Collie’s counterfactuals “bears the quality of a Part IVA scheme and therefore it’s disqualified as a reasonable counterfactual.”
  13. It is not immediately clear to me why the applicant does not, if relying on a counterfactual, bear a burden of proving the counterfactual would not be subject to the operation of Part IVA. Subject to procedural fairness considerations, why is it not part of an applicant’s burden to prove their counterfactual is not precluded from comprising a reliable prediction for any reason, including that the counterfactual would be subject to the operation of Part IVA?
  14. However, this aspect of the burden of proof only arose in passing in the course of oral closing submissions. I have not had the benefit of, and did not seek, detailed submissions on the point. I therefore approach the issue in the manner submitted by Ms Pierce.
  15. The following submissions on this issue were made in the Respondent’s Outline of Final Submissions:
    167. Mr Collie’s counterfactual is itself a Part IVA scheme.

    168. It is a series of steps which involves Mr Collie’s share of the IET income being distributed to trusts with claimed accrued losses (which the evidence does not prove) rather than being paid to Mr Collie so that he can meet his living expenses.

    169. In this case, no genuine commercial purpose, including Mr Collie’s stated objective of asset protection, would be served by distributing income from the Canowindra Trust to loss-bearing trusts or by accumulating that income in trusts associated with Mr Collie. The sole purpose of taking such steps would be to avoid paying tax on either the whole or a portion of Mr Collie’s share of the IET income. In cross-examination before this Tribunal, Mr Collie himself described the main reason for making distributions of funds to companies is that they pay the corporate tax rate. Nor is earning no income personally a strategy required in order to protect one’s assets from putative creditors. Adopting that scheme counterfactual would cause Mr Collie to obtain a tax benefit in the amount of tax avoided on that share of the IET income.

    (Footnotes omitted)

  16. Those submissions do not articulate in any specific way why it is said that Part IVA would apply to Mr Collie’s counterfactuals. They do not address by reference to the mandatory statutory considerations the basis on which it would be reasonable to conclude the counterfactuals would have a dominant purpose of obtaining a tax benefit. They do not address why the scheme would deliver a tax benefit let alone the quantum of any such benefit. To the extent that the scheme would rely on tax losses, the Commissioner acknowledges that merely distributing business income derived by a trust to a beneficiary with carried forward losses would not ordinarily attract the operation of Part IVA.
  17. On the basis of the evidence and submissions before the Tribunal, I cannot determine that Mr Collie’s counterfactuals would be subject to Part IVA. That is not to say they would not. The Commissioner’s submissions to not provide a satisfactory – in the sense of sufficiently detailed - basis on which to reach a conclusion on this issue.

Would any prediction involving IET income being distributed to Mr Collie personally be unreasonable?

  1. For the reasons I have set out regarding Mr Collie’s counterfactuals, I am not persuaded that it would be reasonable to conclude that Mr Collie would not have allowed the trustee of any trust from which he could have derived IET income, directly or indirectly, to distribute any income to him in his own right. For the same reasons, it is not possible to determine that amounts less than the tax benefits, adjusted in accordance with these reasons, would have been obtained by Mr Collie.
  2. It follows that I am not persuaded that Mr Collie has discharged the burden of proving the tax benefits are excessive on this basis.

In respect of the 1997 income year, has Mr Collie proved that in any case he would not have obtained a tax benefit because any IET income attributable to him would have been fully offset by available Tarragunda Trading Trust tax losses of $353,887?

  1. Mr Collie deposed that the Tarragunda Trading Trust had available tax losses of $353,887 for the 1997 income year. As previously noted, the Commissioner says Mr Collie has not provided sufficient evidence to prove the existence of those losses.
  2. Notably, no copy of a tax return evidencing these losses is in evidence. However, Mr Collie complains that the Commissioner accepted these losses and that it is unfair for the Commissioner to now put him to proof.
  3. The material to which Mr Collie refers in support of his contention that the Commissioner had accepted the existence of these losses comprises correspondence that Mr Collie exchanged with the Commissioner in 2005 and the Respondent’s Second Further Amended Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions. The correspondence does not unequivocally accept the existence of the losses and in any case, as the Commissioner points out, was relatively early in the investigation. Paragraph 26 in the Commissioner’s Second Further Amended Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions, referenced by Mr Collie, on a careful reading appears to accept only a concession by Mr Collie but otherwise recount rather than accept Mr Collie’s position.
  4. However, that same paragraph records that a net loss of $353,887 was reported in the trust’s tax return for 1997. Additionally, there is in evidence 2005 correspondence from Mr Collie’s accountant confirming a net loss of that amount for the 1997 income year.[61]
  5. Recalling that the Commissioner does not say Mr Collie’s evidence should not be believed, and having regard to the supporting evidence mentioned, I am persuaded that Mr Collie’s evidence that his share of the IET income derived by the trust for the 1997 income year would have been offset by losses of $353,887 should be accepted. The evidence is consistent with the amounts recorded by the Commissioner as disclosed in the return and with the accountant’s letter. I have no reason to doubt the accuracy of Mr Collie’s testimony or the corroborating documentary evidence.
  6. Mr Collie’s share of the IET income for 1997 is less than $353,887. It follows that Mr Collie did not obtain tax benefit in respect of the 1997 income year.

If the Tribunal concludes that Mr Collie has not discharged the burden of proving he did not obtain a tax benefit in any of the income years in dispute:

Has Mr Collie established that Tarragunda Trading Trust losses of $43,878.25 would have been available in the 1998 income year?

  1. Immediately above, I have accepted that the Tarragunda Trading Trust had available losses of $353,887 for the 1997 income year. Deducting Mr Collie’s share of the IET income for that year - $310,008 – leaves available tax losses of $43,879. Accordingly, I accept Mr Collie’s submission regarding the losses available in respect of the 1998 income year.

What, if any, consequential amendments to the penalty assessments are required?

  1. The conclusions set out above will result in the tax benefit amounts determined by the AAT being further reduced to the amounts set out below.
  2. In accordance with the Full Court’s judgement, there will be no change to the rate at which administrative penalties are to be assessed. However, the reduction in the tax benefit amounts will mean that the penalty rate is applied to lower tax shortfall amounts for each relevant income year (nil in respect of the 1997 income year resulting in the penalty relating for that year falling away).

DISPOSITION OF THE REVIEWS

  1. In light of the conclusions set out above, the objection decisions must be set aside and substituted with decisions allowing the objections in part.
  2. The tax benefit amounts must be reduced to those submitted by Mr Collie in the event that, as has occurred, the Tribunal does not accept Mr Collie’s submission that he did not obtain a tax benefit in any of the relevant income years, but accepts the Tarragunda TradingTrust losses are proved. The adjusted tax benefit amounts are:
    (a) 1997: $0

    (b) 1998 $ 795,495

    (c) 1999: $1,032,525

    (d) 2000: $1,813,825

    (e) 2001: $ 958,710

  3. The administrative penalties must be correspondingly reduced, such that no penalty applies in respect of the 1997 income year and the penalties for the other income years are reduced by the application of the assessed percentage rate of penalty to the reduced tax shortfalls consequent upon the reductions in the tax benefit amounts.
