Jones and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 473 (22 March 1995)

Last Updated: 23 September 2008



Administrative Appeals Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - Late election - Applicant upon resignation from Commonwealth Teaching Service had an imperfect understanding of preservation benefits based upon discussions with colleagues. Failure to make own further and better enquiries. Late election refused.



Superannuation Act 1976 - Ss137(1), 157(1)



Re Anne Margaret Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal Decision No 9987)

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 followed



AAT Decision No 10083

Re: KERRY JONES

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION



22 March 1995 Senior Member M.D. Allen

Mr N.J. Attwood AO, Member

Mr H.G. Julian DSC, Member



  1. By notice lodged 21 June 1994 the Applicant sought review of a decision by a Delegate of the Respondent made 8 June 1994 affirming a prior decision of 7 May 1992 pursuant to Ss157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) not to direct that the election made by the Applicant on 5 September 1990 under Ss137(1) of the Act be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by the said subsection.
  2. The matter came on for hearing before this Tribunal at Canberra on 28 February 1995, the Applicant appearing on his own behalf and the Respondent being represented by Mr Adams, an officer of the Commissioner for Superannuation’s office.
  3. Subsection 137(1) of the Act states:

“Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and is not entitled to pension under this Act, he may, not later than 21 days after he ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to him.”

Subsection 157(1) of the said Act states:

“Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.”

  1. Exhibit A in these proceedings is a statement by the Applicant. From the Applicant’s oral evidence and Exhibit A, the following history of the matter may be stated:

(i) The Applicant was born on 16 August 1946 and has a Bachelor of Arts and a Diploma of Education.

(ii) At the beginning of the 1969 school year the Applicant commenced teaching in Canberra. At that time he was an employee of the New South Wales Department of Education but in 1973, when the ACT school’s system was transferred to the Commonwealth Teaching Service, he transferred to that service. That transfer entailed transfer of Superannuation, Long Service Leave and other benefits.

(iii) Apart from one year spent in teaching in Canada, the Applicant continued to teach in ACT schools until the end of Term 2 in 1983 when he went on Long Service Leave.

(iv) Prior to commencing Long Service Leave the Applicant and his wife had made a decision to transfer to Victoria and to that end had, in the 1982/83 Christmas school holidays, an interview at the Victorian Ministry of Education.

(v) In July 1983 the Applicant’s wife received an offer of a teaching position at a Victorian school. She therefore resigned her teaching position in the ACT and moved to Victoria. The Applicant then took Long Service Leave for the period of Third Term in 1983 and spent this period undertaking casual teaching in Victorian schools. It was during this period that the firm decision was made by the Applicant to resign from the Commonwealth Teaching Service and to seek teaching positions in Victoria.

(vi) The Applicant submitted a notice of resignation to the Personnel Office of the ACT Schools Authority on 13 January 1984.

(vii) During 1984/85 the Applicant had a teaching position at a Victorian school. At the end of Second Term 1985 he resigned from the Victorian Teaching Service to take up a position at the Australian Counter Disaster College at Mount Macedon in Victoria. He resigned from this position in January 1986 and again sought casual teaching positions at Victorian schools. At the beginning of 1987 he obtained a full-time teaching position and stayed in that position until he and his wife returned to the ACT at the end of the 1988 school year.

(viii) Since his return to the ACT the Applicant has engaged in teaching on a casual basis having been unable to obtain a full-time position.

(ix) Whilst in Victoria the Applicant and his wife constructed a home at Woodend. The purchase of the land and construction of the dwelling was financed in part by proceeds of the sale of the Applicant’s house in the ACT, a mortgage plus the refund of the Applicant’s Superannuation contributions from the Commonwealth Teaching Service. The Applicant conceded that although he did not require the refund of Superannuation contributions to pay for the land and dwelling, those funds resulted in the mortgage taken out over the property to be lower than it otherwise would have been.

(x) Since returning to the ACT, the Applicant has invested funds in the Superannuation products with life and investment companies in order to provide a Superannuation Fund for himself and his wife and has also, together with his wife, purchased two housing units as part of a self-funded Superannuation provision.

  1. The Applicant’s case is that had he been aware of the full provisions of the right to elect to preserve benefits upon resignation, he would have done so as at all times he was conscious of the benefits of Superannuation.
  2. The Applicant has resigned from employment with the Commonwealth on two occasions. Whereas he does recollect the circumstances of his first resignation, that is from the Commonwealth Teaching Service, he has no memory of the paperwork surrounding his resignation from the Australian Counter Disaster College. Given the short period of his service with that organisation and its locality remote from its parent organisation, the Department of Defence, we accept the Applicant’s evidence on this point. It is understandable that any understanding or misunderstanding he had about preservation of benefits in 1983 carried through to his resignation from this organisation in 1986.
  3. At the time the Applicant resigned from the Commonwealth Teaching Service he did have a knowledge of the right to elect to preserve benefits albeit an imperfect understanding. The Applicant’s understanding of this right is set out in a letter by him to the Respondent dated 29 May 1991 (Document T14) and reads:

“My understanding at the time of my resignation was that I was entitled to leave my superannuation accumulation in the Commonwealth Fund and that it would continue to accrue interest as the Fund invested monies, but I was not of the understanding that at a future date I would be entitled to not only a refund of my contributions, but also to an employer contribution at that time.”

  1. The Applicant confirmed this understanding in cross-examination. He also stated in cross-examination that his understanding of Superannuation matters had been gained from discussion amongst his colleagues. In particular, he stated that advice he had received from colleagues pointed to the notion that persons who resigned received back their contributions plus interest.
  2. In his statement the Applicant claimed that when he and his wife went to the Personnel Office of the ACT Schools Authority to submit his resignation, he was handed a form numbered S2A for completion. That form contained three options, namely:

“(i) apply for a refund of accumulated contributions

(ii) elect for a payment of a lump sum benefit

(iii) elect for the preservation of superannuation rights”

(see Document T8) and the Applicant stated that he enquired of the officer dealing with him what was required in terms of the three choices on the form. The Applicant claims that that officer stated that those who were resigning chose the first option. In cross-examination the Applicant stated that the officer said that persons who were resigning normally took the first option. (Tribunal’s emphasis.)

  1. This latter version accords with the Applicant’s evidence that he believed on the one hand that persons who resigned normally took a refund of contributions. This understanding, together with his understanding of the effects of preservation, came from talking to colleagues at work.
  2. Because he understood that preservation meant that one’s contributions stayed in the Fund and earned interest only and did not know of the employer’s contribution, the Applicant regarded this option as a bad investment. Upon finding out the true nature of affairs having read in a Union journal in June 1990 an article on Superannuation and Preservation of Rights, the Applicant immediately applied for the right to make a late election.
  3. The principles which should guide the Respondent and the Tribunal in the exercise of the discretion pursuant to Ss157(1) of the Act have been extensively canvassed by a Tribunal consisting of Deputy President McMahon and Messrs Attwood and Julian in Re Anne Margaret Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation (Tribunal Decision No 9987). We do not regard it as necessary for these reasons to recite again that comprehensive review of the authorities which commences at paragraph 18 of the Tribunal’s decision but adopt and apply those principles to this case.
  4. Other factors which are relevant here are that the Applicant made a considered decision to resign from the Commonwealth Teaching Service and to pursue a teaching career in Victoria. Obviously, at the time of the first resignation and also no doubt at the time of his second resignation, ie from the Counter Disaster College, he did not envisage a return to teaching in the ACT and this may well have influenced his decision to seek a refund of contributions.
  5. In particular, the finality of his decision may be reflected in the Applicant’s failure to make any further and better enquiries as to the benefits available upon preservation. He had anecdotal advice as to what the benefits were supposed to be but made no enquires for himself prior to his resignation. Given the level of his education and number of years in the teaching service of both New South Wales and the Commonwealth, one might have expected that he would in the time available to him, have sought more information. As was pointed out by Deputy President Forgie in Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456 at p473:

“However, what the form does do is to alert the reader to the fact that he or she must choose one of three things: a refund of accumulated contributions, a lump sum benefit or preservation of superannuation rights. Certainly, no information can be gleaned from the form itself about these options but the form does, in my view, put the reader on notice that he or she should make further enquiries as to whether these options apply to his or her circumstances and generally find out more information about them.”

  1. The enquiry of the Personnel Officer cannot be said to have misled the Applicant. We accept that the words used were to the effect that upon resignation people normally take the refund option. The Applicant concedes that at the time he was aware, albeit imperfectly, of the right to elect preservation.
  2. That the Applicant relied upon the understanding of the Superannuation Scheme he obtained from discussions with colleagues avails him nothing. This aspect was discussed by the Tribunal in Re Chalk and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 278; 30 ALD 537 at 544 where the Tribunal (Bulley J presiding) pointed out that to rely on the advice of immediate colleagues is akin to accepting advice given in the public bar of an hotel at 7pm on a Friday night.
  3. As part of his case, the Respondent called Mr Whetton, Personnel Manager of the ACT Department of Administrative Services, who was in charge of the Personnel Branch of the ACT Interim Schools Authority at the time of the Applicant’s resignation. We did not find Mr Whetton’s evidence particularly helpful as he could not give direct evidence of dealings between the Applicant and the Personnel Branch. Statements as to what would have happened had all procedures and training had been followed are of little moment against the sworn evidence of an applicant as to what did happen.
  4. Notwithstanding the criticism of the weight of Mr Whetton’s evidence, for the reasons set out above we consider that on balance in all the circumstances of the case, we are not satisfied that it is desirable that the Applicant’s late election should be recognised. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
