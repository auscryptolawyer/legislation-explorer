GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: KAY NITA HOCROFT

REASONS FOR DECISION

12 July, 1991 C.J. Bannon Q.C., Deputy President

M. Allen Esq., Senior Member Dr D. Browne, Member

Kay Nita Hocroft ("the applicant") seeks review of a decision by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the respondent") made on 29 June, 1990 (T.2) confirming decisions to issue a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") in respect of the applicant specifying the "condition causing pain in the right hand radiating to the forearm and elbow", determining 1Q August, 1981 as the deemed date of issue of the BCC and the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's invalidity retirement was caused, or substantially contributed to, by a condition or conditions specified in the BCC, or by a condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions. The above decisions and opinions were taken, or would have been taken, if the applicant had not retired (T34), pursuant to the Superannuation Act. 1976 (Cth.) ("the Act"). The dates on which the decisions were taken and the opinion formed are set out in the document T2.

The delegate's decision (T2) sets out the following matters under the heading "Background".

"Background

  1. Ms Hocroft (nee Nelson prior to her marriage on 28 Nov 1981 and hereafter referred to as Ms Hocroft) was born on 27 Oct 1956. She became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 30 Mar 1981 on her permanent appointment to the Public Service and after having been employed on a continuous temporary basis with the Department of Defence, Sydney, since 10 Nov 1980. She had two separate earlier periods of service with the Commonwealth, most recently with Telecom Australia from February to October 1979. Under subsection 16(2) of the Act the Commissioner required Ms Hocroft to undergo a medical examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer (CMO) on or about 8 Oct 1980.
  2. In the report of that medical examination the CMO, Dr W Breinl, an approved medical practitioner for the purposes of subsection 16(2) of the Act, indicated that he considered that Ms Hocroft met the General Medical Standards (GMS), which is contained in the CMO's Handbook and is used as a guide in assessing entrants to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. The oversighting Senior Medical Officer, in his capacity as Adviser to the Commissioner, endorsed his assessment on 9 Oct 1980.
  3. On 10 Aug 1981, pursuant to subsection 16(4) of the Act, a delegate of the Commissioner considered the medical report and decided that a BCC was not to be issued.

Events leading to retirement

  1. Ms Hocroft was employed as a Data Processing Officer (DPO) Grade 2 with the Department of Defence. On 12 March 1981 and prior to her permanent appointment later that month, she sought advice from Dr E.J. D'Souza physician, concerning pain she was experiencing. He diagnosed the complaint as being suggestive of Carpal Tunnel Syndrome. He ultimately recommended that she be taken off keyboard duties, and this occurred on 6 Apr 1981.
  2. The climate at the time, where repetitive strain injury and related conditions among keyboard staff were becoming increasing evident, dictated that she be referred to the CMO for an assessment of her fitness for continued keyboard duty. She was thus seen on 1 Jun 1981 by Dr P McMahon, CMO, Sydney, who considered that there was no definite evidence of tenosynovitis despite the existence of slight pain in the wrist and associated areas. Coincidentally, Ms Hocroft initiated a request for transfer in order to accompany her husband who had been posted to Canberra. In the process of the negotiations associated with her placement a document was raised which indicated that she could not perform Data Processing Officer duties for medical reasons.
  3. She was accepted on transfer to the Department of Defence, Canberra, as a Clerical Assistant Grade 1 on 22 Jul 1981.
  4. Her employment history was uneventful until she was promoted back into the Data Processing field on 14 Oct 1982 and a subsequent transfer to the then Department of Housing and Construction on 11 Jul 1983. She continued these duties which comprised significant keyboard duties for six months until February 1984 when she consulted her local doctor, Dr J Clyde. He diagnosed her as suffering from “Tenosynovitis" and considered her unfit for duty. He continue to review her condition fortnightly, without any noticeable improvement, and in early May she was referred to the CMO to again assess her fitness for continued duty.
  5. The examination by the CMO on 3 May 1984 confirmed Dr Clyde's diagnosis, and it was recommended that Ms Hocroft utilise a further four months sick leave. In August of that year she saw Dr C. Browne, specialist, who confirmed a relatively severe overuse injury and that she should remain off work for a further three months. Her condition was reviewed in subsequent CMO exams with the following results:

31 Aug 1984 - unfit for duty until re-examination on 1 Nov 1984

1 Nov 1984 - recommended a further 6 months of sick leave

  1. Ms Hocroft was again examined by a CMO, this time Dr A P de Livera, on 16 Apr 1985. Dr de Livera considered that she was suffering from:

"RSI 85%

Back Pain 10%

Reactive Depression 5%"

  1. He indicated on the report of that examination that her prognosis was "poor" and that he considered her "unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds (sic) of invalidity but may, in due course, partially or completely recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment. Suggest review in 2 years time".
  2. Dr B.C. Bromhead, SMO, in his capacity as Adviser to the Commissioner, endorsed the report on 26 Apr 1985. The recommendation was subsequently accepted by the delegate of the then Department of Housing and Construction who signed the instrument of retirement on 9 Sep 1985 having a retrospective effective date of 18 Apr 1985."

When the applicant became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 30 March, 1981, sec. 16 of the Act was in the form set out in the Reprint No. 2 (reprinted as at 3 June 1988) considered in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 2 FCR 472. By sec. 21 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No. 40 of 1990), sec. 16AC was inserted in the Act. Sec. 16AC (9) provides:-

"(9) For the purposes of this section, a person answers a question properly if, and only if, he or she gives in answer to the question all the information that he or she could reasonably be expected to give, on the basis of:

(a) his or her knowledge about the relevant matters; and

(b) the knowledge that, having regard to his or her knowledge about the relevant matters, he or she could reasonably be expected to have about those matters;

if he or she answered the question fully and truthfully on the basis of the knowledge referred to in paragraphs (a) and (b).

Sec. 16AC(9) was considered by a Full Court of the Federal Court in an Meilak v The Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] FCA 96; (1991) 99 ALR 559.

Having regard to the Federal Court decisions in McMullen v Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 61 ALR 189 and to Meilak v Commissioner for Superannuation. already cited, the Tribunal proceeds on the basis that sec. 16AC applies retrospectively. It adopts for present purposes what appears in the passage in the latter decision which states:

"It may be thought that subsec. 16AC(9) was intended to substitute for the standard decided upon in Fry's case a standard which was more exacting and which required the provision of an answer based not only on actual knowledge but also on constructive knowledge."

However that is not to say that answers to a medical questionnaire given on 8 October, 1980, are to be construed with hindsight having regard to later advances in medical knowledge. See Albrighton v Royal Prince Alfred Hospital (1980) 2 NSWLR 542 at 562,563; Paragraphs 64 and 65 per Hope J.A.

The issue before the Tribunal was whether or not the applicant had failed actually or constructively to complete a questionnaire as required by sections 16 and 16 AC of the Act. In these proceedings it is to be observed, as the witness Dr C. Browne, an expert rheumatologist, stated, that medical knowledge and views have changed considerably since the applicant filled in a medical questionnaire on 8 October, 1980 two weeks prior to leaving Qantas (Tr. p. 16). From a situation in which many authorities, including the Commonwealth, questioned whether there was any such thing as repetitive strain injury, e.g. Re Rose and Commonwealth of Australia (1986) 10 ALN, N.97, Dr Browne stated that medical opinion now rejects the diagnosis of tenosynovitis and repetitive strain injury in respect of patients who experience pain in their arms after doing repetitive work and substitutes a new theory of nerve damage due to tractory effects of repetitive work called occupational cervico-bronchial disorder. When the doctors are in dispute the question arises what is the layperson supposed to know, or what is it that the layperson ought to have constructively revealed in the questionnaire on 8 October 1980 (T5). At Tr. pp. 26, 27 and 29 Dr Browne said:

"All right How would you determine - if I can take you back to October or November 1980 - if you were giving advice to the Commissioner for superannuation, how would you determine whether the problem is likely to be ongoing or not? Is there anything you could have recommended? --- Well, at that stage there was not a lot of experience regarding the patterns of capacity for work in people in this situation.

Our experience - my experience was relatively early in that era. We were just coming to terms with the concept of overuse syndromes in relation to repetitive work and various other types of work, such as process work, working with constrained postures of the neck and shoulders for long periods of time. It was just arousing awareness within the medical profession and also in the community at large as to this was a potentially damaging disorder which may, you know, potentially terminate the career of people in - who were working in this role.

Are you saying that in 1980 it was unknown for a person to be retired because of problems with the hands or arms from excessive typing or keyboard work?--I think it was at that time. I can't recall any individuals at that time who had been retired, certainly not within the Commonwealth area, on the basis of what we now call this occupational overuse or ---

What about tenosynovitis? --- Yes. The problem was though there was actually no tenosynovitis to be found and documented. It was assumed that there was tenosynovitis and so much so that there was a - with the State Department of Health in New South Wales a tenosynovitis committee was set up investigating and researching these disorders and that terminology was changed in 1982 to repetitive strain injury advisory committee, but it was assumed that tenosynovitis was the basis for the problem. This was later provided to incorrect."

... Yes, I understand that, doctor, but in relation to your evidence on the situation in October, November 1980, I understand that you said, and correct me if I'm wrong, that you would have been also likely to consider a diagnosis of carpel syndrome or tenosynovitis? --- Well, yes, we were. That was the state of thinking at that time. There were no concepts regarding the neuropathic pain states which are more relevant now.

Firstly Mr R. Whithear, for the Respondent, disputed that the passage quoted by the Federal Court from the judgment of Fletcher Moulton L.J., as he then was, in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation. [1984] FCA 211; 2 FCR 472 at 475 has any application to the new sec. 16 AC of the Act. In that judgment in Joel v Law Union and Crown Insurance Company (1908) 2 KB 863 at 884, 885, Fletcher Moulton LJ. said, inter alia:

"But in my opinion there is a point here which often is not sufficiently kept in mind. The duty is a duty to disclose, and you cannot disclose what you do not know. The obligation to disclose, therefore, necessarily depends on the knowledge you possess. I must not be misunderstood. Your opinion of the materiality of that knowledge is of no moment. If a reasonable man would have recognized that it was material to disclose the knowledge in question, it is no excuse that you did not recognise it to be so. But the question always is, was the knowledge you possessed such that you ought to have disclosed it? Let me take an example. I will suppose that a man has, as is the case with most of us, occasionally had a headache. It may be that a particular one of those headaches would have told a brain specialist of hidden mischief. But to the man it was an ordinary headache undistinguishable from the rest. Now no reasonable man would deem it material to tell an insurance company of all the casual headaches he had had in his life, and, if he knew no more as to this particular headache then that it was an ordinary casual headache, there would be no breach of his duty towards the insurance company in not disclosing it. He possessed no knowledge that it was incumbent on him to disclose, because he knew of nothing which a reasonable man would deem material or of a character to influence the insurers in their action. It was what he did not know which would have been of that character, but he cannot be held liable for nondisclosure in respect of facts which he did not know."

Non-disclosure has been the subject of consideration in Khoury v Government Insurance Office of NSW [1984] HCA 55; (1984) 165 CLR 622 and in Plasteel Windows Australia Pty Ltd v C.E. Heath Agencies Pty Ltd (1990) 19 NSWLR 400; 95 ALR 305.

The document T5 is headed, "Medical Report Form - Permanent Appointments". The section which Mr Whithear claims was filled in incorrectly, is the section marked "B" which has as its sub-heading "Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered from the following disabilities". The Tribunal considers that the phrase "suffering from a disability" connotes not a mere passing problem, but a continuous problem amounting to a disability. It does not escape the connotation of a disability and this seems to have been the approach of the delegate of the respondent in his reasons in Para. 60, T2/24 where he says:-

"60. It is therefore apparent from the above extracts that Ms Hocroft's incapacity was an inability to perform keyboard duties or any other form of general filing or clerical duties without experiencing manifestations of recurrent aching of the hands, forearms and shoulders with accompanying paraesthesiae and numbness in the hands. Various domestic activities were also affected."

The questionnaire is a factual document filled in by a lady seeking employment in the Department of Defence as a public servant doing the work of a Visual Display Unit Operator or the like. It would be remarkable if she was obliged to disclose that her hair was turning grey or that she had bruised her shins playing hopscotch at school. But Mr Whithear would have us believe that such minutiae have to be disclosed so that the lurking omnipresence of the respondent may pass judgment thereon. He was also critical of the applicant who stated that she filled in the form on the basis of what she felt at the time. While her constructive knowledge, in law is required to extend further, this is a far cry from saying that her answers were false or misleading. Indeed from the Tribunal's observation, both the applicant and her husband, who also gave evidence, were decent people whose evidence we accept.

Returning now to the alleged failure of disclosure, Mr Whithear in his final address accepted that there was no failure of disclosure of constructive knowledge because he said, any knowledge the applicant had, was actual knowledge (Tr. pp. 69 & 70, 4 June, 1991). He claimed she had not fulfilled her obligation to provide full answers to the questions numbered 1(j), 5(a), 12(b) and 12(c) in T5. These are set out hereunder:-

1(i) Disturbance of sensation of muscular activity. Answer 'No'.

5(a) Lumbago, paralysis or disorder of the limbs or spine. Answer 'No’.

12(b) Have you had any complaints, illness or injury not mentioned above? Answer 'No'.

12(c) Are you in good health now? Answer 'Yes'

Mr Whithear also criticised upon credit some other answers, notably the answer to No.7(c), but the Tribunal considered this was sufficiently qualified by the Detail under Heading C to No. 13(a) "Nose quarterised (sic)".

Returning to the questions in the Form T5, it appears proper to resolve any ambiguity by adopting a construction favourable to the applicant. Thomson v Weems (1894) 9 A.C. 671, 682. It is by no means clear to the Tribunal that the question concerning "disturbance of sensation of muscular activity" covers recurrent aching and sharp pain in the right hand with radiation of pain to the forearm and elbow. There was no suggestion of disturbance of sensation of muscular activity. Nor in our view are such aches and pains comprehended by the question containing the disparate collocation of words, "Lumbago, paralysis or disorder of the limbs or spine". Questions 12(b) and 12(d) in our view have to be read in their connotation which comes from the heading on T5/53 "Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered from any of the flowing disabilities".

It is remembered that in 1986 the Commonwealth of Australia, through the Commissioner for Employees' Compensation was still disputing the existence of repetitive strain injury, although accepting there was such a thing as a strain injury (Re Rose supra) and Dr C. Browne's evidence is also recalled in which he stated

(Tr.p.34):-

"Yes. So we have mild symptoms, intermittent symptoms, not interfering with the work, and possibly not commencing until after 8 October 1980; that's a fair picture of the history you got, isn't it? --- Yes.

Well, on that basis, doctor, would you agree that there's nothing to suggest that at 8 October 1980 Mrs Hocroft was suffering from anything that you as a medical man might describe as a disability? --- No. She had no impairment and no disability."

Accepting for the moment that the applicant experienced recurrent aching and sharp pain of the dorsal aspects of the right hand with radiation of pain to the volar aspects of the forearm and elbow when working for Qantas, as reported by Dr C. Browne in his report dated 17 August 1984 (T13/71), the Tribunal has to consider when that happened. Evidence which we accept is that she saw her general practitioner Dr D'Souza on 12 March, 1981. He sent her to a specialist physician Dr A.K. Lethlean, whose report of 27 April 1981 is T41/145. This is referred to in more detail later.

The applicant gave evidence that the pain experienced at Qantas did not affect her capacity to perform "strokes" on the VDU's, and she had no knowledge, that she recollects, of any disability or likely disability affecting her when she filled in the form T5 (Transcript pp. 17/18, 31 and 41).

It has to be remembered, as Dr Browne said in evidence, that in October, 1980, the medical profession itself was not certain as to whether there was any such thing as repetitive strain injury, tenosynovitis, or the new name favoured by Dr Browne in his report of 17 April 1990 (T50/175-176) "occupational overuse syndrome". In that report he stated:

"...

On the 10th April, Mrs. Hocroft could not recall any specific symptoms during her employment at Qantas. According to the consultation on the 10th April 1990 she states that she did not report her symptoms while at Qantas and did not seek medical advice.

Her upper limb symptoms associated with overuse syndrome and keyboard operation largely evolved during her term with the Department of Defence from November 1980 onward and continued during her employment until cessation in 1984.

On balance therefore it would seem that although there may have been some early symptoms experienced during her latter (sic) period of employment at Qantas, she did not apparently report the symptoms or seek medical advice and she cannot recall her symptoms interfering with her capacity for work.

I therefore consider that the established disorder of occupational overuse syndrome and associated neuropathic pain was largely established during her employment with the Department of Defence and perpetuated and aggravated during that period of employment in the Department of Defence.

At best only a relatively minor contribution is likely to have occurred during her employment with Qantas as far as I can determine.

Yours faithfully,

(signed)

CHRISTOPHER BROWNE"

In his reports of 17 August, 1984, (T13/71) and 26 July 1990, (Exh. A) Dr Browne states that the history given was that the applicant experienced the recurrent pain and aching described while working at Qantas in 1980. However in his later reports he qualified this in his oral testimony, saying

"There was some confusion as to the timing of symptoms and the place of employment but that was resolved".

He added that the applicant felt her problems began while working at the Department of Defence and that if they did begin at Qantas it was for a relatively short time (Tr. p. 32). While the report of a medical panel dated 1 June 1990, signed by Dr A.H. Lowy, Exh. 1, states on p. 5 that the applicant gave a history of "commencement of bilateral upper limb symptoms and disability" in 1980/81 when she worked for Qantas and the Department of Defence (and see also statement to Dr Wilson Tr. pp. 28 & 29) this should be contrasted to her statement to Dr Browne (Exh. A) that her symptoms then were mild and intermittent (Tr. p. 33). This does not import that at the time of filling in the questionnaire the applicant had present knowledge of suffering from a disability any more than mild and intermittent headaches portend present knowledge of a brain tumour.

The applicant stated in evidence that when she filled in the form T5, she had no problems that she could recall. At that time she was having no problem in maintaining her keystroke rate on Visual Display Units. The applicant resigned from Qantas for some two weeks after filling in the form, T5. (Transcript p.16). It is possible that any recurrent aches and pains experienced by the applicant occurred after completing the form but before leaving Qantas in October, 1980 (see Exh. 1). The Tribunal is not satisfied that at the time of completing the form T5, the applicant was conscious of "suffering from a disability", the phrase used in the form, which compelled a positive or qualified answer to the questions put in issue by the respondent.

While the Delegate's decision in his Background material, Paragraph 6 (T2/6), mentions the report of Dr E.J. D'Souza of 12 March 1981, it fails to expand upon this by mentioning that he referred the applicant to Dr A.K. Lethlean, a consultant neurologist whose 1988 report to the respondent appears at T41/144. The attachment to that report at T41/145 dealing with nerve conduction studies carried out on 3 June, 1981 states "No abnormality detected" and also "No Neuromuscular deficit is evident clinically in this young lady and I would not suspect a neural involvement, carpal tunnel, or other". It appears to the Tribunal that Dr D'Souza's recommendation of 6 April 1981, mentioned in Paragraph 6, that the applicant be taken off keyboard duties was precautionary rather than a diagnosis. When he stated in that report of 18 June, 1981 that the applicant's "problem is due to or a result of repetitive work on a VDU machine", that statement must be read in the light of his earlier words:-

"She has also had neurophysiological tests which have proved beyond doubt that she does not suffer from a neural involvement."

Dr D'Souza's report and his testimony (Tr.p.48) before us do not suggest that the applicant suffered a disability in October, 1980:

"25 August, sorry, 1980. And you saw her on 2 October 1980? --- That's right.

And in relation to the entries for any of those visits, have you recorded anything relating to hands or arms? --- Absolutely not.

...

All right. If you had observed something about her that suggested she had anything wrong with her hands or arms, would you have recorded that in your notes? --- Of course I would have.

Yes, and do you see any recording of anything like that? --- Nothing. I have a habit of putting down what a patient tells me, and adding up anything else that I've observed."

...

All right. Thank you. Now doctor, you say 12 March 1981 was the first time that you reported the pains in the arms, is that correct? --- Yes, that's right. 12 March - do you want me to proceed on this in detail?

Well, would you please check what your cards say about when she said that problem first arose? --- On 12 March 81, this is the first time she came to me complaining of pain in the lower aspect of her right hand, that's there, down to the wrist.

Yes? --- She also noticed some pins and needles of the middle and small fingers, and when I questioned her further she says she uses her hands and wrists all the time. And the wrists are always in a flexed position?

Yes.

Did you get any history from her as to when that problem had first arisen? ---The - I didn't get - she said: "that I've had for a couple of months" or something like that.

Yes all right. Thank you, doctor, no further questions.

THE D.PRESIDENT: Yes Mr Whithear.

THE WITNESS: No, I correct that, I've got it here.

MR WHITHEAR: Yes?

THE WITNESS: 4 to 6 months, maybe 4 to 6 months.

Next we refer to the important Clinical Notes of an examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer Dr P. McMahon on 1 January 1981 (T6/57) which are set out in full hereunder:-

"History. In Jan 1981 began to notice pins and needles in the R[ight] hand. About one week later began to have pain in the volar surface of R. forearm. Then the same thing began to happen in the left hand. Also developed a pain in the left axilla. Now has pain back of the R. shoulder, left axilla, both forearms and hands. She continued to work as a D.P.O. till 6/4 when she saw her LM.O. [Local Medical Officer]. Was put off D.P.O. and worked on a microfiche for 7-8 weeks. However this implied continued repetitive work with the R. hand and no real advantage was evident. It was a little easier than on the D.P. work. Even driving the car worries both forearms and hands. Has been back to D.P.

work since 25/5. Slightly worse. Xrays Cervical spine and wrist. N.A.D. [No Abnormality Detected] Blood Tests Normal.

O.E. [On Examination] generally healthy appearance. Slight tenderness over extensor back to R forearm and over flexor tendons at R wrist. Wrists movements painless and full.

Elbow movements painless. Pronation and supination normal. Slight pain on flexion of R. wrist only. Tender left pectoral muscle. No other tenderness L[eft] side.

Full painless shoulder movements. Opinion. No definite evidence of tenosynovitis. Fit for continued employment in present position."

Following this medical examination and because of pain experienced while operating VDU's in the Department of Defence, in Canberra, the applicant's subsequent history was as detailed in the Delegate's Background Statement. It is to be observed that the further report by Dr J. Clyde of 17 February 1984 which refers to the problem beginning three years ago while working at Qantas illustrates that the applicant's history given to Dr Clyde was in error either as to date, place or both. It is also to be observed that she had been operating VDU's in the Department of Defence and the Department of Housing and Construction for some time prior to seeing Dr Clyde, who diagnosed "tenosynovitis", a diagnosis rejected by Dr Browne.

Apart from medical evidence the Tribunal has the testimony of the applicant's husband. It appears that before marriage on 28 November, 1981 (T22/94), he was interested in what is termed drag racing and flag marshalling. He was then an officer of the Department of Defence and now is shipping manager for Australian Defence Industries. He said he was transferred from Sydney to Canberra in March 1981. Before October 1980 he instructed his fiancee in the activity of flag marshalling of racing cars which appears to consist in giving directions by the vigorous waving of flags. He said she never complained of any pain in her arms during the flag wagging epoch of their lives and her first complaints of pain in the arms to him was in Canberra when she worked for the Department of Housing and Construction.

Having regard to all the evidence before the Tribunal and in particular the evidence already mentioned, the Tribunal is satisfied that at the time of completion of the questionnaire T5, the applicant neither knew nor ought to have known that she was suffering from a disability which should have been disclosed in her answers in that document.

A further point was raised before us, namely, whether or not the Benefits Classification Certificate adequately described a condition within the meaning of sections 16 and 16 AC of the Act, or whether it rather describes mere symptoms. The terms of the BCC which issued are as follows (T2/5) and see (T34/126-127):-

"condition causing pain in the right hand radiating to the forearm and elbow"

The vagueness of the terminology perhaps reflects the Commissioner's uncertainty as to what is involved in a problem variously diagnosed as tenosynovitis, repetitive strain injury or nerve damage due to tractory effects of repetitive work. The Tribunal has generally taken a broad view of the terms of such certificates but does not find it necessary to decide whether the certificate lacked certainly in view of its other findings. The Tribunal is also of the opinion that the ground of invalidity leading to retirement, affecting the applicant, was one connected with the condition specified in the BCC within the meaning of sec. 66(2)(c) of the Act (Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (1989) 86 ALR 239; Commissioner for Superannuation v Perrett [1989] FCA 348; (1989) 89 ALR 385.

The Tribunal also considers that in view of the uncertainty of medical knowledge let alone that of the applicant in October 1986, as explained by Dr Browne, and in view of Dr McMahon's report of his clinical examination (T6/57), on 1 June, 1981 when the applicant's symptoms were known to the Commonwealth, it cannot be said that there was either a "likelihood" (the word used in sec. 16(4) of the Act in 1982) or a "real risk" (the words used in sec. 16 (4) following consolidating legislation of 3 June, 1988) that the Commissioner would have issued a BCC even if the matters stated in Dr McMahon's report were set out in the answers in the Document T5. We adopt the terminology as to "real risk" used by Neaves J. in Re Dakin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 4 AAR 556, 573, when he was sitting as a presidential member of this Tribunal.

For the reasons set out the Tribunal considers that the Commissioner should not have been of the opinion pursuant to subsec. 16(11)(b) of the Act that the applicant was in breach of that sub-section or in breach of sec. 16AC(9) of the Act in her answers to the Questionnaire. The Tribunal directs that the decision of a delegate of the respondent to issue the Benefits Classification Certificate pursuant to sec. 16(11) of the Act be set aside. Further the Tribunal directs that the decision taken by such delegate pursuant to sec. 16(11C) of the Act to determine 10 August 1981 as the deemed date of issue of such a certificate, be set aside.

There only remains to record that despite the meticulous attention to detail given by Mr Whithear, as is his wont, the Tribunal with additional evidence, has reached a different conclusion from that of the respondent's delegate.
