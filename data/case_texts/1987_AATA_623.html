ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income tax - deductions - interest on money borrowed to purchase house - private or domestic

Income tax - partnership - whether seperate entity

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss.51(1), 67(1)

FC of T v Sahhar, 85 ATC 4072

Rowe (B and HG) v FC of T, 82 ATC 4243

Case B11, 70 ATC 46

Case F72, 74 ATC 426

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

11 May 1987 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. The applicant is a public servant who said that he, with his intended spouse, had decided to create a partnership which would acquire investment properties for the purpose of deriving assessable income. The partnership was also intended to increase the assets of the partners so as to provide them with increased income after retirement. The partnership agreement provided that the applicant would hold a 75 per cent share in the partnership with his intended spouse holding the remaining 25 per cent.
  2. It was decided that the partnership should initially purchase the house in which the applicant had been living. As this property ("the first house"), was solely owned by the applicant, the partnership entered into a contract of sale with the applicant on 24 August 1982. Under this contract, the partnership was to purchase the Crown Lease of the first house for $115,000.
  3. The partners obtained an investment loan from a building society of $75,000 on 24 September 1982 and settlement occurred on that day. It was intended that the amount of the loan would be paid to the applicant as seller, and that the remaining consideration of $40,000 owed to the applicant would be allowed to stand as an interest free loan by him to the partnership.
  4. The applicant and his intended spouse on the same day purchased another property for $62,000 which became the matrimonial home ("the second house").
  5. In the partnership taxation return for the year ended 30 June 1983 a deduction was claimed of interest and borrowing expenses payable in respect of the building society loan under s51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act -1936 ("the ITAA" ). The Commissioner disallowed the claim to a deduction on the basis that the loan was used to purchase the second house and was therefore of a private or domestic nature. A deduction was, however, allowed to the extent of 5 per cent of the amounts claimed, $3,705 having been expended out of the loan monies in discharging an existing mortgage of the first house. That decision was confirmed on objection and the objection decision was then referred to a Board of Review for review and subsequently transferred to this Tribunal.
  6. It was submitted on behalf of the applicant that the partnership should be treated as a distinct entity from those who constitute it for the purposes of taxation. In support of this, reference was made to the statement by Lockhart J in FC of T v Sahhar 85 ATC 4072. From that judgment the applicant's representative cited the following passage:

"The Act, however, for some purposes treats a partnership as if it were a distinct entity from those who constitute it: Rowe (B & HG) v FC of T 82 ATC 4243."

7. To put that statement in context, it is however necessary to quote the passage more fully:

"A partnership has, of course, no existence independent of its members. The gross income of a partnership is earned jointly by the partners; and partnership outgoings are joint outgoings of the partners. The partnership profits and losses are the profits and losses of the partners. The Act, however, for some purposes treats a partnership as if it were a distinct entity from those who constitute it: Rowe (B & HG) v FC of T 82 ATC 4243 (a judgment of the Full Court of this court). It is true, as was pointed out in Rowe's case at p.4244, that '[t]here is nothing in the Act which denies or alters the basic legal principle that the profits or net income of a partnership are the profits or net income of those who constitute it';".

8. In Rowe's Case, the joint judgment of the Federal Court read in part at p.4243:

"A partnership is not a company. It has no personality independent of those who constitute it. The gross income of a partnership is earned jointly by the partners. The outgoings of a partnership are joint outgoings of the partners. The profits of a partnership are their profits."

  1. Additional difficulties for the applicant arose from the conduct of the transactions. First, when the applicant completed the 'Application for Loan' he expressed the purpose of the loan to be "To purchase and extend [the second house]". Secondly, the solicitor for the applicant, who was also solicitor for the partnership, banked the loan money into the partnership account from where it was used to purchase the second house. Both of these problems were sought to be explained at the hearing as being errors arising from the confusing nature of the transactions. I do not place any significance upon them for the purposes of deciding the matter.
  2. Evidence was given by the person who was, at the time, loans manager of the building society. He had had an interview with the applicant prior to the granting of the loan. In evidence the loans manager stated in part:

"The substance of the conversation was that [the applicant] wanted to raise money to purchase the [second house]. I was there as, I guess, a go between the money and [the applicant]. I was simply explaining what methods of financing were available to [the applicant]. The purpose was fairly clear, but there was an option of raising that money on the [first house] if he wanted to take it."

11. In the absence of the interposition of the partnership the case would appear to be on all fours with Case B11, 70 ATC 46 and with Case F72, 74 ATC 426. In both of those cases, the taxpayers had obtained loans to purchase new residences while retaining a previous home which was then let. A deduction was claimed, and refused, for interest paid on the loan used to purchase the new residence in both cases. Those decisions, in particular the decision in Case B11, emphasised the principles stated in the well-known decision of the High Court in FC of T v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153. That case made it clear that deductibility of interest was dependent upon the purpose for which the money borrowed was used. In paragraph 9 of its joint reasons (Mr JL Burke (Chairman), Mr RC Smith QC and Mr RE O'Neill (Members)) the Board, having related some factual differences between the case before them and Munro's Case, went on to say (at 49):

"9. But it is only part of the truth to say that the purpose of the borrowing was to enable the taxpayer to retain ownership of A with a view to leasing it. The rest of the truth is that the borrowing also enabled him to acquire ownership of B with a view to occupying it as a family residence. He might have achieved one or other of those purposes without borrowing. For instance, he could, without borrowing, have vacated A and leased it and himself have become a tenant of another dwelling in which event the rent he paid would surely not be deductible from rent received; or he could have sold A and used the proceeds to buy B for his own occupation, in which event he would not be liable for any interest. But without borrowing he could not both retain ownership of A for letting and acquire B for his residence. Then is the taxpayer on the facts as they actually are entitled to a deduction, of a further part of the interest ascertained on some basis of apportionment as being 'the extent to which' interest was 'incurred in gaining or producing' rents from A? We think the answer must be No. Suppose he had decided that he would continue to live in A but would buy B for letting to tenants, and to finance the purchase of B he had to mortgage either or both A and B. In that situation, the converse of what in fact happened, we think that interest paid on the borrowed money could not be apportioned so as to disallow any part of it - the whole of the interest would qualify for deduction. So here, where money borrowed was applied in paying for B used as his private home, no part of the interest paid thereon is deductible."

  1. This to me is a telling exposition. Why should the interposition of the partnership make any difference? For the applicant to distinguish these two cases from the case before the Tribunal in this review, he would have to show first that the loan was made to the partnership to purchase the first house, and, secondly, that for the purposes of that purchase the partnership was a separate legal entity from those constituting it. Neither of these has been established. As to the first proposition, the loan made by the building society was, in my opinion, for the purpose of purchasing the second house and extending the improvements on it, although the security for it was the first house. The latter of the two propositions is quite at odds with the legal concept of partnership.
  2. Although the ITAA treats partnerships as separate entities for certain limited purposes, it is clear from Rowe's Case and Sahhar's Case that for general purposes partnerships are not separate legal entities. The true legal effect of the 'sale' of the first house to the partnership was therefore to transfer a 25 per cent interest in the property to the intended spouse. Both partners then made their respective shares therein partnership property. The proposition that the applicant sold a 75 per cent interest in the first house to himself is untenable.
  3. In all the circumstances, it is clear that I should find that the claimed expenditure was not allowable as a deduction under s51 (1) or s67 (1) of the ITAA, and that the loan was for the private and/or domestic purpose of purchasing the second house. The Tribunal must therefore affirm the objection decision under review.
