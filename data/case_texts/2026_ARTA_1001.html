Decision: The Applicant’s request to issue a summons to an ATO officer is refused.

General Member J Dunne

Catchwords

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – Application to issue a summons to an ATO officer – Application refused

Legislation

Administrative Review Tribunal (Consequential and Transitional Provisions No. 1) Act 2024



Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) ss 9, 49, 74

Cases

Pratten and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 161

ZBDD and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 2197

Statement of Reasons

  1. The Applicant filed his Application for Review in this Tribunal on 2 February 2024.[1] It related to an objection decision from the Commissioner dated 20 December 2023.[2]

Summons request

  1. This case relates to the Applicant’s various efforts to request a summons dated 10 May 2026, 19 May 2026, 20 May 2026 (together the “Request”). The Tribunal advised the Applicant that the Request was not completed correctly and was not a valid request for a summons. This was because it did not specify who it was that the Applicant wished to summons (other than “the relevant ATO official”) nor did it give any clarity as to what documentary material the Applicant wished that person to produce nor why this was relevant to the proceedings.
  2. Subsequent to the Request, on 25 May 2026, the Applicant named Mr J as the relevant ATO official he wanted to summons. Mr J had been involved in an audit of the Applicant. The Applicant did not identify with much clarity what material he was seeking Mr J produce other than (in substance) “working papers” behind various conclusions reached by Mr J in material on the T Documents (such as tables in Position Papers which resulted in the assessments at issue for the Applicant).
  3. The Tribunal made several efforts to assist the Applicant so that a valid written request for a summons could be filed, including by providing guidance about the relevant summons forms and including pointing the Applicant to the relevant case law on the summons power in section 74 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) where a taxpayer sought to summons an ATO official or ATO officials. The cases referred to were Pratten and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 161 (“Pratten”) and ZBDD and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 2197 (“ZBDD”).
  4. It became clear to the Tribunal that it would be easier to discuss this matter with the parties as the Applicant clearly wanted to file a valid written summons request but there was a misunderstanding about how to do this. The Tribunal called the parties together for a case management directions hearing on 3 June 2026 to obtain clarity about what it was that the Applicant was seeking and so the Tribunal could determine the matter if a valid request for a summons was made by the Applicant.
  5. As noted above, the Request does not comprise a valid written request for a summons and the Tribunal explained that to the Applicant in email correspondence. However, as part of my discretion to manage Tribunal proceedings,[3] I have accepted that the Applicant has made a valid oral request for a summons. I am troubled in reaching this conclusion, as the Tribunal’s Common Procedures Practice Direction at [6.13] requires that a summons request be in the approved form. But I nevertheless retain some discretion, in my view, to deal with this matter in order to meet the Tribunal’s objectives in section 9 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth). In the overall circumstances, it would simply add needless time to the determination of this matter for the Tribunal to be spending even more time than it already has with the Applicant in order to obtain a valid written request for a summons using the approved form. Taking any further steps to obtain a valid written request for a summons would be contrary to the Tribunal’s objectives to resolve applications before the Tribunal as quickly as a proper consideration of the matters permits (section 9(b)) and to be accessible and responsive to the diverse needs of the parties (section 9(c)) (recognising that both the Applicant and Mr Ahmed have English as their second language). That is why I have decided to treat the Applicant as having made a valid request for a summons without the approved form having been validly completed. I am also comforted by the fact that I do have the Request using the approved form, albeit wrongly, and this can be said to comply with the Common Procedures Practice Direction in any event.
  6. The request I have treated as a valid summons request is to summons Mr J, an officer of the Commissioner, and for Mr J to produce background material to certain tables and calculations reflected in certain of the Commissioner’s correspondence (specified as annexures to the Request) such as the Position Papers. As a result, I have treated the case management direction hearing as an interlocutory hearing to determine whether to issue a summons.

The parties’ submissions

  1. The Commissioner opposes any request for a summons (if one is validly made) and filed submissions on 1 June 2026 to that effect. In substance, those submissions are that the Request is not valid, and if a valid request for a summons is made, there was no legitimate forensic purpose for such a summons given the Tribunal’s role on review under Part VIC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (“TAA”). The Commissioner cited a number of cases to support its position.
  2. The Applicant filed submissions on 2 June 2026 in support of his request for a summons (on the basis a valid request had been made). In substance, those submissions were to the effect that:
    (a) Conclusions reached by Mr J in materials on the T documents need to be further explained;

    (b) Factual findings made by the Commissioner and its calculations are in dispute and Mr J was “central to the issues in dispute”;

    (c) A ‘sample’ of those issues included private expenditure Mr J alleged should be added back to the Applicant’s income when that was wrong, calculation errors in a table said to be prepared by Mr J that suggested a loss arose where there was a profit, and a failure to properly consider the opening balance of the Commonwealth Bank account.

  3. It appears the Applicant has some focus on the tax position found by the Commissioner in respect of the Applicant’s business, Protect Tiling Pty Ltd, because materials from the Commissioner’s correspondence in relation to that entity are annexed as part of the Request for Mr J to produce documents explaining that correspondence. Protect Tiling Pty Ltd is not an applicant in these proceedings, deliberately so.[4] Material relating to that entity is not relevant. The Applicant is urged to consider this point prior to the hearing, so the Tribunal is not put in a position of hearing irrelevant submissions about a taxpayer not at issue in the proceedings and having to say as much. If the Applicant thinks that he is revisiting matters relating to Protect Tiling Pty Ltd, he is not. His Application for Review relates only to himself and only to the issues specified below as the substantive case.[5]
  4. The Applicant said that questioning Mr J on the “sample” issues and other matters is a proper forensic purpose for the summons as this would assist the Tribunal in “efficiently determining all disputed issues and avoiding unnecessary delay” claiming that “[i]f the Summons is refused, there is a substantial risk that issues requiring clarification from [Mr J] will arise during the trial, potentially resulting in adjournment of the proceedings and the need to recall the witness at a later date.” The Applicant also claimed procedural fairness justifies the issue of the summons. The Applicant said at the hearing that this case was ‘exceptional’ and the case law such as ZBDD and Pratten could be distinguished because of the exceptional facts.

The substantive case

  1. When considering the substantive case, the years in question are the 2019 and 2020 years. The substantive case is about two bank accounts - the Applicant’s Commonwealth Bank of Australia (“CBA”) bank account which may have been used (to an extent) as a combined account for himself personally as well as for his business Protect Tiling Pty Ltd, and the Protect Tiling Pty Ltd bank account with Bendigo Bank which was also utilised by the Applicant.
  2. The questions for determination in the substantive case are whether:
    (a) Certain amounts deposited into the Applicant’s CBA account and/or used by the Applicant from the Bendigo account comprise the Applicant’s ordinary income;

    (b) Any of those amounts should be excluded from the Applicant’s ordinary income as payments were made by the Applicant to subcontractors of Protect Tiling Pty Ltd;[6]

    (c) Penalties for recklessness should be imposed on the Applicant in the 2019 and 2020 years;

    (d) A 20% base penalty uplift should be imposed on the Applicant in the 2020 year; and

    (e) Penalties and the base penalty uplift should be remitted.

  3. The Applicant understands that he bears the burden of proof under section 14ZZK of the TAA. This matter is set down for a hearing on 9 and 10 November 2026.

Decision

  1. For the reasons below, I have decided to dismiss the Applicant’s request to issue a summons:
    (a) The test for issuance of a summons in section 74 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) requires the Tribunal to have reasonable grounds to believe Mr J has information that is relevant to the substantive case. Pratten at [49] held that this is an objective test.

    (b) I have concluded that there is no reasonable and objective basis to suggest anything Mr J might say will be relevant to the Tribunal’s task when hearing the substantive case. That is because under Part IVC of the TAA, when considering the Commissioner’s objection decision, the Tribunal starts afresh. The Tribunal makes findings of fact and stands in the shoes of the Commissioner. The process undertaken prior to the assessments being issued, prior to the objection, and whatever Mr J considered when engaging with the Applicant or making calculations and putting those into correspondence sent to the Applicant in the past is not relevant to the Tribunal’s task. I am not concerned with the rightness or wrongness of Mr J’s actions.

    (c) Contrary to the Applicant’s submissions, there is no risk that the Tribunal will adjourn this case to hear from Mr J. Mr J is irrelevant to the Tribunal’s task. Further, there is also no procedural unfairness in hearing from both parties at the hearing of the substantive matter and giving the Applicant the opportunity to present evidence to demonstrate why the Commissioner’s assessments are wrong and by what extent they are wrong in accordance with his statutory burden of proof. The case before the Tribunal is not about challenging bits and pieces in tables in correspondence issued by the Commissioner. That is, in all frankness, entirely irrelevant and will not be likely to come close to meeting the Applicant’s burden of proof.

    (d) The Applicant’s substantive case before the Tribunal should be approached as a positive case. It is about why the Applicant’s case is right – that is, there is no additional ordinary income and why, any such additional income should be reduced and why, shortfall penalties do not arise at law, the base penalty uplift does not arise at law and/or those penalties and the base penalty uplift should be remitted at law. If the Applicant says the Commissioner’s calculations are wrong, the Applicant can demonstrate the correct calculations to the Tribunal. The Applicant was advised specifically by the Tribunal of the need for him to present evidence to meet his burden of proof, noting that he has no current witnesses. The Applicant is urged to think about his burden of proof and how to meet it. The Applicant was reminded about the translators available at the Tribunal without cost to him.

    (e) Pratten and ZBDD and all the cases cited in those decisions are not distinguishable from the present case. In particular, in Pratten the taxpayer was seeking to summons certain ATO officials on almost exactly the same basis as the Applicant is in this case. The summons was refused. In Pratten at [66]:

    Put simply, the subjective views of ATO officers and their interactions during the course of examining Mr Pratten’s tax affairs will be of no meaningful assistance or relevance to the Tribunal. The Tribunal is required to make findings of fact and apply the law independently, consistent with its objective as set out in s 9 of the ART Act. As the taxpayer, Mr Pratten bears the onus of proof in Part IVC proceedings.

    (f) The Applicant has made the mistake all of the other taxpayers in Pratten, ZBDD and all the cases cited therein made. The Tribunal is considering the Applicant’s case entirely afresh. It does not need to hear from Mr J, and it does not need the background to what the Commissioner said or did in correspondence to determine this case. There is also nothing whatsoever exceptional about this case; it is a straightforward matter relating to penalties and ordinary income.

  2. The Applicant’s request for a summons is refused by the Tribunal.
