Re Gay Brooker and Commissioner of Employees' Compensation [1987] AATA 76 (6 March 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: GAY BROOKER

And: COMMISSIONER FOR EMPLOYEES' COMPENSATION

No. W86/114

AAT No. 3271

Freedom of Information

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.D. Nicholson (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Freedom of Information - access denied to statement accompanying application for compensation - whether statement is document concerning agency - whether statement is document affecting personal privacy - whether statement is confidential document

Freedom of Information Act 1982 s.40, 41, 45

Re Anderson and Australian Federal Police [1986] AATA 79; (1986) 4 AAR 414

Re Anderson and Department of Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1986] AATA 79; (1986) 4 AAR 414

Boots v. Department of Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (Federal Court Decision No. 407 of 1986, 16 September 1986)

Re Brennan and Law Society of Australian Capital Territory (1985) 8 ALD

Re Burns and Australian National University (No. 1) (1984) 6 ALD

Re Chandra and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1984] AATA 437; (1984) 6 ALN N257

Re Edelsten and Australian Federal Police [1985] AATA 350; (1985) 4 AAR 220 August 1986)

Industrial Furnaces Ltd. v. Reaves (1970) 87 RPC 605

Re Maher and Attorney-General's Department (1985) 7 ALD

Robinson and Australian Federal Police (Decision No. 2847, 29 Re Shewcroft and Australian Broadcasting Corporation (1985) 7 ALN

Re Williams and Registrar of the Federal Court of Australia (1985) 8 ALD

Re Wiseman and Department of Transport [1985] AATA 284; (1985) 4 AAR 83

Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD

Young v. Wicks (Federal Court Decision No. 339 of 1986, 30 May 1986)

HEARING

PERTH

6:3:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

Interim Decision and Directions

The interim decision with reasons and directions was given in this matter on 19 November 1986. Those reasons are incorporated in and are to be read with these final reasons.

Directions

2. Pursuant to a direction given at the time of delivery of the interim decision, a directions hearing was held in this matter on 19 January 1987. The date of that directions hearing was chosen to suit the convenience of the Applicant who was overseas for a month prior to Christmas 1986. The Applicant did not attend the directions hearing.

3. Directions were then made to give the Applicant and the Respondent the opportunity of filing further written submissions. No further written submissions have been received from the Respondent as a consequence of such direction.

4. The interim decision was given so that the parties, and particularly the unrepresented Applicant, could assess whether they wished to call sworn evidence on matters germaine to the application of sections of the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the FOI Act") and in particular, whether disclosure would be unreasonable pursuant to section 41 of the Act, whether confidentiality would be breached pursuant to section 45 (or whether it had previously been waived) and whether disclosure would have the consequences in sub-section 40(1) of the Act. No further evidence having been brought to the Tribunal it must proceed to consideration of those matters on the evidence before it.

5. The Applicant has sought dismissal by consent but the Respondent has declined consent because an interim decision has already been given.

Documents Concerning Agencies - FOI Act s.40

6. On behalf of the Respondent it was argued the following paragraphs of sub-section 40(1) of the FOI Act justified exemption of the Statement:-

" 40. (1) Subject to sub-section (2), a document is

an exempt document if its disclosure under this Act

would, or could reasonably be expected to -

...

(c) have a substantial adverse effect on the

management or assessment of personnel by the

Commonwealth or by an agency;

(d) have a substantial adverse effect on the proper

and efficient conduct of the operations of an

agency; or

(e) have a substantial adverse effect on the

conduct by or on behalf of the commonwealth or

an agency of industrial relations."

7. The submission that these paragraphs have application was made subject to clarification by the Applicant of the line of supervision between herself and Mr. Vincent. In response to a question from the Tribunal, not in sworn evidence, the Applicant stated that Mr. Vincent was not within a department over which she had supervision since he had requested a transfer in July 1986 and that had been immediately acceded to. No sworn evidence was called by the Respondent. There is consequently no evidence on which to find that the release of the Statement would have the effects referred to in the paragraphs as a consequence of any impact its release may have on the working relationship between the Applicant and Mr. Vincent. The Statement is therefore not an exempt document pursuant to the above paragraphs of sub-section 40(1).

Documents Affecting Personal Privacy - FOI Act s.41

8. Sub-section 41(1) reads:

" 41. (1) A document is an exempt document if its

disclosure under this Act would involve the

unreasonable disclosure of information relating to the

personal affairs of any person (including a deceased

person)."

9. Sub-section 41(2) reads:

" 41. (2) Subject to sub-section (3), the provisions

of sub-section (1) do not have effect in relation to a

request by a person for access to a document by

reason only of the inclusion in the document of matter

relating to that person."

10. Sub-section 41(3) has no application in this matter.

11. Because of sub-section 41(2), the Applicant is not prevented from access to the Statement by reason only of the inclusion in it of matter relating to her (the purported object of her interest in the terms of the request). Sub-section 41(2), however, does not itself enable access and it is still necessary to consider whether the Statement is exempt within sub-section 41(1): Re Anderson and Australian Federal Police [1986] AATA 79; (1986) 4 AAR 414 at 427 and FOI Act s.32.

12. The question then becomes whether the Statement contains information relating to the personal affairs of a person other than the Applicant. This requires the Tribunal to examine the Statement to determine if, as advised to the Applicant, it contains information relating to the personal affairs of any person.

13. In Re Anderson and Department of Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1986] AATA 79; (1986) 4 AAR 414 at 430-434, Deputy President A.N. Hall reviewed decisions relating to "personal affairs" in the FOI Act. His analysis has been agreed with by Deputy President R.K. Todd in Robinson and Australian Federal Police (Decision No. 2847, 29 August 1986, para. 17). The analysis by Deputy President Hall contains illustrations showing that documents revealing the purpose of a person's medical consultation contain information relating to personal affairs (Re Edelsten and Australian Federal Police (1985) 4AAR 220) but that a document containing an assessment of work capacity and performance does not, except in the circumstances found in Re Wiseman and Department of Transport [1985] AATA 284; (1985) 4 AAR 83. Where information relates to alleged ill-health arising from work, there may be a difficulty in determining on which side of the line the document in issue falls.

14. In Young v. Wicks (Federal Court Decision No. 339 of 1986, 30 May 1986) Beaumont J. said the reference to "personal affairs" in sub-section 41(1) was intended to have its ordinary dictionary meaning, that is to say, to refer to matters of private concern to an individual.

15. Having examined the Statement, the Tribunal concludes it does contain information relating to the personal affairs of a person. That is not to say that it wholly contains such information. Rather, information of that type is included in the Statement and in the view of the Tribunal that is adequate to satisfy this requirement of sub-section 41(1).

16. It then becomes necessary to consider whether under that sub-section disclosure of the Statement would involve the unreasonable disclosure of such information. This requires a balancing of the several interests, public and private, involved: see Re Williams and Registrar of the Federal Court of Australia [1985] AATA 226; (1985) 8 ALD 219 and Re Anderson and Australian Federal Police (supra), at 426. The relevant considerations include "the nature of the information that would be disclosed, the circumstances in which the information was obtained, the likelihood of the information being information that the person concerned would not wish to have disclosed without consent, and whether the information has any current relevance": See Re Chandra and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1984] AATA 437; (1984) 6 ALN N257 at N259.

17. Also to be considered is the public interest in a person having access to documents if his interests are or may be affected by their contents - Re Burns and Australian National University (No. 1) (1984) 6 ALD 193 - and the object in section 3 of the FOI Act.

18. In the letter of 19 December 1985 the Applicant was given the following description of the Statement:

"Mr Vincent submitted a statement in support of his

compensation claim which outlines historically events

leading up to sick leave for acute anxiety and his

decision to lodge a compensation claim. Several

people (including yourself) are mentioned throughout

the report however this is largely in the context of

Mr Vincent detailing the various requests made for

counselling etc. Also considerable extracts of the

statement relate to other people. Overall the

document largely relates to Mr Vincent's personal

affairs."

19. At the hearing counsel for the Respondent said:

"The (Statement) details chronologically the happenings

between 12 July 1985 and 23 July 1985 as seen by Mr

Vincent and as concerns proposed counselling, slash,

discussion about his work performance. Mention is

made in the report of discussions Mr Vincent had with

Mr Horgan, Mr Hubbard and Ms Brooker concerning

arrangements for the meeting and who should and should

not be present. Aspects relevant to his compensation

claim, such as when medical treatment was sought and

why, are also included in the (Statement)."

20. At the hearing the Applicant stated, unsworn, that Mr. Vincent "made it quite clear to several members of the taxation office where I work ... that he was applying for compensation on the grounds of work related stress, so it was made quite publicly known". Despite the opportunity given to her, she has not sought to support this statement with any sworn evidence.

21. The question is whether the relevant information would be unreasonably disclosed if the document were not exempt.

22. In her request for access the Applicant stated:

"There are two principal reasons for requiring

the information; to compile a report on a voluntary

basis requested by your Mr. Dennis O'Keefe and to

consider whether the allegations warrant civil

proceedings."

23. The first reason is no longer applicable. At the hearing the Applicant said she wished to see what comments Mr. Vincent had made in order that she could take up the matter further with the administration if, in fact, a slur had been made on her capacity as an officer. She sought access only to statements made concerning herself, her behaviour, her character and personal capacity as an officer of the Australian Taxation Office and stated she was not interested in the personal affairs or medical history of Mr. Vincent. The motives of the Applicant in seeking disclosure are a relevant factor to weigh in determining such reasonableness - see Re Shewcroft and Australian Broadcasting Corporation (1985) 7 ALN N307.

24. Having examined the Statement again and having weighed the abovementioned factors, the Tribunal is of the view that the disclosure of such information as appears therein relating to the personal affairs of Mr. Vincent would be unreasonable.

Confidentiality of Document - FOI Act s.45

25. Sub-section 45(1) of the FOI Act reads:

" 45. (1) A document is an exempt document if its

disclosure under this Act would constitute a breach of

confidence."

26. Sub-section 45(2) does not apply in this matter.

27. Section 54 of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 ("the CCGE Act") requires that a claim in writing for compensation be served on the Commissioner as a pre-condition to compensation being payable. The claim itself is not marked as confidential nor was the Statement. The question is whether from the nature of the Statement and the statutory and factual circumstances of its lodgment, confidentiality can be implied so that disclosure of the Statement would constitute a breach of that confidence.

28. In Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD 534 at 542, the Tribunal, comprising Davies J., President, Deputy President R.K. Todd and Member G.D. Grant, said:

"In respect of documents which came into existence

before the commencement of the FOI Act we consider

that there is a category of confidence that ought to

be respected but which, without seeking to rule upon

the point, may not exist in respect of documents

coming into existence after the commencement of the

Act. This category covers documents disclosure of

which would constitute a breach of confidence where

that confidence was reposed and received not on the

faith of a legal duty to respect that confidence but

on the faith of a voluntary undertaking or

understanding to respect it either in a specific case

or as part of a tradition of dealing, and where it was

so reposed and received at a time when there was an

expectation that having once been committed to writing

it would not be subjected to disclosure to the public

or to a specific person."

29. In Re Maher and Attorney-General's Department (1985) 7 ALD 731 at 737-738, Davies J., President, said:

" Sections 33(1)(b) and 45 (the general

confidentiality exemption) of the FOI Act are

concerned not only with communications which would be

protected by courts under the common law principles

with respect to express or implied obligations of

confidence or by courts of equity with respect to

communications made under special relationships but

also with information that was communicated to an

agency in confidence, that is to say, information that

was communicated and received under an express or

inferred understanding that the communication would be

kept confidential (cf Re Witheford and Department of

Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD 534, 542; Re Brennan and

Law Society of Australian Capital Territory ((1985) 8

ALD 10, paras. 30, 37-38).

It is not to be expected that precedents can be

found in the legal authorities for all the types of

circumstance that meet that criteria.

...

It is clear, however, from such authorities as there

are, that a conclusion may be drawn that information

was communicated in confidence either because of the

particular relationship between the parties or because

the information communicated was and would be

recognized to be of a confidential nature.

...

If a communication includes confidential

information, that fact may, when taken with the

surrounding circumstances, give rise to an inference

that the information was communicated in confidence,

but it may not be simple to identify information that

was and would be recognized to be confidential."

30. In interpreting sub-section 45(1), the object of the FOI Act as set out in sub-section 3(1) of that Act, is to be borne in mind.

31. The first matter for decision in relation to section 45 therefore, is whether the Statement was communicated and received under an inferred understanding that it would be kept confidential. In the view of the Tribunal, it would be the expectation of any person completing a claim and annexing a statement under the CCGE Act that, once lodged, neither the claim nor the statement would be subjected to disclosure to the public or to a specific person. This is an expectation which, in the view of the Tribunal, would not have been changed as a consequence of the enactment of the FOI Act and specifically sub-section 45(1) of that Act.

32. Section 24A(1) of the CCGE Act (supra) would found an expectation that "information with respect to the affairs of (a) person" would not be communicated to another person, the injunction of that section extending to "affairs" and not only "personal affairs".

33. The second question is whether the oral statements said to have been made by Mr. Vincent have resulted in the contents of the Statement ceasing to be confidential (see Industrial Furnaces Ltd. v. Reaves (1970) 87 RPC 605 at 618 per Graham J.). In her unsworn statement the Applicant said:

"Well, I would accept under normal circumstances that a

document was submitted in confidence. However, as I

have pointed out, Mr Vincent made it blatantly obvious

to several members of the taxation office, and of

course the news spread like wildfire, and I was

informed about it by - you know, inundated with

telephone calls, etcetera and it was certainly

well-known to most people in the office. So I do not

consider then that it was lodged in confidence."

34. Despite the opportunity given to the Applicant, there is no sworn evidence before the Tribunal to show the exact nature of the oral statements alleged to have been made by Mr. Vincent. Descriptions of the nature of the Statement (if made as stated by the Applicant) do not amount, in the view of the Tribunal, to a waiver of confidentiality of the whole Statement (which contains matters in addition to such information). The exact nature of the allegations (used in the sense of assertions) relating to the Applicant in the Statement was not contained in those descriptions nor does the Tribunal know whether they formed the subject of the alleged oral remarks of Mr. Vincent. There is no evidence to found the suggestion that confidentiality no longer attaches to the Statement.

35. Thirdly, there is a question whether the allegations sought could alone be released to the Applicant because (if what she believes is correct) they relate to conduct undertaken by her so that they are not in their nature confidential as far as she is concerned. Leaving aside whether section 24A of the CCGE Act would permit such disclosure, the Tribunal considers there is a distinction between involvement in factual circumstances and knowledge of expression of opinion concerning those circumstances. The latter are not matters known to the Applicant (and, for that reason she seeks knowledge of them) so they provide no basis for considering no confidentiality attaches to them.

36. Furthermore there is nothing in the circumstances of the present case to suggest the confidential information in question was communicated on the footing that its confidentiality was to be qualified to the extent that it could be communicated to the Applicant. Neither expressly nor by implication is that the case.

37. Fourthly, no particular weight is to be attached to the fact that the Applicant seeks access to information about herself. The duty of the Tribunal is to determine whether the document if disclosed would result in a breach of confidence (cf. Boots v. Department of Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (Federal Court Decision No. 407 of 1986, 16 September 1986)).

38. The Applicant seeks access to the Statement in order to obtain some redress, administratively or at law, in relation to what she considers it may contain. To find that the Statement is a document which is exempt because its disclosure would constitute a breach of confidence, as the Tribunal now does, wroughts no injustice to the Applicant whose complaint lies not against the Statement, which remains confidential, but against alleged oral utterances by Mr. Vincent of which she seeks confirmation in the Statement. The Statement has not been the source of the alleged injustice to the Applicant.

Deletion of Exempt Matter - s.22 FOI Act

39. Because the Tribunal considers the whole Statement exempt pursuant to sections 41 and 45 of the FOI Act, it is not appropriate to consider whether deletion of exempt information could be made pursuant to section 22 of that Act.

Conclusion

40. The opinion of the Tribunal is that the Respondent has not discharged the onus upon it pursuant to section 61 of the FOI Act in establishing that the Statement is an exempt document pursuant to sections 38 or 40 of that Act. It has discharged that onus in relation to sections 41 and 45. The decision under review will therefore be affirmed.
