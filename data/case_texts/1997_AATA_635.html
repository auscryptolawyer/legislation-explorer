CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - income - non-cash business benefits - when provided - long-term interest-free loans.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss.21A(1), (5)

FCT v Cooke and Sherden (1980) 80 ATC 4140

DFCT v Sheehan (1986) 86 ATC 4718



REASONS FOR DECISION

4 February 1997 Deputy President A M Blow OAM, QC.,



1. There are fourteen applications before the Tribunal for the review of decisions made by the respondent to disallow objections against notices of amended assessment and notices of assessment. The applications by the first and third applicants relate to the years ending 30 June 1990 to 1994 inclusive. The applications by the second applicant relate to the years ending 30 June 1990 to 1993 inclusive.

2. All the applicants worked at all material times as the agents of the same principal. It is common ground that they were all independent contractors and not employees. In 1986, 1987 and 1988, their principal made substantial loans to each of them. There was a written loan agreement in relation to each such loan.

3. In respect of those loans made in 1986 and 1987, each loan agreement provided that the borrower would repay the loan in full to the lender on termination of the borrower’s appointment as an agent of the lender, for whatever reason. Each of those agreements went on to provide that the borrower agreed to pay interest “at the rate of 14% per annum, such interest to accrue from day to day until the Loan is repaid in full, and to be paid at the time of repayment of the Loan PROVIDED that [the lender] shall waive any interest in respect of the period up to the termination of the appointment of” the borrower.

4. The 1987 agreement was more complicated. It contained a clause which provided that if the agent disposed of, or agreed to dispose of, more than a certain percentage of his business, then he would forthwith repay an amount sufficient to reduce the loan by the percentage by which his business was reduced. However, it still contained a provision for interest and a covenant by the lender to waive any interest in respect of the period up to the termination of the appointment of the agent. There is no suggestion that the agent who was a party to that agreement ever disposed of any of his business, or proposed to do so, at any material time.

5. The respondent contends that pursuant to these loan agreements the applicants have received non-cash business benefits, the value of which must be bought into account as assessable income pursuant to s.21A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). The challenged assessments and amended assessments have been made on that basis. The applicants contend that s.21A does not apply to any benefits received pursuant to the loan agreements. Central to the dispute between the parties is s.21A(1) of the Act, which reads as follows:-

“For the purposes of this Act, in determining the income derived by a taxpayer, a non-cash business benefit that is not convertible to cash shall be treated as if it were convertible to cash.”


  1. The effect of a benefit being treated as if it were convertible to cash is that its value must be treated as income, and can be brought into account in calculating a taxpayer’s assessable income. But for the enactment of s.21A(1), that would not be the case in respect of non-cash benefits: FCT v Cooke and Sherden (1980) 80 ATC 4140.
  2. The respondent contends that the benefits received by the applicants pursuant to the loan agreements constitute, for the purposes of s.21A, services provided in respect of the business relationships between the applicants and their principal, and thus fall within the definition of “non-cash business benefit” in s.21A(5). That sub-section includes the following definitions (from which I have omitted some irrelevant passages):-
“`non-cash business benefit’ means property or services provided after 31 August 1988:

(a) wholly or partly in respect of a business relationship; or

(b) wholly or partly for or in relation directly or indirectly to a business relationship;

...

`provide’:

(a) ...

(b) in relation to services - includes allow, confer, give, grant or perform;

...

`services’ includes any benefit, right (including a right in relation to, and an interest in, real or personal property), privilege or facility and, without limiting the generality of the foregoing, includes a right, benefit, privilege, service or facility that is, or is to be, provided under:

(a) ...

(b) ...; or

(c) an arrangement for or in relation to the lending of money.”


  1. It is common ground that the benefits received by each applicant were wholly or partly in respect of his business relationship with his principal. The use of the money loaned pursuant to each loan agreement until the occurrence of an event triggering liability for the repayment of the principal, and the use of such money interest-free until the termination of the agency relationship, were benefits and rights provided under an arrangement for the lending of money, and thus constitute services for the purposes of s.21A . It is therefore clear that the benefits received under the loan agreement were, for the purposes of s.21A services provided wholly or partly in respect of business relationships. The critical question is whether they were provided after 31 August 1988. If not, s.21A has no application.
  2. The contractual rights of the applicants pursuant to their loan agreements were all conferred prior to 31 August 1988. Although each loan agreement was worded as if the lender was entitled to interest on a daily basis but was required to waive that interest continuously until the termination of each agency relationship, in reality no step was ever taken by the lender by way of conferring any new benefit on any of the applicants after 31 August 1988. When a loan is payable on demand and the lender allows it to remain outstanding, there is a continuous allowing of that benefit to the borrower which constitutes a continuous provision of services for the purposes of s.21A. The situation is less clear in relation to the loans to the applicants. It cannot be said that after 31 August 1988 the lender allowed, conferred, gave, granted or performed any benefit, right, privilege, service or facility to the applicants under the loan agreements. However I do not believe that the list of verbs in paragraph (b) of the definition “provide” in s.21A(5) was intended by Parliament to be exhaustive. It is therefore necessary to consider whether, in the ordinary sense of the word, the lender provided any benefit, right, privilege, service or facility to each applicant throughout the term of each loan, and not just at the time of lending. In one sense of the word, the lender provided the loan monies at the commencement of the term of the loan. In another sense of the word, the lender has provided each applicant with loan funds throughout the years since the lending of those funds. The question before the Tribunal is whether the word “provided” should be given such a wide interpretation.
  3. The purpose of s.21A is not expressly stated in the Act, nor in the Taxation Laws Amendment (No. 4) Act 1988 (“the amending Act”) by which it was introduced. However its purpose is clearly to close the loophole illustrated by FCT v Cooke and Sherden, whereby taxpayers or non-taxpayers could escape or reduce liability to income tax by arranging to receive benefits otherwise than by way of cash or benefits convertible to cash. Adopting the wider interpretation of the word “provided” would promote the purpose or object underlying s.21A. Such an interpretation must therefore preferred, by reason of the provisions s.15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901.
  4. In certain circumstances there operates a principle of statutory interpretation whereby a provision in a fiscal statute ought to be given an interpretation favourable to the taxpayer in preference to an interpretation favourable to the Crown. See Pearce and Geddes, Statutory Interpretation in Australia, 4th ed. para.9.25. However, that principle does not apply when the interpretation favourable to the Crown would facilitate the evident object of the legislature and that favourable to the taxpayer would plainly thwart it: DFCT v Sheehan (1986) 86 ATC 4718 per Tadgell J at 4728.
  5. Counsel for the applicants emphasised the general presumption against the retrospective operation of statutes. However the amending Act, which received the Royal assent on 24 November 1988 and introduced s.21A with effect from that day, was clearly intended to create new tax liabilities retrospectively with effect from 1 September 1988. That is obvious from the express reference to “property or services provided after 31 August 1988” in the definition of “non-cash business benefit” in s.21A(5).
  6. A narrow interpretation of the word “provided” could have absurd results in relation to interest-free or low interest loans made after 31 August 1988 and repayable after a term of years or upon the happening of specified event. If a narrow interpretation is to be adopted, as contended for by counsel for the applicants, s.21A would require all the benefits received under such a loan agreement to be treated as services provided at the time of the making of the loan. Under s.21A(2), the benefits under the loan agreement would have to be brought into account at their arm’s length value. The value of a ten-year interest free loan at the time of the making of that loan, for example, would be close to the value of the principal sum. Similarly, the value of the use of a new car for ten years would be close to the full value of the car. When any such long-term benefit is provided, the entire tax burden relating to the provision of the benefit would fall in the first year. Such a result would be so absurd and undesirable that an interpretation of the legislation spreading the tax burden over all the years during which the benefit of the arrangement was enjoyed or intended to be enjoyed ought to be preferred. It was contended on behalf of the applicants that I should not have regard to the tax consequences of a long-term interest-free loan after 31 August 1988, since taxpayers would no longer enter into such arrangements in the light of the provisions of s.21A. However I do not think it is appropriate or realistic to assume that such arrangements will no longer be made.
  7. Counsel for the applicants contended that, even if the result of the loan agreements was that the applicants received non-cash business benefits by way of “services provided” after 31 August 1988, the arm’s length value of the benefits provided after that date was nil. He argued that, at the commencement of each of the relevant tax years, each taxpayer already had an enforceable right to retain the benefit of his loan or loans, and that nothing of any value was provided to him during the year. If he received the benefit of a notional waiving of the year’s interest, that benefit was of no value because there was a pre-existing binding contractual promise of such waiver.
  8. As I have indicated, the quantification of the value of a non-cash business benefit is governed by s.21A(2). That sub-section reads as follows:-
“For the purposes of this Act, if a non-cash business benefit (whether or not convertible to cash) is income derived by a taxpayer:

(a) the benefit shall be brought into account at its arm’s length value reduced by the recipient’s contribution (if any): and

(b) if the benefit is not convertible cash - in determining the arm’s length value of the benefit, any conditions that would prevent or restrict the conversion of the benefit to cash shall be disregarded.”


  1. The relevant definition in s.21A(5) reads as follows:-
“`arm’s length value’, in relation to a non-cash business benefit, means:

(a) the amount that the recipient could reasonably be expected to have been required to pay to obtain the benefit from the provider under a transaction where the parties to the transaction are dealing with each other at arm’s length in relation to the transaction; or

(b) if such an amount cannot be practically determined - such amount as the Commissioner considers reasonable”.


  1. I think the flaw in this argument is that the benefits provided by the lender pursuant to each loan agreement each year go further than the performance of contractual obligations not to require payment and not to charge interest, and the notional waiving of interest. The benefit provided is the continual provision of the principal sum loaned. The amount that each applicant could reasonably be expected to have been required to pay for the continued provision of the principal sum by the lender, under a transaction where the parties to the transaction were dealing with each other at arm’s length in relation to the transaction, is the amount of interest that would have been payable in an ordinary commercial transaction. I therefore reject the submission that the arm’s length value of each of the benefits is zero.
  2. For these reasons, the decisions under review are all affirmed.
