Last Updated: 8 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax -Allowable deductions - bad debts - whether carrying on business of money-lending - whether "written off" during year of income.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), S 63

Marshall and Brougham Pty Ltd v FC of T 86 ATC 4,469

Income Tax - allowable deductions - loan of money pending its investment in a development project - default by borrower -whether loss of capital.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) S51.

Income Tax - allowable deductions - loan of money pending its investment in a development project - project to be constituted as a "profit-making" scheme - whether loan effected as or in the course of a profit-making scheme".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) S52

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

Re: NT85/4138

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

AAT Decision No 3157

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr PM Roach (Senior Member)

  1. The Applicant is an international pilot who during the rear of income ended 30 June 1982 invested 345,000. Save for $379.35 recovered during the year of income ended 30 June 1987, the entirety of the monies was lost and the Applicant claims an income tax deduction. More particularly he claims a deduction in sum of $45,000 in the year of income ended 30 June 1983. He rests his claims to a deduction on the provisions of section 51,
  2. The evidence establishes that the Applicant, who was born in 1947, has been an "investor" since 1966. In that year he invested $1,750 in finance company debentures from which he derived income until the year of income ended 1970. The highest return of income from that source was $129 in 1969. In 1968 on the occasion of his twenty-first birthday he became an investor in a company controlled by his father and he maintained that investment until at least the year of income ended 30 June 1984. That investment started with $1,155 and peaked at $3,500 in the 1979 year and then remained at that level. In the 1970 year when the finance company debenture matured most of the money was initially deposited with his father's company at interest. That additional investment was held until 1972 when it matured. The proceeds were used to assist in financing the purchase of a matrimonial home.

3. In the 1976 year the taxpayer purchased 100 $2 fully-paid shares in his father's company. In June 1976 he borrowed $2,500 from his bank and invested it in a Building Society at a higher rate of interest. The evidence shows that in the next eight months this venture produced a net gain of $4 (sic). Between November 1978 and March 1980 he unsuccessfully conducted a small shop in an exclusive suburb. In June 1981 he decided to purchase real estate in the capital city of his State of origin. He borrowed from the family company to finance the purchase and pending settlement lent the borrowed monies ($35,000) to a finance company and derived interest. Following the break-down of his marriage he let the former matrimonial home from April 1982, deriving rent of $870 by 30 June 1982; but claiming deductions - not in dispute - of $2,484. (Those deductions included $1,954 to be mentioned later). In addition the Applicant in October, 1981 borrowed $6,323 from a credit union to lend at marginally better rates to a trading organisation.

4. Apart from the transaction in question upon this reference, other actions of the Applicant by way of investment over the years were to maintain monies on deposit with building societies, credit unions, savings banks and the like. The evidence established that at 30 June in each year in the period 1977 to 1984 the number of such investments ranged from four to six; on 20 occasions the only interest generated By those investments was $1; on only one such occasion did the income derived exceed $200.00 and only on 4 occasions did it exceed $100.00. It is against the background of those findings that the present claim falls to be assessed.

5. A became interested in an investment opportunity promoted by a firm of financiers (F). He decided to reorganize finances on the former matrimonial home - still occupied by him as a residence - to enable him to invest. To that end he arranged to borrow $67,000.00 through his solicitors. Upon settlement they paid the principal and interest ($21,949.00) due on the former mortgage - presumably including the $1,954.00 (ante) later claimed as an interest deduction - leaving A with nearly $45,000.00 available (after meeting costs and expenses) to invest in the contemplated project.

6. F's proposal was for the development of a parcel of undeveloped land in a country centre quite close to a major capital city. The land was owned by a company (LCO). A proposal was placed before A that with others - unidentified - he should enter into a contract with a second company (FCO - apparently associated with F) which would provide managerial services. FCO was to arrange for A and others to purchase the land from LCO and then arrange construction of ten town-houses. A was to have a one-fifth interest in the project. That interest was to be acquired at a cost of $45,000.00. FCO would arrange to borrow $514,000.00 to finance construction and A's liability was intended to be limited to one-fifth of the total borrowed. No agreement was ever entered into between A and any co-venturers. No agreement for the purchase of any lands was ever executed by A or on his behalf and nor was any agreement for management of any project entered into with anyone. Instead, at the time the borrowing of the $67,000.00 was being finalised F not FCO - wrote to the solicitors who were solicitors to A and his mortgagees advising that "(FCO), as managers of the project, guarantees the payment of interest on (A's) mortgage of $67,000.00 and on his house property, with interest at 17.5% per annum payable monthly". F in that letter also opened its account of "the step by step procedure" to be followed in relation to the proposed investment by saying "Immediately (A) lodges his funds with (BC0) interest at 16% per annum is paid monthly until such time as a tenants-in-common contract (sic) is executed. The vendor of the land is (LCO)". A pro forma "contract for purchase and immediate resale" headed in the name of FCO accompanied that letter. In that document it was acknowledged that LCO and BCO were "related" to FCO within the meaning of Section 5 of The Companies Act, 1961.

7. Following receipt of that letter and enclosures A settled the mortgage and borrowed $67,000.00. Having repaid the existing mortgage on his residence, he then advanced $45,000.00 on loan to BCO receiving a "certificate of deposit" under the Common Seal of BCO acknowledging an interest bearing deposit at 16% per annum with interest to he calculated monthly and to be held "on 24-hour call pending settlement of contract". That was on 5 February, 1982.

  1. By April, 1982 A was disquieted. He went to inspect the site of the proposed development and there found evidence that work had commenced but was not being continued. He immediately made a demand of BCO for repayment of the loan. That was on 22 April, 1982. The demand was not satisfied and the only payment he thereafter received was by way of a first and final dividend paid by the liquidator in August, 1986 amounting to $379.00.
  2. The first basis relied on by the Applicant in claiming the relief sought is Section 63(1) of the Act which provides that,

"Debts which are bad debts and are written off as such during the year of income, and

(a) have been bought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year; or

(b) are in respect of money lent in the ordinary course of the business for the lending of money by a taxpayer who carries on that business,

shall be allowable deductions".

That claim must fail. There was no suggestion at all from the evidence that the debt had been "written off as such during the year of income", nor was there any suggestion that the amount sought to be written off had been "brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year". Further, I adopt as my own words of Jacobs, J in Marshall and Brougham Pty Ltd v FC of T (86 ATC 4,469 at 4473-4) when he said "...it is in my opinion quite impossible to say that this taxpayer....was...'carrying on the business of lending money'....No one, I would venture to think would suggest that they were carrying on the business of a money lender if they deposited their money regularly in an interest bearing current account with a bank..."or, I would add, in small interest bearing accounts with building societies, credit unions, savings banks, one family company and one trading company.

10. Another ground for deduction relied on by the Applicant is Section 51 of the Act which provides,

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income".

In my view the Applicant cannot successfully rely upon that provision. The $45,000.00 which had been borrowed constituted a capital sum which he intended to apply in order to generate assessable income. In the short-term that income was to be derived by way of interest, having been lent to BCO. In the long-term it was hoped that the monies would come to be invested in the real estate project from which it would generate a profit on sale, which profit would constitute assessable income in due course. Nonetheless in both cases the $45,000 constituted the "profit-yielding subject" which was to generate income. That being so, in my view for the purposes of Section 51, the loss incurred was a loss of capital.

11. It remains to consider whether an entitlement to a deduction arises pursuant to Section 52 of the Act which in its material terms provides,

"Any loss incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income....from the carrying on or carrying out of any undertaking or scheme, the profit (if any) from which sale, undertaking or scheme would have been included in his assessable income shall be an allowable deduction, provided that ....".

From the evidence of the taxpayer the 'project which was to generate the ultimate profit was to involve the acquisition of an interest in real estate as a tenant-in-common in association with other investors; the development of that site by the construction of town-houses; and the sale of the interest arising at a profit. His evidence was that there would have been such a scheme profit and that his intention throughout was to effect such a sale, thereby making the profit assessable income. That was not challenged and I accept it. The contention for the Applicant is that it was an integral part of that scheme that he should first invest the $45,000.00 with BCO. I reject that as a finding of fact. There is nothing before me in the evidence which suggests that at any point of time A had any obligation, whether to F, FCO, LCO, BCO or to others who might have become investors, to acquire any interest in any land or invest in any "project". In my view the investment with BCO is to be considered as a lending of money pending its investment in such a scheme or project. It stands on the same footing as the earlier investment in a finance company pending the purchase of real estate in another city. The loan was an investment which was expected to generate assessable income by way of interest but in my view the fact that the interest would be assessable income does not constitute it as a "profit" within the meaning of Section 52. Nor does it constitute the lending of money at interest as a transaction which in itself constitutes the carrying out of any "profit-making undertaking or scheme".

12. I would uphold the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection.
