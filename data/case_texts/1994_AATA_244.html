QT93/38 and Commissioner of Taxation [1994] AATA 244; 94 ATC 400; (1994) 29 ATR 1064 (22 August 1994)

Last Updated: 18 May 2009

INCOME TAX - Assessable Income - Whether the ex gratia payment by a State Government of the amount of a taxpayer’s legal expenses in appearing at an Inquiry relating to his suspension from employment by a local government authority constitutes assessable income in the hands of that taxpayer under sub-s 25(1) or s 26(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Whether the deductibility of those legal expenses has an effect upon the question of assessability of that ex gratia receipt - The relationship between the operation of sub-s 25(1) and s 26(e) discussed.

3. The abovementioned submissions on behalf of the applicant are as follows:



Introduction



1-3. [repeated much of the facts set out above]



No product of employment



  1. The general principle to be applied in determining whether a voluntary payment is a capital receipt or income was expressed in Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, per Fullagar J at 54:

A voluntary payment of money or transfer of property by A to B is prima facie not income in B’s hands. If nothing more appears than that A gave to B some money or a motor car or some shares, what B receives is capital and not income. But further facts may appear which show that, although the payment or transfer was a ‘gift’ in the sense that it was made without legal obligation, it was nevertheless so related to the employment of B by A, or to services rendered by B to A, or to a business carried on by B, that it is, in substance and in reality, not a mere gift but the product of an income earning activity on the part of B, and therefore to be regarded as income from B’s personal exertion.



  1. Whether an amount is income according to ordinary concepts depends upon its quality in the hands of the recipient: see Hayes v. FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, 55; Scott v. FCT [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514, 526; Federal Coke Co. Pty Ltd v FCT 77 ATC 4255; FCT v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, 4479. The whole of the circumstances must be considered: Squatting Investments Pty Ltd v FCT [1953] HCA 13; (1953) 86 CLR 570, 627; [1954] UKPCHCA 2; (1954) 88 CLR 413; AL Hamblin Constructions Pty Ltd v FCT 74 ATC 4001, 4009; FCT v Harris 80 ATC 4238, 4240. Where a receipt is a product, in a real sense, of the employment or services rendered by the recipient or of any business or income earning operations carried on by him, in general it will be income according to ordinary concepts: Squatting Investments Pty Ltd v FCT [1953] HCA 13; (1953) 86 CLR 570, 633; [1954] UKPCHCA 2; (1954) 88 CLR 413; Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, 56-57; Scott v FCT [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514, 527-528; FCT v Harris 80 ATC 4238, 4241. This will be so even if the services are rendered once and once only and whether the compensation is received in a lump sum or by periodical receipts: FCT v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, 4480. In general, the test to be applied will be objective rather than subjective: Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, 55: FCT v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, 4479. The motives of the donor in making the payment do not determine the answer, but are not irrelevant: Scott v FCT [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514, 526; Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, 56; Kelly v FCT 85 ATC 4283; FCT v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, 4479. Even if an ex gratia payment is not made because of the personal qualities of the recipient, that is only one factor to be taken into account: see Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, 55; FCT v Spedley Securities Pty Ltd 88 ATC 4126, 4130.

  2. A temporal connection is not sufficient to make a receipt income: FCT v Spedley Securities Pty Ltd 88 ATC 4126, 4130. A fortiori, neither is an historical connection. In FCT v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, the voluntary payments made to augment the taxpayer’s military pay by his former employer were held not to be a product of his former employment, or any suspended employment or possible future employment. However it was held to be income because its periodical nature satisfied the description of income in his hands. Similarly, in FCT v Harris 80 ATC 4238, where voluntary payments were made by a bank to its retired employee, the Court held that the balance of factors was against the receipts being a product of the taxpayer’s former employment, as there was no expectation of receipt and the amount was not calculated by reference to the quality or length of his past service to the bank. By way of contrast, in FCT v Blake 84 ATC 4661 where the taxpayer had an expectation of continuing to receive the periodical payments, they were held to be income: see also Case T80 86 ATC 1107.

  3. While the Applicant accepts that if payments are really incidental to an employment, it is unimportant whether they come from the employer or from somebody else and are obtained as a contractual right or merely as a recognised incident of the employment or work: see FCT v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, 556; Scott v FCT [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514, 526. But where a payment is received neither pursuant to a contractual right nor as a recognised incident of an employment, the fact that the payment is made by someone other than an employer is antithetical to its characterisation as incidental to an employment.

    1. In the present case -

(a) the payment was an “ex gratia” payment;



(b) the payment was not made by the former employer but by the State Government;



(c) the payment was not one of a series of recurrent or periodic payments but was a single lump sum payment;



(d) the payment was not made pursuant to or in discharge of an express or implied term of the Applicant’s contract of employment;



(e) the payment was not made as a reward for services rendered by or on behalf of the Applicant to or at the request of the employer in the course of the employment or at all;



(f) the payment was not made as a reward for services rendered by or on behalf of the Applicant to or at the request of any other person;



(g) the payment was not made during the employment or in consequence of its termination, and was not received until a substantial time after the termination of the employment;



(h) nothing in the Applicant’s contract of employment or the terms of the Inquiry or the statutes providing for the same caused or could have caused the Applicant to expect or anticipate the payment;



(i) the payment was not made to reimburse outgoings of his employer paid by the Applicant, either voluntarily or at the express or implied request of the employer;



(j) the payment was not made pursuant to or in discharge of any legal or commercial obligation that was in existence at the time of the Inquiry or any time thereafter, but even if it was, the obligation was not an incident of his employment or any business;



(k) if the payment was an incident of his employment, it should have been recoverable even if the Applicant had not been exonerated by the Inquiry, but here, whatever the outcome of the Inquiry, it was not recoverable, from the employer or any other person;



(l) further or alternatively, the relevant relationship, if any, was between the receipt and the Inquiry and not with the employment or services of the Applicant.



  1. In the premises, the Applicant submits that the receipt was not income according to ordinary concepts and usages and therefore not assessable income under s.25(1) or any other section of the Act.

Section 26(e)



  1. In Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, 54, Fullagar J said he doubted very much whether s.26(e) had the effect of bringing into charge any receipt which would not be bought into charge in any case. In FCT v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, 553-554, Dixon CJ and Williams J said that they had difficulty in seeing that the payment was allowed or given in respect of, or in relation directly or indirectly to any employment or service rendered by the taxpayer. They observed that a direct relation may be regarded as one where the employment is the proximate cause of the payment, and an indirect relation as one where the employment is a cause less proximate or, indeed, only one contributory cause. However, they concluded:

We are not prepared to give s.26(e) a construction which makes it unnecessary that the allowance, gratuity, compensation, benefit, bonus or premium shall in any sense be a recompense or consequence of the continued or contemporaneous existence of the relation of employer or employee or a reward for services rendered given either during the employment or at or in consequence of its termination.



Conversely, in FCT v Smith 86 ATC 4463, where a bank made study incentive payments to an employee during his employment, the Court said that once it was accepted that the payments would not have been made if the relationship of employer and employee had not existed, it was impossible to deny that the employment was one contributing cause.



  1. In the present case, the payment was not in any relevant sense attributable to any service performed by the taxpayer, so s.26(e) does not apply: see AL Hamblin Equipment Pty Ltd v FCT 74 ATC 4001, 4009; FCT v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, 4482.

Refunds of amounts claimed as allowable deductions



  1. The voluntary refund of an amount (other than of rates or taxes or obtained in carrying on a business), previously allowed as a deduction does not mean that the receipt is income: see Allsop v FCT [1965] HCA 48; (1965) 113 CLR 341, 350; HR Sinclair & Son Pty Ltd v FCT [1966] HCA 39; (1966) 114 CLR 537, 542-543, 545.

  2. Nor does S.26(j) apply, as the subsection relates only to amounts received by way of “insurance or indemnity”, and the voluntary payment from the State Government does not satisfy either description: see Commercial Banking Co. of Sydney Ltd v FCT 83 ATC 4208; affirmed on other grounds FCT v National Commercial Banking Corp of Aust Ltd 83 ATC 4715.

  3. Further or alternatively, the receipt was to reimburse outgoings of capital so the reimbursement was in any event also on capital account.

Capital gains tax



  1. No asset was disposed of or deemed to have been disposed of by the taxpayer for the purposes of Part IIIA of the Act, so the amount received by the Applicant is not an assessable capital gain.

4. The abovementioned submissions on behalf of the respondent are as follows:



BACKGROUND



[repeated much of the facts set out above]



THE DISPUTE



The taxpayer contends that the $24748.24 received by him from the [State] Treasurer was a receipt of capital and not assessable to him within his 1989 return of income. In support of this contention he claims:



  • that the ex gratia payment was not assessable to him since it was compensation made in respect of legal expenses and costs incurred in disputes with a former employer due to unlawful dismissal
  • that the payment was made as an undissected lump sum
  • that the sum paid was not derived from activities in the financial year
  • that the sum was not incidental to the business being carried on
  • that the payment was in the form of a lump sum and was not recurrent or regular

SUBMISSIONS



(1) The Commissioner’s first submission is that the sum of $24,748.24 is a reimbursement of deductible expenditure.



These expenses were incurred by the taxpayer in the course of gaining or producing his assessable income and were allowed on appeal under subsection 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (ITAA).



It is submitted that a reimbursement of expenses which are deductible under subsection 51(1) constitutes assessable income under subsection 25(1) of the ITAA).



The authority for this proposition is found in Allsop v FCT [1965] HCA 48; (1965) 113 CLR 341, in particular the joint judgement of Barwick C.J. and Taylor J. at pp. 351-352.



It is supported by the New Zealand case of Cromwell Jockey Club v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (1954) 6 AITR 188.



(2) The taxpayer and his representatives have submitted that the amount of $24,748.24 represents an undissected lump sum. That is, that this amount contains both capital and income components which cannot be identified.



The circumstances under which payment was made by the state government make it clear that this amount was not paid as an undissected lump sum. Having agreed in principle to make a payment to the taxpayer, the state government sought an itemisation of the taxpayer’s legal costs. When this was provided, the precise amount of the taxpayer’s legal costs was reimbursed.



Although the taxpayer had presented a claim for “interest lost” of $13,957.53, no amount was paid for interest.



No part of the amount of $24,748.24 represents anything other than a direct reimbursement of legal costs incurred by the taxpayer. That is, there is no capital component.



(3) The Commissioner’s final submission is that the amount paid to the taxpayer is in the nature of:



“.........., gratuities, compensations, benefits, .........., given or granted to him in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly to, any employment of or services rendered by him..........”



and is therefore assessable under paragraph 26(e) of the ITAA.



It does not matter if someone other than the employer pays this amount to the taxpayer. The authority for this contention is found in FCT v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540 and Smith v FCT 87 ATC 4883.



To quote from the joint judgement of Dixon C.J. and Williams J. at page 556 of the former case:



`In the definition of “income from personal exertion” the expression “allowances and gratuities received in the capacity of employee or in relation to any services rendered”, while it does not appear to us to include, as a matter of meaning, allowances and gratuities received by an employee after he has ceased to render any services and after his employment has completely terminated, nevertheless does seem to indicate that no contractual right to the payment need exist. Indeed, it is clear that if payments are really incidental to an employment, it is unimportant whether they come from the employer or from somebody else and are obtained as of right or merely as a recognized incident of the employment or work.’



It is further contended that paragraph 26(e) will be applicable whether the amount in question is capital or income.



The authority for this contention is found in Haggarty and Ors v FCT 89 ATC 4485. At page 4493 Wilcox J. said:



`In stating that conclusion I do not overlook the fact that counsel for the applicants submits that the payments were payments of a capital nature. This proposition is resisted by counsel for the respondent who submits that the payments should properly be regarded as income, within ordinary concepts. Indeed, counsel for the respondent contends that the Tribunal erred in failing to so find and submits that, if the applicants’ argument regarding section 26(e) should be successful, the matter ought to be remitted to the Tribunal for determination of that question. Upon the view I take, of course, there is no need for this to be done. It seems to me apparent that, if a payment falls within section 26(e) of the Act, it is irrelevant to determine whether that payment would, or would not, constitute income according to ordinary concepts. Were the position otherwise, section 26(e) would be unnecessary. The relevant receipts would already have been caught by section 25. See also the comments made in Smith by Brennan J. at ATC pp 4887-4888; ALJR p 542 and by Toohey J at ATC pp 4894-4895; ALJR p 547.’



5. The abovementioned reply on behalf of the applicant to the respondent’s submissions is as follows:



  1. In reply to the respondent’s submission that a reimbursement of expenses which are deductible under s.51(1) constitute assessable income under s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (the “Act”), the applicant contends that the submission is incorrect -

(a) There is no general principle that refunds of amounts allowed as deductions in previous years of income are themselves income, for the answer depends on an analysis of the nature of the refunds: see H.R. Sinclair & Son Pty Ltd v FCT [1966] HCA 39; (1966) 114 CLR 537 at 542-543 per Taylor J, 545 per Owen J; Allsop v FCT [1965] HCA 48; (1965) 113 CLR 341 at 350 per Barwick CJ and Taylor J (in which Cromwell Jockey Club v IRC (NZ) (1954) 6 AITR 188 was cited in argument); cf. British Mexican Petroleum Co. Ltd v IRC (1932) 16 Tax Cas. 570.



(b) In any event, the amount in question here is not a refund but an ex gratia payment from Consolidated Revenue.



  1. Even if the respondent’s characterisation of the amount in question as a “reimbursement of legal costs” is correct, it does not follow that the amount was not of a capital nature in the hands of the taxpayer -

(a) In general, a payment received as compensation for or in consideration of the abandonment or cancellation of a contract of service is of a capital nature: see Reseck v FCT [1975] HCA 38; (1975) 133 CLR 45 at 48-49; Bennett v FCT [1947] HCA 25; (1947) 75 CLR 480 at 485; Scott v C of T (NSW) (1935) 35 SR(NSW) 215; cf. Henley v Murray (1950) 31 Tax Cas. 351.



(b) An analogous principle can be found in the agency cases, where the compensation for cancellation or substantial variation of a principal contract pursuant to which most of the taxpayer’s income is generated is characterised as a capital receipt: see Californian Oil Products Ltd v FCT [1934] HCA 35; (1934) 52 CLR 28; Barr Crombie & Co Ltd v IRC (1945) 26 Tax Cas. 406; Sabine v Hookers Ltd (1958) 38 Tax Cas. 120.



(c) Even if the legal costs of the Committee of Inquiry were incurred in connection with the attempted cancellation of the contract of service of the applicant with the [name] Shire Council, if the applicant had received compensation (which is not admitted) for the termination of his contract of service, it would have been characterised as a capital receipt. If a component of that compensation had included legal costs, it would have been of the same character. Similarly, on the same premise, any payment received on account for the legal costs of the Inquiry should also be characterised as of a capital nature as the Inquiry into the allegations of breaches of the contract of service involved, in effect, legal costs incurred in defending a “structural” asset of the taxpayer rather than as incidental to his performance of service thereunder.



(d) Further or alternatively, in any event, the reimbursement of legal costs was not in any relevant sense a product of either the contract of employment or the service rendered or to be rendered by the applicant thereunder to the Council. Nor can the payment be fairly characterised as “compensation” received as a usual or unusual incident of that contract of employment or the performance of the services required thereunder.



  1. In reply to the final submission of the respondent -

(a) In Constable v FCT [1952] HCA 64; (1952) 86 CLR 402 at 415, Dixon CJ, McTiernan, Williams and Fullagar JJ said in relation to s.26(e):



Upon the text of the paragraph it would seem that the liability of the sum, or any part of the sum, received by the present taxpayer during the year of income to inclusion in his assessable income must depend upon the answers to one or other of the following questions. Can that sum or any part of it be described as an allowance, gratuity, compensation, benefit, bonus or premium? If so, can it be said that it was “allowed, given or granted to him” during that year? If an affirmative answer is given to these two questions, then is it correct to say of the amount or any part of it that it was so allowed, given or granted to him “in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly, to any employment of him or services rendered by him? The employment or services must be employment by, or services rendered to, the Shell Co. of Australia Ltd. It is evident that it is enough for the taxpayer if any of the foregoing questions is answered in the negative.



(b) If the respondent was correct in characterising the payment by the [State] Government as “a direct reimbursement of legal costs” for the purposes of its second submission, then it is submitted that as a reimbursement the payment was not “an allowance, gratuity, compensation, benefit, bonus or premium” within the meaning of those expressions in s.26(e): cf Case 153 (1942) 10 CTBR 480 for a discussion of the difference between a reimbursement and an allowance.



(c) Further or alternatively, the payment was not “in respect of any employment of him, or services rendered by him” to the Council, but in respect of legal costs incurred by him in respect of his employment of legal representatives in the preparation for and conduct of the Committee of Inquiry. Not even the use of the words “directly or indirectly” extends the paragraph far enough to catch the present case, where the payment in question was a separate and independent voluntary payment made a substantial time after the conclusion of the Committee of Inquiry.



6. The respondent submits that the amount in issue in this case is assessable income under sub-s 25(1), or alternatively s 26(e), of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”), the latter of which relevantly provides:



26. The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include ---



(e) the value to the taxpayer of all allowances, gratuities, compensations, benefits, bonuses and premiums allowed, given or granted to him in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly to, any employment of or services rendered by him, whether so allowed, given or granted in money, goods, land, meals, sustenance, the use of premises or quarters or otherwise ... [followed by a list of exclusions not applicable here]



7. Before considering the application of each of each of those provisions, it is, I consider, appropriate to say something about the relationship between the two provisions. Although the issue is not completely settled, it would now appear that the better view is that s 26(e) is not limited to including in assessable income receipts or benefits which are, according to general concepts, of an income nature: See for example the comments of Wilcox J in Haggarty & Ors v FCT 89 ATC 4485, at 4493-4, and Brennan and Toohey JJ in Smith v FCT 87 ATC 4883, at 4888 and 4891-2 respectively, which are contrary to the earlier views of Fullagar J in Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, at 54, and Windeyer J in Scott v FCT [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514, at 525-6. In any event, for reasons which appear below, the result is the same when the facts are considered under either section.



8. As stated above, the respondent’s first submission is that the sum in issue is a reimbursement of expenses which are deductible under sub-s 51(1) and therefore constitutes assessable income under subsection 25(1) of the Act. The respondent cited Allsop v FCT [1965] HCA 48; (1965) 113 CLR 341, and in particular the joint judgement of Barwick CJ and Taylor J, and the New Zealand case of Cromwell Jockey Club v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (NZ) (1954) 6 AITR 188; 10 ATD 431, as authority for that proposition. However, before stating my conclusions on the law in relation to this submission, it is worthwhile for me to state certain findings of fact which I have made which will not only clarify the issues relating to the respondent’s first submission, but will resolve the issues involved in the respondent’s second submission.



9. I accept the respondent’s submission that the amount in issue does not contain both capital and income components which cannot by identified. I am satisfied that no part of the amount in issue represented anything other than an ex gratia reimbursement of legal costs incurred by the applicant. Looking at the relevant documents, it would appear that the letter dated 6 March 1989 from the [State] Treasurer to the applicant actually preceded the letter dated 2 March 1989 from the applicant’s solicitors to the Department of Treasury. Although I do not have any evidence before me which enables me to say with any certainty what date that letter was sent, I am satisfied that the 2 March 1989 letter is incorrectly dated and was sent in response to the 6 March 1989 letter. The 6 March 1989 letter, addressed to the applicant, states:



Dear [applicant],



I refer to the matter of your costs in relation to the Committee of Inquiry into your suspension as the [name] Shire [occupation].



Cabinet has agreed in principle to the State providing you with an ex gratia payment equivalent to the costs you incurred in the conduct of the Inquiry.



In order to allow a proper assessment of your costs it would be appreciated if you could request your solicitors to supply the Treasury with a statement of your costs on the same basis as would be provided to a Court Taxing Officer.



A determination and ex gratia payment will be effected as soon as possible on receipt of this information.



Yours sincerely,



In response, the applicant’s solicitors sent the letter wrongly dated 2 March 1989 which states:



Department of Treasury

...



Dear Sirs



RE: [Applicant] & [name] SHIRE COUNCIL



We have as requested prepared an itemisation of [applicant]’s legal costs in taxable form, which is enclosed for your attention.



Our Client has also provided a calculation, which is enclosed, of interest lost on his savings withdrawn of $23,951.79 to pay part of the legal costs. In that regard we note [applicant] had requested the Council to meet his costs as early as 1 November 1985 as appears by his letter to the [name] Shire Council of that date, a copy of which is enclosed. Had the Council honoured its obligation to reimburse him at that date, the ensuring loss of interest would not have occurred.



The sum sought for reimbursement is then:



Legal costs and outlays $24,748.24

Interest lost 13,957.53

$38,705.77



We await your further consideration of the matter.



Yours faithfully



The Treasurer then responded to that letter by letter dated 26 April 1989 which states:



Dear [applicant],



I refer to the matter of your costs in relation to the Committee of Inquiry into your suspension as the [name] Shire [occupation].



The claim for costs and interest submitted by your Solicitors has now been examined and I advise that in accordance with Cabinet’s decision on this matter, an ex-gratia payment of $24,748.24 has been approved in settlement of the claim. A cheque for this amount is enclosed.



Yours sincerely,



Notwithstanding the wording of that last letter, I readily agree with paragraph 15 of the parties Statement of Agreed Facts that “[t]he payment was not made subject to it being accepted in settlement of any legal claim or any other entitlement. The payment was made totally on an ex gratia basis.” Further, as I stated above, I am satisfied that the payment was reimbursement of the applicant’s legal costs; the payment did not contain a component related to the claim for lost interest. Having made those findings of fact, is the respondent’s submission correct that reimbursement of those legal costs, which are deductible under sub-s 51(1), therefore constitutes assessable income under subsection 25(1) of the Act?



10. Having considered the matter at length, I have concluded that the applicant is correct that no such general principle exists: See the joint judgment of Barwick CJ and Taylor J in Allsop at 350, and, even more emphatically, Taylor and Owen JJ in HR Sinclair & Son Pty Ltd v FCT [1966] HCA 39; (1966) 114 CLR 537, at 542-543 and 545 respectively. Further, as the applicant rightly points out, the Cromwell Jockey Club case was cited in argument in the HR Sinclair & Son case and expressly considered by Owen J (at 547) not to be of any assistance. Indeed if one looks at the judgment of McGregor J in the Cromwell Jockey Club case one can readily see why that is the case. McGregor J, at 433, described the refund there in question as “more accurately, a subtraction from the payment made by the club and deductible for income tax purposes”. But Taylor J in the HR Sinclair & Son case (at 543) expressly stated that, unlike England where such an approach had not infrequently been used, there was “no power to adopt this course in Australia”. It is true that in the HR Sinclair & Son and Cromwell Jockey Club cases (where there was not the complication as there was in Allsop that neither the whole nor any part of the lump sum in issue there could be attributed solely to a refund of the relevant payments) that the refunds in issue were held to be of an income nature, but this was not from application of a general principle of the nature espoused by the respondent, but because in both cases, the relevant receipts were held to have been received as part of the proceeds from the carrying on of the relevant businesses: see in the HR Sinclair & Son case, per Taylor J at 543-4, and per Owen J at 547; and in the Cromwell Jockey Club case at 433-4 and 434-5.



11. As a result, it is necessary to consider “the many guiding principles which the authorities have laid down as relevant to the distinction” between income and capital (FCT v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, per Hill J at 4479) to determine whether the receipt is of an income nature. I would, however, state at the outset that I agree with the statement of Lord Greene MR in IRC v British Salmson Aero Engines Ltd [1938] 2 KB 482, at 498 that “in many cases it is almost true to say that the spin of a coin would decide the matter almost as satisfactorily as an attempt to find reasons” for concluding that an amount is either of an income or capital nature. In other cases the same view has been expressed somewhat more subtly in statements to the effect that the distinction is a question of “emphasis” or of “fact and degree”.



12. The following are some of those “guiding principles” (most of which were usefully set out in the applicant’s submissions):



(a) Whether an amount is income according to ordinary concepts depends upon “a consideration of the whole of the circumstances”(see for example The Squatting Investment Co Ltd v FCT [1953] HCA 13; (1953) 86 CLR 570, at 627; AL Hamblin Equipment Pty Ltd v FCT 74 ATC 4001, at 4009; FCT v Harris 80 ATC 4238, at 4240) and “its quality in the hands of the recipient.” (Hayes at 55; Scott at 526; Federal Coke Co Pty Ltd v FCT 77 ATC 4255, at 4273; Harris at 4240; Cooling at 4479.)



(b) The test to be applied is an objective rather than subjective one: see for example Hayes at 55; Cooling at 4479.



(c) The motives of the donor of the payment are a relevant consideration but not decisive: see for example Hayes at 56; Scott at 526; Harris at 4240; Kelly v FCT 85 ATC 4283, at 4288; Cooling at 4479.



(d) The regularity and periodicity or otherwise of the payment will be a relevant though not decisive consideration: see for example FCT v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540, at 568; FCT v The Myer Emporium Ltd 87 ATC 4363; Cooling at 4480.



(e) Although the cases have tended to fail in some degree to discriminate in what context use is being made of such phrases (i.e. whether in relation to sub-s 25(1) as distinct from s 26(e)), it would appear that a generally decisive consideration is whether the receipt is “the product of”, “incidental to”, “an incident of”, “a consequence of” or “in relation to” any employment of, or services rendered by, the recipient, or of any business or any revenue producing activity of the recipient (see for example Dixon at 554; Hayes at 56-7; Scott at 527; AL Hamblin at 4010; Harris at 4241 and 4245; Kelly at 4287 and 4288-9; Smith at 4886) and it matters little that the payment is gratuitous or made by someone other than the recipient’s employer.



13. Based on the facts and documentary evidence in this case, I am satisfied, particularly having regard to the following findings:



(a) the payment or receipt in question was a single rather than a recurrent or periodic one;



(b) the payment was not made by the applicant’s former employer, but by the State Government;



(c) the payment was truly of an ex gratia nature, there being no obligation whatsoever on the part of the State Government to make the payment (either on its own behalf or on behalf of the applicant’s former employer pursuant to the applicant’s contract of employment or otherwise);



(d) the payment was not made to the applicant until a substantial time after the termination of his employment by the Shire Council;



(e) the money was not received by the applicant in return for any services rendered by him or on his behalf, or in consequence of his termination; and



(f) the receipt was not “the product of”, “a consequence of” (or any of the other oft-mentioned phrases referred to above) the applicant’s employment or any services rendered by him. Indeed, I am satisfied that any connection between the receipt and the applicant’s employment with the Shire Council was no more than a mere historical one.,



that the subject receipt is not of an income nature in the hands of the applicant, and thus not assessable under sub-s 25(1) of the Act.



14. As far as assessability of the amount under s 26(e) is concerned, I have already cited some of the more important cases in relation to the interpretation of that section, and thankfully am spared the task of setting out the various features of s 26(e) by the useful summary made by Deputy President Layton of this Tribunal in Case V76 88 ATC 538, at 541-2, which was referred to by the respondent in his “Reasons for Decision” contained in the “T” documents. Having regard to the facts and documentary evidence in this case, and particularly the findings I made when considering the application of sub-s 25(1), I consider that the receipt is also not assessable under s 26(e) of the Act. In particular, I consider that the necessary nexus between the benefit and the applicant’s employment required for assessability under s 26(e) is not present.



15. For the above reasons, I set aside the objection decision under review and allow the applicant’s objection in full.
