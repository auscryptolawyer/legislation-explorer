Tribunal

Decision
The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



........(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe.........

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - contribution to superannuation fund - whether deduction, whether eligible person - income in year on past employer contributions - whether benefits attributable to year - whether benefits out of moneys not representing personal contribution or income thereon - whether contribution shortly before retirement to obtain superannuation benefits

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 82AAS, 82AAT



REASONS FOR DECISION



21 December 1998
Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member




1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against a notice of amended assessment of income tax for the year ended 30 June 1992. The amendment was issued on 31 July 1996 to disallow a deduction for personal superannuation contributions of $228,000 previously allowed in the original assessment.

2. At the hearing the applicant, Mr Yates, was represented by Mr N. Rosenbaum of counsel and the respondent by Mr G. Davies of counsel. Evidence was given by Mr Yates and by his accountant, Mr D. Neilson.

3. The basis facts were not in dispute and can be taken from the respondent’s statement provided pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as follows:

1. The Applicant was born on 15 August 1927.
  1. On 11 September 1991 the Applicant gave written notice of his resignation as an employee of Prentice Builders Ltd.
  2. Until his retirement on 10 December 1991, the Applicant was, at all other material times:
(a) an employee of Prentice Builders Ltd;

(b) a member of the Prentice Executive Superannuation Fund (“the PESF”); and

(c) a member of the Prentice Executive Supplementary Superannuation Fund.
  1. At all material times the PESF was a complying superannuation fund for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”);
    1. The Applicant made a contribution of $303,000 to the PESF (“the contribution”) on or about 11 November 1991.
    2. The contribution was made just 28 days before the Applicant retired, on 10 December 1991.
    3. The contribution was the only contribution made by the Applicant to the PESF during the applicant’s membership.
    4. The source of the contribution was primarily funds from the Yates Family Trust.
    5. In response to the Respondent’s questionnaire dated 14 April 1993 the Applicant’s agent advised by letter dated 11 May 1993 that the source of the funds for the contribution was personal funds of the Applicant.
    6. In a letter to the respondent dated 20 October 1993 the Applicant’s agent stated:
‘The source of the funds accumulated in the taxpayer’s account and applied to the payment of the superannuation contribution was primarily loan funds from the Yates Family Trust.’
  1. On or about 7 November 1991:
11.1 An amount of $300,000 was drawn on the Yates Family Trust loan account of FR and MPM Yates.

11.2 An amount of $300,000 was withdrawn from Balno Pty Ltd’s cash management trust account.

11.3 Balno Pty Ltd, the trustee of the Yates Family Trust, paid an amount of $300,000 into the Potter Warburg Cash Management Account in the name the Applicant and his wife.
  1. On or about 11 November 1991:
12.1 A cheque for $303,000 was drawn on the Potter Warburg Cash Management Account.

12.2 An amount of $303,000 was paid into the PESF on behalf of the Applicant.

12.3 An amount of $303,000 was deposited in the National Australia Bank 11am call account in the name of Proback Pty Ltd (the trustee of the PESF).
  1. On or about 10 December 1991 the Applicant was paid the sum of $815,115 by the PESF.
  2. In the period between December 1991 and 30 June 1992 the amount of $300,000 was repaid by the applicant to the Yates Family Trust.
  3. Since the year of income ended 30 June 1989, Prentice Builders Ltd did not make contributions to the PESF in respect of the applicant.
  4. The Applicant’s employer, Prentice Builders Ltd continued to make contributions to the PESF in respect of other employees in the years ended 30 June 1989 to 30 June 1992.
  5. In his return of income for the year ended 30 June 1992 the Applicant claimed a deduction of $228,000 for his contribution to the PESF (calculated pursuant to s.82AAT(2):
Claim $3,000.00

Plus 75% of the amount of the amount of

the contribution over $3,000 (75% x $300,000) $225,000.00

Total $228,000.00
  1. The Respondent disallowed the Applicant’s claim for a deduction for the contribution.

Another relevant fact included in the documentation provided was that the $815,115 received by Mr Yates from the PESF was made up of:

Balance at beginning of the year $511,079

Allocated earnings during the year 35,236

Member contribution 303,000

Income Tax Expense on Member Contribution (34,200)

(15% of $228,000) _______

Benefit Paid $815,115

4. Mr Neilson gave evidence that he provided advice to Mr Yates in October 1991 in relation to superannuation. He was unable to recall whether the advice resulted from an approach by Mr Yates or initiated by his firm. He recollected that, at the time, there was emphasis on the need for adequately funded superannuation benefits and believed that it was appropriate for Mr Yates to maximise the amount available by way of superannuation. After reviewing legislation and information from the respondent, Mr Neilson said that he recommended a personal contribution to maximise the superannuation benefit within the reasonable benefit limit rules. He thought that the maximum contribution then available was $590,000 and believed that the actual contribution was based on available resources of Mr Yates. He believed that the deductibility of the contribution was not critical to the decision to make the contribution, the emphasis being on increasing benefits. He said that he was conscious of an unwritten expectation of leaving contributions in the fund for a period of time but thought that one month was sufficient.

5. Mr Yates gave evidence that he joined Prentice Builders Ltd in 1956 and, at the time of his resignation in 1991, was Chairman and Managing Director. He said that the company was taken over in 1988 and progressively acrimony developed between the new shareholders and management. Finally, on 11 September 1991, Mr Yates presented a letter of resignation giving the required three months’ notice. He said that under the terms of his employment contract, he was not permitted to work in the building industry for two years after resignation. He said that he sought professional advice with a view to maximising investments and income available to him in retirement. He believed that the contribution of $303,000 was based on the recommendation of his advisors and maintained that deductibility of the contribution was not an issue, solely the maximising of benefits. He said that, prior to the contribution, personal savings had been advanced to the family trust and it made sense to fund the superannuation contribution by drawing on the trust. He accepted that loans of the magnitude of $300,000 from him and/or his wife could not be demonstrated but could not recall any discussion with his advisors in relation to the source of the funds. Mr Yates referred to accusations by the new shareholders of dishonesty in tendering arrangements and an investigation by a firm of solicitors. He maintained that this attack led him to seek to protect his assets by contributing to a superannuation fund. However, his evidence was that the accusations commenced in February 1992, some months after the contribution to and payment from the superannuation fund.

6. The deduction claimed by the applicant was pursuant to section 82AAT which, as far as relevant, provided:

“82AAT(1) Subject to this section, where:

(a) an eligible person makes a contribution to a fund to obtain superannuation benefits for the eligible person or, in the event of the death of the eligible person, for dependants of the eligible person; and

(b) the fund is a complying superannuation fund, within the meaning of Part IX, in relation to the year of income of the fund in which the contribution is made;

the amount of the contribution is an allowable deduction in respect of the year of income of the eligible person in which the contribution is made.

...

82AAT(2) The deduction allowable to a taxpayer under this section from the assessable income of a year of income shall not exceed:

(a) if the taxpayer is an unsupported eligible person in relation to the year of income — the lesser of the following:

(i) the sum of:
(A) $3,000; and

(B) 75% of the amount (if any) by which the total amount of the contributions exceeds $3,000;
(ii) the amount ascertained in accordance with the regulations as the taxpayer’s maximum deductible contributions for the year of income;

(c) in any other case — $3,000.”

An “unsupported eligible” person is defined simply as an eligible person which was defined in section 82AAS in the following terms:

“82AAS(2) A person (in this subsection referred to as the ‘relevant person’) is an eligible person in relation to a year of income for the purposes of this Subdivision unless—

(a) during the whole or a part of the year of income circumstances existed by reason of which it was reasonable to expect that superannuation benefits would be provided for the relevant person upon retirement or for dependants of the relevant person in the event of the death of the relevant person (whether or not any condition other than the retirement or death of the relevant person would be required to be satisfied in order that those benefits be provided); and

(b) to the extent to which those benefits would be attributable to the year of income—

(i) the benefits would be wholly or partly attributable to contributions made to a superannuation fund in relation to the relevant person by a person other than the relevant person; or

(ii) the benefits would, in whole or in part, be paid out of money that would not represent—
(A) contributions made by the relevant person to a superannuation fund;

(B) contributions made by the relevant person under a scheme for the payment of benefits upon retirement or death, being a scheme constituted by or under a law of the Commonwealth or of a State of Territory; or

(C) income or accretions arising from contributions referred to in sub-subparagraph (A) or (B).”

7. It was submitted for the respondent that, to the extent to which the benefit of $815,115 was attributable to the year ended 30 June 1992, it partly represented earnings of the fund in that year on all contributions including past contributions made by the employer. It was said that, as a consequence, either or both of paragraph (b)(i) and paragraph (b)(ii) of section 82AAS(2) were satisfied resulting in the applicant not being an “eligible person” in relation to the year ended 30 June 1992. Alternatively, it was submitted, the applicant made a contribution to the fund in order to obtain a perceived tax deduction, not to obtain a superannuation benefit, and section 82AAT did not operate to allow any part of the contribution as a deduction.

8. For the applicant, it was submitted that he was an eligible person in relation to the year ended 30 June 1992 in that the only superannuation benefits attributable to that year were from the contribution made by him and no other person contributed or could be expected to contribute in that year. It was said that the purpose of the contribution was to maximise superannuation benefits, was based on professional advice and complied with the requirements of the Act.

9. It is appropriate to consider, initially, the principal argument as to whether Mr Yates was an “eligible person” in relation to the year ended 30 June 1992, the year in which the personal contribution to the superannuation fund was made and claimed as a deduction. The answer to this question depends upon whether the earnings allocated to the credit of Mr Yates during the year and included in the payment of $815,115 on 10 December 1991 can be regarded as benefits attributable to the year ended 30 June 1992 and either attributable to contributions made by the employer or paid out of moneys that would not represent contributions made by Mr Yates or income or accretions arising from such personal contributions. There was no dispute that section 82AAS(2)(a) was satisfied in that it was reasonable to expect that superannuation benefits would be provided for Mr Yates upon his retirement during the year of income.

10. It is difficult to see that part of such benefits were not attributable to fund earnings credited to the account of Mr Yates in that year of income. The use of the word “attributable” should be taken to mean no more than benefits being able to be ascribed, imputed, traced to or having a causal connection with the relevant year of income. Benefits arising from any superannuation fund would normally come from contributions to the fund and income and accretions thereon. There was no suggestion that the income of $35,236 credited for the period 1 July 1991 to date of payout did not, at least in part, arise from the balance of Mr Yate’s account in the fund at 1 July 1991 which, in turn, arose from employer contributions plus earnings in prior years. It may well be that some part of the earnings related to the personal contribution of Mr Yates which was in the fund for some 29 days but there was no indication or likelihood that this contribution generated the whole of the $35,236 credited. As a consequence, it is difficult to see, on the plain wording of section 82AAS(2)(b), that benefits attributable to the year ended 30 June 1992 consisting of the whole or part of that $35,236 could be said as being other than from moneys not representing Mr Yates’ contribution or income or accretions arising from that contribution.

11. The applicant’s case appeared to be based on the word “benefits” in section 82AAS(2) meaning contributions and allowing Mr Yates to be an eligible person in a year in which no contributions other than his own were made or could be expected to be made. Some reliance was placed on a ruling by the respondent, IT19, released on 15 December 1983. Paragraph 6 of that ruling stated:

“It is noted in this context that the existence of an accumulation for the benefit of a taxpayer held in an employer-supported superannuation fund which relates to contributions for an earlier year of income or earlier years of income does not constitute the provision of superannuation benefits by another person attributable to a later year of income. Consequently, such an accumulation (including current earnings on it) would not render the taxpayer ineligible for deductions under section 82 AAT in respect of personal contribution made in that subsequent year or years.”

There are two things which must be said about this ruling. Firstly, it was issued well prior to the enactment of Part IVAAA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 and is not capable of being a “public ruling” with the benefits of section 170BA of the Act. Secondly, it was issued in the context of a discretion then available to the respondent under section 82AAS(3) to deem an otherwise non eligible person to be an eligible person in relation to a year of income. This discretion was removed by Act No. 135 of 1990 applicable to contributions made on or after 1 July 1990. I am unable to see how this then non-binding expression of the respondent’s view in the context of exercising a previously available discretion can assist in this case.

12. It is relevant to note, also, that Act No. 208 of 1992 amended section 82AAS(2)(b) by inserting paragraph (D) to exclude from disqualifying benefits:

“(D) income or accretions arising from contributions made to a superannuation fund in relation to the relevant person by a person other than the relevant person during an earlier year of income, where there is no reasonable likelihood that any such contributions will be made at any time after the beginning of the first-mentioned year of income.”

This amendment applied first to the year of income ended 30 June 1993 and was not applicable in the year with which this matter is concerned. Mr Rosebaum submitted that this amendment simply made clear the original intention of section 82AAS(2) and that the section should be interpreted as if this exclusion was always there. He noted that the Explanatory Memorandum to the amending Bill stated (at page 38):

“A further amendment is necessary because income and accretions on employer superannuation contributions already made are currently within the meaning of superannuation benefits. Therefore, former employees and employees who no longer receive employer superannuation support may not be eligible persons if they have accrued superannuation benefits attributable to previous employer contributions. The proposed amendments ensure that these people come within the definition of eligible person if they are genuinely unsupported.”

and (at page 44):

“Former employees and employees no longer receiving active employer superannuation support may be disentitled from eligible person status by income or accretions arising from previous employer superannuation contributions. That is because, although such income or accretions does not come within subparagraph 82AAS(2)(b)(i) as being benefits attributable to superannuation contributions, the income or accretions does come within the terms of subparagraph 82AAS(2)(b)(ii) (Case S72 ATC 523).”

Comfort was sought from the use of the words “may not be eligible persons” and “may be disentitled from eligible person status”. However, I am unable to see that comfort is provided. Given the opening positive sentence on page 38 and the second quoted sentence from page 44, the use of the word “may” is more likely to have the meaning of “will” and the somewhat less forceful word has been used solely to allow for other possible reasons for non eligibility. In any event, it is unnecessary to consider extrinsic material in ascertaining the meaning of the provisions as they were in relation to the year ended 30 June 1992. As indicated earlier, the words are clear. If any part of the benefit attributable to that year would be (and, in this case, was) paid out of moneys not representing the personal contribution or income or accretions arising therefrom, the person was not an eligible person. Here the benefit representing the income on the balance of the fund prior to the personal contribution was attributable to the relevant year of income and would appear to satisfy section 82AAS(2)(b)(i) and certainly satisfied paragraph (ii) so that Mr Yates was not an eligible person in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1992.

13. Having made the finding that Mr Yates was not an eligible person and a deduction is not available under section 82AAT, it is unnecessary to deal in detail with the respondent’s alternative submission that the contribution was not for the purpose of obtaining superannuation benefits. However, I am of the view that this argument cannot succeed. Initially, the respondent had indicated that the contribution could be attacked pursuant to Part IVA of the Act. Prior to and at the hearing, the respondent ceased to argue that Part IVA applied and sought to rely solely on the words of section 82AAT. It is clear that section 82AAT has been included in the Act to allow deductibility for contributions by what are frequently described as unsupported persons. “Superannuation benefits” are defined in section 6 of the Act to mean “individual personal benefits, pensions or retiring allowances”. It is difficult to see that the personal contribution can be said to have been for any other purpose than obtaining such benefits, albeit a short time thereafter. The simple matter is that section 82AAT allows a deduction if the contribution is made to produce such benefits provided the taxpayer is an eligible person. The fact that concessional treatment of the contribution and the benefits is provided under the Act is a consequence and intention of the Act itself. The section does not, in my view, contemplate subjective motives. If such motives are sought to be relied upon to defeat a deduction available under the Act, then Part IVA may be a weapon in the respondent’s armory to be used as contemplated by those provisions. However, if not used, I am unable to see that deductibility under section 82AAT can be based on whether the contribution is 29 days, 290 days or 2900 days prior to retirement.

14. At the time of objecting to the assessment, the applicant also contested the calculation of the assessment on the termination payment. At the hearing it was conceded that, if the applicant was unsuccessful in the deduction issue, the respondent’s calculations were accepted. In view of my findings, the decision under review should be affirmed.
