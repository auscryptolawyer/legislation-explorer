REASONS FOR JUDGMENT

FEUTRILL J:

Introduction

  1. This is an appeal from a decision of a Deputy President of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on review of a reviewable objection decision of the applicant (Commissioner) under s 14ZZ(1)(a) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth). The respondent (taxpayer) had lodged income tax returns for the financial years ended 30 June 2014 and 30 June 2015 and the Commissioner had made assessments of the taxpayer’s income tax for those years under s 166 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth). After an audit, the Commissioner had made amended assessments of income taxation for the taxpayer for the relevant taxation periods under s 167 of the 1936 Assessment Act.
  2. As a consequence of the amended assessments, the Commissioner also made assessments of administrative penalties under Subdiv 284-B, Pt 4-25, Ch 4 of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act. These assessments were made on the ground that the taxpayer’s tax returns for the relevant years made statements about the taxpayer’s taxable income in those years which were false or misleading in a material particular and the amount of the taxpayer’s tax-related liability for those years was less than it would be if the statement were not false or misleading (shortfall amount): s 284-75(1)(b) and s 284-80(1). The Commissioner made assessments of the shortfall penalty on the basis that the base penalty amount was 50% of the shortfall amount because the shortfall amount resulted from recklessness by the taxpayer or his agent as to the operation of a taxation law: s 284-85, s 284-90(1) Item 2 of Sch 1. The Commissioner made the assessments of shortfall penalty under s 298-30(1) of Sch 1. (In these reasons, all references to Chapters, Parts, Divisions, Subdivisions, Sections or Schedules are references to the 1953 Administration Act unless otherwise stated.)
  3. As a person dissatisfied with an amended assessment under s 167 of the 1936 Assessment Act and assessment of penalty under s 298-30(1) of Sch 1, the taxpayer was entitled to make and made an objection in the manner set out in Pt IVC to the assessments under s 175A(1) of the 1936 Assessment Act: s 298-30(2) of Sch 1. Upon an objection the Commissioner must decide whether to allow a taxation objection in whole or in part or disallow it: s 14ZY(1). A decision of the Commissioner on a taxation objection is referred to as an ‘objection decision’: s 14ZY(2). In this case, the Commissioner made objection decisions on the taxpayer’s taxation objections and these decisions were ‘reviewable objection decisions’ for the purposes of s 14ZZ(1)(a): s 14ZQ. The reviewable objection decision the Commissioner made was to disallow the taxpayer’s taxation objections.
  4. In the review proceeding in the Tribunal the taxpayer had the burden of proving that the Commissioner’s assessments of income tax for the relevant years were excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the amount of the assessments should have been: s 14ZZK(b)(i). The Tribunal was not satisfied that the taxpayer had discharged that burden. The taxpayer also had the burden of proving that the Commissioner’s assessments of shortfall penalty were wrong. The issues in this appeal centre on the manner in which the Tribunal addressed that burden of proof. The Tribunal concluded that the taxpayer was required to prove that the Commissioner’s ‘taxation decision’ to impose the penalties ‘should not have been made or should have been made differently’ under s 14ZZK(b)(ii) and the taxpayer had discharged that burden. The Tribunal also concluded that the taxpayer had discharged the burden of proving that the assessments of shortfall penalty were excessive or otherwise incorrect and that there should have been no penalty under s 14ZZK(b)(i). The Commissioner contends those conclusions were erroneous.
  5. The Commissioner’s amended notice of appeal identifies 12 questions of law and six grounds for the purposes of s 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) and r 33.12 of the Federal Court Rules 2011 (Cth). Relevantly, the questions of law and grounds upon which the Commissioner relies may be summarised and restated as the following issues and contentions.
(1) Was the Tribunal’s conclusion (at T [82]-[85]) that the taxpayer’s burden of proof in the review of the taxation objection is that described in s 14ZZK(b)(ii) an error of law? The Commissioner contends that on the proper construction of the 1953 Administration Act the applicable burden of proof is that described in s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the 1953 Administration Act: Questions 1.1 and 2.1; Grounds 1 and 5.

(2) Was the Tribunal’s consideration of the Commissioner’s reasons for assessing the taxpayer’s conduct as reckless (at T [86]-[91]) and conclusion that the taxpayer had proved that the Commissioner’s assessment should not have been made or should have been made differently (T [92]) demonstrative of a misapplication or misconstruction of the taxpayer’s burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b) of the 1953 Administration Act? The Commissioner contends that the taxpayer had the burden of proving that his conduct was not reckless, the Commissioner’s reasons for his assessment were not a relevant consideration, and the relevant considerations were the grounds stated in the taxpayer’s taxation objection, which the Tribunal failed to consider: Questions 1.4, 2.2 and 2.4; Ground 2.

(3) Was the Tribunal’s conclusion (at T [93]) that the taxpayer had proved that the Commissioner’s assessments of penalty were excessive or otherwise incorrect and that the assessments should have been nil an error of law? The Commissioner contends that on the facts as found and the stated grounds in the taxpayer’s taxation objection, the Tribunal was bound to conclude that the taxpayer had not discharged his burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the 1953 Administration Act: Questions 1.1, 1.2, 2.4 and 2.5; Ground 3.

(4) Was the Tribunal’s consideration of the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(i) and s 14ZZK(b)(ii) (T [82]-[93]) demonstrative of an error of law in that the Tribunal had regard to grounds that were not stated in the taxpayer’s taxation objection and issues not raised in the review proceeding? The Commissioner contends that in reaching the conclusion that the taxpayer had discharged the burden of proving that the assessments of penalty should have been nil it is implicit that the Tribunal must have considered exceptions to the penalties imposed under s 284-75(1) of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act that were not stated in the taxpayer’s taxation objection. The Commissioner contends that the taxpayer was limited to the stated grounds because the Tribunal had not otherwise ordered pursuant to s 14ZZK(a). The Commissioner also contends that it was common ground between the parties that the applicable burden of proof was that described in s 14ZZK(b)(i) and not that described in s 14ZZK(b)(ii). The Commissioner contends that the Tribunal failed to accord him procedural fairness in dealing with grounds not stated in the taxpayers taxation objection with respect to s 14ZZK(b)(i) and applying the burden of proof in s 14ZZK(b)(ii): Questions 1.2, 3.1 and 3.2; Grounds 4 and 6.

  1. The Commissioner also raised a further issue to the effect that the Tribunal erred in law in requiring (T [88]-[89]) a direct temporal and (or) causative relationship between the taxpayer’s recklessness and the false or misleading statement: Questions 1.3, 1.5 and 2.3. These questions were neither addressed in the Commissioner’s written submissions nor developed in his oral submissions to the extent that the Commissioner’s asserted questions of law and grounds emerged with any clarity. As best as can be discerned, it appears to be linked to the Commissioner’s contention that the Tribunal misconstrued s 284-75 and 284-90 of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act in concluding that the Commissioner had imposed shortfall penalties and that there were statutory preconditions to the imposition of the penalties whereas, on the proper construction of the 1953 Administration Act, shortfall penalties arise automatically on the facts as decided and the admissions of the taxpayer and his taxation agent: Ground 5. As this issue was not fully developed or pursued and it not possible to discern the nature of the asserted error of law or ground for it from the amended notice of appeal, it is not addressed in these reasons and has been disregarded.

Legislative framework

  1. Part IV of the 1936 Assessment Act makes provision for taxpayers to give the Commissioner tax returns for a year of income. Section 166 provides, relevantly, that from the returns, and from other information in the Commissioner’s possession, the Commissioner must make an assessment of the amount of the taxable income of any taxpayer and the amount of the tax payable. Section 167 provides, relevantly, that, if the Commissioner is not satisfied with the return furnished by a taxpayer, the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his or her judgment income tax ought to be levied, and that amount shall be the taxable income of that person for the purposes of s 166. The Commissioner may amend assessments in certain circumstances: ss 170, 170A. A taxpayer who is dissatisfied with an assessment made in relation to the taxpayer may object against it in the manner set out in Pt IVC of the 1953 Administration Act: s 175A(1) of the 1936 Assessment Act.
  2. Chapter 4 Div 284 of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act makes provision for taxpayers to become liable to pay administrative penalties in circumstances that involve making false or misleading statements, taking a position that is not reasonably arguable and entering into schemes. Section 3AA(1) of the 1953 Administration Act gives Sch 1 effect as a taxation law.

  1. Chapter 4 of Div 298 of Sch 1 has machinery provisions for penalties. Subdivision 298-A applies if an administrative penalty is imposed on a taxpayer by, relevantly, s 284-75(1): s 298-5(a). The Commissioner must give written notice to the taxpayer of the liability to pay the penalty and of the reasons why the taxpayer is liable to pay the penalty unless the Commissioner decides to remit all of the penalty: s 298-10. The Commissioner may remit all or part of the penalty: s 298-20(1). Otherwise, the Commissioner must make an assessment of an amount of an administrative penalty under Div 284: s 298-30(1). A taxpayer that is dissatisfied with such an assessment may object against it in the manner set out in Pt IVC of the 1953 Administration Act.
  2. Part IVC makes provision for taxation objections, reviews and appeals. Part IVC applies if a provision of an Act, such as s 175A of the 1936 Assessment Act or s 298-30(2) of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act, provides that a person who is dissatisfied with an assessment, determination, notice or decision, or with a failure to make a private ruling, may object against it in the manner set out in that Part: s 14ZL(1).
  3. Section 14ZU provides (notes omitted):
14ZU How taxation objections are to be made
A person making a taxation objection must:
(a) make it in the approved form; and

(b) lodge it with the Commissioner within the period set out in section 14ZW; and

(c) state in it, fully and in detail, the grounds that the person relies on.

  1. Relevantly, the Commissioner must decide whether to allow a taxation objection wholly or in part or disallow it: s 14ZY(1). Such a decision of the Commissioner is an ‘objection decision’: s 14ZY(2). Subject to an exception that is not relevant, an objection decision is also a ‘reviewable objection decision’: s 14ZQ. At the relevant time, Pt IVC Div 4 made provision for the AAT Act to apply to the review of a reviewable objection decision: s 14ZZA. With respect to a review of the Tribunal, s 14ZZK provided:
14ZZK Grounds of objection and burden of proof
On an application for review of a reviewable objection decision:
(a) the applicant is, unless the [Administrative Appeals] Tribunal orders otherwise, limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates; and

(b) the applicant has the burden of proving:

(i) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment—that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been; or

(ii) in any other case—that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently.

Part IVC Div 5 made provision for an alternative process of appeal against an objection decision to this Court.
  1. Separately, where the Tribunal made a decision on review of a reviewable objection decision, there was a right of appeal to this Court from that decision on a question of law under s 44(1) of the AAT Act. The AAT Act was repealed by Sch 17 of the Administrative Review Tribunal (Consequential and Transitional Provisions No 1) Act 2024 (Cth). The Tribunal has been abolished and replaced by the Administrative Review Tribunal established under s 8 of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth). However, where there were proceedings in the Court relating to a decision made by the Tribunal that were not finalised before the ART Act commenced anything the Court could have done in relation to the Tribunal before commencement of the ART Act may be done in relation to the Administrative Review Tribunal: Item 25 of Sch 16 to the Administrative Review Tribunal (Consequential and Transitional Provisions No 1) Act. Thus, in effect, the right of appeal from decisions made by the Tribunal has been preserved. Further, as explained later, the power of the Court to make appropriate orders by reason of its decision on an appeal under s 44(4) of the AAT Act is also, in effect, preserved.

Background

  1. The taxpayer was the beneficiary of the Rong Family Trust. He was the sole director of Austin Computers Pty Ltd which was the corporate trustee of that trust. In the relevant years the taxpayer derived income from distributions from the trustee of the Rong Family Trust. The taxpayer lodged income tax returns and amended income tax returns for the financial years ended 30 June 2014 and 30 June 2015 that included income received from the trustee: T [4]-[9].
  2. The Commissioner conducted an audit of the Rong Family Trust after which an amended notice of assessment was issued together with a notice of assessment of a shortfall penalty. The foundation for the amended assessment and penalty was that the trust had made supplies of imported goods which were subject to goods and services tax under s 9-5 of the A New Tax System (Goods and Services Tax) Act 1999 (Cth), the trust was not entitled to claim GST credits on those transactions and the trust had not reported the correct amount of business income in its income tax returns and business activity statements for the period 1 July 2013 to 30 June 2015. The Rong Family Trust lodged an objection to that assessment. The Commissioner made a decision that disallowed most of the objection. The trustee of the Rong Family Trust made no appeal from that decision: T [10]-[16].
  3. The Commissioner then conducted an audit of the taxpayer after which the taxpayer was issued with amended notices of assessment for the 2014 and 2015 financial years under s 167 of the 1936 Assessment Act and notices of shortfall penalties for each of those years under s 298-10 and 298-30(1) of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act. The foundation for the amended assessments and penalties was that the taxpayer was the sole director of Austin Computers, and that company, as trustee of the Rong Family Trust, had lodged incorrect tax returns significantly understating its income. Further, the Commissioner contended as the taxpayer had not declared the correct amount of income as trust distributions (as a percentage of the correct trust income) in his tax returns, the taxpayer had made a false or misleading statement. The applicable penalty was $1,436,043.85 (50% of the shortfall amount) for recklessness: T [17]-[21]. The taxpayer lodged an objection to the Commissioner’s decision which was disallowed and he then applied to the Tribunal for review of that reviewable objection decision: T [21]-[24].

Tribunal decision

Issues before the Tribunal

  1. In the taxpayer’s taxation objection he objected to the notices of assessment for the income tax periods 1 July 2013 to 30 June 2014 and 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2015 and the notices of assessment of shortfall penalty for those periods. The taxpayer objected to the notices of assessment of income tax on a number of stated grounds by which he contended that the assessments were excessive, the Commissioner’s methodology of assessment was flawed and that the trustee of the Rong Family Trust had correctly reported its income in the relevant income tax periods. Regarding the assessments of shortfall penalty, the taxpayer objected on the basis that the assessments were excessive and should be reduced to nil. The taxpayer contended that he had not made a statement to the Commissioner that was false or misleading in a material particular because he had correctly reported his taxable income in each of the relevant tax returns. He also contended that, if he had made a false or misleading statement, he had not been reckless, in substance, because the Commissioner had applied a flawed analysis of the trustee’s tax records, applied an erroneous benchmark, and had taken into account certain irrelevant third-party transactions. He contended no other part of s 284-75 of Sch 1 or any other part of the 1953 Administration Act or any other Commonwealth legislation applied to make him liable to a penalty and that the shortfall penalties should be remitted to nil under s 298-20 of Sch 1.
  2. The Tribunal set out and summarised the parties’ statements of facts, issues and contentions and opening written submissions: T [36]-[40]. The taxpayer’s grounds of objection were not stated in more expansive terms than those stated in his taxation objection and there is no suggestion in the Tribunal’s reasons that an order was made to the effect that the taxpayer was not limited to those grounds. Nor was there any evidence before the Court to the effect that the Tribunal had made an order to the effect that Mr Huang was not ‘limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the [reviewable objection] decision relates’ for the purposes of s 14ZZK(a).
  3. The Tribunal set out and summarised the evidence and the parties’ closing submissions: T [45]-[53]. The Tribunal considered the operation of s 14ZZK(b)(i) (T [54]-[62]). The Tribunal then considered the parties’ submissions regarding the operation of that provision in the context of the Commissioner’s assessments of the taxpayer’s income tax (T [63]-[67]). It concluded that the issue before the Tribunal was not whether the methodology adopted by the Commissioner to calculate the amount of the default assessments was flawed or potentially inappropriate, but whether the taxpayer bore the onus of proving that the Commissioner’s assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been (T [66]).

Assessments of income tax

  1. The Tribunal next considered if the taxpayer had discharged his burden of proving that the Commissioner’s assessments of income tax were excessive or otherwise incorrect (T [68]-[73]). In substance, the evidence the taxpayer presented to the Tribunal was directed to undermining the methodology the Commissioner had adopted for his assessment of the taxpayer’s income tax in the relevant taxation periods. The Commissioner had relied on certain cost of sale of goods benchmarking data. The taxpayer sought to demonstrate that the benchmarking data were inapposite to the trust business for various reasons. The taxpayer also sought to demonstrate through the evidence of the trustee’s accountant (Mr Hollyock) that certain spreadsheets that Mr Hollyock had prepared and provided to the Commissioner correctly reflected the trust income for the relevant taxation years and were consistent with the trust’s income tax returns (T [68]).
  2. The taxpayer presented direct evidence regarding the manner in which sales were recorded during the relevant period. The effect of that evidence was that the Rong Family Trust operated a business of retailing and wholesaling computers, computer hardware and computer accessories from stores at seven physical locations in suburbs of Perth and through a web-based online store. A store in Osborne Park was sold to a company controlled by the taxpayer’s son in 2015 and from 2016 the trust franchised the physical store locations and became a wholesaler. Before the franchising of the locations, each physical store had a point-of-sale (POS) system. Each day the trustee received a statement from its bank for each store indicating sales through the POS system. Sales data was manually entered into the trust’s accounting software records (MYOB system) by a bookkeeper (Ms Yan). Invoices and bank statements were periodically sent to the Bibra Lake store for storage. The franchisee of the Bibra Lake store and tenant of a Midland store disposed of hard copy sales records for the relevant financial years some time after 2016 and before the audit. Electronic data was backed-up every 30 days, but that data was no longer available from the cloud server used to back it up (T [46]). The Tribunal characterised the evidence regarding the loss of the hard copy records as ‘not disputed’ (T [88]).
  3. There was also direct evidence from Mr Hollyock to the effect that he prepared tax returns based on MYOB records and Digital Assist software which detailed the stock held at each store. The Digital Assist software and MYOB were not integrated which meant that the bookkeeper also had to manually enter information for Digital Assist into the MYOB records. Mr Hollyock gave evidence that to verify the MYOB records access to the hardcopy records that were no longer available would be necessary. He also gave evidence to the effect that bank statements were reconciled on a weekly basis and that if the bookkeeper had made an error with data entry the bank statements would not have reconciled, but cash sales could not be reconciled in that manner. Further, he had no reason to believe the MYOB records were inaccurate (T [48]-[51]).
  4. Notwithstanding the evidence concerning the manner in which sales were recorded in the MYOB records, the Tribunal was not satisfied that the taxpayer had discharged his onus of proving that the assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect. The Tribunal reasoned:
    1. Mr Hollyock’s statement and those of the [taxpayer] described how the RFT sales and other data was collected and recorded. The process described by the [taxpayer] and Mr Hollyock involved the manual transfer of certain data into the MYOB system. The financial statements and tax returns were prepared based on the MYOB financial statements prepared by the RFT bookkeeper, and debtor and stock reports. However, Mr Hollyock, who prepared the tax returns and financial statements from 2000-2017, did not verify the MYOB records with cash sales records from Digital Assist or original invoices. The person responsible for inputting that data into the MYOB system was not called to give evidence. There is no direct evidence to establish that the data transfer from the POS system into the MYOB system was undertaken accurately.
    2. Most significantly, however, the [taxpayer] is unable to produce the source records which contain the data which would verify the figures apparently used by [the [taxpayer] and Mr Hollyock or which would establish RFT’s actual income. Pursuant to s 262A of the ITAA 36 (see [35] above) it is the obligation of the RFT/the [taxpayer] to keep those records. The reason that the Commissioner had to issue a default assessment under s 167 of the ITAA 36 (see [32] above) was because of the [taxpayer’s] failure to keep the records required by s 262A.
    3. As noted above, at most the [taxpayer] raised an assertion that RFT trading history showed that the COG/sales ratios were different to that in the Benchmark used by the Commissioner in making the default assessment. There are several things to note about that argument. Firstly, there was no independent or documentary evidence establishing RFT’s historical profit or, for the purposes of distinction, the data used in establishing the Benchmark, or at least none to which I was taken. Secondly, even if there were, that would not discharge the [taxpayer’s] onus of establishing that the default assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect. As Charlesworth J put it in McPartland, “the burden of showing that an assessment is excessive could not be discharged by pointing to some error of fact or law affecting the methodology of the assessment” (see [60] above). At the highest, the [taxpayer] argues that the methodology used by the Commissioner in calculating the default assessments was inappropriate.
    4. Similarly, even if I were to accept the [taxpayer’s] evidence about the cash transactions and “suspicious activity” in the bank accounts included in the default assessments, that would not establish that the default assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect. It would again simply be an attack on the methodology employed by the Commissioner in making the default assessments, default assessments which were necessitated by the [taxpayer’s] failure to keep records as required by s 262A of the ITAA 36. In any event, I do not find the [taxpayer’s] explanations of these transactions particularly convincing. It is not, in my view, sufficient explanation for the [taxpayer], the controller of the RFT and its business, to say that he cannot really explain these transactions which, he says, were associated with his wife’s business. His wife did not provide a statement or give any evidence to support that assertion.
    5. I do not accept that the [taxpayer] has discharged the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(i) to prove that the assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect.

  1. The Tribunal then addressed the issue of the shortfall penalties.
  2.   3. The Tribunal next considered the application of s 14ZZK(b) to the Commissioner’s assessment of the administrative penalties.   4. Regarding s 14ZZK(b)(ii) the Tribunal was satisfied that Mr Huang had proved that the Commissioner’s objection decision in relation to the penalties should not have been made or should have been made differently (T [92]). To reach that conclusion the Tribunal said that the logical starting point was the objection decision and the reasons given by the Commissioner for ‘imposing the penalty under Item 2 of s 284-90 of Sch 1 to the [1953 Administration Act]’ (T [86]).

The Tribunal said that the shortfall amount did not result from the reckless disregard of the taxpayer’s obligation under s 262A of the 1936 Assessment Act. The Tribunal reasoned that the failure to maintain records was not causative of the relevant income tax returns being false or misleading, but caused the taxpayer to be unable to discharge his onus of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(i) regarding the assessments of income tax (T [88]-[89]).

The Commissioner’s second reason for the shortfall penalty was an assertion that the taxpayer had failed to reconcile financial reports, MYOB system and income tax returns with source documents or ensure that the transactions in the bank accounts were verified. The Tribunal did not accept that there was such a failure. I am therefore satisfied that the [taxpayer] has established the two elements of s 14ZZK(b)(i) of TAA and thereby discharged the burden of proof under that subsection in relation to the penalties
  9. On a fair reading, the effect of the Tribunal’s reasons was to express doubt regarding the applicability of s 14ZZK(b)(i) and, in substance, a preference for the application of s 14ZZK(b)(ii) on the basis that the Commissioner’s objection decisions about the administrative penalties were not decisions concerning an ‘assessment’, but rather concerned a ‘determination, notice or decision’. However, the Tribunal also applied s 14ZZK(b)(i) and concluded that the administrative penalties were ‘excessive or otherwise incorrect’ and the assessment should have been that no penalty was applicable.

Was the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(ii) erroneously applied?

  1. It was common ground between the parties in the proceedings before the Tribunal that the taxpayer had the burden of proof described in s 14ZZK(b)(i) with respect to the assessments of income tax and the assessments of shortfall penalty (T [36(c)], [39(a)]).
  2. Although in his written submissions the taxpayer sought to support the Tribunal’s application of s 14ZZK(b)(ii), in his oral submissions, he accepted that the weight of authority favoured the view that the Commissioner’s assessment of the administrative penalties under s 298-30(1) was an ‘assessment’ for the purposes of s 14ZZK(b)(i). Therefore, the taxpayer conceded that he bore the onus of proving that the Commissioner’s assessment of the administrative penalties was excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the administrative penalties should have been. That concession was well made.
  3. The relevant question of statutory interpretation is whether the Commissioner’s reviewable objection decision relating to the assessments of shortfall penalty fall within the description of a ‘taxation decision’ constituting an ‘assessment’ in s 14ZZK(b)(i). The expression ‘assessment’ is not defined in the main provisions of the 1953 Administration Act. That expression is defined in s 6(1) of the 1936 Assessment Act and in s 995-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth). However, for the reasons that follow, the expression ‘assessment’ where used in s 14ZZK(b)(i) does not have the same meaning as that expression in either the 1936 Assessment Act, 1997 Assessment Act or Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act.
  4. Section 3AA provides that Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act has effect, but the meaning of expressions in Sch 1 do not have effect for the purposes of the 1953 Administration Act other than Sch 1 or as provided elsewhere in that Act: s 3AA(1), s 3AA(1A). Further, an expression has the same meaning in Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act as in the 1997 Assessment Act: s 3AA(2). Therefore, the meaning of an expression used in the main provisions of the 1953 Administration Act (such as s 14ZZK) is not necessarily the same meaning of that expression as used in Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act and the 1997 Assessment Act. Likewise, so far as a provision of the 1936 Assessment Act gives an expression a particular meaning, the provision does not also have effect for the purposes of the 1997 Assessment Act or for the purposes of Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act except as provided in the 1997 Assessment Act or the Schedule: s 6(1AA) of the 1936 Assessment Act. Otherwise, the expressions defined in the 1936 Assessment Act, including ‘assessment’ are qualified in s 6(1) by the expression: ‘In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears’. Therefore, it is evident that the legislature did not intend the meaning of ‘assessment’ as defined in s 995-1 of the 1997 Assessment Act or ‘assessment’ as defined in s 6(1) of the 1936 Assessment Act to apply to the general provisions of the 1953 Administration Act, including s 14ZZK(b)(i).
  5. Section 14ZL(1) of the 1953 Administration Act provides that Pt IVC (tax objections, reviews and appeals) applies if a provision of an Act provides that a person who is dissatisfied with an assessment, determination, notice or decision, or with a failure to make a private ruling, may object against it in the manner set out in Pt IVC. Section 14ZQ defines the term ‘taxation decision’ to mean ‘the assessment, determination, notice or decision against which a taxation objection may be, or has been made’. It follows that the expression ‘the taxation decision concerned is an assessment’ in s 14ZZK(b)(i) means ‘the assessment ... against which a taxation objection may be, or has been made’ in accordance with a provision of an Act that provides that a person who is dissatisfied with the assessment may object in the manner set out in Pt IVC.
  6. As already mentioned, a person is liable to an administrative penalty if the person makes a statement to the Commissioner and the statement is false or misleading in a material particular: s 284-75(1) of Sch 1. The amount of the penalty is a percentage of the base penalty amount worked out in accordance with the formula in s 284-85 of Sch 1. The base penalty amount is worked out using the table in s 284-90(1). That is a percentage of the shortfall amount of 25%, 50% or 75% depending upon the culpability of the person.
  7. Division 298 of Sch 1 contains machinery provisions relating to penalties. Section 298-30 of Sch 1 provides:
298-30 Assessment of penalties under Division 284 or section 288-115
(1) The Commissioner must make an assessment of the amount of an administrative penalty under Division 284 or section 288-115.

(2) An entity that is dissatisfied with such an assessment made about the entity may object against it in the manner set out in Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953.
  1. Liability for an administrative penalty and the amount of the penalty is imposed by operation of the 1953 Administration Act. There is no discretion or election exercised by the Commissioner. Under s 298-30 Sch 1, the Commissioner must make an assessment of the penalty. The Commissioner must give written notice to pay a penalty and the penalty then becomes due for payment on the date specified in the notice: Sanctuary Lakes Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2013] FCAFC 50; 212 FCR 483 at [190]- [193] (Greenwood J), [239] (Griffiths J).
  2. Section 995-1 of the 1997 Assessment Act (which applies to Sch 1 of the 1953 Administration Act and the expression ‘assessment’ in s 298-30) provides that in that Act except so far as the contrary intention appears:
assessment
(a) of an *assessable amount, means an ascertainment of the assessable amount; and

(b) in relation to a *tax-related liability not covered by paragraph (a), has the meaning given by a *taxation law that provides for the assessment of the amount of the liability.

(Notes omitted.)
  1. The term ‘assessable amount’ is defined to have the meaning given by s 155-5(2) of Sch 1 and ‘tax-related liability’ is defined to have the meaning given by s 255-1(1) of Sch 1. Section 155-5(2) of Sch 1 sets out a list of items that are each an ‘assessable amount’ and the items are, in turn, defined in s 995-1 of the 1997 Assessment Act. None of the items is relevant to an assessment of the amount of an administrative penalty under s 298-30 of Sch 1.
  2. Section 255-1 of Sch 1 provides:
255-1 Meaning of tax-related liability
(1) A tax-related liability is a pecuniary liability to the Commonwealth arising directly under a *taxation law (including a liability the amount of which is not yet due and payable).
Note 1: See section 250-10 for an index of tax-related liabilities.

Note 2: A taxation law, or a provision of it, may be excluded from being applied to this Part. See section 265-65.
(2) A civil penalty under Division 290 of this Schedule or Part 5 of the Tax Agent Services Act 2009 is not a tax-related liability.
  1. Section 250-10 of Sch 1(2) sets out a table that is ‘an index of each tax-related liability under other Acts’. Item 140 in the table refers to administrative penalties under s 298-15 of Sch 1. Section 298-15 provides that the penalty becomes due for payment on the day specified in the notice.
  2. It follows from these provisions that the Commissioner’s assessment of an administrative penalty under s 298-30(1) of Sch 1 is an ‘assessment’ in relation to a tax-related liability that is not an ‘assessable amount’ within the meaning of s 995-1 of the 1997 Assessment Act, and further, that is an ‘assessment’ against which an objection may be taken in accordance with Pt IVC under s 298-30(2) of Sch 1. That is, an assessment of an administrative penalty under s 298-30(1) of Sch 1 is an assessment to which s 14ZZK(b)(i) applies and s 14ZZK(b)(ii) has no application to the objection decision of the Commissioner regarding the assessment of the administrative penalties in this case. This interpretation of s 14ZZK(b)(i) is consistent with the weight of authority that has considered appeals from decisions of the Tribunal upon reviewable objection decisions involving assessment of administrative penalties.
  3. In Bosanac v Commissioner of Taxation [2018] FCA 946 Steward J (Bosanac FCA) said the following in relation to an objection to the Commissioner’s assessment of the administrative penalty under s 298-30(1) to Sch 1:
    1. In each case, the onus was on the applicant to prove that these penalties were excessive. As Hill and Hely JJ said in Hart v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2003] FCAFC 105; (2003) 131 FCR 203 at 215 at [38]:
The onus is on the appellant to prove that the assessment of additional tax by way of penalty was excessive. The taxpayer could discharge that onus by proof that the factual circumstances which are necessary preconditions to the operation of s 226H, or any other section in Part VII of the Act did not exist: BRK (Bris) Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) (1999) 42 ATR 409.
  1. That observation, made in relation to the former regime for additional tax, applies equally to penalties imposed by Div 284 of Sch 1 of the TAA. That is because of s 14ZZO of the TAA (supra).
  1. Bosanac FCA concerned an appeal to this Court from an objection decision under Pt IVC Div 5 of the 1953 Administration Act. An appeal to this Court is an alternative means of obtaining review of an objection decision. With respect to such an appeal s 14ZZO is in materially the same terms as s 14ZZK. That is, s 14ZZO(b)(i) is in the same terms as s 14ZZK(b)(i) except that it applies to an appeal to this Court by an appellant rather than an application for review of a reviewable objection decision by an applicant. Justice Steward found that the relevant taxpayer had failed to discharge the onus of proving that the assessment of the penalty was excessive: Bosanac FCA at [150]. An appeal to the Full Court was dismissed: Bosanac v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2019] FCAFC 116; 267 FCR 169 (Bosanac FCAFC). Amongst other things the Full Court (Greenwood, Burley and Colvin JJ) (at [128]) said that the relevant taxpayer’s submissions failed to recognise the nature of the task that fell upon him before the primary judge and ‘[i]t was for Mr Bosanac to demonstrate on the fresh hearing before the primary judge that the assessment of penalty was excessive’. The relevant taxpayer applied to the High Court in that Court’s original jurisdiction for writs of certiorari to quash the decisions of the Full Court and Steward J. Justice Nettle dismissed that application: Bosanac v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2019] HCA 41; 93 ALJR 1327 (Bosanac HCA). In so doing his Honour made the following observation:
[14] Granted, s 14ZZO(b)(i) is awkwardly drafted, for, in terms, it applies only “if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment”, and, as has been seen, an objection decision is not an assessment but rather a decision to allow or disallow an objection against an assessment. Plainly, however, the reference in s 14ZZO(b)(i) to “an assessment” is intended to apply in circumstances where “the taxation decision concerned is a taxation objection decision in respect of an objection against an assessment”. That construction alone is consistent with each of the preceding relevant provisions of Pt IVC of the Taxation Administration Act and precisely equates to the effect of the former s 190(b) of the ITAA (from which s 14ZZO(b)(i) was derived [See Australia, House of Representatives, Taxation Laws Amendment Bill (No 3) 1991, Explanatory Memorandum, p 281 [26.40]]). It is also confirmed by s 14ZZQ(1) of the Taxation Administration Act, which provides that:
When the order of the court in relation to the decision becomes final, the Commissioner must, within 60 days, take such action, including amending any assessment or determination concerned, as is necessary to give effect to the decision.

(Emphasis original.)
  1. In Howard v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2012] FCAFC 149; 206 FCR 329 (at [26]) the Full Court (Middleton, Perram and Dodds-Streeton JJ) expressed the view that the taxpayer had the onus of proving that the penalty assessment was excessive citing s 14ZZO(b)(i). With respect to the assessment of a penalty under s 226L of the 1936 Assessment Act, in Commissioner of Taxation v White (No 2) [2010] FCA 942; 117 ALD 335 (at [18]), Gordon J also concluded that the taxpayer bore the onus of showing the imposition of the penalty was excessive under s 14ZZK(b)(i).
  2. In none of Bosanac FCA, Bosanac FCAFC, Bosanac HCA, Howard or White was it doubted that s 14ZZO(b)(i) or s 14ZZK(b)(i) applied to an appeal or review of an assessment of a penalty.

Was there a misapplication or misconstruction of the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)?

  1. Whichever limb of s 14ZZK(b) is applicable, the taxpayer had the burden of proving either the taxation decisions concerning the administrative penalties were excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the taxation decision should have been or that the taxation decision should not have been made or should have been made differently. Therefore, the main issue is whether the Tribunal made an error of law in reaching its conclusion that the taxpayer had discharged his burden of proof. Three of the Commissioner’s grounds of appeal are directed to this issue.
  2. Ground 2 contains a contention that the Tribunal erred in law by finding that the taxpayer had discharged his burden of proof by considering, in effect, whether the reasons for the Commissioner’s assessments of the administrative penalties were sound and made out. Ground 3 contains a contention, in effect, that the Tribunal erred in law because on, the established facts and admissions, no properly instructed Tribunal correctly applying the onus of proof could have reached the conclusion that the taxpayer had discharged his burden of proof. That is, the taxpayer had not proven that the false or misleading statements he made to the Commissioner were not made recklessly and that, otherwise, he had no liability for an administrative penalty. Ground 5 contains a contention that the Tribunal erred in law in misconstruing s 284-75 and s 284-90 of the 1953 Administration Act by concluding that there were statutory preconditions for the imposition of administrative penalties (failure to take reasonable care to comply with, recklessness as to the operation of, or intentional disregard of a taxation law) whereas the administrative penalties apply automatically by operation of the law. These three grounds are interrelated.
  3. The Commissioner submits that error is revealed in the Tribunal’s reasons (at T [84] and T [86]) where references are made to the ‘imposition of penalties’ and the ‘Commissioner imposing the penalty’ under s 284-90 of Sch 1. These expressions were used in the context of the Tribunal’s consideration of s 14ZZK(b)(ii) which, for the reasons already given, was erroneous and, in substance, addressed the reasons for the taxation decisions imposing penalties under s 298-30(1) of Sch 1. In these paragraphs, the Tribunal addressed the question of whether the ‘taxation decisions’ ‘should not have been made or should have been made differently’. That is, the Tribunal was not addressing a ‘taxation decision’ on the footing that the decision concerned an assessment of a liability to pay a penalty imposed by the taxation legislation.
  4. Whether the assessment of the administrative penalty under s 298-30 of Sch 1 be a taxation decision falling within s 14ZZK(b)(i) or s 14ZZK(b)(ii), for the reasons already given, the assessment decision does not involve the Commissioner exercising a discretion or election. Therefore, the relevant question for the purposes of s 14ZZK(b)(ii) or the first limb of s 14ZZK(b)(i) was whether, by operation of s 284-75(1) and s 284-90 of Sch 1, the taxpayer was liable to pay an administrative penalty calculated on a base penalty amount of 50% of the shortfall amount. As the Tribunal had found, in effect, that there were shortfall amounts as a result of false or misleading statements, the taxpayer had the burden of proving that the shortfall amounts or part of those amounts did not result from recklessness by the taxpayer or his agent as to the operation of a taxation law. The correctness of the means by which the Commissioner made his assessments of the shortfall penalty was not the issue. The taxpayer had to demonstrate that the assessment should not have been made or should have been made differently: s 14ZZK(b)(ii); or that the assessment was excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been: s 14ZZK(b)(i): Bosanac FCAFC at [56], [65], [73], [98], [128] (Greenwood, Burley and Colvin JJ). Therefore, the taxpayer had to positively prove the absence of recklessness.
  5. It follows that there is force in the Commissioner’s submission that the Tribunal misconstrued or misapplied s 284-75 and s 284-90 of Sch 1 in considering, in effect, that the Commissioner had made a decision to impose the shortfall penalties rather than make a decision concerning an assessment of the penalties imposed by the legislation. Further there is also force in the Commissioner’s submission that the Tribunal erred when expressing the view that the logical starting point for determining whether the Commissioner’s assessments of the administrative penalties should not have been made or should have been made differently was the Commissioner’s objection decisions and reasons for the decisions (T [86]). There is ample authority for the proposition that there is no onus on the Commissioner to demonstrate the assessments were correctly made and the Commissioner is entitled to rely upon any deficiency in the taxpayer’s proof in seeking to uphold the assessment: Gashi v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2013] FCAFC 30; 209 FCR 301 at [61]- [67] (Bennett, Edmonds and Gordon JJ); Bosanac FCAFC at [56]-[57]; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; 168 CLR 614 at 624 (Mason CJ, Brennan, Deane, Dawson, Gaudron and McHugh JJ agreeing).
  6. In the context of an assessment under s 167 of the 1936 Assessment Act, the taxpayer does not discharge the burden of proving that the assessment was excessive by simply showing that the Commissioner formed a judgment about the amount of taxable income on a wrong basis. The taxpayer must positively prove the amount of the taxpayer’s taxable income: Rigoli v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2014] FCAFC 29; 63 AAR 152 at [12]- [13] (Edmonds, Jessup and McKerracher JJ).
  7. In substance, the taxpayer submits that the Tribunal correctly understood that he had the onus of proving that the false or misleading statements in his tax returns were not reckless or careless and that the Tribunal found on the balance of probabilities that the taxpayer had discharged that onus. While the Tribunal had focussed on the Commissioner’s reasons for assessing the administrative penalties on the basis that the taxpayer’s statements were made recklessly, part of the Tribunal’s consideration of the evidence included the evidence of the taxpayer and Mr Hollyock about the manner in which sales data was collected and recorded in the trustee’s MYOB records and that data was used to prepare the trust financial statements and tax returns that were, in turn, the foundation for the trust distributions and the taxpayer’s false or misleading statements. The taxpayer submits that the Tribunal understood and applied the correct test of recklessness to the facts found on that question (T [90]). Further, on the facts found the Tribunal understood and applied the correct test to the question of carelessness (T [91]). Accordingly, the taxpayer submits no error has been demonstrated in the Tribunal’s conclusions about recklessness or carelessness.
  8. I do not accept the taxpayer’s submissions. For the reasons already given, the Tribunal made no positive finding to the effect that the shortfall amounts did not result from recklessness or a failure to take reasonable care. The Tribunal’s conclusions were that it was not satisfied that the reasons the Commissioner gave for his assessment that the taxpayer was reckless were sound. Also, that even if there had been the reconciliation failure the Commissioner asserted, such a failure did not amount to recklessness or a failure to take reasonable care. The Tribunal made no positive finding to the effect that the taxpayer was not reckless or that he had taken reasonable care. In substance, the Tribunal was satisfied that the Commissioner should not have made the ‘decision’ as to his assessments of shortfall penalty for the reasons the Commissioner gave in his objection decision. But, that was an answer to the wrong question.

Was the Tribunal bound to conclude that the taxpayer had failed to discharge his burden of proof?

  1. The conclusions that I have reached on the first two issues render it unnecessary to deal with the issue of whether the Tribunal was bound to conclude that the taxpayer had failed to discharge his burden of proof. Nonetheless, as it was fully argued and in the event I am wrong on those other issues, I will give brief reasons for my conclusion that the Tribunal was so bound.
  2. First, for the purposes of addressing this issue, it may be accepted that a failure to conclude that the taxpayer had not discharged his onus of proof may be an appeal on a question of law if the facts as found by the Tribunal require a single answer: Hayes v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 21; 96 CLR 47 at 51 (Fullagar J).
  3. Second, on the grounds stated in the taxation objection and statement of facts issues and contentions, once the Tribunal concluded that the taxpayer had not discharged his burden of proving that he had not made material false or misleading statements to the Commissioner, the Tribunal was bound to conclude that the taxpayer had also failed to discharge his burden of proving that the assessments of shortfall penalty were excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessments should have been. The taxpayer’s stated grounds of objection did not permit him to contend what the assessment should have been if the Tribunal were satisfied that the taxpayer was not relevantly reckless. The taxpayer’s stated grounds required the Tribunal to consider whether the taxpayer was not reckless for the reasons given in his taxation objection and, if not, whether the assessments of shortfall penalty should be remitted under s 298-20 of Sch 1.

Was there a failure to accord the Commissioner procedural fairness?

  1. It is also not necessary to address the issue of whether the Tribunal had regard to grounds that were not stated in the taxation objection contrary to s 14ZZK(a) or failed to accord the Commissioner procedural fairness in other respects. But, again, in the event that I am wrong on the other issues, I will briefly give reasons on these issues.
  2. As already mentioned, the Tribunal did not consider grounds or contentions that were not stated in the taxation objection. But, if I am wrong about that conclusion, the Tribunal was in error because it made no express or implied order to expand the stated grounds and the parties did not treat them as so expanded.
  3. For the reasons already given, the Tribunal’s consideration of the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(ii) was erroneous. But, I do not accept that the Tribunal’s consideration of that issue, if it had been correct as a matter of law, was outside the scope of the grounds stated in the taxation objection. As also already mentioned, in the taxation objection the taxpayer raised an alternative ground of objection based on no false or misleading statement by which he contended that he was not reckless. If and to the extent that the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(ii) was applicable, then that ground was sufficient to permit the Tribunal to consider and make findings to the effect that the assessments of shortfall penalty should not have been made on the basis of a base penalty of 50% of the shortfall amounts because the taxpayer was not relevantly reckless. However, for the reasons already given, the Tribunal was confined to dealing with the contentions the taxpayer made in the taxation objection as to the reasons that he was not relevantly reckless.
  4. As also already mentioned, it was common ground between the parties in the Tribunal that the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(i) was applicable. Nonetheless, the Tribunal was bound to apply the law correctly. Therefore, it was not bound to accept the parties’ agreement regarding the applicable law. However, as a matter of procedural fairness, it may have been necessary for the Tribunal to give notice to the parties, in particular the Commissioner, that the Tribunal was considering departing from the parties’ consensual position regarding the applicability of s 14ZZK(b)(i) and the Tribunal was considering applying s 14ZZK(b)(ii) to the facts and circumstances of the case: SZBEL v Minister for Immigration and Multicultural and Indigenous Affairs [2006] HCA 63; 228 CLR 152 at [25]- [43] (Gleeson CJ, Kirby, Hayne, Callinan and Heydon JJ); Commissioner for Australian Capital Territory Revenue v Alphaone Pty Ltd [1994] FCA 293; 49 FCR 576 at 590-592 (Northrop, Miles and French JJ). If the Commissioner could have met the contention that the assessments of shortfall penalty should not have been made or should have been made differently with evidence or submission that could have resulted in a different outcome, the Commissioner may have been denied procedural fairness. However, the Commissioner accepted that he would not have met the legal point with different evidence. No submissions were made regarding what, if any, submission the Commissioner could otherwise have made on the evidence that could have affected the outcome.
  5. I am not persuaded, if the Tribunal was correct to apply s 14ZZK(b)(ii), that there was any practical injustice in the sense that the Commissioner could have made a submissions on the application of that provision to the evidence and facts that could have made a difference: Stead v State Government Insurance Commission [1986] HCA 54; 161 CLR 141 at 144-146 (Mason, Wilson, Brennan, Deane and Dawson JJ). Nonetheless, as s 14ZZK(b)(ii) was not the correct provision to apply, the Commissioner was deprived of the opportunity to make the submissions made in this appeal to the effect that the correct and applicable burden of proof is that described in s 14ZZK(b)(i) and thereby avoid the Tribunal making the relevant error of law. Thus, while there was a failure to accord the Commissioner procedural fairness, the Tribunal’s decision is erroneous and liable to be set aside by reason of error of law.
  6. In short, if the Tribunal correctly applied s 14ZZK(b)(ii), I would not conclude that the Tribunal’s decision should be set aside for a failure to accord the Commissioner procedural fairness. If the Tribunal incorrectly applied s 14ZZK(b)(ii), there is no need to set aside Tribunal’s decision for a failure to accord procedural fairness because the decision should be set aside for the reasons given earlier.

Disposition

  1. For the foregoing reasons, the Tribunal made one or more of the following errors of law.
(1) To the extent that it applied the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(ii), it applied the incorrect burden of proof to the assessments of penalty.

(2) To the extent that it found that the taxpayer was not reckless, it applied the incorrect burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b).

(3) To the extent that it applied the burden of proof under s 14ZZK(b)(i):

(a) on the taxpayer’s stated grounds of objection and the Tribunal’s findings of fact, the Tribunal was bound to conclude that the taxpayer had failed to discharge his burden of proof and it failed to reach that conclusion; or

(b) it implicitly expanded the stated grounds of objection, without notice to the Commissioner, to include a ground that the taxpayer had taken reasonable care and the exception in s 284-75(5) applied and, in so doing, it failed to perform the review under Pt IVC Div 4 of the 1953 Administration Act according to law.

(4) In deciding to apply the burden of proof described in s 14ZZK(b)(ii) the Tribunal failed to accord the Commissioner procedural fairness.

  1. The Commissioner submits, in effect, that the Court should set aside that part of the Tribunal’s decision relating to the assessments of administrative penalty and make an order affirming the Commissioner’s objection decision in respect of the taxpayer’s liability for administrative penalty under s 284-75(1) and Item 2 of s 284-90 of Sch 1. Otherwise, the Commissioner acknowledges that the Tribunal has not determined the merits of the taxpayer’s contention that the administrative penalties should be remitted in whole or in part under s 298-20 of Sch 1 and a decision on that matter should be remitted to the Administrative Review Tribunal in accordance with Item 25 of Sch 16 of the Administrative Review Tribunal (Consequential and Transitional Provisions No 1) Act 2024 (Cth).
  2. Item 25(2) of Sch 16 provides that anything the Court could have done in relation to the AAT before the commencement of the ART Act may be done in relation to the ART. Before the ART Act commenced and the AAT Act was repealed, s 44(4) of the AAT Act conferred a wide discretion on the Court to make ‘such order as thought appropriate by reason of its decision’ on the appeal. There is authority for the proposition that the Court had power under s 44(4) of the AAT Act to make an order setting aside a decision of the Tribunal in part and remitting that part of the decision to the Tribunal for determination according to law: see, e.g., Commissioner of Taxation v Bazzo (No 2) [2024] FCA 560 at [3]- [4] (Colvin J) and the authorities there cited.
  3. Having regard to the discretionary power of the Court under s 44(4) of the AAT Act before commencement of the ART Act and the nature of the errors of law of the Tribunal to which reference has been made, I accept that it is appropriate that an order be made to set aside that part of the Tribunal’s decision regarding review of the Commissioner’s objection decisions on the taxpayer’s taxation objections to the assessments of the shortfall penalty. It is also appropriate that there be an order or declaration to the effect that the taxpayer was required to discharge the burden of proof described in s 14ZZK(b)(i) and, on the facts as found, he failed to discharge the burden of proving that the Commissioner’s assessments of penalty were excessive. However, it is not appropriate to make an order affirming the Commissioner’s decisions on the objections to the assessments of penalty. As mentioned earlier, the grounds of the taxpayer’s taxation objections included that the penalties should be remitted under s 298-20 of Sch 1. The Commissioner’s decision under review by the Tribunal was to not allow the taxpayer’s objection. The Commissioner’s reasons for not allowing the objection included reasons for not exercising his power to remit the penalties in whole or in part. Therefore, it is appropriate to set aside the Tribunal’s decision in part and to remit determination of the remaining issues raised in the review proceeding for determination according to law. I will hear the parties on the precise form of the order for setting aside and remitting the matter to the ART as well as what, if any, directions should be given to the ART regarding determination of the remaining issues including such matters as the constitution of the tribunal, identification of the remaining issues and receipt of further or additional evidence and (or) submissions on those issues.
  4. The taxpayer should pay the Commissioner’s costs of the appeal. The costs should be fixed on a lump-sum basis in accordance with the principles explained in paragraph 4 of the Costs Practice Note (GPN-COSTS). If the parties are not able to agree a lump-sum, determination of that amount will be referred to the Registrar.
