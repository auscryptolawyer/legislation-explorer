ORDER

The objection decision shall be varied by decreasing the assessable income of the applicant for the income tax year ended 30 June 1981 by $1; the differential constituting the return of issued capital.

DECISION

This matter was first heard before his Honour, Mr Justice Hartigan in April 1989. Regrettably, his Honour died before being able to write his decision. In the result, the parties agreed on 15 May 1990 that I should decide the matter from the transcript, subject to the right of either party being at liberty to file any additional material which may since have come to light. In fact, when the matter was re-listed for further argument on 17 September 1990, Mr Searle of learned counsel, who appeared for the applicant both before President Hartigan and before me, sought to tender some additional material which was claimed not to have been "available" at the time the matter was heard before President Hartigan. Mr Fajgenbaum QC who, with Mr G McEwen appeared on behalf of the Crown on both occasions, vigorously opposed the admission of this additional evidence (of which it had prior notice). After submissions from both sides, I admitted this supplemental material unconditionally for reasons which are set out below. I might add that, in the end, it had no effect on the outcome.

2. The applicant/taxpayer ("T") left school at thirteen years of age and has spent much of his working life as a contractor, repairing and maintaining earth-moving equipment.

3. In 1975, T joined forces with one Joe to form the joint enterprise of T (Quarries) Pty Ltd ("Quarries"). Quarries was incorporated on 5 October 1977 and T and Joe were appointed directors on 11 October 1977. Both each originally owned one ordinary share in Quarries.

4. T and Joe had clearly defined responsibilities in Quarries, T being responsible for the carrying out of projects undertaken by Quarries and, in particular, his expertise was used in supervising work-site personnel and ensuring the continued operation of plant and equipment. He played no active role in the financial affairs or administration of the company, spending his time predominantly "on-site", whilst Joe controlled the personnel who were responsible for the company's administration and financial affairs, as well as running the office and doing the paperwork.

5. Quarries' first Company Secretary was a Mr Smith. T deposed that he left the financial affairs of Quarries to Joe, Smith and the accounting firm of Jones and Partners. Significantly, he also entrusted Smith and Jones and Partners with the management of his personal affairs.

6. At an extraordinary meeting of Quarries' two shareholders, allegedly held on 20 May 1981, it was sought to restructure the company in a way which would ultimately facilitate the transfer of Quarries to a Mr Barry Rumpf, a chartered accountant well known for his expertise in minimising the incidence of tax on corporate profits. The minutes record that the two directors/shareholders, i.e. T and Joe, were present at that meeting. Having carefully studied the evidence as it emerges from the transcript, I am satisfied that T (i) was not present at that meeting, (ii) knew nothing about the proposed restructuring of the company's shareholding, and (iii) was unaware that the machinery for a projected "dividend strip" was about to be put in place by Joe, Smith and Rumpf. It follows that the document purporting to be the minutes of this meeting is a false document (and thus a sham), intended to be used for a fraudulent purpose. It is therefore unnecessary to make a further finding whether or not the offence of uttering has been committed, it is sufficient for my purpose that a finding of sham has the effect that the purported "restructuring" of the company, said to have been authorised by the two directors/shareholders, is a nullity. I will deal with this aspect of the case in more detail when I turn to the Crown's argument on "sham". However, in case this matter goes further, and because the Crown placed considerable reliance on sec 260 and Part IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the "Act"), I will deal with both these aspects in some detail.

7. The minutes of this extraordinary meeting of 20 May 1981 ("extraordinary" in more ways than one), stated that "all shareholders being present it was resolved unanimously (sic)that the meeting be held at less than the statutory period of notice." It was then "resolved" that Clause 6 of the Articles of Association be cancelled and replaced with the following words:

"6(a) The capital of the company shall be comprised of

9. A Certificate of Incorporation on Change of Name of Company, lodged with the Corporate Affairs Commission, states that on 17 September 1981, the company Quarries changed its name to Quanom, and a form headed "Particulars And Changes of Particulars In Register of Directors, Principal Executive Officer and Secretaries" was filed with the Corporate Affairs Commission on August 4, 1987, recording, inter alia, that both T and Joe had resigned as directors on 26 May 1981, and that Rumpf was appointed Company Secretary of Quarries on 12 June 1981.

10. The minutes of an extraordinary meeting of shareholders held on 26 May 1981 record that the Articles of Association of the company be altered by substituting for Article 6(a) the following Article:

"6(a) The capital of the Company is $10,000 divided

into 5,000 ordinary shares of one dollar ($1.00) each

($1.00) each.

All shares numbered from 1-5,000 inclusive shall be

11. The minutes of the directors' meeting on 20 May 1981 detail the significant future involvement of Rumpf in the affairs of Quarries (and later Quanom), the extent of which will become evident as the chronology of events is set out below.

12. On 18 November 1980, Quarries executed an agreement with Aluminium (Aust) Ltd ("Aluminium") for contracting services at Longreach. The Longreach project represented the major income earning activity of Quarries.

13. T devoted all his energies to ensuring the successful completion of the Longreach project. In fact, he virtually lived in Longreach for some eighteen months, returning home on Saturday nights, weather permitting.

14. T testified that some time in May 1981, Joe visited the Longreach site and requested him to attend a meeting there and then at the office of Jones and Partners. The evidence suggests that Joe arrived at the site shortly after lunch on the 26th of May 1981, and that the two directors then drove off to a meeting convened at the office of the accountants. I find that the following were present at this meeting: T, Joe, Mr Smith, Mr Jones (from Jones and Partners), as well as Rumpf and a Mr Bolt (an associate of Rumpf).

15. I am satisfied that T at no time initiated the contact with Rumpf nor had personally met him, or even heard of him, before the meeting of 26 May 1981.

16. T's recollection of this meeting is clear: The following was elicited in examination-in-chief:

17. What clearly comes through the transcript is that T is an innocent abroad, a lamb led to the slaughter. Nevertheless, he seems always to stumble on to the right questions. Thus, having been told by Smith that both he and Joe "have got to sell to him (Rumpf)" he says, somewhat philosophically: "Well, all right then, if I agree with Joe to sell to him, who pays the tax bill?". He also conceded in cross-examination that he agreed to transfer control of Quarries to Rumpf upon being satisfied that the latter would pay the company's tax bill. He was likewise well aware that in the year in question, the company had earned a very large income.

18. It was assumed all round that Rumpf secured control of Quarries by the various purported alterations to the Articles of Association, which were, I have concluded, altered for the sole purpose of re-defining the rights attaching to ordinary shares and deferred ordinary shares. Thus T and Joe, by the cancellation of Article 6 of the company's Articles of Association, effected at the extraordinary shareholders "meeting" of 20 May 1981, held ordinary shares in Quarries, as distinct from the newly created class of deferred ordinary shares.

19. The extraordinary "meeting" of shareholders of 26 May 1981, effecting a change to Article 6(a), had the result - or rather, was intended to have the result - that the original shareholders now held deferred ordinary shares, whilst Anderson Nominees held the only ordinary share in Quarries.

20. These, then, were the mechanics by which Rumpf, as the controller of Anderson Nominees, sought to gain control of Quarries; the original shareholders - T and Joe -now only holding deferred ordinary shares with restricted rights (cf Article 6(b)).

21. In the result, T received an amount of $1,452,500, which Mr Searle submitted represents the price paid for his agreeing to vote in favour of a resolution to suppress the rights attaching to the class of shares held by him in favour of the rights attaching to the share held by Anderson Nominees. He further submitted that the sum constitutes a receipt of capital, and is not assessable under either sec 25(1), 26(a) or 44(1) of the Act.

23. Exh 92 is an affidavit by one AB, an officer of the Australian Taxation Office, sworn on 7 October 1987, having been prepared for the purpose of recovery proceedings by the respondent against T. Para 5 of the affidavit states:

"A transfer of control of Quarries occurred between 20

rights attaching to various classes of shares, hereby

made on 3 June 1981 by means of a 'round-robin' of

24. The affidavit records that a cheque for $3,500,000 was drawn by Quarries and made payable to Seventh Wacol Pty Ltd ("Seventh Wacol"). The Commissioner submitted that this sum represents the cashed up value of Quarries, so that the payment had the effect of Quarries being rendered worthless. Mr Searle vigorously denied such an assertion and contended that the sum of $3,500,000 paid by Quarries constituted the consideration for an "annuity" purchased from Seventh Wacol. The vehemence of his protest stands in stark contrast to the lack of any real evidence of the existence of such an annuity. I shall deal with this aspect later.

25. Para 6 of AB's affidavit goes on to state that Seventh Wacol subsequently drew a $3,500,000 cheque in favour of Eighth Wacol Pty Ltd ("Eighth Wacol"), which company then drew a cheque for $3,500,000 in favour of Tenth Wacol Pty Ltd ("Tenth Wacol").

26. Seventh Wacol, Eighth Wacol and Tenth Wacol were all companies controlled by Rumpf. Each of these payments occurred at the CBA Bank in Cloncurry on 3 June 1981.

27. On 3 June 1981, Tenth Wacol made the following payments:

(a) $2,905,000 to T and Joe Excavations Pty Ltd

("Excavations"), the trading name of T and Joe Contractors,

which the Commissioner asserted represents the consideration

to the previous controllers of Quarries for acquisition of

(b) $208,250 to Bolt, which the Commissioner submitted

was his commission for introducing the previous controllers

of Quarries to Rumpf;

(c) $386,750 to Rumpf.

28. The payment to Excavations was disclosed in the books of the T Family Trust as a payment to T, which he then on-lent to Empire Investments Pty Ltd as Trustee of the T Family Trust, which in turn on-lent the amount to Excavations. The loan of $1,452,500 to Excavations is, of course, exactly half of the $2,905,000 paid by Tenth Wacol.

29. In parenthesis, I find it significant that at the "meeting" of directors on 20 May 1981, it was "resolved" that Quarries would cease operations as an earthmoving contractor at the close of business on 26 May 1981 and that the assets be disposed of at the directors' discretion. In fact, what actually occurred was that Quarries sold virtually its entire business at written down value to the related unit trust trading under the name of Excavations.

30. A "Minute of Interview" of 1 October 1984 between Smith and Messrs F and B of the Australian Taxation Office records that the business activities of Quarries had been wound down "prior to June 1980" (I am satisfied that the correct date is 1981). Smith recalled that by June 1981, the only income of Quarries would have been derived from leasing machinery to Excavations. (Quarries continued to lease this machinery and did not transfer the leases to Excavations, as this would have rendered Quarries ineligible for the investment allowance previously claimed).

31. The Minute of Interview also records that the sale of Quarries to Excavations had been contemplated for several weeks preceding the date of sale and that "there was quite a rush to have the accounts finalized at 26 May 1981".

32. Quarries failed to lodge a Company Tax return for the year ended 30 June 1981. In the result, the Commissioner issued a default assessment on Quanom on 30 April 1985 pursuant to sec 167 of the Act, asserting that the the company's taxable income was $3,500,000. Quanom was assessed in the notice of assessment to primary company tax of $1,610,000 (at the rate of 46 cents in the dollar), and additional tax for failure to lodge a return when required by the Act, calculated at 50 per cent of the assessed tax. Late payment penalty pursuant to sec 207 of the Act has since accrued at the rate of 20 percent per annum.

33. In respect of the 1981 income tax year, T was issued with an amended assessment dated 30 April 1985. This assessment increased his assessable income from $54,588 to $1,507,088. The Commissioner considered the increase of $1,452,500 constituted an assessable distribution from Quarries in 1981.

34. T objected against his personal assessment, which the Commissioner disallowed for the following reasons:

(i) that the amount of $1,452,500 has been correctly

included in the taxpayer's assessable income for the year of

income ended 30 June 1981 as income derived by him pursuant

to sub-sec 25(1) of the Act, or is deemed to have been

derived by him by operation of sec 19 of the Act;

(ii) alternatively, the amount of $1,452,500 is

assessable income pursuant to sec 26(a) of the Act;

(iii) alternatively, sec 260 of the Act operates to

void as against the Commissioner the contracts, agreements

or arrangements entered into whereby the income was

purported to have been derived by other persons;

(iv) alternatively, the transactions by which the

other persons purported to have derived or received the

income should be disregarded as being a fiscal nullity;

(v) alternatively, that the sum of $1,452,500 is a

deemed dividend pursuant to secs 108 and 109 of the Act;

(vi) alternatively, the amount of $1,452,500 is

assessable pursuant to sec 44(1) of the Act;

(vii) alternatively, that the transactions by which

other persons purported to have derived or received the

income are a "scheme", and having regard to the operation of

Part IVA of the Act, the income has been correctly assessed

to the taxpayer under the provisions of sections 19, 25(1),

26(a), 44(1), 108 and 109 of the Act; and

(viii) alternatively, that the whole arrangement is a

sham.

35. I propose to deal with the Commissioner's reasons for disallowing T's objection seriatim.

Assessability under secs 25(1), 26(a) and 44(1)

36. Mr Searle submitted that a voting right which attaches to a share is an integral part of that piece of property and may of itself form a real and separate component of the value of the share, and may thus be dealt with separately. He submitted further that the sum of $1,452,500, allegedly received by T, is a capital sum paid by Rumpf in consideration of T relinquishing his voting rights - and thus control of Quarries. Hence, he submitted, the sum is not "income", and therefore not assessable pursuant to the provisions of secs 25(1), 26(a), or 44(1) of the Act.

37. At the hearing, Mr Fajgenbaum, of senior counsel for the Commissioner, spent little time in support of the proposition that the amount of $1,452,500 was assessable under either sec 25(1), 26(a) or 44(1) of the Act. His main argument was that the whole arrangement was void against the Commissioner pursuant to sec 260 of the Act; alternatively was a "sham".

38. Turning to the application of secs 25(1), 26(a) and 44(1), it is merely stating the obvious that companies may have various classes of share capital. Whatever its share structure, a share is merely a collective parcel of rights. The basis for distinguishing between classes of shares is that, typically, different rights attach to the different classes of share capital, for example preferential rights to dividends, or preferential rights to a return of capital on winding up.

39. It follows that if shares in a company are assets of a capital nature, the realisation of the whole of the company's issued capital removes from the proceeds thus realised a tax liability which would have otherwise resulted from, say, a liquidation or dividend distribution; cf per Stephen J in Slutzkin and Ors v F.C. of T. 77 ATC 4076, at p 4081. I am therefore satisfied that, on the instant facts, the receipt by T of $1,452,500 is an affair of capital and not assessable pursuant to secs 25(1), 26(a) or 44(1) of the Act.

40. Mr Searle submitted that the payment to T of $1,452,500 was solely for relinquishing his voting rights in Quarries in favour of Rumpf. I reject such a submission. If anything, this result was merely an incidental consequence of the alteration to Quarries' Articles of Association on 26 May 1981, which was undertaken with the dominant purpose of reorganising the company's share capital so as to transfer control of Quarries to Rumpf. This was achieved by imposing additional restrictions on T's rights as a shareholder; viz "no right to payment of dividend without the consent of a majority of ordinary shareholders, and no right to a return of capital of any amount exceeding in aggregate the paid up value of the share but (he) shall be entitled in the event of a winding up, to be paid in full all returns of capital due in priority to the holders of any other class of share". The payment of $1,452,500 is thus the consideration for agreeing to the restriction on T's rights as a shareholder in Quarries and the receipt in his hands is one of capital; cf F.C. of T. v McArdle 89 ATC 4051.

Does Part IVA apply?

41. Section 177D of the Act provides that Part IVA "applies to any scheme that has been or is entered into after 27 May 1981, and to any scheme that has been or is carried out or commenced to be carried out after that date (other than a scheme that was entered into on or before that date...)" (my italics)

42. Apart from the transfer of funds, which occurred on 3 June 1981, the arrangement, attempting to transfer control of Quarries to Rumpf, was essentially complete on 26 May 1981. I am therefore satisfied that Part IVA does not apply to the instant dispute. If a "scheme" does in fact exist, it was entered into before 27 May 1981, thus precluding the operation of Part IVA.

Section 260

43. Mr Fajgenbaum referred the Tribunal to the decision of F.C. of T. v Gregrhon Investments Pty Ltd and Ors 87 ATC 4958, and submitted that the arrangement in the instant case constitutes a dividend-strip of Quarries' profits and is thus void against the Commissioner pursuant to sec 260 of the Act. Mr Searle, on the other hand, submitted:

(i) that sec 260 has no application as the present case

is governed by Slutzkin (supra), and that Gregrhon is

distinguishable;

(ii) that even if sec 260 did apply, it acts only as an

annihilating provision, and if the Tribunal were to

annihilate this arrangement, there is no transaction exposed

upon which T could be assessed;

(iii) that in any event, there was no dividend strip,

if only because sufficient moneys were retained in Quarries

to enable the company to meet its 1981 tax liability;

(iv) that even if T did receive a distribution from

Quarries, such distribution was not "out of profits", and is

thus not an assessable dividend pursuant to sec 44(1) of the

Act.

44. The evidence records that Messrs Rumpf and Bolt were, between them, to receive a commission fee of $595,000. Mr Searle submitted this sum represents funds retained in Quarries after the alleged dividend strip, which would then be used to meet the tax liability of Quarries and, on the basis of a set of reconstructed financial statements prepared by a Mr Walters, a successor to Smith as Company Secretary, the taxable income of Quarries in 1981 was said to be $805,535.58, and the tax payable thereon $370,546.37. On that view, Mr Searle submitted that the $595,000 left in Quarries was thus more than sufficient to satisfy the tax debt for the income year ended 30 June 1981.

45. Mr Fajgenbaum rejected this reconstruction and submitted that the true taxable income of Quarries was approximately $3,500,000, and not $805,535. The figure of $3,500,000 was derived from a draft set of accounts prepared by Smith, which purportedly presented the financial position of Quarries as at 31 May 1981.

46. The Commissioner clearly had previously expressed some reservations about the accuracy of Smith's draft set of accounts, which showed that Quarries' net income was $3,500,000, since the record of interview of 1 October 1984 reveals that it was mentioned to Smith that $3,500,000 appeared to be a very high return on gross receipts of approximately $10,000,000. Smith did, however, apparently satisfy the Commissioner that his accounts were accurate.

47. My initial task is, therefore, to ascertain the true taxable income of Quarries for the year ended 30 June 1981.

48. Mr Searle vigorously attacked the reliability and accuracy of the draft financial statements prepared by Smith. In particular, he identified three objections to the accounting methodology adopted. These objections were that:

(i) Progress Billing No. 7 of $1,254,123 constitutes work

in progress, and should not have been incorporated into

Quarries' assessable income;

(ii) a "retention fee" of $1,489,962 has been incorrectly

included in Quarries' assessable income; and

(iii) no allowable deduction has been granted for

accrued creditors of Quarries.

49. I propose to deal with each of these submissions in turn.

(A) Progress Billing No. 7

50. By an invoice dated 31 May 1981, Quarries prepared Progress Billing No. 7. The billing records the "net amount due" as $1,254,123.61. Mr Searle submitted, in reliance on F.C. of T. v Australian Gas Light Co and Anor 83 ATC 4800, that as work in progress had not been billed as at 26 May 1981, Quarries had not "derived" assessable income of $1,254,123 at the date the business of Quarries was transferred to Excavations.

51. Mr Clark, the taxation manager of Aluminium, testified as to the billing procedure used on the Longreach project. Mr Clark deposed, albeit by way of hearsay evidence, that an Aluminium engineer would meet with a representative of Quarries "in the last couple of days of each month" and agree upon the amount of work performed during the month. 55. When, for tax purposes, is income "derived"? In Henderson v F.C. of T. 69 ATC 4049, Windeyer J stated:

"Money is not, I think, earned income until it is in

law recoverable as a debt. Of course, if an amount

were recoverable as a quantum meruit although a task

was not completed, it could be said to be earned. And,

as I have said, services may create debts before a bill

is sent to the debtor. But services rendered do not

produce taxable income until they create a debt. And

it must be a debt which is in fact recoverable, not a

bad debt." (at p 4060-4061)

On appeal to the Full High Court (70 ATC 4016), Barwick CJ held:

"When the service is so far performed that according to

the agreement of the parties or in default thereof,

according to the general law, a fee or fees have been

earned, then it or they will be income derived in the

period of time in which it or they have become

recoverable. But until that time has arrived, there

is, in my opinion, no basis, when determining the

income derived in a period, for estimating the value of

the services so far performed but for which payment

cannot properly be demanded, and treating that value as

part of the earnings of the professional practice up to

that time and as part of the income derived in that

period. It may be that a different course can be taken

if an estimation of profits is being made for some

other purpose than the present. Consequently, in

determining the income of the partnership in either of

the years in question for the purposes of assessment of

tax, only accrued fees may be included in that income."

(at p 4020).

In Stapleton v F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4818, Sheppard J noted that:

"The principle which is to be derived from the

judgments in Henderson's case is that a person engaging

in the practice of a profession, such as that of an

accountant or solicitor, does not in ordinary

circumstances become entitled to payment of fees until

the work he or she has been engaged to do is complete.

Until then, there is no debt." (at p 4827).

56. I find the decision in F.C. of T. v Australian Gas Light Co 83 ATC 4800 helpful in the resolution of the issue now before the Tribunal.

In that case, the taxpayers were required to supply a meter

to each domestic user to measure gas consumption. The

supply of gas was on the basis of units which were recorded

by the gas passing through the meter supplied. The tariff

was struck in terms of a price per unit used in the period

of a quarter. The registration of the meter was prima facie

evidence of the quantity of gas used by the consumer. The

quarterly reading of meters - and the sending out of

accounts based on those readings - was staggered over

different days throughout the year. As a result, as at 30

June in any one year, there were vast numbers of domestic

consumers whose current quarter would not have expired and

whose meters would not, therefore, have been read, and who

would not receive accounts until after the close of the

quarter some time in the ensuing financial year. The

taxpayers in that case failed to include in their accounts

for any year ending 30 June an amount in respect of gas

consumed up to 30 June where the relevant meter had not been

read and an account based on that reading issued. The

Commissioner considered that the value of such gas (referred

to as "unbilled gas") was to be regarded as assessable

income of the taxpayers. The taxpayers appealed on the

ground that no account should have been taken of unbilled

gas in determining their assessable income.

At first instance (83 ATC 4220) Lusher J allowed the appeals

on the basis that no debt or obligation on the part of the

consumer to pay for gas used during the relevant quarter

arose until the meter had been read and an account based on

that reading issued. Accordingly, as there was no sale of

unbilled gas in the relevant quarter up to 30 June each

year, the unbilled gas supplied up to 30 June did not result

in the taxpayers deriving any income during the relevant

year assessable pursuant to sec 25(1) of the Act. The

Commissioner's appeal to the Full Federal Court was

dismissed, Bowen CJ, Fisher and Lockhart JJ holding that:

"It is necessary to determine whether the consequence

is that a debt has been created or whether the taxpayer

is obliged to take further steps before becoming

entitled to payment." (at p 4805)

57. In my opinion, an analogy may be drawn between the reading of meters in Australian Gas Light Co and the preparation of work logs recording the units completed in the instant case. The "work log" is the basis for quantifying the amount due to Quarries from Aluminium. The amount due upon an invoice is simply the product of units completed and the respective price per unit completed (less the 15 percent retention fee and any amounts previously billed). The difference between the Australian Gas Light Co case and the one now before the Tribunal is that in the former, the court held that income had not been derived in circumstances where meters had not been read and accounts based on that reading issued, whilst in the instant case, work logs were completed on 26 May 1981, and exh 38 discloses that Aluminium had raised an accrued liability on 28 May 1981 for $1,254,123 in respect of Progress Billing No. 7

58. I am therefore satisfied that a recoverable debt existed at 26 May 1981 in respect of Progress Billing No. 7. It follows that since the billing fee was a recoverable debt at 26 May 1981, Smith had correctly classified Progress Billing No. 7 as derived income in his draft financial statements; cf Henderson's case.

62. 63. 64. It is with no disrespect to these two eminent experts that I prefer the authority of Henderson's case, i.e. whether the retention fee in this case is derived income on 26 May 1981 depends upon whether it is a recoverable debt at that date. In other words, the retention fee is a recoverable debt on 26 May 1981 if - and only if - Quarries was entitled to receive the fee and Aluminium was obliged to pay it.

65. I am furthermore satisfied that until Quarries complies with Articles 14B and 14C of the agreement (and so satisfactorily completes the contract and effects a release of all liens), the company has no legal entitlement to recoup the retention fee. It thus follows from the decision in Australian Gas Light Co that Quarries had not "derived" the retention fee, and that such sum ought not to be included in the company's taxable income.

Conclusion

95. I find on the evidence, that the arrangement whereby control of Quarries was transferred to Rumpf constitutes a profit-strip and was designed to render Quarries unable to meet its 1981 tax liability. Mr Searle's submission that T purchased an annuity from Seventh Wacol (and hence no profit-strip) comes close to the rabbit-in-hat trick. If there was such evidence, I have been unable to find it, save for a single entry in the balance sheet of Quarries prepared by Smith - a key player in effecting this whole arrangement - recording an asset described as "Purchased Annuity - $3,500,000". In these circumstances, I am not prepared to act on such an entry. Furthermore, Mr Searle, having himself attacked the veracity of Smith's financial accounts, can hardly be seen, like the curate and his egg, to "pick and choose" from amongst the entries, and select the one that suits him whilst whilst rejecting the rest. In the result, given the "rubbery" nature of Smith's accounts, I am not prepared to accept that Quarries acquired an annuity from Seventh Wacol without corroborating evidence. In the absence of such corroboration, I am satisfied that this "asset" recorded by Smith was designed to conceal the reality of what actually occurred.

96. I have no doubt that the arrangement whereby T and Joe divested themselves of control in Quarries and transferred its business to Excavations was designed to render Quarries a mere skeleton of its once thriving self, and in consequence unable to meet its impending 1981 tax liability. As in Gregrhon (supra), this transaction was executed by a series of interlocking steps and a circular flow of funds. The payment of monies was effected on 3 June 1981 at the Cloncurry branch of the CBA Bank, Smith being present representing T and Joe. What happened in slow motion is that Seventh Wacol received a bank cheque for $3,500,000 from Quarries, which sum then passed through the accounts of the Wacol group controlled by Rumpf. The end result of these shenanigans was that T received $1,452,500 as his half-share of $2,905,000, which he proceeded to on-lend to Excavations via his family trust. Whilst I draw no adverse inferences against T, for the sake of completeness, it should be noted that Joe followed the same pattern. In other words, the monies were employed by Excavations in its operations, which included the Longreach project it acquired from Quarries.

97. Mr Searle submitted that even if sec 260 were to operate, there remains no transaction upon which T could be assessed. I disagree. Once the arrangement transferring control of Quarries to Rumpf is annihilated, a factual situation is exposed, which involves a liability to tax; viz T's receipt of a distribution which, if paid "out of profits", would be a dividend assessable pursuant to sec 44(1) of the Act. On this view, T (and Joe) each received $1,749,999 out of Quarries' accumulated profits ($3,499,998). Included in this sum is the capital profit of $1,489,962 for the retention fee arising on the transfer of the Longreach project to Excavations. Total shareholders funds as at 26 May 1981 were $3,500,000 before the distribution; the balance of $2 represents the issued capital of T and Joe. Thus, T is prima facie assessable on $1,452,499 being:

$

Share in Profits of Quarries 1,749,999.00

Less: Share of fees paid to Wacol 297,500.00

1,452,499.00

98. This brings me to the decision in Slutzkin (supra) on which heavy reliance was placed by Mr Searle.

99. In that case, Francis Richard Holdings Pty Ltd, a target company, was not carrying on any business at the relevant time and had become redundant. It was the case that no tax problem confronted the company and that it had no difficulty in respect of an impending primary tax liability. All that the taxpayers endeavoured to do was to realize their shares and the assets of the target company in a manner designed to ensure that the tax liability, which would be payable if accumulated profits were distributed by way of dividend or on liquidation, would not be attracted. At the time of sale, all the assets of the target company remained within its control in a bank account. In short, the taxpayers sold their shares in a dormant company in a manner intended to ensure that the realisation itself did not attract a liability to tax. In those circumstances, the High Court held that the sale of the shares did not defeat or frustrate the provisions of the Act and was capable of explanation as an ordinary business dealing.

100. The facts in Slutzkin are a far cry from the facts now before me, where it can hardly be claimed that the transaction was an ordinary business dealing, or that the so-called target company was no longer significant, let alone dormant. What clearly emerged was that both this company and its two shareholders faced significant tax "problems" if a sufficient distribution were made. The profits for the current year, judged by past years, were exceptional and substantial, and Joe, being "streetwise", apparently sought advice on how to avoid the tax consequences of a distribution. Whether Slutzkin (supra) would be decided differently today is of no concern to me. I am bound by the law as it stands. Applying the existing law as I understand it to the facts of this case, I am satisfied that the case is governed by the res decisa of the Full Federal Court in Gregrhon (supra), and that there is nothing in Slutzkin which would lead to a different result.

101. As a final crie de coeur, Mr Searle submitted that T was merely an innocent party to the arrangement, a lamb led to the slaughter, and, relying on Stamp v F.C. of T. 88 ATC 4803, he submitted that sec 260 cannot be used against an innocent party, and a stranger to a tax avoidance scheme.

102. Mr Fajgenbaum, on the other hand, submitted that T is not an innocent party, and that even if he were, it can avail him nothing; cf Gregrhon (supra). In any event, Mr Fajgenbaum endeavoured to establish that Smith, who was active in executing the tax avoidance scheme, acted as T's authorised agent.

103. I am satisfied that the evidence established that a relationship of principal and agent existed between T and Smith, and that the latter had actual authority to operate T's bank account, even to paying the milkman and newsagent on T's behalf. We know that Smith attended at the bank on 3 June 1981 when the "round-robin" payment of monies occurred, and that he ensured that T's interests were protected.

104. Given that an agency relationship between T and Smith did exist, T nevertheless denied that he authorized Smith to enter into a tax avoidance arrangement on his behalf. Having been denied the opportunity of observing the witness giving his evidence, I am prepared to assume, for present purposes, that this statement is true. Does it assist T?

105. In Davis and Anor v F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4377, Hill J observed that Pincus J in Stamp (supra) did not state that it is necessary for the taxpayer personally to be a party to a tax avoidance scheme before sec 260 can operate. Hill J stated:

"what his Honour did say was that it was necessary for

the scheme to have the purpose or effect of avoiding

the tax of the taxpayer". (at p 4408)

Hill J concluded that there were two reasons why, on the reasoning of Pincus J, the applicant in Stamp had to succeed:

(i) the arrangement entered into was not entered into to

avoid the applicant's tax; and

(ii) the case involved a trustee and beneficiary, and the

purpose of the arrangement, in part at least, was to avoid

the liability of the beneficiary although the beneficiary

was unaware of the scheme.

106. In Davis - as in this case - the applicant did not actively participate in the scheme, but, because she was the sole beneficiary, it was clear that the purpose of the arrangement was to avoid her tax. Nevertheless, Hill J recognized the need to enquire whether it is necessary that the taxpayer know of, or participate in the scheme before sec 260 operates. Hill J stated:

"In many cases it may well be critical that a taxpayer

either alone or through an agent participate in the

scheme as a whole albeit that it will quite often be

the case that a taxpayer is not conscious of each and

every step in the scheme. In one sense, such a

distinction may well underline the differing results in

Slutzkin on the one hand and F.C. of T. v Gregrhon

Investments Pty Ltd 87 ATC 4988, see e.g. in Gregrhon

per Fisher J. at pp 4996-4997, per Lockhart J. at pp

5007-5008 and per Spender J. at p 5012. However, with

respect to Pincus J, I would be of the view that sec

260 of the Act could apply in a case such as the

present... There is, in the language of sec 260, no

requirement that a taxpayer necessarily be a party to

the scheme provided that it is correct to characterise

the scheme as a whole as one having a purpose or effect

of avoiding the tax of the taxpayer." (at p 4409)

107. In Gregrhon, Fisher J observed:

"... Whether or not there is an arrangement such as is

contemplated by sec 260 is a matter for objective

assessment and the attitudes of the taxpayers are

little if anything to the point. It is after all the

'purpose or effect' of the arrangement and not the

motives and intentions of the taxpayers which

determines the issue. If it were otherwise the law

would place a premium on naivety, ignorance and

calculated abstention from knowledge and understanding.

In any event however all the surrounding circumstances

in this matter were in my opinion such that the

taxpayers, and more particularly their accountant,

whose knowledge and intention may be imputed to the

taxpayers (F.C. of T. v Bidencope 78 ATC 4222 at p 4230

per Gibbs J.) should have been alerted to a scheme of

tax avoidance ......". (at p 4996)

108. In this case, there can be no doubt that Smith, who was the Company Secretary, was a party to the arrangement. By applying the reasoning of Fisher J in Gregrhon (supra) to the circumstances of this case, it follows that Smith's knowledge and intention must be imputed to T, if only because he was both aware of the fact that the company faced a substantial tax liability, and that the various manouveres, which he left to the company's Secretary, had a fiscal purpose. It is not to the point that he was unaware of the particularity by which this result was brought about. In these circumstances, he cannot shield behind "lack of authority", nor can he escape the operation of sec 260. In any event, even if the knowledge of Smith cannot be so imputed to the taxpayer, he is still bound by sec 260, if only because on any objective assessment, the arrangement can only be characterised "as one having a purpose or effect of avoiding the tax of the taxpayer". (per Hill J, Davis (supra) at p 4409).

109. As a last, final and desperate argument, Mr Searle contended that, whilst it is true that sec 260 has operated in dividend stripping cases involving the sale of shares, it should not be applied to a case where there was no sale of shares, but merely an agreement to suppress the rights attaching to such shares. Like the Charge of the Light Brigade, it is a quixotic assault on the enemy but it has no effect on the outcome of the battle.

Sham

114. It was one of the Crown's submissions that this whole arrangement is a "sham".

115. The fons et origo of cases dealing with "sham" is Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786, in which Lord Diplock discussed the nature of a sham in the following passage:

"... it is, I think, necessary to consider what, if

any, legal concept is involved in the use of this

popular and pejorative word. I apprehend that, if it

has any meaning in law, it means acts done or documents

executed by the parties to the 'sham' which are

intended by them to give to third parties or to the

court the appearance of creating between the parties

legal rights and obligations different from the actual

legal rights and obligations (if any) which the parties

intend to create. But one thing, I think, is clear in

legal principle, morality and the authorities, ... that

for acts or documents to be a 'sham', with whatever

legal consequences follow from this, all the parties

thereto must have a common intention that the acts or

documents are not to create the legal rights and

obligations which they give the appearance of

creating." (at p 802)

116. I may say at the outset that an assertion of "sham" is tantamount to an allegation of fraud and requires proof beyond reasonable doubt. A finding of "sham" is unusual in tax avoidance arrangements, if only because the tax advantage sought to be achieved will almost invariably depend upon the parties bringing into existence documents designed "to create the legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating". This case is an exception in that I find that the pivotal document - the so-called "minutes" of 20 May 1981 - is a classic example of a "sham" as defined by Lord Diplock in Snook (supra). I am satisifed beyond all doubt that T, despite the assertion in the "minutes" to the contrary, was not present at this critical meeting and that the "resolution", as recorded in the minutes, viz to cancel and replace clause 6 of Quarries' Articles of Association is a nullity. It goes without saying that a company, consisting of two shareholders, each holding one ordinary share, cannot be restructured by one shareholder in the absence - and without the knowledge - of the other. Put bluntly, the "minutes" record a mythical event and do not create the legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating.

117. Exhibit 54 purports to be a Notice of an extraordinary general meeting of shareholders, to be held "at the company's administration centre, Brighton on Wednesday 20th May, 1981 at 8.00 pm". The minutes of that meeting, allegedly "held at Brighton on Wednesday 20th May, 1981 at 8.00 pm" record that T and Joe were both present. 114. The final document is a minute of a meeting of directors "held at Brighton on Wednesday 20th May, 1981 at 9.00 pm", again noting that both T and Joe were present.

118. The next document is a further Notice of an extraordinary general meeting of shareholders, to be held "at The Esplanade, Brighton (T's residence) on Tuesday 26th May, 1981 at 8 am". The minutes of this meeting record T and Joe as present. (The minutes of all these meetings have already been set out in paragraphs 7 and 8 above). 116. Beginning with the Notice of the extraordinary meeting scheduled for 20 May 1981 at 8 pm. The venue of said meeting was to be "at the company's administration centre, Brighton". (Brighton is the suburb in which T resides). T was adamant that he neither received such Notice, nor that any such meeting took place at his home. Furthermore, he was confident that on the evening of 20 May 1981 he was not at home, but working several hundred kilometres away upon the Longreach project. There is some confusion about the reference to the company's "administration centre". Thus the following occurred in cross-examination:

"MR FAJGENBAUM: I just want to tell you something

119. Again, T's evidence relating to the meeting in the office of Jones and Partners on 26 May 1981 makes it clear that that was the first occasion on which he was made aware that the fuse, leading to the dividend strip, had been lit and that the balloon was about to go up. T comes through the transcript as an honest, albeit unsophisticated and simple person whose evidence I find plausible.

120. Reconstructing the events as best as I am able, I am satisfied that the meeting of 20 May 1981 was called together by a combination of Rumpf, Smith and Joe who, between them, "resolved" that Quarries would cease its business as earthmoving contractors on 26 May 1981, and that the assets of the business be disposed of at the directors' discretion. It goes without saying that such "resolution" by one director/shareholder in the absence of the only other director/shareholder is of no legal effect. This explains T's clearly expressed dismay on learning on 26 May 1981 that Quarries was going to close down and that its business was to be transferred to another company (Excavations) - a dismay only explicable on the basis that that was the first occasion on which he was made aware of what was afoot.

Sec 108 and 109

121. Since I have concluded that the arrangement is a "sham"; alternatively void against the Commissioner pursuant to sec 260 of the Act, it is unnecessary to consider the application of either sec 108 or 109 of the Act to the present case. For the same reason, I do not propose to deal with "fiscal nullity", a defence which by now, one might be forgiven for assuming, the Commissioner should know has been held not to apply to a taxing statute containing its own anti-avoidance provisions. This notwithstanding, it is raised with metronomic regularity ever since it was first enunciated by the House of Lords in WT Ramsay Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioners [1981] UKHL 1; (1982) AC 300.

122. In the result, the objection decision will be varied by decreasing the assessable income of T for the income tax year ended 30 June 1981 by $1, being the differential constituting the return of issued capital.
