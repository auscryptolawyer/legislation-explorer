VT93/214-215 and Commissioner of Taxation [1994] AATA 644; ( 1994) 29 ATR 1178; 94 ATC 476 (11 October 1994)

Last Updated: 18 May 2009



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



Income Tax - Retention payments - lump sum payment under agreement to remain in employ for twelve months - whether income - whether capital excluded from capital gain - application for leave to extend grounds of objection - whether payments were fringe benefits.



Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936

Taxation Administration Act, 1953

Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act, 1986

Case Y53, 91 ATC 464

Hayes v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47

Paykel v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 94 ATC 4176

Dickenson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1957[1958] HCA 62; , 1958) 98 CLR 460

Hepples v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 91 ATC 4808

Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4942

Case 16/94, 94 ATC 194

Gilder v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 91 ATC 5062

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Citibank Ltd & others, 93 ATC 4691

) No. : VT93/214-215

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION



Mr B. H. Pascoe, Member 11 October 1994



1. These are applications by two applicants for review of the decisions of the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow objections lodged against assessments of income tax based on income of the year ended 30 June 1991 in respect of the first applicant (“A”) and the year ended 30 June 1992 in respect of the second applicant (“B”). At the request of the parties the two applications were heard together as the substantive question was the same for both applicants.



2. The dispute related to whether lump sum payments made to the applicants and described as “retention payments” constituted assessable income under the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 as amended (“the Act”).



3. Both A and B were employees of S Ltd which was a subsidiary company of E Ltd. The retention payments were made pursuant to a contract evidenced by a letter of offer from E Ltd dated 26 October 1990 addressed to each of the applicants and, in turn, signed by each applicant accepting the terms and conditions offered. Letters to each were identical and were as follows:-



“As you know, E Ltd (“the Company”) intends to divest itself of all its non-core assets. S Ltd., its subsidiaries and/or their respective assets will, in due course, be sold to a new owner.



Your continued employment, involvement and support are seen by us as vital to the maintenance of the profit levels and health of the business.



The Company therefore offers to make to you a one-off retention payment of (A$94,485, B$108,254) (“the retention payment”). This offer is subject to the following terms and conditions:



  1. That you agree to remain in your current employment with S Ltd. or S Corporation or their successors or assigns (“S”) for a period of twelve months from the effective date of the forthcoming change of ownership of the part or parts of the S business in which you are engaged and to give your full time attention, commitment and energy to the business of S during that time. This requirement operates quite independently of your terms of employment and is not intended to have the effect of converting that employment into one for a fixed term. This offer, if accepted, will give rise to obligations between you and the Company only.

  2. In the event that you resign your employment with S before the completion of twelve months’ service following the change of ownership, you agree to refund to the Company the amount paid to you under this agreement, less an abatement of one-twelfth of the total retention payment paid to you for each completed month of service or part thereof. No such refund will be payable if S terminates your employment or you die or become permanently disabled before the expiry of the 12 month retention period.

  3. That you sign and return to Mr C the attached copy of this letter to signify your acceptance of the terms and conditions set out in this letter.

  4. The retention payment of A$94,485 B$108,254 will be made to you when:

(a) C holds the attached copy of this letter duly signed by you and witnessed; and

(b) the contract of sale of the part or parts of the S business in which you are engaged is for practical purposes completed and the purchase moneys paid”.



4. The applicants were represented by Mr Power and Mr Rosenbaum of Counsel who submitted that the retention payments were not income pursuant to either Section 25 or Section 26(e)of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) but were receipts of a capital nature and, in turn, not subject to tax under Section 160M(7) or any other of the capital gains provisions in Part IIIA of the Act. Alternatively, it was submitted that the amounts constituted fringe benefits under the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (“FBT Act”) and are, therefore, exempt from income tax under Section 232 of the Act and excluded from Section 26(e) of the Act pursuant to paragraph (iv) of that section. It is appropriate to deal with this latter argument later and separately as the question arises as to whether the argument can be considered pursuant to the provisions of Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (“Administration Act”) which limits an applicant to the grounds stated in the objection to which the decision under review relates.



5. Evidence was given by the two applicants, F, who was executive director strategy of E Ltd at the time the agreements were entered into and R who was the legal adviser to E Ltd and who drafted the agreements. From this evidence it was clear that E Ltd saw the retention of the management team as a significant factor in maximising the sale price of S Ltd or any of its divisions. A further factor which appeared relevant in the decision to enter into agreements for the retention payments was that control of S Ltd would be passing to a fifth owner in less than three years. As a result of a succession of take-overs and change of control of holding companies, the Senior Management considered that considerable strains had been imposed upon them in adapting to different corporate cultures and, as a consequence, sought some security and incentive for the future. The background to the payments was summarised by F at the commencement of his evidence when he said:-



“One of the most important assets in that group, one of the most saleable it was considered was S. But frankly, a crucial part of the attractiveness of that business, because it is trading business, was its management because in a trading business a large percentage of the assets are the people. As it was once put to me in a trading business your assets go up and down in the elevator every day. So it was important that we retain the loyalty of those key executives for the period of the sale and for a period beyond the sale. And we were strongly advised by our financial advisers, an investment bank called CF First Boston, that we should make sure that these guys were retained or locked up, I think they said. And how did you go about trying to achieve that?----Well, we obviously realised that they were already we thought quite well paid for their ongoing day-to-day commitment to the business under their normal employment contract but we figured that we had to try and tie them in another way and make a specific and very tangible, attractive payment for that purpose and it was then that we did seek or, in fact, I didn’t personally but then through our personnel management then did seek legal advice as to the best way to set up and enforce that contract with a penalty if it wasn’t honoured.”



6. R, in his evidence, gave the reasons for the agreements being in the form in which they were finally presented and the following extracts from his testimony provide an appropriate summary:-



“And what were your specific instructions in relation to those agreements; do you remember?---My specific instructions were quite simple. I was told simply to devise a method of ensuring that any of these key executives of E Limited subsidiaries would be locked into their employment contracts or their employment relationships with the company for a period which, in most cases, I understand was to be 12 months and that they would not be free to work for competitors of the company during that time.



Was it explained to you what the purpose for that was? Was that part of the instructions?---It was explained to me that the company had been advised that the retention what I suppose one might call human capital, the key executives of these organisations were seen as being essential to obtaining realistic prices for all the non-core assets of E Limited and for that reason the company - when I say the company I mean E Limited - was determined to keep these people, and my job was to find the way of doing it.”



“You have said you are an expert - well, you have said that your expertise is employment law. What were the difficulties - or could you tell the tribunal what the difficulties are in your expertise in drawing up such a document in relation to employees?---There were initially three possible methods that I considered might have achieved the result that I’d been instructed to achieve. The first of these was to amend the employment contracts of the employees by inserting into them a restraint of trade agreement. This was an agreement in terms of which they would not be entitled to work for competitors of the company for a specified period and that period would obviously contemplate a time when they were in employment and a time after they had finalised their employment after they had been terminated or had resigned. I advised the company against using that particular strategy because, in my experience, restraint of trade agreements are notoriously difficult to enforce. That court’s general attitude towards them are that they are void as being contrary to public policy. And I suggested to the company that that was probably the least effective way of achieving the result. The second method that we considered was the making of a payment to the employees at the time when the shareholding of their employer company changed. So they would get a substantial payment when the actual shareholding of the company changed. That was not possible and was rejected for two reasons. The first was that in a case of a listed company like S Limited. When I say listed, I mean listed in Australia. An arrangement like that would be a breach of the Stock Exchange’s listing rule 3J16A, and the other thing was that it would not necessarily ensure that the employee stayed in employment following the change of shareholding. And the company’s intention was that the purchaser of one of these companies should have some reassurance of stability as to senior staff even after an asset sale or an acquisition of the shares of the company. And it was for that reason that we decided after considerable debate to use the form that you find in these letters.”



7. In essence, the argument for the applicants was that the payments were not income under normal concepts and not derived from employment. They were fully renumerated by their employer for their services and there was no employer - employee relationship between the payer and the recipient. The payments, it was said, were capital in that the applicants were effectively restrained under the terms of the agreement from seeking employment elsewhere for a period of twelve months.



8. Not unexpectedly, Mr Sest, for the respondent, argued that the retention payments formed part of the assessable income of the applicants as income under ordinary concepts under Section 25(1), as income under 26(e) or alternatively, assessable as a capital gain pursuant to Section 160M(7) of the Act (as in force at the relevant time). It was argued that the agreements under which the retention payments were made did not involve the forfeiture of any right to seek employment elsewhere but provided an incentive to remain in the current employment in a manner consistent with past service.



9. In many ways this case is somewhat similar to that considered by Dr Gerber in this Tribunal and published in Case Y53, 91 ATC 464. There, one of the questions to be determined was whether a payment of $5,000, described as a retention payment, which was paid to a rugby player to ensure he played for Queensland only was income. It was held that the payment was made principally as an inducement to play in Queensland and bore the “stigma” of income. Even if the emphasis could be put to the restraint nature of the consideration for the payment it could be regarded as capital only if there was a significant detraction of a substantial and enduring pre-existing right. A restraint for only two seasons was not seen in that light.



10. Whilst it was sought to provide evidence as the motive and intention of the parties to the retention agreement, the relevant question is the character of the receipt in the hands of the recipient. As Fullagar J. Said in Hayes v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47 (at p.55):-



“While I would not say that the motive of the donor in making the payment or transfer is, in cases of this type, irrelevant, motive as such will seldom, if ever, in my opinion, be a decisive consideration...

The question in each particular case is as to the character of the receipt in the hands of the recipient: Moorhouse v Dooland (1955) Ch. 284. The test to be applied is an objective, not a subjective test.”



11. Mr Power spent much of his closing submission in seeking to apply the reasoning of Heerey J. In the case of Paykel v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 94 ATC 4176 to the retention payments received by these applicants. In that case the taxpayer and his family owned most of the shares in Paykel Bros which company was acquired by a United States corporation. The taxpayer entered into an agreement by which he agreed to retire as a director and employee and covenanted that he would not divulge any of the company’s confidential information and that for a period of five years thereafter he would not carry on any business in Australia and South East Asia which competed with any business carried on by the companies. In consideration of these restraints it was agreed to pay to the taxpayer his heirs and assigns the sum of $400,000. It was held that the payment was not an assessable capital gain caught by S.160M(7). It was further held that the payment was not income, the covenant was in no way incident to the taxpayer carrying on the income earning activity of managing the Paykel companies and it was a one-off lump sum paid for a restraint for a substantial period. Henry J. Considered that the case fell within the principle of Dickenson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] HCA 62; (1957-1958) 98 CLR 460.



12. In my view the circumstances of these applications can be clearly distinguished from those in Paykel and Dickenson. Here the retention payments were incident to the applicant’s continuing income earning activity as employees of S Ltd. The amounts of the retention payments were calculated by reference to the annual renumeration package of the recipients and it was a condition of the agreement that they remain in their current employment for twelve months. There was no specific restraint on leaving the employ within that period other than a monetary penalty based on any unserved balance of twelve months if they departed voluntarily prior to the expiration of the twelve months. It was, in my view, simply a financial incentive to remain in employment for one further year. There was no cessation of employment and no restraint for any substantial period against competition with the employer or its holding company. To the extend that any element of restraint could be seen as contained within the agreement, the period of twelve months could not be regarded as a significant detraction of a substantial and enduring right so as to regard the receipt as being of a capital nature. In my view the receipts were derived from employment and constituted income under ordinary concepts. I am satisfied that the retention payments were made as an incentive and additional compensation to each of the applicants to remain in employment with S Ltd or its successors for twelve months and, as such, were income under S.25(1) of the Act. Having come to that conclusion it is unnecessary to consider in detail the applicants’ arguments which sought to exclude the receipts from S.26(e). Suffice it to say that I regard the receipts as constituting gratuities, compensation or benefits granted in respect of employment of or services rendered by the applicants. In the case of B, part of his retention payment was at his direction paid to the superannuation fund to discharge a home mortgage. Apart from referring to this factor within the FBT argument, no other argument was directed at seeking to differentiate this payment from the balance of the retention payment. Given the foregoing findings, any such argument would be of little benefit.



13. It is also unnecessary (and fortunate, given the complexities and difficulty of Section 160M(7) as it then was and as demonstrated in the case of Hepples v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4808) to consider the arguments advanced against the receipts being capital gains under Section 160M(7) or any other provisions of Part IIIA of the Act. To the extent that any of these provisions could apply the operation of Section 160ZA(4) would reduce any resultant capital gain to nil.



14. Having made the findings set out above, it is then necessary to consider the final and alternative submission on behalf of the applicants that the payments are exempt from income tax as being fringe benefits within the meaning of the FBT Act. As indicated earlier the initial question which arises in relation to this submission is whether or not it was a ground stated in the objections, the decisions on which are the subject of these applications. Section 14ZZK of the Administration Act provides:-



“On an application for a review of a reviewable objection decision:

(a) the applicant is, unless the AAT orders otherwise, limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates;”



The objections lodged by the applicants were very similar in content. The notice of objection lodged by B was as follows:-



“I, B, object against the Assessment dated 3 March 1993 and contend that the taxable income shown on the Notice of Assessment for the year ending 1992 should be reduced by $108,254.00 being an amount received by me which is not considered by me as income due to salary and wages or income in any sense of the word which would be the subject to Income Tax under the Act; (neither is it a payment which falls under the Capital Gains Tax provisions of the same Act.)



The Assessment is invalid and should be set aside, cancelled or withdrawn on the grounds that:-



(a) the Assessment was made and issued in error,

(b) the Assessment was made in an arbitrary manner and was no made in good faith,

(c) the Assessment is unauthorised and beyond the power of the Commissioner, and

(d) the Assessment is improperly addressed and improper in form.



In support of my contention I claim that:



1. The receipt of $108,254.00 was not a receipt of income (in the ordinary sense and therefore not subject to Tax under Section 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act) because it was not paid as a result of any duties performed or work undertaken in relation to my employment.



2. The amount of money was given to me as recompense for GIVING UP MY RIGHT to make a free choice of employment for a given period of time.



Consequently that payment had nothing whatever of the character of a payment made for services rendered or duties undertaken in employment. Those factors were already accounted for in my regular salary, bonuses etc. and taxed accordingly. I simply gave up my PERSONAL RIGHT to leave my employer and the payment was unequivocally, solely and directly related and derived from that forbearance.



3. It was no a payment in recognition of any service or duty or of a physical or mental contribution by any effort whatsoever on my part, whether express or implied, in relation to my employment service. (Neither was it a payment or an allowance given or granted to me in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly to, any employment of or services rendered by me. It is therefore not subject to tax under paragraph 26(e) of the same Act.) It was a payment clearly for the forfeiture of my PERSONAL RIGHT which may be summed up as a right to seek employment elsewhere.



4. Because it was not a proprietary right but a PERSONAL RIGHT which I surrendered for a payment, it is considered not subject to tax under (Section 160(M) (7) or any other of the Capital Gains Tax provisions.



5. Therefore the amount of assessable income shown on your assessment Notice for the year ending 30 June 1992 should be reduced by $108,254.00 and the Assessment restored to that amount of income as assessed by me.



The statements of claim contained in the foregoing paragraphs represent my grounds of objection for the purposes of Sec. 14ZU of the Taxation Administration Act”.



The words in brackets were not contained in the objection lodged by A. The only other difference between the two objections was that A had added the following words at the end of paragraph 2:-



“There is not one single factor in the circumstances and events that took place which supports your ruling dated 25 May 1992 “that the payment of $ 94,485 was made for services rendered”. The sole purpose and consideration for the payment was for the forfeiture of a personal right.”



It is quite clear that there is nothing in these objections which would have directed the respondents mind to the argument that the retention payments or any part of them could be said to be fringe benefits within the meaning of the FBT Act and, therefore, exempt from income tax. The objections argue solely for exclusion from income tax as not being income but capital and, being capital not subject to tax as a capital gain. No reference to exemption or fringe benefits can be found or even faintly imputed.



15. Counsel for the applicants requested the Tribunal to exercise its discretion under Section 14ZZK(1) of the Administration Act if the Tribunal came to the view that the ground was not covered by the objection. During the course of the closing submission by counsel for the applicant the Tribunal indicated, without ruling on the matter, that the applicants may need to rely on the exercise of the discretion. Little was put forward by the applicant in support of the request for leave to amend the grounds of objection to allow reliance on the operation of the FBT Act. A subsequent examination of the transcript of the hearing also showed that no detailed submissions were provided on behalf of the respondent in relation to the request of the applicants. Consequently, and at a subsequent Directions Hearing, the respondent was directed to file and serve a written submission on this question with the applicants to file and serve a reply. A submission was filed by the respondent but no reply was provided by the applicants. In essence, the argument against exercising the discretion not to limit the applicants to the ground stated in this objection were:-

  1. The particular ground sought to be added was first raised part way through the closing submission by Counsel for the applicants. It was not mentioned in the objection, in subsequent discussions with officers of the respondent, in the statement of facts, issues and contentions provided by the applicants at the direction of the Tribunal prior to the hearing nor adverted to in the opening address at the hearing.

  2. The argument was only brought to the notice of the respondent in the closing address after completion of evidence from witnesses and the respondent had no opportunity to pursue by cross examination or by the introduction of evidence the issues raised by the FBT argument.

  3. The respondent advanced limited argument in the closing address against the FBT argument as a result of only becoming aware of the issue in the course of the closing address by counsel for the applicants.

  4. The applicants had been professionally represented at all times including a member of counsel at interlocutory stages, two members of counsel at the hearing and by the accountant throughout.

16. In the leading case on the question of the exercise of the discretion to allow amendment of grounds of objections, Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 91 ATC 4942, the joint judgement of Lockhart, Burchett and Hill JJ stated (at p.4949):-



“It follows that the Tribunal or the Court has power to permit a taxpayer to argue that the taxable income and tax payable are incorrect and “excessive” for reasons not initially advanced, even if those reasons involve, as in the present case, entirely fresh grounds in substitution for the original grounds, or even if they require consideration of matters not considered by the Commissioner in the original assessment process.



The decision whether to allow an amendment ought to be made on the same considerations of justice upon which such decisions are regularly made in litigation”.



In that case the Full Federal Court was concerned with the interpretation of Section 190(a) of the Act. This section was replaced by an equivalent provision in Section 14ZZK of the Administration Act in 1991. One significant difference between the facts of Lighthouse Philatelics case and this case is that, in the former, the taxpayer had previously sought an amended assessment based on the new ground of objection some three years prior to the hearing by the Tribunal from whose decision the appeal to the Federal Court was made. The comment was made by their Honours (at p.4944) “Given the length of time that passed between June 1987 and the hearing by the Tribunal, it can hardly be said that the Commissioner was taken by surprise”. Similarly in Case 16/94, 94 ATC 194 the applicant had advised the respondent of the proposed new argument some six months prior to the hearing and the respondent had sought and obtained affidavits as to the matters raised.



17. In this case no mention was made of the FBT argument until counsel for the applicants was well into his closing address to the Tribunal and after all evidence had been led. In refusing to exercise such a discretion, Davies J in Gilder v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 5062 said (at p.5072):-



“The discretion is unfettered. But that is not to say that regard should not be had to the time limits imposed by the Act. The Act gives effect to a policy that taxation affairs should be dealt with efficiently and promptly. There are time limits for the lodgement of returns (s. 161), for the lodgement of objections (s. 185), for the lodgement of requests for reference (s. 187), and upon the Commissioner for the amendment of assessments (s.170). This policy should be taken into account. And so also should the policy of this Court that proceedings in the Court be handled efficiently. These present proceedings, like other proceedings in the Court, have been the subject of directions hearings in which a Judge has given directions to ensure that the parties were aware of the legal and evidentiary issues which would arise at the hearing. In such circumstances, the Court will rarely permit additional and different issues to be raised at the hearing, certainly not unless good reason for doing so has been shown, which in the present case has not been done.”



The whole purpose of pre-hearing proceedings including exchange of statements of facts issues and contentions between parties required by this Tribunal is to ensure that proceedings in the Tribunal are efficient and both parties are fully aware of the case for each side so that proper evidence and argument can be presented. To permit the applicants to introduce a new issue at such a late stage in proceedings would prejudice the respondent and the application is refused. In this matter, I adopt the view of Hill J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Citibank Limited and others, 93 ATC 4691 (at p.4703)



“Counsel for the respondents sought leave to amend the grounds of objection to claim a deduction for such a loss. It was not in issue between the parties that the Full Court could in an appropriate case grant such leave. The present case, however, was not an appropriate case because the point was not taken at first instance in circumstances where, had it been taken, evidence might well have been admissible on the question.”



18. Having refused to exercise the discretion to allow an additional ground of objection it is not appropriate to consider fully the merits of the new argument which was sought to be introduced. Whilst not necessarily agreeing with the views of the respondent that the “FBT argument is unmeritorious and untenable”, it is a complex argument requiring an analysis of the interrelationship of the Act and the FBT Act. For example, the submissions on behalf of the applicants did not explore the interpretation of paragraph (m) of the definition of “fringe benefit” in Section 136(1) of the FBT Act. This paragraph excludes as a fringe benefit “consideration of a capital nature for, or in respect of - (i) a legally enforceable contract in restraint of trade by a person.”



Fringe Benefits Tax being a tax levied on the payer of fringe benefits, it may well be argued that this provision refers to payments of a capital nature in the hands of that payer, not the recipient. Counsel for the applicants consistently argued that the amounts were capital in the hands of the recipients and were in consideration of a contract in restraint of trade. Whilst I have found that the receipts were income this does not necessarily remove them from the above quoted exclusion. If not then the exemptions in Section 23L and 26(e)(iv) of the Act do not apply.



Given the lateness of the introduction of the argument (with the possibility that this new line of reasoning only occurred to counsel when he came to the word ‘benefit’ in his submission on the application of Section 26(e) of the Act) and the limited ability of the respondent to deal with this new and unexpected argument, it is not surprising that the Tribunal was not provided with a full analysis of the merits of the argument.



19. For the foregoing reasons, the decisions under review are affirmed.
