Alan Bond and the Trustee of the Property of Alan Bond, A Bankrupt [1993] AATA 286; (1993) 26 ATR 1338 (1993) 18 AAR 103 (1993) 30 ALD 232 (10 September 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ALAN BOND v. THE TRUSTEE OF THE PROPERTY OF ALAN BOND, A

BANKRUPT

No. W93/99

AAT No. 8980

Number of pages - 13

Bankruptcy

[1993] AATA 286; (1993) 26 ATR 1338

[1993] AATA 286; (1993) 18 AAR 103

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.J. MCMAHON (Deputy President), T.E. BARNETT (Senior Member), R.D. FAYLE

(Member)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - contribution assessment period s 139K - assessment of income likely to be derived during contribution assessment period s 139W - applicant liable to pay to respondent a contribution calculated in accordance with s 139S - application under s 139ZF to review a decision of the respondent to make an assessment and to give a consequent notice under Division 4B of Part IV - whether certain benefits formed part of the applicant's income as defined in s 139L - benefits provided by way of gift and not provided in relation to any employment or any work done or any services rendered - whether or not gifts fall within the definition of "income" in s 139L(3)(e) - whether gifts, if provided by an employer, are fringe benefits for the purposes of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (Cth)

Acts Interpretation Act 1901, s 15AA

Bankruptcy Rules, 45K-N

Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth), ss 78(1)(f), 139J, 139K, 139L, 139Q, 139S, 139W, 139ZF, 139ZO, 149A, 149D

Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (Cth) ss 16, 18, 23, 26, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 58P, 136(1)

Income Tax Ruling MT 2016, issued 16 June 1986

Australian Postal Commission v Burgazoff 10 AAR 296

Beckwith v The Queen [1976] HCA 55; (1976) 135 CLR 569

Calderaro v Secretary, Department of Social Security [1991] FCA 638; 14 AAR 421

HEARING

PERTH, 23 August 1993

10:9:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: C.D. Steytler QC with

S.R. Paterniti

Solicitors for the Applicant: Parker and Parker

Solicitors for the Respondent: Mallesons Stephen Jacques

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that a fresh assessment of the applicant's income be made upon the basis that in the contribution period commencing 1 July 1992, the applicant derived income, as defined, of $713,390-56.

DECISION

B.J. MCMAHON, T.E. BARNETT AND R.D. FAYLE This is an application brought under s 139ZF of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 ("the Act") to review a decision by a trustee to make an assessment and to give a consequent notice under Division 4B of Part VI of the Act. On 11 December 1992, the respondent trustee made an amended assessment under s 139W and concluded that the applicant's income likely to be derived during the contribution assessment period was $440,964. The "actual income threshold amount" was $23,232 and the contribution payable under the consequent notice was calculated by deducting that amount from the assessed income and by dividing the result by 2. The notice given pursuant to s 139W(1) therefore required the applicant to pay $208,866 to the respondent trustee on instalment dates set out in the notice.

2. The sum of $440,964 was made up of a value attributed to 6 different items -- accommodation, use of office space, use of secretarial services, use of telephone facilities, travel expenses and legal expenses. The applicant lives in premises that have been provided for him by his family. He has the use of an office and the use of secretarial services and a telephone, all of which have been provided at no cost to himself. In addition he has travelled extensively on the proceeds of funds that were not his own. The principal item however was a large sum of legal expenses. It was alleged that the value of these items form part of the applicant's income as defined in s 139L, to which reference will later be made.

3. By the time the hearing took place, the amounts attributable had been varied and the parties had agreed on the items in dispute. The amounts involved were as follows--

Housing $8,525-00

Use of office $491-00

Use of secretarial services $2,878-04

Use of telephones facilities $1,600-00

Travel expenses $32,654-50

Legal expenses $667,242-02

-----------

Total $713,390-56

4. An agreed statement of facts was prepared in which it was acknowledged that the first 5 items were provided to the applicant by his son, Mr John Bond, and by Topsfield Pty Limited, a company of which Mr John Bond is a director and the only beneficial shareholder. The statement of agreed facts continued that these benefits were provided to the applicant by way of gift and that none of them were provided to the applicant in relation to any employment or any work done or any services rendered by him for Mr John Bond, or any other person, or entity.

5. The largest income element in the assessment consists of a sum of $667,242-02 paid to the applicant's solicitors, Parker and Parker, during the contribution assessment period. Of this amount, $262,257-02 was paid by Topsfield Pty Limited and $204,990 was paid by the applicant's daughter, Jody Bond, In each case there were a number of payments, typically in round sums of $50,000, on various dates during the contribution assessment period. The John Bond interests made 4 payments of $50,000 and one payment each of $53,303-39, $6,668-63 and $2,285. Although no explanation was given to show how each amount was made up, documents before the Tribunal suggest that the payments of $50,000 were made on account from time to time and that the odd sums were in payment of specific accounts. The payments by Jody Bond consisted of 3 payments of $50,000 each, one of $5,000 and one of $49,990. These payments appeared to be of the same nature as those made by the John Bond interests. The remaining sum of $199,995 was said to have been paid variously by a firm known as Hemisphere Management and by a person known as Graham Ferguson Lacey. It was assumed in the course of the hearing that Hemisphere Management was a firm of which Mr Lacey was the sole proprietor. However some cross-examination seemed to suggest that there was no such firm. Mr Bond was unable to assist. The records of Parker and Parker showed that the amounts were received from an entity known as Hemisphere Management. The only document produced in relation to these payments came into existence a considerable time after the payments had been made.

6. This consisted of a letter dated 14 June 1993 which was not produced to the respondent trustee until 19 August 1993 (2 working days before this hearing). In this letter addressed to Mr Bond, Mr Lacey said--

"Dear Alan

I was pleased to facilitate you in the transfer of

A $200,000 to your lawyers to meet your legal expenses.

For good order, I would appreciate your undertaking by

return that as soon as you have been discharged from

your bankruptcy, and circumstances permit, you will

repay the A $200,000 plus interest at the rate of 7 per

cent per annum.

I trust that soon your affairs will be in order.

With every good wish

Yours sincerely

(Signed) Graham Ferguson Lacey"

7. The letter is addressed from a post office box in Bermuda. Mr Bond said in evidence that, so far as he was aware, Mr Lacy had offices in London and New York and also owned property in Australia. There was tendered in evidence another copy of this letter with a blank endorsement intended to be signed by Mr Bond in which he agreed to the terms. Mr Bond could not say for certain whether he had signed the copy and returned it as requested. We were asked to believe that he could not remember whether he had undertaken this obligation to repay the money. If the letter were to constitute the only evidence of a loan agreement, it might well be void for uncertainty. However no attempt was made to defend its terms. As will be seen, counsel for the applicant abandoned the suggestion that the moneys advanced by Mr Lacey had been by way of loan.

8. When the agreed statement of facts was prepared, a paragraph was included to the effect that none of the amounts paid in respect of legal expenses were paid on behalf of the applicant in relation to any employment or work done or services rendered by the applicant. When the agreed statement was prepared, there was also still an issue as to whether the amounts referred to in the Lacey letter were by way of loan or by way of gift. Although the letter refers only to a global sum, the respondent's working papers show that payments were made by Hemisphere Management to Parker and Parker on 11 occasions for varying amounts, suggesting that those particular payments were in satisfaction of particular legal accounts.

9. The same working papers (contained in the bundle of documents numbered T101 and supplied pursuant to the provisions of s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act ("the AACT Act")) indicate that legal expenses were incurred for a variety of purposes, including a defamation action, appearances before a Royal Commission, defence of criminal proceedings and general unspecified litigation some of which was particularised in one of the trustee's letters as including matters relating to "Queensland Nickel Mines, Pinkertons, and Mr Bond's application for return of his passport".

10. When the matter came on for hearing, no attempt was made to explore these matters further. We were not told the true nature of the legal expenses. The circumstances under which the payments were made were not explored. It was no longer contended that the sums paid by Mr Lacey were by way of loan. We were simply asked to assume that all payments were by way of gift. Upon the basis of this assumption, the applicant then argued that the payments did not fall within the scope of the definition of income in s 139L of the Act and the respondent argued to the contrary.

11. Counsel for the applicant did not defend the suggestion that the Lacey advances were by way of loan. Nevertheless, without admissions, both he and counsel for the respondent asked us to treat these amounts "as if" they were gifts. We are not prepared to proceed on that basis. In Sullivan v Department of Transport 1 ALD 383 at 402 Deane J said--

"In the ordinary case, a tribunal which is under a duty

to act judicially and which has the relevant parties

before it, will be best advised to be guided by the

parties in identifying the issues and to permit the

parties to present their respective cases in the manner

in which they think appropriate."

In Australian Postal Commission v Burgazoff 10 AAR 296 at 298 Davies J affirmed that this Tribunal should not follow an inquisitorial process and added that the Tribunal "never itself makes investigations outside the conduct of the hearing". Nevertheless we are bound by s 43 of the AAT Act to bring in findings on material questions of fact and must make a reference to the evidence or other material on which those findings are based. We can not proceed on supposed facts. To do so would amount to giving an advisory opinion, a procedure which is beyond the power of this Tribunal. We are not bound to accept concessions made by the parties (Calderaro v Secretary Department of Social Security [1991] FCA 638; 14 AAR 421). If it is necessary in reviewing the decision before us, we are entitled to consider issues not raised by the parties (Re Playcorp Pty Limited and Collector of Customs [1992] AATA 18; 15 AAR 86).

12. There was very little oral evidence put before us. Mr Bond was called simply to assert that he provided no services to Mr Lacey. He did say that he had been offered the position of a consultant by Mr Lacy some time ago but insisted that this was a position that was no longer open to him. He also denied that Mr Lacey held any moneys for him or that Mr Lacey was a trustee of any trust of which Mr Bond was a beneficiary or that Mr Lacey's company managed any of Mr Bond's money. He did not offer any explanation as to why Mr Lacey was not called to give evidence, although he thought that he was on holidays at the time of the hearing and in any event he did not live in Australia.

13. On the basis of the oral evidence put before us and on the basis of the material contained in the s 37 documents and on the basis of what we were told from the bar table, we find as a fact that all the payments comprising the assessment under review were made without consideration and were therefore gifts. In the applicant's statement of facts and contentions lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to its General Practice Direction, it was contended that any benefits received by the applicant from these payments were received by him in an ordinary family setting as normal family incidents of family relationships and consequently none of these benefits constituted income within the meaning of s 139L(e) of the Act. This argument was not pursued at the hearing. It is no doubt based upon the ruling by the Commissioner for Taxation referred to later in these reasons. There is some correspondence in the s 37 documents indicating that the applicant has given conflicting explanations in relation to the payments received from John Bond and his company. The applicant no longer seeks to defend the version that would support a series of loans from John or Jody Bond. Although the "family setting" argument was not raised at the hearing, we feel that we would note there is no evidence to suggest that payments of the nature we have described, which were made in the circumstances to which we have referred, could be described as payments "in an ordinary family setting".

14. In the statement of facts and contentions, it was also suggested that the applicant is not obliged by s 139P of the Act to pay a contribution to the respondent, when the bankrupt does not in fact have the means to make any such payment. This contention was not pursued at the hearing. There was no examination of any means available to the applicant and the legal proposition was not defended. If the applicant has such ready sources of credit or beneficence that have been disclosed in the evidence before us, it is understandable why this argument was not pursued. Another argument included in the statement of facts and contentions was that the respondent's assessment was issued for purposes beyond the scope of Division 4B of the Act and was therefore invalid. The meaning of this assertion was not explained to us and the argument was not pursued.

15. Division 4B of Part VI of the Act came into effect on 1 July 1992. Because of the definition of "contribution assessment period" in s 139K, it has applied in respect to Mr Bond from that date. The objects of the Division are set out in s 139J in the following terms--

"S 139J The objects of this Division are:

(a) to require a bankrupt who derives income during the

bankruptcy to pay contributions towards the bankrupt's

estate; and

(b) to enable the recovery of certain money and

property for the benefit of the bankrupt's estate."

16. In the operation of the Division, the trustee is to make an assessment of the bankrupt's income that is likely to be derived during the contribution assessment period (s 139W) and, following service of a notice upon the bankrupt, a liability is created to pay the assessed contribution to the trustee (s 139P and s139Q). The extent of the contribution is calculated in accordance with the formula set out in s 139S.

17. In arriving at that assessment, the trustee is obliged to take account of the bankrupt's actual or likely income. This is defined in s 139L in the following terms--

"S 139L In this Division:

"Income", in relation to a bankrupt, means any amount

derived by the bankrupt that is income according to

ordinary usages and concepts, and includes:

(a) a payment to the bankrupt from a provident,

benefit, superannuation, retirement or approved deposit

fund; and

(b) a payment to the bankrupt in consequence of a

termination of any office or employment; and

(c) an amount received by the bankrupt under a policy

of life insurance or endowment insurance; and

(d) an amount received by the bankrupt as a beneficiary

under a trust to the extent to which the amount was paid

out of income of the trust; and

(e) the value of a benefit that is, or if it were

provided by an employer would be a fringe benefit for

the purposes of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act

1986, being that value as worked out in accordance with

the provisions of that Act but subject to any

modifications of those provisions made by the rules; and

(f) the amount of any money, or the value of any other

consideration, received by a person other than the

bankrupt from another person as a result of work done or

services performed by the bankrupt, less any expenses

(other than expenses of a capital nature) necessarily

incurred by the first-mentioned person in connection

with the work or services;

but does not include an amount that is not income for

the purposes of the Social Security Act 1991 because of

subsection 8(8) of that Act (other than paragraph (a),

(b), (h), (ha), (j), (k), (ka), (m), (z), (za) or (zb)

of that subsection)."

18. The paragraph of that section with which we are concerned is paragraph (e). It is alleged that the gifts made to, or gratuitous payments made on behalf of, the bankrupt are benefits which, if they were provided by an employer, would be fringe benefits for the purposes of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 ("the FBT Act"). There is no dispute between the parties as to the value to be attributed to these benefits if they would be found to fall within the terms of that Act.

19. "Fringe benefit" is defined in s 136(1) of the FBT Act in the following terms--

"s136(1)

"fringe benefit", in relation to an employee, in

relation to the employer of the employee, in relation to

a year of tax, means a benefit--

(a) provided at any time during the year of tax; or

(b) provided in respect of the year of tax,

being a benefit provided to the employee or to an

associate of the employee by--

(c) the employer;

(d) an associate of the employer; or

(e) a person (in this paragraph referred to as the

"arranger") other than the employer or an associate of

the employer under an arrangement between--

(i) the employer or an associate of the employer; and

(ii) the arranger or another person,

in respect of the employment of the employee, but does

not include ......"

20. Benefit is defined in the following terms--

"benefit" includes any right (including a right in

relation to, and an interest in, real or person

property), privilege, service or facility and, without

limiting the generality of the foregoing, includes a

right, benefit, privilege, service or facility that is,

or is to be, provided under--

(a) an arrangement for or in relation to--

(i) the performance of work (including work of a

professional nature), whether with or without the

provision of property;

(ii) the provision of, or of the use of facilities for,

entertainment, recreation or instruction; or

(iii) the conferring of rights, benefits or privileges

for which remuneration is payable in the form of a

royalty, tribute, levy or similar exaction;

(b) a contract of insurance; or

(c) an arrangement for or in relation to the lending of

money."

21. Other relevant definitions in s 136(1) are those of "external expense payment fringe benefit" and "in respect of", which are respectively in the following terms--

"external expense payment fringe benefit" means an

expense payment fringe benefit other than an in-house

expense payment fringe benefit."

"in respect of", in relation to the employment of an

employee, includes by reason of, by virtue of, or for or

in relation directly or indirectly to, that employment."

22. Not all fringe benefits are taxable benefits. The FBT Act is structured to provide for particular types of benefits which attract taxation. These benefits include the provision of the use of cars, debt waivers, payments of expenses, the provision of no or low rental housing, cash allowances to employees living-away-from-home, the provision of subsidised airline fares to employees in the airline industry, some meals provided to employees, certain entertainment, the provision of property and other unspecified benefits called residual benefits. Division 13 of Part III of the FBT Act is a miscellanea of exempt benefits which, because they do not have taxable values as defined, are excluded from the "fringe benefits taxable amount" upon which tax is imposed. For the purposes of subparagraph (e) of s 139L of the Act, exempt benefits, being firstly fringe benefits would prima facie be included. However the paragraph provides that there is to be included in the bankrupt's income "the value of a benefit ... as worked out in accordance with the provisions of that Act ...". As no taxable value attaches to exempt benefits, then there can be no "income" derived in relation to exempt benefits under the FBT Act. This however, is of no consequence in the present case because none of the payments made or services or property provided by any of the John Bond interests, Jody Bond or Mr Lacey would, if those providers were the applicant's employer, qualify as exempt benefits under the FBT Act.

23. The gifts referred to in paragraph 3 above, if granted in respect of the applicant by his employer would be benefits under the FBT Act according to the following provisions--

The provision of housing accommodation - s 25 Housing Benefit

The use of office facilities, telephone and staff - s 45

Residual Benefits

The payment of travel expenses - s 20 Expense Payment Benefit

The payment of legal expenses - s 20 Expense Payment Benefit

The taxable value of a housing benefit is fixed by s 26. The taxable value of a residual benefit of the present kind is fixed by one of sections 48, 49, 50 or 51 as reduced, where applicable, by s 52. The taxable value of an expense payment benefit of the particular kind is fixed by s 23. However, as the parties have agreed that those values shall be successively as set out in paragraph 3 above, the quantum issue is settled.

24. Loan benefits and their valuation are dealt with in ss 16 and 18 of the FBT Act. In view of our finding that the Lacey payments were by way of gift, it will not be necessary to examine these sections.

25. Paragraph 139L(e) of the Act incorporates as income any benefit that might be caught by the provisions of the FBT Act, as modified by any provisions made under the Bankruptcy Rules. Extensive amendments have been made through the effect of Rules 45K to 45N and the detailed provisions appearing in schedule 1B of the Rules. None of the modifications are relevant for present purposes, except to illustrate that detailed consideration has been given to the application of the FBT Act to the concept of income under Division 4B.

26. The applicant's argument was that in order for a benefit to be a fringe benefit for the purposes of the FBT Act it must be one which is provided to an employee "in respect of the employment of the employee". The inclusion in paragraph 139L(e) of the Act of the phrase "if provided by an employer would be" merely eliminates the requirement that the benefit be paid by an employer. In the submission of the applicant, it does not remove the requirement that the benefit must still be provided "in respect of the employment of the employee". The respondent argued that the inclusion of the phrase in paragraph (e) eliminated both requirements.

27. The applicant submitted that his interpretation was consistent with the purpose of the legislation. It was submitted that the reason that paragraph (e) had been included was to prevent a bankrupt from defeating the operation of the provisions of Division 4B of the Act by arranging to be paid in kind rather than in cash for work done or services rendered. Counsel for the applicant further submitted that the purpose behind the extension of the concept of fringe benefits, so as to encompass benefits provided by someone other than the employer in respect of work done or services rendered by the bankrupt, is to stop a bankrupt from defeating the operation of the provisions of Division 4B by arranging for someone other than the employer to provide the benefit in return for, or in respect of, that work or those services.

28. It does not seem to us that this is an interpretation which the words can fairly bear. The definition of "fringe benefit" in the FBT Act already encompasses a benefit provided to an employee by a person other than the employer or an associate of the employer, pursuant to an arrangement made by the employer or associate with a third party called an "arranger". If Parliament, in enacting s 139L(e) of the Act, merely wished to incorporate only those benefits which had related employment connotations, it would not have been necessary to include the phrase "or if it were provided by an employer would be". These plain words, in our view, simply serve to include as "income" of the bankrupt benefits provided to him or her regardless of their source and regardless of the employment context. Paragraph (f) of s 139L addresses a different matter - it operates to include as "income" of the bankrupt any remuneration entitlement of the bankrupt derived from employment or the rendering of services which are appropriated to third parties for whatever reason. This provision is a constructive provision and prevents avoidance of the income test by the simple device of directing or deflecting such remuneration away from the bankrupt.

29. It seems to us that the inclusion of the phrase "or if it were provided by an employer would be" in paragraph (e) has a clear purpose. It is to include in a bankrupt's statutory income benefits of the individual types characterised by the FBT Act as fringe benefits which are received by the bankrupt, whether or not in the course of any employment, or in the course of rendering any services to the provider or whether received for altruistic or other reasons. Thus a provider who makes available benefits in relation to a car, housing, board or living-away-from-home allowance, for example, within the scope of these concepts as defined in the FBT Act is not himself liable to tax under the FBT Act if the payments are made outside the employment context. Nevertheless, the value of such benefits are included in the bankrupt's income because of the specific provisions of the Act.

30. It was submitted by the applicant that such a view would lead to absurd and capricious consequences. It was suggested that a wide approach would include within the meaning of income, the value of meals provided by a family member, or even the value of a birthday gift. In our view this consequence by no means follows. The Commissioner for Taxation certainly does not share that view of assessability of minor benefits. If they fall within the ambit of s 58P of the FBT Act, they are exempt benefits and therefore have no taxable value. In his ruling MT 2016, issued on 16 June 1986, the Commissioner advises in paragraph 12 that gifts given in an ordinary family setting which would have been a normal incident of a family relationship would not normally be provided in respect of either past or current employment of the recipient family member. We were invited to conclude that the construction contended for by the respondent, if literally correct, should be rejected in accordance with the principles discussed in Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1981) 147 CLR 297 at 321. In our view it is not necessary to read the provisions down. The consequences of the interpretation, which appears to us to be correct, would not lead to absurd and capricious results. So long as the nature and value of gifts included in a bankrupt's income equate with the types of benefits that would attract fringe benefits tax if they occurred in an employment context, no absurd consequences will ensue.

31. The fact that there has been a detailed examination made of the provisions of the FBT Act in the Rules to which we have referred, indicates that if there is a consequence which brings in to the definition of income gifts outside the ordinary family context, then clearly this was an intended result. One of the arguments raised by the applicant was that it was contrary to the public interest that a bankrupt should be denied the right to have counsel of his choice in defending criminal proceedings and that if moneys provided by his family for that purpose were to be included in his statutory income, he would be unfairly punished. There is no merit in this argument. The purpose of the Act is not to punish bankrupts. The object set out in s 139J is to require a bankrupt to pay contributions to his creditors if he derives income as defined by the Act. Such an obligation cast upon him by a statute may, in some cases, cause a diminution in the bankrupt's resources or in those of his family. We see no objection to this result from a public policy point of view. It is not unreasonable to expect a change, even a material change in a bankrupt's standard of living, brought about by the operation of this Act intended to provide some balance to the hardship endured by creditors. The Act has been significantly amended to ensure that appropriate contributions are made from moneys available to the bankrupt for the benefit of creditors. Upholding the policy of this particular statute is the primary public policy.

32. It was also contended by the applicant that because of the serious consequences of failure to comply with a Division 4B notice, paragraph (e) should, if ambiguous, be read down in favour of the bankrupt. It is true that failure to pay the amount of any contribution assessed under s 139P has serious consequences for a bankrupt. Some of these consequences are referred to in s 149D, setting out the grounds upon which objection may be made to the discharge of the bankrupt, s 149A providing for an extension of the term of bankruptcy when an objection is made, and s 78(1)(f) empowering a court ultimately to issue a warrant for the arrest of a bankrupt who has failed to comply with an obligation under the Act. It is also true that in Beckwith v The Queen [1976] HCA 55; (1976) 135 CLR 569 at 576, Gibbs J observed that in determining the meaning of a penal statute the ordinary rules of construction must be applied but if the language of the statute remains ambiguous or doubtful, the ambiguity or doubt may be resolved in favour of the subject by refusing to extend the category of criminal offences. Similar views were expressed in Trade Practices Commission v TNT Management Pty Limited and Others (1985) 58 ALR 423 at 471-2.

33. In our view however it is not appropriate to pray in aid this rule of construction. Section 139L does not extend the category of criminal offences. It simply provides a statutory definition of income going beyond the ordinary usages and concepts of income for bankruptcy purposes. Criminal offences may arise if the bankrupt fails to comply with certain obligations (s 139ZO). However that is a matter within the control of the bankrupt and is not a matter that concerns the interpretation of a definition section, such as s 139L.

34. In any event, we see no ambiguity which would require such a reading down of the plain words of the statute. We are obliged by s 15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901, when interpreting a provision of an Act, to give a construction that would promote the purpose or object underlying the Act whether that purpose or object is expressly stated in the Act or not. The purpose or object of the Act is expressly stated as set out above. It is consistent with that purpose or object that gifts of the nature under consideration in the present circumstances be regarded as income in accordance with the definition.

35. We have concluded that the gifts discussed in these reasons fall within the definition of income in paragraph (e). Had the values of these gifts remained constant since the date of the original reviewable decision, we would simply have affirmed the decision. As both parties have now agreed that the extent of the gifts is considerably in excess of those included in the amended December 1992 assessment, we propose to set aside the decision under review and to remit the matter to the respondent with the direction that a fresh assessment of the applicant's income be made upon the basis that in the contribution assessment period commencing 1 July 1992, the applicant derived income, as defined, of $713,390-56.
