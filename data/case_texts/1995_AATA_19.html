Anne Margaret Ward and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 19; (1995) 21 AAR 26 (6 February 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ANNE MARGARET WARD v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A94/170

AAT No. 9987

Number of pages - 8

Superannuation

[1995] AATA 19; (1995) 21 AAR 26

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

BJ McMAHON (Deputy President), NJ ATTWOOD (Member) AND HG JULIAN (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - late election - whether to be treated as within time - discretion - applicant not aware of ability to preserve benefits -no information provided by employer - reasons for delay - no prejudice to respondent - commitment to superannuation - requirements of justice - decision set aside and remitted

Superannuation Act 1976 ss 137, 157

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307

Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870

Hunter Valley Development Pty Limited v Minister for Home Affairs and
Environment (1984-1985) 58 ALR 305

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239

Re Secretary, Department of Social Security v Diepenbroeck [1992] AATA 149; 15 AAR 411

HEARING

CANBERRA, 11 January 1995

6:2:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr H Selby

Solicitors for the Applicant: Pamela Coward and Associates

Solicitors for the Respondent: Ms T Munoz

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the election made by the applicant on 16 January 1989 under sub- section 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act

DECISION

BJ McMAHON, NJ ATTWOOD AND HG JULIAN This is an application to review a decision of the delegate of the respondent made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 not to direct that the election made by the applicant on 16 January 1989 under sub-section 137(1) of that Act be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub- section of the Act.

2. The relevant terms of the sub-sections are as follows -

"s 137 (1) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and is

not entitled to pension under this Act, he may, not later than 21

days after he ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in

writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to

him.

............

s 157 (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of

the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the

election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the

circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should

be recognized, the Commissioner may direct that the election be

treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the

election shall have effect accordingly."

3. The applicant was born on 3 November 1946. She grew up in a country town where she obtained her Leaving Certificate. She has not acquired any further formal educational qualifications since then. On leaving school, she worked for the Commonwealth Bank until she joined the Public Service.

4. Following her appointment to the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) on 22 November 1965, she became a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1922. When that Act was replaced by the 1976 Act, she became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the later Act on its commencement from 1 July 1976. Although a member of the 1976 fund for only a short period prior to her resignation, to which reference will later be made, she was however a contributor to a then current public service superannuation fund for a period of 12 years prior to that resignation.

5. In 1976 Mrs Ward had 2 young children. She decided to seek part time work so as to enable her to spend more time with the children. As employment of this nature was not available with ABS, she decided to seek it elsewhere. Before resigning, she decided to use up whatever leave was available to her.

6. From 5 August 1976 until 15 August 1977 she took up periods of sick leave and maternity leave which were due to her, although she said in her evidence that her health was as good as could be expected for a mother looking after 2 young children.

7. By August 1977 she knew that she would be obliged to resign or to go back to full time work with ABS. She chose to pursue her goal of part time work. Accordingly she resigned by letter sent from her home to her supervisor on 5 August 1977. At that time, she was a clerk Class 5 in the demography section of ABS. Her job consisted of dealing with vital statistics and disseminating them through publications. She would receive forms, codify them and distribute the information. She had a staff of 2 working for her. She was then aged about 30. Her husband also worked in a subject matter section of ABS.

8. She was sent by ABS, acting as agent for the respondent, a Form S2A which has become familiar in cases of this nature. It is headed "Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights". In the first box the applicant is required to "delete whichever inapplicable" one or more of 3 phrases -" apply for a refund of accumulated contributions, apply for payment of a lump sum benefit, elect for preservation of superannuation rights". On neither side of the form is there an explanation of what is meant by "elect for preservation of superannuation rights".

9. Mrs Ward's evidence was that in 1977 her understanding, acquired through general conversation with other public servants, was that on resignation all that one was entitled to was a refund of accumulated contributions. She understood the position to be different in the case of age retirement. It never occurred to her, she said, that she would be entitled to anything else and no person explained her rights to her. As she had no idea that she could have been entitled to anything else, she did not make any enquiries. She did not know that there were any enquiries to be made.

10. She completed the form S2A by leaving as the only applicable option, an application for refund of accumulated contributions. We accept her evidence that at the time, it was her intention to return to the Public Service full time as soon as her children were old enough to look after themselves.

11. Within 2 months of resigning, she gained temporary part time evening and weekend employment with the Capital Territory Health Commission and remained in this employment until 30 June 1986. It was not necessary for her to work for financial reasons. Her husband continued to be employed in the ABS. She said in her evidence that she liked to work. She said "it enabled me to hang in there and to deal with technological changes".

12. In 1981 she became aware that shortly prior to that date, the AMP Society had established superannuation facilities for employees. On 30 June 1981 she became a member of the AMP fund because of her belief that superannuation was a worthwhile investment plan. She was still in part time employment with the Health Commission.

13. After the Form S2A had been processed, she received a refund of her superannuation contributions in September 1977 amounting to $2,005.47. On 12 December 1977, she received a further sum of $391-40 being interest to that date and her share of the allocation of the surplus distribution under the 1922 Act. These moneys she placed in an interest bearing account until April 1978, when they were used to purchase some blue chip shares as a form of investment. Later the shares were sold and the proceeds used towards financing the purchase of a block of land upon which Mr and Mrs Ward later built the house in which they now live.

14. In 1986 Mrs Ward decided to rejoin the Public Service and attempted to obtained employment with ABS at her former level. She found this quite difficult and applied for several other positions before she was finally accepted by ABS as a typist grade 1 in July 1986. She was given a booklet entitled "Starting Work" which was recommended to her to read at her leisure. Paragraph 5 of page 36 of a 74-page booklet touched on the question of preservation of superannuation rights. However there was nothing in the booklet to suggest that such a preservation right could be post dated and that it was open to Mrs Ward to make a late election. We accept Mrs Ward's evidence that in the context of the booklet, she understood the reference to be an option for the future not for the past.

15. In the period between rejoining the ABS and requesting the respondent to consent to a late election 2_ years later, Mrs Ward had applied for at least 7 positions within the ABS. All of these applications detailed her ABS service prior to her resignation in 1977. In addition, on 29 June 1987 she consulted the Inspector at her personnel department to clarify her position relating to long service leave entitlements arising from her part-time work with the Health Commission. At no time, as a result of any of these applications, was she given any information concerning her right to make a late election to preserve her previous superannuation rights. The fact that she enquired after her long service entitlements indicates to us that she was not indifferent to claiming her entitlements.

16. It was not until her husband saw a program on local television on preservation of superannuation rights that the applicant became aware of the option open to her. Not long afterwards, on 22 December 1988, an article appeared in the Public Eye, a Public Service magazine, from which she gathered that she could make a late election to preserve those rights she thought she had previously forfeited. Within a week of learning that she could apply retrospectively, she wrote seeking approval to make a late election. She had in 1986 increased the rate of contributions from 5 per cent to 10 per cent of her salary. We take this as further evidence of a commitment to superannuation planning. We accept her evidence that had she known that the option to preserve her contribution was open to her at the time of her resignation, she would have exercised it. Her later conduct in relation to superannuation and the saving strategy which she and her husband had, indicate that this would have been a likely choice.

17. In recent times, the application of sub-section 157(1) has been considered by this Tribunal frequently. There have been 2 decisions in which a presidential member has presided, namely Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307 and Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870. The former concentrated upon a discussion of the meaning of "desirable" in sub-section 157(1). The latter concentrated upon the application in cases of this nature of principles gathered together and expressed by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Development Pty Limited v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984-1985) 58 ALR 305. Broadly, both of these decisions have been followed or departed from depending upon the particular facts of the case in the many other decisions which this Tribunal has made. Neither decision has been disapproved as such in later Tribunal or Federal Court decisions. The emphasis in both cases however has been modified by observations made in 2 judgments of the Federal Court. To the extent that they have not been disapproved, we consider that they are still of assistance and ought to be followed wherever possible.

18. A Full Court judgment given by Black CJ, Davies and Cooper JJ in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450 was followed by a later judgment of Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239. From a consideration of extracts from both judgments, assistance can be derived in relation to various aspects which recur in each of the cases coming before the Tribunal. We would summarise them as follows -

A General considerations setting the context within which the

discretion is to be exercised

(1) The discretion given by s 157 should be exercised by reference

to the words of the statute and the contents within which the

discretion is conferred. Section 157 uses simple words and the

issues which arise under it are invariably issues of fact, the

ultimate issue being whether it is desirable that a late election

should be recognised (Chalk at 455).

(2) On the one hand an election recognised under s 137(1) will

extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits which

fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible employee.

That would be in accordance with the general policy of the Act. On

the other hand the orderly administration of the superannuation

scheme requires that time limits be applied, generally speaking, so

that decisions which impinge on investment and accounting procedures

are made as soon as possible after an event such as resignation

occurs. Matters of this kind set the context within which the

discretion under s 157(1) is conferred and the context in which the

discretion must be exercised (Boardman at 246 and 247).

(3) In s 157(1) "desirable" is not referable to a state of mind -

whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late

election is "desirable" is to be judged objectively having regard to

all the circumstances of the case...whether the recognition of a

late election is "something worthy of achievement" must depend on

what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to

make the late election and the requirements of the administration of

the fund (Boardman at 247).

B Observations on whether extension of time principles apply -

(1) Wilcox J referred to the general principles on which time for the

performance of an act may be extended but His Honour did not limit the

factors which might arise from the context in which a particular

statutory discretion to extend time arises (Chalk at 455).

(2) It is understandable, indeed inevitable, that a decision-maker

considering an application under s 157(1) will have close regard to the

assistance which decisions such as Hunter Valley Developments and A'hern

offer (Boardman at 247 and 248).

(3) There is a public interest in enforcing procedural time limits which

extends beyond the immediate parties (Boardman at 248).

(4) The time limit specified in the statute gives a prima facie rule as

to when the application should have been made. Before an exercise of

discretion in the applicant's favour can occur, it must be made to

appear that it is fair and just in all the circumstances to extend time.

One of the relevant considerations will be whether there was a

reasonable explanation for the delay, although the absence of a

reasonable explanation is not necessarily fatal : Comcare v A'hern at

88. There is therefore an evidentiary burden on the applicant to place

information before the decision-maker sufficient to satisfy the

decision-maker that in all the circumstances of the case, it is

desirable that the late election should be recognised (Boardman at 249).

(5) The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that there

will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant requires that

the time limit be relaxed. In such cases it is the policy of the Act,

and the legal right of the applicant, to a favourable exercise of

discretion under s 157(1). If the proper application of s 157(1) to

individual cases, results in many people obtaining recognition of late

election, so be it, that is the law (Boardman at 252).

C Application of extension of time principles may not always give the

correct answer -

(i) Most provisions which authorise an extension of time are instances

of beneficial legislation which, accordingly should be applied

beneficially (Chalk at 455).

(2) In the exercise of the discretion under s 157 it may be more

important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to

extend time, than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time

(Chalk at 456).

(3) Extension of a procedural time limit gives a party an opportunity of

establishing rights but to recognise a belated election immediately

confers rights. However in each case the question is whether it is fair

and just that the party who is out of time should by the exercise of the

discretion be extended the rights which would have been enjoyed had the

act in question been done within time (Boardman at 248).

D The existence or non existence of fault on the part of the respondent

or its agent will have little bearing on desirability -

(1) The operation of s 157 is not dependent upon the existence of fault

on the part of officers of the employer or of the scheme, although

matters such as lack of advice may well be relevant (Chalk at 455).

(2) Whether or not there is a fiduciary duty by the Commissioner, is not

relevant. The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the

exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under s

157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a

timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is

itself a relevant consideration (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(3) Section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether

there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any

other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the

discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of

attributable fault...the section merely requires that the Commissioner

"be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the

election should be recognised" (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(4) It is unnecessary to decide whether the Commissioner was under a

legal or equitable duty to provide to contributors to the scheme

information as to choices available to them under it. The relevant fact

may be that the Commissioner did not provide the information (per Cooper

J in Chalk at 459).

E What obligation is there on an employee to make enquiries?

(1) In the application of s 157 it may be of little consequence that the

employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s 137 or

even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157

confers a discretion which operates in either case (Chalk at 456).

(2) Wherever it is necessary to have regard to factual circumstances as

they existed at a date in the past, delay in making the application

which initiates that consideration is likely to add to the difficulties

of ascertaining those circumstances. This difficulty is likely to be a

consideration in almost every application to extend time no matter what

the context (Boardman at 249).

(3) Depending on the circumstances, the decision-maker might proceed on

the footing that there is no reasonable explanation for delay beyond the

point when lines of enquiry so suggested would have revealed to the

applicant the right to apply for an extension. But where the evidence

establishes that the suggested lines of enquiry would have produced no

useful information, so as to leave the applicant in the same state of

ignorance or misinformation, the absence of enquiry becomes irrelevant

(Boardman at 251).

F Does delay of the applicant create prejudice to the respondent?

(1) Frequently the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts

which the employee took into account when he or she decided not to

elect, subsequently turned out differently. Often times the discretion

is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health

problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One

of the objects of s 157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a

change in circumstances (Chalk at 456).

(2) It is difficult to see prejudice to the fund in a late election. A

favourable exercise of the discretion would extend to the respondent the

benefit of additional years of service, but this liability would have

arisen in any event had the election been exercised in time. To grant

the respondent the benefit of years of service actually performed is

consistent with the policy and objects of the Act (Boardman at 252).

(3) In saying that the issue before the Tribunal involved a

"determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case,

when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be granted

is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that extension is

granted" the Tribunal did not apply a wrong test (Boardman at 247).

G Should the decision-maker have regard to the totality of service?

(1) A decision-maker exercising the s 157 discretion could think it fair

to the employer, to the employee, and to the scheme that the benefit

should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee's

service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums

which must be refunded (Chalk at 456).

H What significance should attach to the form of investment of the lump

sum?

(1) Where the accumulated contributions were immediately invested in a

business enterprise such a use of the money would in itself often

indicate that a deliberate decision had been made to reject the option

of preservation in favour of receiving the immediate benefit of the

accumulated contributions (Boardman at 250).

19. By and large the Federal Court has taken a broad brush approach to the exercise of the s 157(1) discretion. Ultimately the justice of the case will determine the outcome. The Federal Court has shown a reluctance to limit the elements to be considered in deciding what is just. Some of those elements, however, have been established. They include a consideration against the background that the sub-section is beneficial legislation. They include the fact that excusable ignorance of rights is a relevant factor to be taken into account. They include an assertion of the applicant's evidentiary burden to explain the delay, although failure to offer an acceptable explanation is not necessarily fatal. Other elements have been considered in individual contexts in the many decisions of this Tribunal. It may well be that in the absence of the adoption of detailed criteria of universal application by the Federal Court, other steps should be taken.

20. Promoting amendments to the Act is probably no longer feasible in view of the establishment of a later scheme. There appears to be scope, however, for the development of appropriate guidelines outlining matters for consideration in the exercise of the discretion. The decision-maker's freedom to decide can not, of course, be fettered and guidelines can not be applied blindly. However if they are developed after appropriate consultation, for example with unions and other consumer groups, and are preceded by appropriate debate, the Tribunal would normally respect them in appropriate cases (Re Secretary, Department of Social Security and Diepenbroeck [1992] AATA 149; 15 AAR 411). Circumstances that commonly recur and that could appropriately be dealt with in guidelines include the results of remoteness of information sources or points of enquiry such as might be encountered by a field technician. Some guidance could also be given when considering the circumstances behind the separation. One might expect that where an employee has made a mature and considered decision to switch from the public to the private sector to take a different career path, considerations of justice could be different from those applicable where the applicant has demonstrated a commitment to a consistent career in the public sector but who, by force of circumstance such as pregnancy or family care needs or the vagaries of governing legislation requiring resignation and re-appointment, finds him or herself seeking an exercise of the section 157 discretion.

21. In the absence of a more precise guide, the Tribunal must arrive at the justice of the case on the basis of the above broad principles. Having regard to these principles, we have concluded that the election Mrs Ward made on 16 January 1989 should be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by sub-section 137(1).

22. We consider that she has given an acceptable explanation for the delay in making her election. We accept that as she was totally unaware of her right to preserve her superannuation rights on resignation (as distinct from retirement) and as no attempt was made to enlighten her at the time of her resignation or subsequently, we consider that it is not unreasonable to accept her explanation for the delay.

23. There is nothing in the above principles to indicate that there is any duty, or even an expectation, on the part of an employee to make enquiries as to his or her rights when there is an absence of knowledge that there are enquiries to be made. If Mrs Ward did not know, and had no reason to suspect, that she was entitled to preserve her superannuation benefits, why should she make further enquiries of her personnel officer while she was on leave prior to retirement or subsequently?

24. This factor also militates against a finding that there has been any delay which might create prejudice. There has certainly been no delay since Mrs Ward became aware of her rights (at least delay on her part). In our view, there was no prejudicial delay between the time she rejoined the Public Service and the time she made application to the respondent for approval of the late election. Although she may have been made aware of the right to preserve her contributions, we accept that the information given to her did not extend to advice that it was possible to preserve that right retrospectively.

25. We have had regard to the totality of her service. She was an eligible member for 12 years prior to her resignation. As a result of being unaware of her preservation rights upon her resignation in 1977, she forfeited approximately $5,500 in employer contributions to the scheme. It is proper to take into account the totality of her service.

26. We accept that her investment of the lump sum, her entry to the AMP superannuation scheme, her enquiries concerning her entitlements to long service leave and the increase she authorised in the rate of her superannuation contributions, all indicate a commitment to superannuation. We consider that this is a factor that ought to be taken into account in order to do justice between Mrs Ward and the requirements of the administration of the Fund.

27. The decision under review will be set aside and the matter will be remitted to the respondent with the direction that the election made by the applicant on 16 January 1989 under sub-section 137(1) be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.
