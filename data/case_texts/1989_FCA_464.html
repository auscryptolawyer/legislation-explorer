Re Arthur William Stansfield Trading As Gaucho Apparel Ex Parte: Mcleay & Sons Carpets Pty Ltd v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1989] FCA 464 (14 November 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: ARTHUR WILLIAM STANSFIELD trading as GAUCHO APPAREL

Ex Parte: McLEAY & SONS CARPETS PTY. LTD

And: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. 1353 of 1988

FED No. 808

Bankruptcy

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

SOUTH AUSTRALIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

BANKRUPTCY DISTRICT OF THE STATE OF SOUTH AUSTRALIA

O'Loughlin J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - notice of opposition stating four grounds of opposition - whether debtor was ever indebted to original petitioning creditor - whether debtor able to pay his debts - whether Deputy Commissioner of Taxation had agreed to payment on terms - whether Deputy Commissioner estopped from seeking sequestration order.

Bankruptcy Act 1966, s.52

HEARING

ADELAIDE

14:11:1989

Counsel for the Applicant : Mr. G. Fisher

Solicitors for the Applicant : Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel for the Debtor : Mr. N. Rochow

Solicitors for the Debtor : Finlaysons

ORDER

1. The debtor's Notice of Opposition be dismissed.

2. A Sequestration order be made against the estate of the

debtor Arthur William Stansfield.

3. The substituted creditor's costs, in default of

agreement, be taxed and paid in accordance with the

Bankruptcy Act 1966.

Note: Settlement and entry of order is dealt with in Bankruptcy Rule 124.

DECISION

On 5 July 1988 a bankruptcy notice was issued out of this Court against the debtor Arthur William Stansfield at the suit of McLeay and Sons Carpets Pty. Limited, ("McLeays") the judgment creditor. The bankruptcy notice claimed the sum of $2,612.75 as being the sum that was due by the debtor to the judgment creditor under a final judgment obtained by the judgment creditor against Stansfield in the Local Court of Adelaide on 25 May 1988.

2. As the debtor had not paid or otherwise compromised the amount claimed in the bankruptcy notice, the judgment creditor issued a creditor's petition on 6 October 1988 petitioning the Court for a sequestration order. The act of bankruptcy alleged was the debtor's failure on or before 25 July 1988 to pay the sum specified in the relevant bankruptcy notice. However the debtor paid the amount owing to McLeays in December 1988.

3. In February 1989 the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation entered into the matter. Mr. Kelton, the Deputy Commissioner, swore an affidavit which was filed on 21 February 1989 alleging that the debtor was indebted to the Australian Taxation Office for group tax deductions, penalties, additional amounts for late payment, costs and interest, all totalling, as at the date of the swearing of the affidavit, the sum of $22,493.71. That affidavit supported an application which was filed on 21 February 1989 in which the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation sought orders, including an order granting him leave to be substituted as the petitioning creditor and, an order of sequestration of the estate of the debtor.

4. On 29 March 1989 a Registrar of this Court ordered that the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation be granted leave to substitute but, to this day, the question of the sequestration of the estate of the debtor remains outstanding. In fact, the proceedings before me are in the nature of an application at the suit of the debtor opposing the application by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation for a sequestration order. The debtor raises four grounds of opposition.

5. His first ground is that McLeays' bankruptcy notice and subsequent petition were faulty for the reason that he never was a debtor of McLeays. His second ground of opposition is his claimed solvency. His third ground of opposition is that he had negotiated a settlement, or at least an arrangement for payment, with the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation. His fourth ground of opposition was an argument that the Deputy Commissioner was, by virtue of the action of his officers, estopped from pursuing a sequestration order against the debtor.

6. I will deal with each of those grounds in turn but, before I do so, it will be necessary to give a brief summary of the relevant facts. Prior to November 1987 the debtor was involved in a business which was registered under the name of "Gaucho Apparel". He claims that in November 1987 he sold his business, including the business name "Gaucho Apparel", to his company Railview Pty. Limited, for $200,000, and that the sum of $200,000 was credited in the books of account of the company for his benefit.

7. However, the debtor claims that through some oversight, the registration of the business name was not transferred from his name to that of his company so that to this day he still appears in the records of the Corporate Affairs Commission as the proprietor of the business name "Gaucho Apparel". It is an uncontroverted fact that in January of 1988 the debtor personally attended upon McLeays and there ordered some carpet. It was the ordering of that carpet that gave rise to McLeays taking action against the debtor.

8. The debtor has claimed in his evidence that he made it clear to McLeays that he was placing the order in the name of, on behalf of, and as agent for Railview Pty. Limited. He points to the cheque, exhibit A6, being a deposit for $200 which shows the drawer to be Railview Pty. Limited trading as "Gaucho Apparel". There is, as I readily concede, other evidence indicating that the debtor had indeed transferred his business to Railview at the time when he ordered the carpets from McLeays.

9. So even though the debtor has paid out McLeays personally, he now claims that the debt was never his, that the bankruptcy notice and the petition should never have been issued against him, and that the bankruptcy notice and the original petition being bad, the Deputy Commissioner is now unable to proceed under the order for substitution. In my opinion, there is an evidentiary answer to that proposition. Upon the hearing of a creditor's petition, there is an onus on the petitioning creditor to prove indebtedness and the other matters that are stated in the petition. I refer generally to s.52(1)(a) of the Bankruptcy Act. Hence, if McLeays had not been paid out, it would have been necessary for them to prove that the debtor was indebted to them before a sequestration order could be made. However, McLeays had deposed to that indebtedness in the name of the debtor in affidavits filed in support of the petition and, as a consequence, unless the debtor led acceptable evidence to the contrary, the Court would have been entitled to accept the contents of those affidavits.

10. In attempting to meet that switch of evidentiary onus it is not sufficient for a debtor to state that at the time that he ordered the subject goods,- (in this case the carpet) - he was acting as an agent for his company. He must also lead evidence that he gave that information to the creditor, that is to McLeays, and that the creditor accepted his role as an agent and his company's role as the principal debtor. That evidence was not forthcoming in this case.

11. It should not be overlooked that this plea by the debtor has only recently surfaced. He permitted McLeays to obtain a judgment by default. He ignored the bankruptcy notice. Over 12 months elapsed before the debtor first made a claim that he was not in fact the party indebted to McLeays. Assuming that he did clearly explain to McLeays that Railview Pty. Limited was the purchaser, then one would be entitled to assume that some officer from McLeays could support the debtor's claim and that some documents would be held by McLeays that would show Railview as the party that ordered the goods; but no such evidence was forthcoming.

12. The unexplained failure by the debtor to call such a witness or tender such a document may, in appropriate circumstances, lead to an inference that the uncalled evidence would not have assisted his case - Blatch v Archer, [1774] EngR 2; (1774) 1 Cowp 63; Cross on Evidence, Third Edition, paragraph 1.43. In my opinion the circumstances are appropriate in this case, and I infer adversely to the debtor that no evidence would be forthcoming from McLeays that would support the debtor.

13. I conclude, therefore, that there is no substance in the debtor's first ground of opposition. The second ground of opposition raises the question of the debtor's solvency. I refer again to the provisions of s.52(2)(a) of the Bankruptcy Act. That section provides that a Court may dismiss a petition if it is satisfied by the debtor that he is able to pay his debts. The expression used, "able to pay his debts" has been considered by the Courts on a variety of occasions.

14. One of the best known decisions is that of Barwick C.J. in Sandell v Porter, [1966] HCA 28; (1966) 115 CLR 666. His Honour said at page 670;

"Insolvency is expressed in s.95 as an inability to pay

debts as they fall due out of the debtor's own money.

But the debtor's own moneys are not limited to his cash

resources immediately available. They extend to moneys

which he can procure by realization by sale or by

mortgage or pledge of his assets within a relatively

short time - relative to the nature and amount of the

debts and to the circumstances, including the nature of

the business, of the debtor."

15. His Honour was there referring to the 1924 Act which was the precursor to s.120 of the current 1966 legislation, but although there are some differences in terminology, in my respectful opinion the passage that I have quoted is equally opposite today. But in this case the debtor, who carried the onus of satisfying the Court that he was solvent, failed abysmally. The only assets to which he made any reference were his loan account of $200,000 with Railview Pty. Limited and his right to claim as an insured party under a fire policy.

16. Evidence was lead that in November 1988 the premises occupied by "Gaucho Apparel" were severely damaged by fire. Twelve months later claims against the insurer remained unresolved. Perhaps the debtor has an asset by way of a claim under an insurance policy, but if he does so there is no evidence to quantify or value it. As to his loan account, no evidence was lead whatsoever save that Railview is now said to be in receivership.

17. Far from the evidence showing that the debtor has the ability to pay his debts as they fall due and become payable out of his own money, there are positive indications that he presently cannot pay his debts. His second ground of opposition must be dismissed. I will deal with the third and fourth grounds together because they rest upon factual assertions that I am compelled to reject.

18. The debtor claimed that in discussions and negotiations with Mrs. Haw, an officer of the Australian Taxation Office, an arrangement had been struck for the payment of the moneys owing to the Australian Taxation Office at the rate of $500 per calendar month until the fire insurance proceeds were received, at which time the balance of the debt owing to the Australian Taxation office would be discharged.

19. Mrs. Haw rejected this proposition in her evidence and I positively prefer her evidence to that of the debtor. Mrs. Haw said that the debtor had indeed put such a proposition to her and that she had untimately replied that she would have to refer it to a senior officer for comment. That she did but she stated, and I accept, that the debtor's proposition was never accepted by the Australian Taxation Office.

20. Mr. Genders, the debtor's accountant who was present during the relevant negotiations, gave evidence on behalf of the debtor. Ultimately he agreed that he knew a senior officer's approval was to be obtained before the arrangement could be regarded as having been implemented. I am satisfied that the Australian Taxation Office has not compromised its claim against the debtor in any way. I am satisfied that it has not engaged in any arrangement that would inhibit its right to proceed on the petition as if it had been the petitioning creditor.

21. The third and fourth grounds of opposition must therefore be dismissed. Accordingly, the debtor's application, containing his notice of opposition to the application by the Australian Taxation Office for a sequestration order, is dismissed. I will hear the parties now as to the orders that should be made as a consequence of the reasons which I have just published.
