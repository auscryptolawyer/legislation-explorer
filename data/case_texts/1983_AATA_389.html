Re Marjorie Birtles and Commissioner of Superannuation [1983] AATA 389 (22 November 1983)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: MARJORIE BIRTLES

And: COMMISSIONER OF SUPERANNUATION

No. V82/404

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Senior Member)

Dr H.W. Garlick (Member)

W.B. Tickle (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - contributor accepted for Provident Account only - diabetes mellitus specified as relevant condition on benefit classification certificate - death due to cardial arrhythmia - cardiac arrhythmia caused or substantially contributed to atherosclerosis of coronary arteries - causal connection between diabetes and that atherosclerosis

Superannuation Act 1922 ss.5(1)

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.3(1), 81(2), 184(2), 184(7)

Re Vaughan and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 36

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN 1

Re Brown and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD 185

Re Hegedues and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. A81/26: decided on 16 April 1982

HEARING

CANBERRA

22:11:1983

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

1. Alfred Birtles was an aircraft maintenance engineer employed by the Australian National Airlines Commission, an approved authority as defined in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"), in its Trans-Australia Airlines operation. On 24 July 1980 he died in New York. The applicant in these proceedings is his widow.

2. Mr Birtles was born on 8 July 1938. He commenced employment with Trans-Australia Airlines in April 1970. On 3 April 1970 he was medically examined for the purpose of ascertaining whether he was fit or not to contribute to the Superannuation Fund established by the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") and, if so, whether he was fit to contribute for pension benefits or only to the Provident Account. Although the doctor who examined him wrote "Yes" against a printed statement that in his opinion the examinee was fit to contribute to the fund for pension benefits and struck out the reference to contribution to the Provident Account, in stating the reasons for his recommendation he wrote "fit Provident Account only diabetes meets requirements of para 97A". The reference to para 97A was apparently to a set of guidelines for medical officers for carrying out such examinations. The medical history which Mr Birtles himself completed at the time of the examination contained a statement that he suffered from diabetes which was well controlled with one daily injection of insulin-Lente-40, 32 units, and that he had had diabetes since 1962. On 23 June 1970 an officer of the Superannuation Board recommended that Mr Birtles be accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account and on 24 August 1970 that was approved by a delegate of the Board.

3. On 16 September 1980 a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") issued a benefit classification certificate pursuant to section 184(2) of the 1976 Act that he was of the opinion that Mr Birtles' death was caused or was substantially contributed to either by the physical condition that was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act or by a physical condition connected with that relevant condition, and specified the relevant condition as diabetes mellitus. As a result, the entitlement of the applicant to payment of benefits under the 1976 Act was calculated in accordance with the provisions of section 81(2) of that Act. Her solicitor then requested the Commissioner to reconsider that decision and he did so. However, on 24 September 1982 he came to the conclusion that the benefit classification certificate had been correctly issued and he confirmed the decisions of his delegate to issue that certificate and that Mr Birtles' death was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate or by a condition or conditions connected with that condition. That is the determination under review in these proceedings.

4. The provisions of sections 81(2) and 184(2) of the 1976 Act are as follows:-

"81. (2) Where -

(a) an eligible employee who dies before attaining his maximum retiring

age is survived by a spouse;

(b) there was in force in respect of the eligible employee, immediately before his death, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the eligible employee's death was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions,

the spouse is entitled -

(d) where the period of contributory service of the eligible employee is not less than 8 years -

(i) if the spouse does not make an election under section 86 or 87 - to spouse's pension in accordance with section 85 and, where the eligible employee had paid supplementary contributions, a lump sum in accordance with that section;

(ii) if the spouse makes an election under section 86 - to spouse's pension, and a lump sum benefit, in accordance with that section; or

(iii) if the spouse is entitled to make an election under section 87 and makes such an election - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 87(2) and, where the eligible employee had paid supplementary contributions, an additional lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 87(3); or

(e) where the period of contributory service of the eligible employee is less than 8 years - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 88(1) and, where the eligible employee had paid supplementary contributions, an additional lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 88(2).

184. (2) Subject to sub-section (4), where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to -

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement."

5. Provision for medical examination of employees under the 1922 Act was

contained in section 5 of that Act. The provisions of that section relevant for the purposes of these proceedings are:-

"5. (1) Except as otherwise provided by this Act, and notwithstanding anything contained in any other Act, a person who becomes an employee after the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965 shall not contribute

to the Fund under Part III or to the Provident Account unless -

(a) he has, before becoming an employee, or within such period after becoming an employee as the Board allows, undergone a medical examination by a legally qualified medical practitioner approved by the Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination has been furnished by the medical practitioner to the Board; and

(c) the Board is satisfied, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical fitness of the person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund under Part III or to the Provident Account, as the case may be."

6. Paragraph (c) of section 184(7) of the 1976 Act provides (so far as is

relevant to the case of Mr Birtles):-

"184. (7) In this section -

(a) . . .

(b) . . .

(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which -

(i) the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund; or

(ii) . . .

other than -

(iii) a physical or mental condition that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, did not exist -

(A) at the time the person became a contributor to the Provident Account; or

(B) . . .

(iv) . . ."

7. As referred to above, in 1970 Mr Birtles was accepted only as a

contributor to the Provident Account. The Commissioner, when issuing the benefit classification certificate after Mr Birtles' death, was of the opinion that the physical condition by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under section 5(1) of the 1922 Act that his health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund for pension benefits was diabetes mellitus. He was also of the opinion that that condition existed at the time when Mr Birtles became a contributor to the Provident Fund and consequently, as already stated, that it was the condition which was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act.

8. At the hearing evidence was given by the applicant herself, by two specialist endocrinologists, Dr P. B. Greenberg and Dr F. I. R. Martin, and by two consultant general physicians, Sir John Frew and Dr Andrew Newell. The documents lodged with the Tribunal under the provisions of section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") included two reports by Dr Greenberg dated respectively 17 October 1980 and 17 March 1981, a report by Dr A. V. Stepanas, the Director of the Endocrine Unit at the Woden Valley Hospital, dated 24 June 1982 and a report by Dr John Parker dated 20 March 1970 relating to the likely effect of Mr Birtles' diabetes on his eyesight. At the hearing a number of further reports were tendered, two by Dr P. Taft, an endocrinologist, dated respectively 28 March 1983 and 13 April 1983, one by Dr D. Hunt, a cardiologist, dated 5 April 1983 and one by Dr Martin dated 12 April 1983. In addition the medical records of the Royal Melbourne Hospital relating to Mr Birtles and a written account by the applicant of the events leading up to Mr Birtles' death were placed before the Tribunal as evidence.

9. The respondent did not seek to impugn the applicant's account of those events. We accept that she stated them accurately and find that they are as follows. The applicant and Mr Birtles, together with their children, went to the United States of America on holiday in June 1980. They left Melbourne on 20 June and toured to various parts of the USA. On the evening of 23 July 1980 they went to bed in Buffalo at 9.00 p.m. as they had to rise early on the morning of 24 July 1980 to catch a plane to New York. They rose at 4.00 a.m. on 24 July and Mr Birtles ate a coffee scroll and drank a glass of natural orange juice at the hotel before they started their journey. They then travelled by taxi to the airport, where he had some coffee and at 6.30 a.m. gave himself his regular daily injection of insulin. After that he drank some more coffee and ate half a coffee scroll. The plane departed at about 7.00 a.m. and the flight to New York took about 46 minutes. On the flight Mr Birtles ate a continental breakfast. At La Guardia Airport in New York the applicant, Mr Birtles and their children caught the airport bus into the New York central terminal, arriving at about 9.30 a.m.

10. After spending about an hour there arranging for accommodation at a hotel, they set off to walk to the hotel as it was not far away and they were not in a hurry. They pulled their suitcases along on sets of wheels, the applicant pulling one suitcase and Mr Birtles another. However, after they had walked for one and half blocks the wheels of Mr Birtles' suitcase broke and, as it was too heavy for him to carry, they hailed a taxi and travelled in that to the hotel. Although the distance was not great, it took about 25 minutes because of heavy traffic. On the journey Mr Birtles took a packet of sugar and poured it into his mouth and subsequently swallowed the contents of two more packets of sugar; each packet contained about 2 teaspoonfuls of sugar. He was not perspiring or shaking.

11. When the taxi arrived outside the hotel they alighted and he lifted one of the suitcases out of the taxi; it was extremely heavy. The taxi driver lifted the other suitcase out and placed in on the footpath. Mr Birtles asked the applicant to pay the taxi driver, which she did. He then looked at her and was confused as to why after taking so much sugar he had neither recovered nor gone into a state of hypoglycaemia. He told her "I have taken three packets of sugar but I still don't feel right. I suppose I had better take another half a packet of sugar". The applicant gave him another packet and he took half of it. He then asked the applicant what the hotel looked like which she considered was strange as he was then facing it. She told him that it looked all right, put her arm around his shoulders and told him to sit down on the suitcases and sort himself out. As she said that, he fell down crushing a suitcase. She obtained some water, dissolved another packet of sugar into it and poured it into his mouth; but there was no response. A bystander gave him mouth to mouth rescusitation while another man gave him heart massage. Someone sent for an ambulance. The time was then 11.05 a.m. The man giving the heart massage then told her "I am sorry but he is dead". The ambulance men, after examining Mr Birtles, told the applicant that he was dead.

12. Mr Birtles complained of no pain at any time, not even when he lifted the suitcase out of the taxi and he had no shortness of breath and was not gasping. He had been standing up wondering why he had not recovered from feeling not right when he suddenly collapsed onto the ground. His body was taken to the City Mortuary and the applicant answered a number of questions on a form. These included a statement that he was a diabetic and was taking insulin and that he was taking tablets for blood pressure. No autopsy was performed but, as a result of an external examination of the body and the information given to him by the applicant, the coroner was satisfied that death resulted from natural causes and recorded on the certificate of death that the "immediate cause of death" was "hypertensive and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease" which was "due to or as a consequence of diabetes mellitus". A copy of the certificate of death was included in the documents lodged with the Tribunal under section 37 of the AAT Act.

13. Evidence of Mr Birtles' medical history comprised the statement which he made in 1970 when he was taking up employment with Trans-Australia Airlines that he had been diabetic since 1962, the Royal Melbourne Hospital records and evidence given by the applicant. There is no doubt that he was a diabetic for nearly 20 years before he died. The first occasion on which he was found to be suffering from blood pressure above that normally regarded by medical practitioners as within acceptable limits was in December 1975. That was when he was examined at the Royal Melbourne Hospital after becoming hypoglycaemic. His blood pressure reading was found to be 160/100. However, the doctors apparently thought no treatment for hypertension was necessary. In August 1976 his blood pressure was recorded at the hospital as 160/95; again there was no treatment. In August 1977 it was 140/90, which is within the acceptable limits. In March 1978, when he experienced a severe incident of hypoglycaemia and was admitted to hospital for a week to stabilize his blood sugar levels, his blood pressure was found to be 190/100. The following day it had dropped to 145 systolic, the next day it was 170/105, the next day 180/90 but after that for the following four days it was down to 140/90, 130/95, 140/95 and 130/95.

14. It seems that no treatment was commenced for the applicant's hypertension until 31 August 1979, when alphamethyldopa was prescribed. In February 1980, when Mr Birtles was on that medication, his blood pressure was recorded as 140/90 lying down and 150/104 seated, in May 1980 as 140/80 lying down and 150/80 seated, and on 6 June as 150/90 lying down and 150/80 seated. By 6 June 1980 he was taking 250 mg of alphamethyldopa three times a day on medical advice.

15. Over the years from 1975 to 1980 he experienced a number of hypoglycaemic incidents, that is to say manifestations of an unduly low blood sugar level. The applicant gave evidence of such incidents occurring every four or six weeks. When Mr Birtles suffered those incidents his skin would become clammy, she said, and he would shake. He would then take about half a packet of sugar to return his blood sugar to a level at which he did not suffer any such symptoms. As already stated, in March 1978 he was admitted to hospital for his blood sugar level to be stabilized. Until then he had been taking insulin-Lente-40 by one injection of 40 units or 44 units a day. In hospital the use of insulin-Lente-80 was tried and it appears that, when he left hospital, he was given a prescription for that strength of insulin. However, the applicant gave evidence that he was aware of that and adjusted the dosage which he took so that the quantity of the higher strength of insulin which he was taking was the equivalent of the dosage of the lower strength insulin which he had previously been taking.

16. All the doctors who gave evidence in these proceedings expressed the opinion that Mr Birtles' death was due to cardiac arrhythmia. The majority were further of the opinion that that arrhythmia was itself due to a disease of the coronary arteries, that is to say atherosclerosis. Sir John Frew took a different view, namely that the arrhythmia was caused directly by Mr Birtles' hypertension. None of the doctors were prepared to accept a suggestion put to them by Mr W. R. Wilde, representing the respondent, that the arrhythmia was caused by an attack of hypoglycaemia. They pointed to the absence of the symptoms of clamminess of skin and shaking which had typified Mr Birtles' attacks of hypoglycaemia.

17. In his report dated 17 October 1980 Dr Greenberg answered as follows questions put to him by the applicant's solicitor:-

"1. Mr Birtles had longstanding diabetes and the presence of retinopathy

suggests microvascular disease secondary to diabetes.

2. Mr Birtles had mild hypertension for several years prior to his death.

3. In my opinion, the immediate cause of death was cardioarrhythmia complicating myocardial infarction. In all probability, he had coronary artery disease although there was no clinical or electrocardiographic evidence for this. It is possible that the tendency for coronary artery disease was aggravated by the presence of hypertension and diabetes."

He continued:-

"In my opinion, diabetes mellitus and hypertension should be regarded as 'contributory causes' rather than direct causes of his death."

18. In his report dated 23 July 1981, after stating that it was most unlikely that the treatment of diabetes mellitus contributed to Mr Birtles' death in any way by causing hypoglycaemia, he continued "the only relationship between diabetes mellitus and his death is the extent to which diabetes may have contributed to the degree of coronary artery disease". After referring to statistics relating to the prevalence of coronary artery disease in diabetics and non-diabetics and stating that as far as he was aware there was no added risk of coronary artery disease in relation to the duration of diabetes in age-matched controls, he observed that Mr Birtles was a non-smoker, had minimal family history of coronary artery disease but was known to have mild hypertension. He noted also that, although he had an early diabetic background retinopathy consistent with disease of small blood vessels, that change was very common in longstanding diabetes and, as far as he was aware, correlated only poorly with the type of large arterial disease which pre-disposes to coronary thrombosis. As a result of that, he expressed a further opinion that "diabetes mellitus was a minor contributory factor in causing coronary artery disease which almost certainly resulted in his final illness and death".

19. In his report dated 5 April 1983 Dr Hunt stated:-

"I believe that Mr Birtles' death was substantially contributed to by diabetes as a risk factor. Atherosclerosis tends to be more extensive and more severe in diabetics than in non-diabetics and this is associated with an increased frequency of infarction. Indeed, coronary artery disease is a leading cause of death among adult diabetics (Braunwald's textbook on Heart Disease printed 1980). Braunwald also states that the incidence of coronary disease correlates more closely with the duration of diabetes than with its severity. A man with glucose intolerance has about a 50 per cent greater chance of developing coronary disease than would men with no evidence of glucose intolerance."

He summarizes his conclusions as follows:-

"I believe that the presence of diabetes mellitus would make a significant contribution to the chance of a man developing atherosclerotic heart disease and this contribution was perhaps in the order of the contribution made by modest hypertension."

20. Dr Stepanas made in his report a number of statements relating to the relationship of diabetes and atherosclerotic heart disease and to the circumstances of Mr Birtles himself. However, counsel for the applicant, Mr R. Hinkley, made it known that he wished to cross-examine Dr Stepanas on the opinions which he expressed; although Mr Wilde indicated at the commencement of the hearing that it was his intention to call him as a witness, he was not called or made available for cross-examination. In the circumstances we think that it would be unfair to place any reliance on opinions of Dr Stepanas that are not supported by the opinions of other doctors who have given evidence in these proceedings or whose reports have not been challenged. We merely note, therefore, that it was his opinion that the cause of death was cardiac arrhythmia due to myocardial infarction due to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease as a consequence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension and obesity.

21. Dr Martin in his report dated 12 April 1983 stated that he would agree that "diabetes mellitus is generally accepted as a risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease". He continued: "Hypertension is also an associated risk factor for the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease". After commenting that T. L. Drury in Diabetologia 24:1-9.1983 stated that the relevant contribution of hypertension to the risk of coronary artery disease in diabetic patients is uncertain, he referred to statistics which indicate that there is an approximately 1.5 increased risk of coronary artery disease in diabetics and non-diabetics with hypertension, and that with high blood glucose levels similar to those of Mr Birtles the cardiovascular risk factor in men is 2.2. However, he noted a defect in the research on which those statistics were based, namely that diabetes mellitus was not differentiated in terms of type or duration of disease. He then referred to a publication in which the specific risks of mortality over a period of twenty years were analysed for life assurance purposes in respect of a large number of diabetics in the United States of different ages of onset. He said that "for someone of Mr Birtles characteristics who, I believe, would have been rated as of good initial diabetic control to be accepted for life assurance, mortality from cardiovascular disease would be of the order of 2.3 times above that of non-diabetics".

22. Although statistics relating to mortality in relation to blood pressure are not directly relevant to someone like Mr Birtles who developed hypertension only five years before death, the figures are, Dr Martin considered, "very suggestive that hypertension in association with diabetes provides an additive risk which between the ages of 40 and 50 would be not less than 1.4 times above that of diabetics of the same age and duration of disease who did not have hypertension". He then referred to other statistics which indicated that there was "an excess mortality from cardiovascular disease in diabetes which is particularly marked below the age of 45. That excess, he said, "is as much as 12.2 times above non-diabetics at ages 20-30 to 2.2 times at ages 45-60. The major cause of death in both males and females with diabetes mellitus whose onset was between 20 and 39 and who had the disease 20-29 years was cardiac death in 58%. The duration of diabetes increased the risk by 1.4 at 15-20 years duration between ages 40 and 49".

23. He then expressed the opinion that:-

"diabetes mellitus did contribute to the occurrence of cardiovascular disease for which there is good evidence on clinical grounds was the cause of Mr Birtles' death. From the statistical figures quoted diabetes of the nature and duration that Mr Birtles had would have raised his chance of dying from cardiovascular disease by at least twice above a non-diabetic man of similar age. The presence of hypertension of five years treated for one year would, in my opinion, also have been a contributing factor towards the likelihood of death from cardiovascular disease. I think that it is most likely that this risk factor would have been independent of diabetes mellitus from the balance of evidence available. The degree of hypertension was not severe and it is not possible to quantitate this as an additional risk factor more accurately".

24. Finally, Dr Martin concluded:-

"I am forced by the available data from these sources to conclude that diabetes mellitus was very likely a significant contributing factor by

virtue of increased coronary atherosclerosis to Mr Birtles' death."

and

"The presence of moderate hypertension, even though treated, was also a significant contributing factor to Mr Birtles' coronary artery disease."

25. Dr Taft in his report dated 28 March 1983 stated:-

"Intuitively, based on my understanding of the problem, it is my view that diabetes of the duration Mr Birtles had experienced, commencing at the age it did (20 years), I would feel, in light of the absence of other known pre-disposing factors to the development of myocardial infarction other than the hypertension noted in 1975, that diabetes was indeed a substantial contributing factor to that putative cause of his death - myocardial infarction."

He then quoted statistics relating to the prognosis of diabetics with diabetes onset before the age of 31, which showed that myocardial infarction was the second most common cause of death in 307 patients diagnosed before the patient was 31 years old; that was 26 per cent of all such patients. The percentage of deaths from myocardial infarction in the general population in similar age groups was 20.9 per cent. However, the information on which the statistics were based had apparently been collected at different times and Dr Taft sounded a caution because of that.

26. In his report dated 13 April 1983 Dr Taft referred to further statistics which he had found. They were based on studies made in the United States of America over a period from 1948 and, he said, were widely regarded as an authoritative examination of the natural history of disease. They showed that the average absolute annual incidence of cardiovascular disease death per thousand male patients in the non-diabetic population was 8.5 compared with 17.4 in the diabetics. They showed also that relative risk for cardiovascular disease death in diabetics was 1.7 times that for non-diabetics after adjustment for age and the influences of blood pressure, cigarette smoking, cholesterol levels and electrocardiographic changes. He commented that "each of those two parameters of measurement, the absolute annual incidence and relative risk, indicated an approximate doubling of death rate from cardiovascular disease in diabetic males compared with non-diabetic males". Dr Taft concluded:-

"Since the evidence suggests a doubling of the annual death risk rate from cardiovascular disease in diabetic males and that the relative risk for cardiovascular disease caused death in diabetic males is 1.7 times that of non-diabetic males, I conclude that Mr Birtles' death was substantially

contributed to by his known diabetes."

and

"My assessment of 'substantial contribution' reflects the near doubling of death risk due to diabetes."

27. In his report dated 7 July 1983 addressed to the applicant's solicitors

Sir John Frew states:-

"You have good documentation from endocrinologists and specialists in diabetes, Drs Greenberg, Martin and Stepanas and Professor Pincus Taft, indicating the increased use (sic) of coronary arterio-sclerotic and ischaemic heart disease in longstanding insulin dependent diabetics."

After noting that there was no evidence of nephropathy or renal infection, Sir John said:-

"The hypertension is likely to have been coincidental and it would have an effect on the diabetes . . . . Sudden cessation of hypertensive therapy such as could occur under the conditions of travel as outlined by Mrs Birtles could lead to rebound rise in blood pressure and this could certainly have a critical effect in the production of cardiac arrhythmia even with minimal coronary sclerosis. The symptoms detailed by Mrs Birtles could easily have applied to a life-threatening arrhythmia and myocardial ischaemia without actual myocardial infarction and this would answer your three queries."

28. Giving evidence Dr Greenberg expressed the view that the form of arrhythmia which Mr Birtles was most likely to have suffered was ventricular fibrillation, in which there is very rapid electrical activity of the heart but the mechanical activity of the heart ceases. Asked whether the hypertension that Mr Birtles was suffering had caused damage to the heart itself, Dr Greenberg replied that with longstanding uncontrolled hypertension the heart muscle fibres increase in size but that there was no definite evidence for that in Mr Birtles' case. Questioned about a passage in his report dated 13 July 1980 in which he expressed the opinion that it was difficult to quantitate the exact degree that diabetes mellitus might have been a contributory factor or cause of Mr Birtles' death, he replied: "I feel, based on my knowledge of the information concerning Mr Birtles and based on my knowledge as a physician, that there is no doubt that diabetes mellitus was a contributory cause. The difficulty that I face is in trying to quantitate the magnitude of that contribution". He then confirmed that in his view it was a contributory cause because it contributed to causing cardiovascular disease and cardiovascular disease was a factor causing the myocardial infarction which Mr Birtles suffered. Questioned about the directness of causation, he replied "I was satisfied that diabetes mellitus contributed to the development of cardiovascular disease which caused his death". However, he agreed to a suggestion put to him by Mr Hinkley that, if Mr Birtles had not had cardiovascular disease but had hypertensive heart disease, a subsequent pattern of myocardial infarction and arrhythmia could still have followed precisely the same sequence as did in fact occur.

29. Questioned about the manner in which the death certificate was completed showing the immediate cause of death as hypertensive and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus, Dr Greenberg stated how he would have filled in a medical certificate concerning death of the type used in Victoria. He said that he would probably not have shown diabetes as an antecedent cause of death but as an "other significant condition contributing to the death but not related to the disease or condition causing it". He considered that the link between diabetes and coronary heart disease was "not as tight as that for it necessarily to be included as an antecedent cause". He likened it to the part which smoking plays in the development of lung cancer. He said that the association between cigarette smoking and lung cancer was a much tighter association than the association between diabetes and coronary heart disease, but he would put it also in Part II of the medical certificate as another significant condition contributing to the death. Asked about the risk factor resulting from hypertension in Mr Birtles' case he replied that it was probably of the same order of magnitude as the part contributed to by diabetes. He agreed, however, that the statistics reflected the overall pattern of 100 people like Mr Birtles but would not "tell us anything in particular about Mr Birtles as a person".

30. He said that he would have completed a medical certificate in respect of the death of Mr Birtles showing the disease or condition directly leading to death as ventricular fibrillation due to myocardial infarction due to coronary heart disease and would have shown both diabetes mellitus and hypertension as other significant conditions contributing to the death but not related to the disease or condition causing it. He considered that the atherosclerotic disease of the coronary arteries had evolved over some years, perhaps 5 to 10. Questioned by the Tribunal about a statement in Williams' "Textbook of Endocrinology" at page 572 (which was included in the documents lodged under section 37 of the AAT Act) that one researcher found that coronary heart disease accounted for 53.3 per cent of the deaths in diabetics, Dr Greenberg replied: "It seems a little bit high but not many times high. I could not honestly answer the question because I certainly do not have the control data from that population which may not be the same as our population". Asked about his opinion in his report dated 23 July 1981 that diabetes mellitus was a minor contributory factor in causing coronary artery disease in Mr Birtles' case, he said: "Whether it is mild or minor or moderate or contributory - I am sure it is significant . . . . I think that diabetes had something to do with this man's death. It was a factor". Asked whether proper control of diabetes prevented large vessel arterial disease developing he said that he was not aware that it did so. There was quite good animal evidence that, if blood sugar levels were kept as low as possible, disease of the smaller blood vessels was prevented, although even that was not perfect scientific data, but he was unaware of any real evidence supporting the concept that good control of blood sugar levels prevented disease of major blood vessels.

31. Having read the medical reports which were in evidence in these proceedings and seen the Royal Melbourne Hospital records relating to Mr Birtles, Dr Newell expressed the opinion that Mr Birtles had probably had significant hypertension for seven or eight years and that was likely to have resulted in a significant degree of hypertrophy, i.e. enlargement, of the left ventricle of his heart and also a significant degree of coronary artery disease. However, he considered that the coronary artery disease would not have been entirely related to hypertension and that the atheroma would have been due also to diabetes. In his opinion Mr Birtles' death had almost certainly been the result of arrhythmia due to significant coronary disease and enlargement of the left ventricle of his heart. Arrhythmia was the initiating cause of death and was itself related to the disease of the coronary arteries, aggravated by enlargement of the heart. Dr Newell said that a significant percentage of longstanding diabetes sufferers are "survivors", that is to say persons who in spite of the diabetes do not develop diseases which cause them to die at an early age. He observed that Mr Birtles was a non-smoker and that his diabetes was well under control. He stated that atheroma of the coronary arteries is caused both by diabetes and by hypertension and that it was not possible to do more than speculate which caused it in Mr Birtles' case. He attached significance to the fact that, when a cardiograph examination was made in 1978, no abnormality of Mr Birtles' heart was revealed by it.

32. He agreed with a statement from a textbook which was read to him that in 50 per cent of all heart disease deaths death is contributed to by conditions which are known to be risk factors but in the other 50 per cent death is due to unknown factors. He agreed also that epidemiological studies give an overview of the risk factors but do not show the position with respect to any one person. He said that any degree of hypertension carries an appreciable risk and that he would rather have diabetes than hypertension. He said that the possibility of a person suffering from diabetes developing coronary artery disease was correlated with the length of time he suffered from it and that for that reason in Mr Birtles' case diabetes could well have been a significant contributing factor to the disease of his coronary arteries. He thought that in a case such as Mr Birtles' both hypertension and diabetes were likely to have played a significant part in the development of atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries. Nevertheless he thought that Mr Birtles was quite probably a "survivor" because he had not developed any observable cardiovascular pathology earlier, as many diabetics do.

33. Dr Martin gave evidence affirming the opinions which he had expressed in his report dated 12 April 1983. He said that it was a recognized clinical fact that the incidence of atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries in diabetics was higher than in non-diabetics. He said that atherosclerosis developed in the same way in diabetics as it did in others in whom it developed for other reasons, such as hypertension. There were various theories as to the reason for diabetics being predisposed to suffer atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries. Some, he said, were simplistic, for instance the levels of glucose in the blood, and some more complex. Nevertheless there was no doubt about that predisposition. 58 per cent of all deaths of persons suffering from diabetes for between 20 and 30 years were due to cardiac problems. The risk of cardiovascular deaths increased, he said, with the duration of the disease.

34. He accepted that hypertension was also a risk factor in Mr Birtles' case. He said that the fact that hypertension was reasonably controlled apparently did not reduce the risk of cardiovascular death. He considered that Mr Birtles' hypertension was not severe but that the risk of cardiac death resulting from it was 1.5 to 2 times above the normal. He said that both his diabetes and his hypertension were potential risk factors. He would not have expected Mr Birtles to die at the age of 42 with only that degree of hypertension; on the other hand he would also have regarded death from diabetes at that age as unlikely to occur in view of Mr Birtles' record of apparent good health apart from his diabetes. But he noted that it was a well recorded possibility that a diabetic such as Mr Birtles might die at that age of cardiovascular disease resulting from diabetes. He considered, therefore, that diabetes was a significant contributing factor in Mr Birtles' death.

35. Like Dr Newell he accepted that there was a big gap in the medical profession's knowledge about the causes of cardiac death and that in possibly as many as 50 per cent of cases the reasons for it were not known. However, there was a high correlation between diabetes and the development of atheroma of the coronary arteries and between hypertension and the enlargement of the heart. Atherosclerosis would not have shown on an X-ray, although he would have expected an enlarged heart to do so. He agreed that many diabetics live 30 to 50 years with the disease and that it is not possible to say why some people had complications and others did not. He agreed that it was possible that Mr Birtles was one of those persons who had not developed atheroma as the result of diabetes and that it was caused entirely by hypertension. Nevertheless, he thought it very unlikely that, as he had suffered from diabetes for 20 years, it was not related to the development of atheroma of the coronary arteries if he was in fact suffering from that. Based on his clinical experience, he said, he regarded it probable that the applicant did suffer from atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries and that diabetes was a contributing cause of the atherosclerosis.

36. Sir John Frew, while agreeing with the other medical witnesses that Mr Birtles' death was due to arrhythmia, expressed the opinion that the arrhythmia was not due to disease of the coronary arteries or to enlargement of the left ventricle but was the result directly of the hypertension and the drug which Mr Birtles was taking as treatment for it. He said that one of the features of methyldopa is a tendency to produce postural hypotension and also to produce bradycardia as well as some other sorts of complications. He thought that the description of the discomfort which Mr Birtles was obviously suffering in the taxi was an arrhythmia associated with a slowing of the heart beat, i.e. bradycardia associated with a drop in blood pressure.

37. He considered that there was some significance in the manner in which the coroner had expressed the immediate cause of death, that is to say "hypertensive and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease". He thought that the coroner, having heard the applicant's account of the events immediately preceding Mr Birtles' death, had reached the same conclusion as himself. With respect, we find it impossible to accept that hypothesis; if that had indeed been the coroner's opinion, he would not in our view have included the words "and atherosclerotic cardiovascular" nor would he have recorded on the following line that that immediate cause of death was due to "diabetes mellitus". However, that does not affect the validity of Sir John's opinion based on the applicant's account of the events immediately preceding Mr Birtles' death. Asked whether there was anything in that description which made the death diagnostically one due to hypertensive heart disease, he replied: "In a negative sort of way the absence of any pain, the sort of feeling of discomfort or whatever . . . and not a comment of breathlessness, the sort of sudden flop at the end of it". He stressed that he was putting forward the theory of an arrhythmia independent of any atherosclerosis only as a reasonable possibility and he did not assert that it was a certainty. However, asked whether it would be more or less probable than the explanation of atherosclerosis, he said "I think . . . reading it through, I rather would sort of put a shade of odds on my proposition as distinct from the other".

38. Mr Hinkley drew attention to the fact that there was no evidence of any changes to Mr Birtles' coronary arteries or heart as a result of either diabetes or hypertension. He referred to the evidence that in about 50 per cent of cases of cardiac death the cause of death is an unknown factor and not atherosclerosis or an enlarged heart. He pointed out that the evidence of the increased risk of atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries among diabetics and persons with hypertension was statistical only and not related specifically to Mr Birtles. He conceded that we had to make our findings as to the cause of Mr Birtles' death on a balance of probabilities but submitted that the evidence did not establish that Mr Birtles' death was due to atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries or, if it was, that that atherosclerosis was due to diabetes. In this case, however, we are not considering the death of a man who was apparently healthy before he suffered a cardiac death. We have evidence that 58 per cent of persons who have suffered diabetes for between 20 and 39 years die as the result of cardiovascular disease. We also have evidence that a person who has had even mild or moderate hypertension for a period of five years or longer is likely to suffer atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries. As already stated, we are satisfied that death was due to arrhythmia. We are unable to accept the opinion expressed by Sir John Frew that it is more likely than not that the arrhythmia was not related to coronary artery disease. On the contrary, because of Mr Birtles' medical history, we accept the opinions of the other medical witnesses that it is most likely that it was. We are satisfied on a balance of probabilities that the diabetes and the hypertension both played a part in causing the atherosclerosis of his coronary arteries.

39. It is not necessary for us to consider at any length the meaning of the expression "connected with" in section 81(2)(c) and section 184(2)(c)(ii) of the 1976 Act. It has been the subject of consideration in a number of decisions of the Tribunal notably Re Vaughan and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 36, Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN 1, Re Brown and Acting Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD 185 and Re Hegedues and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. A81/26: decided on 16 April 1982). For one condition to be regarded as being "connected with" another condition for the purposes of those two sections there must be a real or demonstrable connection; it is not enough that the one is sometimes, or perhaps not uncommonly, associated with the other. We would only add that in our view it is not sufficient for one condition generally to be connected with the other condition; it must be connected with it in the specific case of the deceased contributor. In the present case we have found on a balance of probabilities that Mr Birtles' diabetes was causally connected with atherosclerosis of his coronary arteries, and that his death was caused or substantially contributed to by that atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries.

40. It remains, therefore, only to consider a submission made by Mr Hinkley that the condition "diabetes" which was relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act, that is to say the physical condition that in the opinion of the Commissioner was the physical condition by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under section 5(1) of that Act that the health and physical fitness of Mr Birtles were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund for pension benefits, was a condition different from the condition of diabetes mellitus which we have found was connected with the atherosclerosis of his coronary arteries which caused his death. There are only two types of diabetes, diabetes mellitus and diabetes insipidus. Diabetes insipidus, unlike diabetes mellitus, results not from high blood sugar levels but from low blood levels of an anti-diuretic hormone, vasopressin. Consequently the treatment of diabetes insipidus does not include injections of insulin. As already noted, in the details of his personal medical history recorded by Mr Birtles in 1970 when he presented himself in 1970 for medical examination for the purposes of admission as a new contributor to the Superannuation Fund under the 1922 Act he stated that he was suffering from "diabetes which is well controlled with one daily injection of insulin-Lente-40, 32 units". There is no doubt, therefore, that the diabetes to which the Government Medical Officer referred in making his recommendation in relation Mr Birtles' acceptance as a contributor and which was the reason for the Superannuation Board's decision to accept him only as a contributor to the Provident Account and not for pension benefits was diabetes mellitus.

41. Accordingly, we are satisfied that the benefit classification certificate issued by the Commissioner's delegate on 16 September 1980 that diabetes mellitus was the condition relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act was correct and that the Commissioner's confirmation of the decisions to issue that benefit classification certificate and that Mr Birtles' death was caused or was substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate or by a condition or conditions connected with that condition was also correct. That decision must, therefore, be affirmed.
