Frizelle and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation and business) [2026] ARTA 752 (8 May 2026)

Applicant: Megan Frizelle

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number/s: 2024/7099 & 2024/7100

Tribunal: Senior Member C Lyford

Place: Perth

Date: 8 May 2026

Decision: The Tribunal:

(i) affirms part of the reviewable objection decision as follows:
  1. half of Distribution One (i.e. GBP 39,422.85) from the Megan Trust to the Applicant, was assessable income of the Applicant, under s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, in the income year ended 30 June 2019;
  2. the whole of Distribution Two (i.e. GBP 58,229.81) from the Megan Trust to the Applicant, was assessable income of the Applicant, under s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, in the income year ended 30 June 2020; and
  3. shortfall interest charge and administrative shortfall penalties (for ‘recklessness’) apply to half of Distribution One (referred to in (i) a. above) and the whole of Distribution Two (referred to in (i) b. above), under Schedule 1 of the Taxation Administration Act 1953; and

(ii) sets aside part of the reviewable objection decision as follows:

  1. half of Distribution One (i.e. GBP 39,422.85) from the Megan Trust to the Applicant, was not assessable income of the Applicant, under s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, in the income year ended 30 June 2019;
  2. the whole of Distribution Three (i.e. AUD 20,000) from the Megan Trust to the Applicant, was not assessable income of the Applicant, under s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, in the income year ended 30 June 2020,

(iii) remits, the set aside part of the reviewable objection decision (referred to in (ii) above), to the Respondent, with an order that the Respondent:

  1. adjust the Applicant’s shortfall interest charge for the year ended 30 June 2019 to reflect the Tribunal’s decision that half of Distribution One (i.e. GBP 39,422.85) was not assessable income of the Applicant under s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, in the 2019 income year; and
  2. adjust the Applicant’s administrative shortfall penalties (for ‘recklessness’) for the years ended 30 June 2019 and 30 June 2020 to reflect the Tribunal’s decision that half of Distribution One (i.e. GBP 39,422.85) and the whole of Distribution Three (i.e. AUD 20,000) was not assessable income of the Applicant under s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, in the 2019 and 2020 income years, respectively.

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. On 17 September 2024, the Applicant applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT), now the Administrative Review Tribunal (Tribunal),[1] for review of the Respondent’s (Commissioner’s) objection decision, dated 13 September 2024,[2] in which the Respondent (Commissioner) disallowed the Applicant’s objections to amended assessments and penalty assessments for the income years ended 30 June 2019 and 30 June 2020 (Relevant Years) and decided not to remit the shortfall interest charge for the income year ended 30 June 2019.[3]
  2. For the following reasons, the Tribunal affirms the reviewable objection decision, in part, sets aside the reviewable objection decision, in part, and remits the set aside part of the reviewable objection decision to the Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with the Tribunal’s orders, as set out below (at [183]).[4]

PROCEDURAL HISTORY

  1. On 16 January 2023, the Commissioner wrote to the Applicant advising her that the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) had commenced a review of her tax affairs for the 2017 to 2020 income years (ATO Review Letter).[5]
  2. In the ATO Review Letter, the Commissioner offered the Applicant an opportunity the provide ‘voluntary disclosure’ and advised the Applicant of what she needed to provide the ATO for its review. In the ATO Review Letter, the Commissioner stated that the ATO was aware that the Applicant is a beneficiary of a foreign trust and has received distributions from the foreign trust, asked the Applicant to answer the following eight questions and to substantiate her answers, including but not limited to the trust deed and bank statements:
    1. What distributions have you received from the foreign trust? Please note that this includes distributions that you have received directly and indirectly, whether received into Australia or elsewhere.

2. How have you treated these distributions for taxation purposes?

  1. Save for the distributions referred to in Question 1 above, have you received any further funds from international sources?
  2. If yes, please describe the nature of the funds, the source of the funds and how the funds were treated for taxation purposes.
  3. Do you have access to any international bank accounts, whether in your own name or otherwise?
  4. If the answer to question 5 is yes, please provide details of each account including the financial institution, account name, account number and current balance.
  5. Please provide details of all credit cards used by you, including international and domestic cards.
  6. Did you hold, control or manage, whether directly or indirectly, the disposition of any funds, property, assets and investments including shares (sic.) portfolios located offshore for the review period. If your answer is yes, provide details of:

8.1 value of the funds, assets, property and investments at the end of each financial year.

8.2 a description of the funds, assets property, investments and location.

8.3 the amount of any foreign income earned, or capital gain/loss made from the funds, assets, property and investments.

8.4 the name and address of the person or entity holding or controlling the funds, assets, property and investments.

  1. On 21 February 2023, the Applicant’s tax agent/accountant, Ken Thomas of Nimbus Chartered Accountants (Mr Thomas),[6] responded to the ATO Review Letter (Applicant’s Review Response).[7] In summary, in the Applicant’s Review Response, the Applicant stated that:

(i) she was not aware she was a beneficiary of any foreign trust;

(ii) was not aware she had foreign property or bank accounts; and

(iii) the amounts she received in the Relevant Years were ‘gifts and loans’ from her mother.[8]
  1. On 24 April 2024, the Commissioner completed an audit of the Applicant for the Relevant Years (ATO Audit Letter).[9]
  2. As set out in the ‘Reasons for Decision’, attached to the ATO Audit Letter, the reasons for the audit of the Applicant by the Commissioner were, in summary, as follows:
    1. The Commissioner became aware of information to suggest that [the Applicant] had received funds from a foreign trust whilst [the Applicant was] a resident of Australia. This information was inconsistent with information in [the Applicant’s] lodged income tax returns and therefore a risk review was initiated to assess this risk.
    2. [The Applicant] was notified of the risk review on 16 January 2023 and [was] provided with an opportunity to review [her] own affairs and answer specific questions. The Commissioner was not satisfied with [the Applicant’s response] to the risk review and therefore the matter was escalated to audit with notification on 10 August 2023.
    3. The Commissioner considered that a taxation risk had been identified and issues a Position Paper on 22 March 2024 to set out the ATO’s analysis of the facts as [it] understood them, application of the relevant law to those facts and details [of] the proposed changes and recommendations that have been made as a result of [the ATO’s] compliance activities...[The Applicant] provided a response to the position paper on 19 April 2024, [she] raised no new material issues and provided no new information.[10]
  3. As a result of the audit, on 2 May 2024, the Commissioner issued the Applicant with:

(i) amended assessments to for the Relevant Years (Amended Assessments);[11] and

(ii) shortfall penalty assessments for the Relevant Years, pursuant to Schedule 1 of the TAA (Penalty Assessments).[12]
  1. In short, the taxable income of the Applicant, for the Relevant Years, was adjusted (increased) by the Commissioner because of:
(i) distributions from a non-resident trust, pursuant to s 99B(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (ITAA 1936);

(ii) interest charges on the non-resident trust distributions, pursuant to s 102AAM of the ITAA 1936; and

(iii) administrative penalties (of 50%) for ‘recklessness’, pursuant to s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (TAA); and

(iv) shortfall interest charge (SIC) for the unpaid tax, pursuant to s 280-100 of Schedule 1 to the TAA.[13]

  1. On 4 June 2024, the Applicant, objected to the Amended Assessments, Penalty Assessments and the Commissioner’s decision not to remit SIC for the Relevant Years.[14]
  2. On 13 September 2024, the Applicant’s objection to the Amended Assessments, Penalty Assessments, and the Commissioner’s decision not to remit SIC for the year ended 30 June 2019, were disallowed by the Commissioner (Objection Decision).[15] By reason of s 280-170 of Schedule 1 to the TAA, a valid objection could not be made by the Applicant to the Commissioner’s decision not to remit SIC for the year ended 30 June 2020. This is discussed in further detail below. Accordingly, the Applicant’s invalid objection, to the Commissioner’s decision not to remit SIC for the year ended 30 June 2020, was treated by the Commissioner in the Objection Decision as a request by the Applicant to reduce the SIC for the 2020 income year, which request was denied.[16]
  3. As stated above (at [1]), on 17 September 2024, the Applicant applied to the Tribunal for review of the Objection Decision.[17]

The non-resident trusts

(i) The Plet Trust

  1. By a deed, dated 8 February 2001 (Plet Trust Deed),[37] a trust named ‘The Plet Trust’ (Plet Trust) was established, with Beresford Trustees Ltd, a company incorporated in the British Virgin Islands, as its trustee. The initial trust property of the Plet Trust was GBP 100: First Schedule to the Plet Trust Deed.[38] The initial beneficiaries of the Plet Trust included the Applicant’s Father and Mother and the Applicant herself: Second Schedule to the Plet Trust Deed.[39]
  2. The Plet Trust was established under the laws of the Island of Jersey: clause 2 of the Plet Trust Deed.[40]
  3. The Plet Trust Deed provides that the trustee may pay income or capital of the Trust Fund of the Plet Trust to the ‘Grantor’ (who is not identified in the Plet Trust Deed) or, with the consent of the ‘Grantor’ to any one or more of the beneficiaries as the trustee sees fit: clauses 4(a)(i)-(ii) and (b) of the Plet Trust Deed.[41]
  4. To the extent that income was not paid to the ‘Grantor’ or to beneficiaries, the Plet Trust Deed provides that the trustee shall accumulate the income of the Trust Fund and the accumulations become capital (i.e. corpus): clauses 4(a)(iii) and (d) of the Plet Trust Deed.[42]
  5. A ‘Deed of Removal’, dated 16 April 2011 (Plet Trust Deed of Removal),[43] recites the following:
(i) on 18 December 2001, Beresford Trustees Ltd (Beresford), with an address in Jersey, became the trustee in place of the previous trustee (with an address in the British Virgin Islands);

(ii) on 23 January 2003, Oak Trust (Guernsey) Ltd (Oak), with an address in Jersey, became the trustee in place of the previous trustee (Beresford); and

(iii) on 15 April 2011, the Robin Earl Gilmour Trust was appointed as a beneficiary of the Plet Trust.

  1. Under the Plet Trust Deed of Removal, the Applicant, the Applicant’s Mother and others were removed as beneficiaries of the Plet Trust leaving the Robin Earl Gilmour Trust as the sole beneficiary of the Plet Trust.[44]

(ii) The Megan Trust

  1. By a deed dated 9 August 2006 (Megan Trust Deed),[45] a trust named ‘The Megan Trust’ (Megan Trust) was established, with Oak as trustee. The Megan Trust was established under the laws of the Island of Guernsey: clause 2 of the Megan Trust Deed.[46] The initial trust property of the Megan Trust was GBP 100: The Second Schedule to the Megan Trust Deed.[47] Initially, the only beneficiary of the Megan Trust was the Applicant: The Third Schedule to the Megan Trust Deed.[48]
  2. The Megan Trust Deed provides that the trustee, Oak, may:

(i) pay income to beneficiaries: clause 4(a) of the Megan Trust Deed;[49]

(ii) accumulate income and add accumulations to the capital of the trust fund: clause 4(c) of the Megan Trust Deed;[50] and

(iii) pay or apply capital of the trust fund to or for the benefit of beneficiaries: clause 5(b) of the Megan Trust Deed.[51]

  1. By a ‘Deed of Addition’, dated 1 May 2012,[52] the Applicant’s Husband was added as a beneficiary of the Megan Trust.
  2. By an ‘Instrument of Addition of Beneficiary’, dated 23 November 2020,[53] being after the Relevant Years, the Applicant’s Mother was added as a beneficiary of the Megan Trust.

(iii) The REG Trust

  1. By a ‘Declaration of Discretionary Trust’, dated 14 April 2011 (REG Trust Deed),[54] a trust named ‘The Robin Earl Gilmour Trust’ (REG Trust) was established, with Oak as trustee. The initial trust property was GBP 100: Schedule 2 of the REG Trust Deed.[55] The beneficiaries of the REG Trust included the Applicant’s Mother and the Megan Trust: schedule 3 of the REG Trust Deed.[56]
  2. The REG Trust was established under the laws of Guernsey: clause 27.1 of the REG Trust Deed.[57]
  3. The REG Trust Deed provides that the trustee, Oak, may:

(i) pay income to beneficiaries: clause 4.1(a) of the REG Trust Deed;[58]

(ii) accumulate income: clause 4.1(b) of the REG Trust Deed;[59]

(iii) apply accumulated income as if it were income arising in the current year: clause 4.1(c) of the REG Trust Deed;[60] and

(iv) pay or apply any part of the trust fund or its income to beneficiaries: clause 4.1(d) of the REG Trust Deed.[61]

BURDEN OF PROOF

  1. In proceeding for review by the Tribunal of a reviewable objection decision, the Applicant has the burden of proving, under s 14ZZK(b) of the TAA:
(i) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment – that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been; or

(ii) in any other case – that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently: s 14ZZK(b)(i) and (ii) of the TAA.

  1. The taxpayer must prove these matters on the balance of probabilities.[92] That is, facts relevant to a Tribunal decision, are to be established on the balance of probabilities.[93]
  2. As Derrington J observed in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Ross (2021) 174 ALD 77; [2021] FCA 766 at [46]:

...the effect of s 14ZZK(b)(i) is that the taxpayers bear the burden of proving, on the balance of probabilities, both that the assessment is “excessive” and, also, what the assessment should have been to make the assessment right, or “more nearly right”: Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 (Trautwein) at 88 per Latham CJ; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco (1990) 168 CLR 614 (Dalco) at 623-625 per Brennan J and at 632-634 per Toohey J; Gashi v Commissioner for Taxation [2013] FCAFC 30; (2013) 209 FCR 301 (Gashi) [61] – [67]...It should always be kept steadily in mind that the rationale for the onus imposed by s 14ZZK(b)(i) is that the facts relating to a taxpayer’s taxable income are peculiarly within their knowledge, and they must be taken to know what their income is and how it was derived: Trautwein at 87. It follow[s] that there is no undue harshness in requiring a taxpayer, who failed to lodge a return or whose return is not compliant with the taxation legislation, to bear the onus of establishing their true taxable income for the relevant income year.

(emphasis added)

  1. The courts have held that it is not necessary, for the purpose of s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA, for the taxpayer to determine their taxable income to the exact dollar amount.[94] However, taxpayers must establish an amount which is ‘more nearly right’.[95]
  2. If the taxpayer is unable to establish that the assessment in question is excessive, then the assessment will stand irrespective of whether there are any facts or circumstances which would prima facie support the Commissioner’s assessment.[96] The taxpayer will not necessarily prove that an assessment is excessive by showing the Commissioner made an error in forming a judgment as to the amount of the assessment.[97]
  3. As stated by the High Court of Australia, in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614 at 624 per Brennan J:

The Act [i.e. the TAA] does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with s 190(b) for it is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.[98]

Role of the Tribunal

  1. The role of the Tribunal is generally to make the correct and preferable decision based on the evidence before it.[99]
  2. The effect of the shift in onus on the taxpayer, under s 14ZZK(b) of the TAA, was discussed by the Full Federal Court in Rawson Finances Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (2013) 133 ALD 39; [2013] FCAFC 26 (Rawson). In Rawson, Jagot J, with whom Nicholas J agreed (at [110]) observed that a distinction is drawn between the obligation of the taxpayer to establish that the assessment is excessive and the usual role of the Tribunal to determine what is the correct and preferable decision based on the evidence before it. It is only the former issue that is before the Tribunal in tax proceedings. Rawson was endorsed by a differently constituted Full Federal Court in Rigoli v Commissioner of Taxation (2014) 141 ALD 529; [2014] FCAFC 29 (Rigoli (2014)) at [26] setting aside the decision of the AAT. Rigoli (2014) returned to the AAT in Rigoli and Commissioner of Taxation (2015) 147 ALD 182; [2015] AATA 169 (Rigoli (2015)). In Rigoli (2015), the AAT undertook an extensive review of the numerous cases relating to s 14ZZK of the TAA which was accepted by the Full Federal Court in Rigoli v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (2016) 68 AAR 478; [2016] FCAFC 38 at [25] ff, in dismissing an appeal from the AAT’s decision.[100]

ISSUE ONE: DISTRIBUTIONS FROM MEGAN TRUST – SECTION 99B(1)

Relevant law – Issue One

Property paid to or applied for the benefit of a beneficiary

  1. Subsection 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936 provides:

Where, at any time during a year of income, an amount, being property of a trust estate, is paid to, or applied for the benefit of, a beneficiary of the trust estate who was a resident at any time during the year of income, the assessable income of the beneficiary of the year of income shall, subject to subsection (2), include that amount.

(emphasis added)

  1. Accordingly, s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936 is engaged where property of a trust is ‘paid to, or applied for the benefit of, a beneficiary’ of the trust.
  2. Whether an amount has been ‘applied for the benefit’ of a beneficiary is governed by s 99C of the ITAA 1936. The phrase ‘applied for the benefit’, in s 99C(1) of the ITAA 1936, is expressed in extremely broad terms. Section 99C(1) of the ITAA 1936 provides:

In determining for the purposes of section 99B whether any amount has been applied for the benefit of a beneficiary of a trust estate, regard shall be had to all benefits that have accrued at any time to the beneficiary (whether or not the beneficiary had rights at law or in equity in or to those benefits) as a result of the derivation of, or in relation to, that amount, irrespective of the nature or form of the benefits.

(emphasis added)

  1. Because of the inclusion of the words ‘irrespective of the nature or form of the benefits’, in s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936, provided an amount was ‘paid to, or applied for the benefit of, a beneficiary’, it is irrelevant, for the purpose of s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936, what the amount was for. Relevantly, it is irrelevant, for the purpose of s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936, that an amount ‘applied for the benefit of a beneficiary’ was a ‘gift’ to the beneficiary.
  2. Section 99C(2)(c) of the ITAA 1936 provides that an amount shall be taken to have been ‘applied for the benefit’ of a beneficiary if, relevantly:
(c) the beneficiary has received or become entitled to receive any benefit (including a loan or a repayment, in whole or in part, of a loan, or any other payment of any kind) provided directly or indirectly out of that amount or out of property or money that was available for the purpose by reason of the derivation of the amount;

(emphasis added)

Knowledge of taxpayer irrelevant

  1. Sections 99B and 99C apply as a matter of law. The knowledge of the taxpayer is irrelevant to the application of ss 99B and 99C of the ITAA 1936.

Corpus exception – s 99B(2)(a)

  1. An exception to s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936 applies where the amount paid to or applied for the benefit of a beneficiary represents ‘corpus of the trust estate’: s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936. The expression ‘corpus of the trust estate’, as it appears in s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, is not defined in the ITAA 1936. However, the ITAA 1936 provides a framework, in Division 6, for determining the assessable income of beneficiaries, including from trust estates.
  2. The Commissioner’s view, as to what is meant by the term ‘corpus’, in the context of s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, is set out in Taxation Determination TD 2024/9, titled ‘Income tax: factors taken into account in applying paragraph 99B(2)(a) and (b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936’ and dated 27 November 2024 (TD 2024/9),[101] is as follows:
    1. Corpus, in the context in which it is used in section 99B and Division 6 more broadly refers to trust capital which is represented by the assets of the trust, excluding income which has not been accumulated. In determining whether an amount distributed represents corpus, for the purposes of paragraph 99B(2)(a), regard is to be had to the trust property distributed. The accounting records of the trust may assist in evidencing this but are not determinative of what the amount represents.
    2. Accumulated income is included in corpus, but it will be corpus which is attributable to amounts which would be included in assessable income if derived by a hypothetical resident taxpayer. Accordingly the amount assessed under subsection 99B(1) will not be reduced by an amount attributable to accumulated income under paragraph 99B(2)(a) [footnote omitted].
  3. That is, section 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936, is subject to s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, which relevantly provides:

The amount that, but for this subsection, would be included in the assessable income of a beneficiary of a trust estate under subsection (1) by reason that an amount, being property of the trust estate, was paid to, or applied for the benefit of, the beneficiary shall be reduced by so much (if any) of the amount, as represents:

(a) corpus of the trust estate (except to the extent to which it is attributable to amounts derived by the trust estate that, if they had been derived by a taxpayer being a resident, would have been included in the assessable income of that taxpayer of a year of income);

(emphasis added)

  1. Accordingly, s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936 does not apply where the amount that was ‘paid to, or applied for the benefit of, a beneficiary’ represents ‘corpus of the trust estate’ (corpus exception).
  2. In deciding whether the corpus exception, in s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, applies, the Tribunal cannot accept mere assertions by a taxpayer that an amount represents the ‘corpus’ of a trust estate. The question is whether an amount objectively represents ‘corpus of the trust estate’, as that expression is understood to mean, having regard to objective evidence of the source of the relevant amount.

Hypothetical resident taxpayer test

  1. However, the corpus exception, in s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936 (as set out above at [75]), does not apply to the extent that the corpus is attributable to amounts derived by the trust estate, if the amounts had been derived by a resident taxpayer and the amounts would have been included in the assessable income of that hypothetical resident taxpayer in the relevant income year (hypothetical resident taxpayer test).
  2. One purpose of the hypothetical resident taxpayer test is to ensure that amounts paid to or applied for the benefit of a beneficiary, out of accumulated income of a trust estate, are taxed to the beneficiary. That is, the hypothetical resident taxpayer test is intended to ensure that s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936 cannot be avoided where a foreign trust accumulates income, such that the income becomes corpus, and the corpus is then ‘paid to, or applied for the benefit of’ an Australian resident taxpayer (beneficiary).[102]
  3. In Traknew Holdings Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4272, Hill J summarises the history of s 99B of the ITAA 1936 (at 4284) as follows:

The provisions of s. 99B can only be understood in their historical context. The need for some such provision was discussed by the Taxation Review Committee (the Asprey Committee) in its report of 31 January 1975. The problem exposed by cases such as Union Fidelity Trustee Co of Australia Ltd v FC of T 69 ATC 4084; [1969] HCA 36; 119 CLR 177 was that ss. 99 and 99A had no application where accumulated income was derived from a source outside Australia. If the trust income was accumulated and became capital, its subsequent receipt by a beneficiary was neither assessable income under ss. 25 or 26(b). Section 99B together with ss. 99C and 99D were introduced into the Act by the Income Tax Assessment Amendment Act No. 5 of 1978. As the Explanatory Memorandum circulated with that Act discloses to deal:

“...primarily with the receipt by resident beneficiaries of distributions from non resident trust estates of previously untaxed foreign sourced income.”

The Explanatory Memorandum makes the following relevant comments on s. 99B:

“The proposed section 99B will require the inclusion in a beneficiary’s assessable income of amounts paid to or applied during a year of income for the benefit of a resident beneficiary where that amount represents trust income of a class which is taxable in Australia but which has not previously been subject to Australian tax in the hands of either the beneficiary or the trustee. It will normally apply where accumulated foreign income of a non resident (or of a resident trust estate that previously was not able to be taxed in Australia in the light of the Union Fidelity decision) is distributed to a resident beneficiary.”

  1. It is for taxpayer (not the Commissioner) to establish that the corpus exception applies and that the hypothetical resident taxpayer test should be answered negatively.[103]
  2. The Commissioner’s view in relation to the hypothetical resident taxpayer test, in s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, is set out in TD 2024/9 as follows:
    1. For the purposes of the hypothetical resident taxpayer tests, the only characteristic of the hypothetical taxpayer is that they are an Australian resident.
    2. Further, in applying the hypothetical resident taxpayer tests to determine whether or not an amount would be assessed in the hands of the hypothetical resident taxpayer, it is necessary to consider the circumstances that gave rise to the relevant amount.
    3. Determining whether a distribution represents an amount which is ‘attributable to’ amounts which would be assessed in the hands of the hypothetical resident taxpayer for the purposes of paragraph 99B(2)(a) or whether an amount ‘represents’ an amount that would not have been assessable if derived by the hypothetical resident taxpayer for the purposes of paragraph 99B(2)(b) involves considering how the amount became an asset of the trust.

(emphasis added)

  1. Put simply, the question posed by the hypothetical resident taxpayer test is this: would the amount representing corpus, derived by the trust estate, be assessable income if it had instead been derived by a hypothetical resident taxpayer. This was articulated by the Full Federal Court in Howard v Commissioner of Taxation (2012) 206 FCR 329; [2012] FCAFC 149 (Howard) at [37], as follows:

...The excision requires the positing of a hypothesis and the posing of a question premised on that hypothesis. The hypothesis posited is that the amount received by the Esparto Trust estate was derived by a resident taxpayer; the question posed on its assumption is whether that resident taxpayer would have been required to include the amounts it received as assessable income.

  1. The Commissioner’s view, as stated in TD 2024/9 at [26], is that the hypothetical resident taxpayer test raises the following two questions:
    • In posing the hypothesis, what characteristics are ascribed to the hypothetical resident taxpayer, other than residency?
    • In answering the question as to whether amounts would or would not be included in the assessable income of that taxpayer, it is relevant to take into account the circumstances giving rise to the amount?
  2. Further, in TD 2024/9, the Commissioner states:
    1. The hypothesis posited under paragraphs 99B(2)(a) and (b) involves an assumption that the amount held or derived by the trust estate was instead held or derived by a resident taxpayer. That resident taxpayer is a separate fictional entity.
    2. Paragraphs 99B(2)(a) and (b) adopt the indefinite article ‘a’ to identify a non-specific taxpayer deriving the amount in a non-specific year is income. Neither paragraph refers to any particular category of taxpayer (such as an individual, trust or a company) indicating that the marginal tax rates or other specific taxing characteristics of specific types of taxpayer are irrelevant for the purposes of applying the hypothesis.
    3. Therefore, the characteristics of the hypothetical resident taxpayer are limited to residence in Australia only. This is clear from the text in paragraphs 99B(2)(a) and (b). No other characteristics can be assumed.

(emphasis added)

  1. That is, when applying the hypothetical resident taxpayer test in s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, the trust estate is substituted with a hypothetical resident taxpayer, with its only characteristic being that it is a resident taxpayer.
  2. In Howard, the Full Federal Court’s approach to characterising the hypothetical resident taxpayer recognised (at [37] – [42]) that, in relation to s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, that the hypothetical resident taxpayer of a separate fictional entity and that the hypothesis differs from the actual fact, which is that there was an amount derived by a trustee of a non-resident trust.
  3. The Commissioner’s view, as set out in TD 2024/9 at [26] and [33], is that to determine whether an amount would be included in the assessable income of a hypothetical resident taxpayer, it is relevant to consider the circumstances giving rise to that amount. Further, according to the Commissioner:
    1. It is relevant and necessary to consider at least certain facts about the amount being tested which could form part of the circumstances of the hypothesised derivation. Without importing some facts, it is not possible to make an assessment as to whether, or what part of, that amount a hypothetical resident taxpayer would be assessed on: TD 2024/9.

Chain of foreign trusts & the hypothetical resident taxpayer test

  1. As was the case in Howard and, as is the case here, an amount received by a resident beneficiary may involve a distribution received by the resident beneficiary through a chain of trusts. For example, in Howard, the distribution was received by the resident beneficiary taxpayer, Mr Howard, from the Esparto Trust (first trust) which received the amount from the Juris Trust (second trust). In such a situation, the application of the hypothetical resident taxpayer test involves the application of s 99B of the ITAA 1936 to the distribution from the first trust, and considering whether the distribution represents corpus of the first trust. If so, the next step is to apply the hypothetical resident taxpayer test (i.e. ask, when that corpus was derived by the first trust, would it have been treated as assessable income of a hypothetical resident taxpayer?).[104] Where there are multiple layers of foreign trusts, s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936 requires that the above step be repeated.
  2. In Howard, the Full Federal Court made the following observations regarding the application of the hypothetical resident taxpayer test in s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936 when a chain of foreign trusts are involved:
    1. There, having grasped the initial hypothetical transformation of the Esparto Trust estate into a resident taxpayer, one is now required (as part of that hypothesis’s inevitable working through) hypothetically to treat the Juris Trust estate as a resident taxpayer and to ask whether the amounts received by it would have been included in such a resident taxpayer’s assessable income.
    2. In this case, having penetrated two layers of trusts – first the Esparto Trust; then the Juris Trust – one encounters for the first time a non-trust relationship. The trustee of the Juris Trust received non-trust distributions from another Jersey company called Esparto Ltd. Although the process of conjoining Mr Howard to the amounts paid by Esparto Ltd seems complicated, in reality it is not. Section 99B(2)(a) will simply apply as many times as there are interposed layers of trusts. Each application of s 99B(2)(a) leads to a hypothetical question about whether the amounts received by the trust estate would have been assessable income if they had been earned by a resident taxpayer. Once an answer to that question is known at the level of the deepest trust the answer cascades back up to the original (genuine) resident taxpayer. To unpick that slightly: if the Juris Trust estate had been a resident taxpayer and amounts received by it had been assessable income, then the amounts received by the Esparto Trust, although corpus, would have fallen within the parenthetic excision in s 99B(2)(a) and would have been assessable income in its hands. This, in turn, provides the affirmative answer to the question posed by s 99B(2)(a) as to whether the amounts received by the Esparto Truste estate would bad been assessable income on the hypothesis that the Esparto Trust estate was a resident taxpayer. But it is that answer on that hypothesis which applies to Mr Howard himself. What is revealed therefore is not complexity but repetition.
    3. Once that is understood the question simply becomes whether the amounts received by the Juris Trust estate from Esparto Ltd would have been assessable income had it been (as it certainly was not) a resident taxpayer.
    4. What amounts did it receive? It received the proceeds of a share buy-back which occurred when Esparto Ltd purchased its own shares back from the trustee of the Juris Trust...

(emphasis added)

‘Source’ of distribution relevant

  1. The Commissioner’s view, as set out in TD 2024/9 (at [51] and [52]), is that s 99B(2)(a) and (b) of the ITAA 1936 require a determination of what the relevant amount received by the Australian beneficiary represents and is attributable to (i.e. the source of the amount). That involves looking beyond the distribution by the trustee to consider how the trust property became a trust asset. Further:
    1. If the distribution is made from the proceeds of sale of an asset which was settled upon the trust, the distribution well represent and be attributable to the sale proceeds of that asset. However, if a trustee acquires a capital asset with accumulated income not preciously subject to tax in Australia, the amount received by the Australian beneficiary will represent or be attributable to amounts which would be included in the assessable income of the hypothetical resident taxpayer because that is the ultimate source of the distribution.[105]

(emphasis added)

(iii) at the time of the Distribution Two, the only beneficiaries of the Megan Trust were the Applicant and the Applicant’s Husband;

(iv) the journal entries and bank statements show that GBP 58,229.81 was paid to the Applicant, via the Applicant’s Mother, on 4 November 2019; and

(v) the Applicant has not proved otherwise.

  1. Therefore, the Megan Trust (a foreign trust) derived Distribution One and Distribution Two from the REG Trust (a foreign trust).
  2. As stated above (at [103]), s 99B(2) of the ITAA 1936 requires a determination of what the relevant amount received by the Australian beneficiary represents and is attributable to (i.e. the ‘source’ of the amount) which involves looking beyond the distribution by the trustee to consider how the trust property became a trust asset. The Applicant has provided no evidence of the ‘source’ of the distributions or, more particularly, how the relevant ‘Funds settled’: see [59] to [62]. For the reasons expressed above (at [67] to [68]), the Tribunal places no weight on the content of the Signed Oak Trustee Letter and, specifically, places no weight on the evidence or opinion of the two unidentified (unnamed) ‘Authorised signatories’ of the Signed Oak Trustee that the distributions from the Megan Trust to the Applicant in the Relevant years represented ‘corpus’ or ‘capital’ of the Megan Trust.
  3. As contended by the Respondent, it appears that at least a substantial proportion of the funds, represented as being ‘Funds settled’ with the REG Trust, came from the Plet Trust.[112] However, to the extent distributions came from another source, the Applicant has provided no evidence of their source. If the REG Trust derived the amounts from the Plet Trust, the Applicant has not proved the ultimate ‘source’ of the amounts and, accordingly, not proved that the hypothetical resident taxpayer test should be answered negatively.
  4. Even if the amounts derived by the Megan Trust from the REG Trust represented ‘corpus’ of the REG Trust (which the Applicant has not proved on the balance of probabilities), the Applicant has not proved that the hypothetical resident taxpayer test should be answered negatively.
  5. As stated above (at [91]), one purpose of the hypothetical resident taxpayer test, in s 99(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936, is to ensure that s 99B(1) of the ITAA 1936 cannot be avoided where a foreign trust accumulates income, such that the income becomes corpus, and the corpus is then ‘paid to, or applied for the benefit’ of a resident taxpayer. As also stated above (at [34] Plet Trust, [38] Megan Trust and [43] REG Trust), each of the relevant trust deeds provided for accumulated income to become corpus.
  6. For the reasons provided above (at [90] to [100]), under the hypothetical resident taxpayer test in s 99B(2) of the ITAA 1936, even if the Applicant established that Distribution One and Distribution Two were ‘corpus’ of the Megan Trust, the Applicant also needs to establish that when the amounts were derived by the Megan Trust, they would not have been included in the assessable income of the Megan Trust, had it been a hypothetical resident taxpayer. The Applicant has failed to prove this, on the evidence before the Tribunal, on the balance or probabilities.
  7. Further, for the reasons provided above (at [101] and [102]), s 99B(2)(a) of the ITAA 1936 dictates that if the Megan Trust received Distribution One and Distribution Two from another trust (i.e. the REG Trust), the Applicant also needs to establish that, when the other trust (i.e. the REG Trust) derived Distribution One and Distribution Two, the amounts would not have been assessable income had that other trust (i.e. the REG Trust) been a hypothetical resident taxpayer.[113] The Applicant has failed to prove this, on the evidence before the Tribunal, on the balance of probabilities.

ISSUE THREE: REMITTAL OF SIC FOR YEAR ENDED 30 JUNE 2019

Relevant law – Issue Three

  1. In short, s 280-100(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a taxpayer is liable to pay a SIC on an additional amount of tax that the taxpayer is liable to pay because the Commissioner amends the taxpayer’s assessment.
  2. The Commissioner ‘may’ remit all or part of an amount of a SIC if the Commissioner considers it ‘fair and reasonable’ to do so: s 280-160(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA.
  3. In deciding whether to remit, the Commissioner must have regard to:
(i) the principle that remission should not occur just because the benefit the taxpayer received from the temporary use of the ‘shortfall amount’ is less than the SIC; and

(ii) the principle that remission should occur where the circumstances justify the Commonwealth bearing part or all the cost of delayed payments: s 280-160(2) of Schedule 1 to the TAA.

  1. Relevantly, a taxpayer can object against a decision not to remit a SIC, but only if the amount of the charge that was not remitted is more than 20% of the additional amount: s 280-170 of Schedule 1 to the TAA.

Consideration & Conclusion – Issue Three

ISSUE FOUR: PENALTY ASSSEMENTS

Relevant law – Issue Four

  1. In summary, s 284-75(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a taxpayer is liable to pay an administrative penalty if they make a statement to the Commissioner and the statement is false or misleading in a material particular.
  2. Where a shortfall amount results from a false or misleading statement, s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that the ‘base penalty amount’ is:
    • 75% of the tax shortfall amount if the shortfall amount resulted from an ‘intentional disregard’ of a taxation law by the taxpayer or the taxpayer’s agent;
    • 50% of the tax shortfall amount if the shortfall amount resulted from ‘recklessness’ by the taxpayer or the taxpayer’s agent as to the operation of a taxation law; and
    • 25% of the tax shortfall amount if the shortfall amount resulted from a failure by the taxpayer or the taxpayer’s agent to take ‘reasonable care’ to comply with a taxation law.
  3. The terms ‘intentional disregard’, ‘recklessness’ and ‘reasonable care’ are not defined in the TAA.
  4. In Miscellaneous Taxation Ruling MTR 2008/1, titled ‘Penalty relating to statements: meaning of reasonable care, recklessness and intentional disregard’ and issued on 12 November 2008 (MT 2008/1), the Commissioner provides his views on the meaning of those terms for the purpose of s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA.
  5. In MT 2008/1, the Commissioner describes what is meant by ‘recklessness’, for the purpose of s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA, in part, as follows:
    1. ...a finding of recklessness depends on the application of an essentially objective test. There must be the presence of conduct that falls short of the standard of a reasonable person in the position of the entity....dishonesty is not an element of establishing recklessness. The actual intention of the entity is of no relevance.
    2. Behaviour will indicate recklessness where it falls significantly short of the standard of care expected of a reasonable person in the same circumstances of the entity...it is the extent or degree to which the conduct of the entity falls below that required of a reasonable person that underscores a finding of recklessness.
    3. Recklessness assumes that the behaviour in question shows disregard of or indifference to a risk that is foreseeable by a reasonable person...
  6. In Hart v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (2003) 131 FCR 2003; [2003] FCAFC 105 at [33] and [43], the Full Federal Court endorsed the following comments of Cooper J in BRK (Bris) Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2001] FCA 164; 2001 ATC 4111; [2001] FCA 164; (2001) 46 ATR 347 at [77]:

...Recklessness’ in this context means to include a tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing that there is a real, as opposed to fanciful, risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act or regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the context of the tax statement. So understood, the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to gross carelessness.

  1. Section 284-75(2) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a taxpayer is liable to an administrative penalty if they make a statement to the Commissioner under an income tax law and in the statement they treated an income tax law as applying to a matter or identical matters in particular way that was not reasonably arguable and they have a shortfall amount as a result that is more than their relevant reasonably arguable threshold of $10,000.
  2. Section 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that the base penalty amount is 25% of the shortfall amount.
  3. Section 284-15(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that a matter is reasonably arguable if it would be concluded in the circumstances, having regard to relevant authorities, that what is argued for is about as likely to be correct as incorrect, or is more likely to be correct than incorrect.
  4. Section 284-90(2) of Schedule 1 to the TAA provides that if two or more base penalty amounts apply and one of them produces a greater base penalty amount than any of the others, the Commissioner is to use that greater base penalty amount.
  5. The Commissioner ‘may’ remit all or part of a penalty: section 298-20 of Schedule 1 to the TAA. The discretion is unconstrained but must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA and according to law.[134] The purpose of the penalty regime is to defer infringement of the law in an environment of self-assessment.[135]
  6. The existence of ‘special circumstances’ is not a prerequisite for remission.[136] Rather, the question is whether, having regard to the taxpayer’s particular circumstances, it is appropriate to remit the penalty in whole or in part.[137]
  7. The Commissioner’s practice in relation to penalties imposed for false or misleading statements is set out in Practice Statement Law Administration PSLA 2012/5, titled ‘Administration of the false or misleading statement penalty – where there is a shortfall amount’ and issued 23 August 2012 (PSLA 2012/5). PSLA 2012/5 is an ‘internal instruction to ATO staff’ in relation to the administration of the penalty, under section 284-75(1) of Schedule 1 to the TAA, for making a false or misleading statement that results in a shortfall amount: PSLA 2012/5 at [1].

Consideration – Issue Four

Applicant’s conduct was reckless

  1. The Applicant did not include the distributions from the Megan Trust in her Australian income tax returns for the Relevant Years.
  2. There is no evidence before the Tribunal explaining why the Applicant did not include the distributions from the Megan Trust in her income tax returns for the Relevant Years (other than that she was not ‘aware’ of the existence of the Megan Trust or that she was a beneficiary of the Megan Trust), no evidence in support of the assertions that the Applicant was unaware to the source of the distributions she received from the Megan Trust in the Relevant Years and no evidence of any inquiries made about the distributions she received from the Megan Trust in the Relevant Years.
  3. Further, there is no evidence, only the Applicant’s assertions, that she relied on professional advice about the distributions she received from the Megan Trust in the Relevant Years, and no evidence of what she provided to such a professional adviser.
  4. For the above reasons, the Tribunal considers that the Applicant’s conduct in relation to her income tax returns for the Relevant Years was, as found by the Commissioner in the Objection Decision, ‘reckless’, as that term is understood to mean: see [153] to [155] above. As the Applicant’s conduct was ‘reckless’, the shortfall amounts also resulted from the less culpable failure by the Applicant to take ‘reasonable care’ to comply with a taxation law.
  5. Further, with no evidence in support of the Applicant’s assertions, she does not have a ‘reasonably arguable’ position.
  6. Whilst the Applicant has base penalty amount of 50% for recklessness, 25% for failure to take reasonable care and 25% for not having a reasonably arguable position, only the higher base penalty amount of 50% for ‘recklessness’ can be applied: s 284-90(2) of Schedule 1 to the TAA.
  7. There is no evidence to support the Applicant’s assertions, in the Applicant’s SFIC, that the Penalty Assessments are excessive.[138]
  8. As explained above (at [4]), the Applicant was given the opportunity to provide ‘voluntary disclosure’, but instead simply asserted that the distributions were ‘gifts and a portion as a loan’ from her Mother. That is insufficient to discharge her burden of proof, under s 14ZZK of the TAA, that the Penalty Assessments were excessive and what they ought to have been.
  9. Further, as noted above, provided that an amount was ‘paid to, or applied for the benefit of, a beneficiary’, it is irrelevant that an amount was a ‘gift’ to a beneficiary.

  2. Otherwise, the Applicant has not discharged her onus of proving, on the balance of probabilities, that the Penalty Assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect: see [69] to [74] above.
