Taxation Appeals - whether Applicant derived income under several names - onus and standard of proof - failure by-Applicant to establish assessment excessive

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 October 1987 Deputy President RD Nicholson

The following reasons were given orally:

Objection Decisions Under Review

The Applicant seeks review of two objection decisions. The first relates to the 1980 year of income and the second to the 1981 year of income. The essence for the disallowance of the taxpayer's claim by way of objection in each of those years is the contention by the Respondent that he derived income from sources other than those shown in the returns for each of those years, specifically in 1980 that he derived wages from P Company under the names of "BA", and that in 1980 and 1981 that he derived subcontract income from employment with G firm.

Onus and standard of proof

It is important in this proceeding to bear in mind the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") in relation to the onus of proof upon a taxpayer. Section 190 of the Act provides that:

"190. In proceedings under this Part on a review before the Tribunal or on appeal to a court -

(a) the taxpayer shall, unless the Tribunal or court otherwise orders, be limited to the grounds stated in his objection; and

(b) the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer."

The assessment as made is prima facie right, and remains right, until the person seeking the review shows it to be wrong: Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 at 88 per Latham CJ.

It is important also to emphasize in this proceeding that the degree or standard of proof which is required of the taxpayer is not proof beyond all reasonable doubt as in criminal proceedings, but conviction as a matter of belief or on the balance of probabilities. In Pascoe v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 11 ATD 108 Fullagar J said that the question which he had to ask himself was whether he was convinced - not beyond reasonable doubt - but as a matter of belief of the question then in issue before him. Just as there is no criminal standard of proof upon the Applicant, this proceeding cannot be approached on the basis that there is a criminal standard of proof resting on the Respondent so that the Applicant would be entitled to succeed if the Respondent did not discharge such a burden. That is not the position: the burden under section 190(b) remains throughout upon the taxpayer and is of the different character already described.

It is important to notice something distinctive about the nature of the burden on the Applicant in this proceeding. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hines (1952) 9 ATD 413 the Full High Court in allowing a taxpayer's appeal in respect of a default assessment said at page 420:

"Is it possible, in this state of affairs, to justify a refusal to accept Hines' statement on oath that in this year he had no other source of income than the business? In our opinion, it is not ... The burden of proving a negative is not, as a rule, easily discharged, but in this case it could not fairly or reasonably be said that Hines had failed to discharge it. The case must be decided on the evidence, and on the evidence there is a very definite preponderance of probability in his favour." (Emphasis added)

In a decision of the Board of Review - Case 83 - 15 CTBR 525 it was said at page 534:

"The proof required in criminal proceedings is proof beyond all reasonable doubt; but the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive' which, by sec 190(b) lies upon the taxpayer, does not contemplate proof of this degree. It is our opinion that if 'D' has given sworn evidence that he incurred expenditure in connexion (sic) with any of the properties under consideration, but is unable to support this by reference to book-entries, receipts, or vouchers, and that evidence has not been contradicted by another witness and there has been nothing in his demeanour or otherwise upon which properly to found a belief that it is untrue and unreliable, then the Board should accept his evidence unless it is inherently unreasonable and improbable in respect of the matter of which he has spoken." (Emphasis added)

The Tribunal, therefore approaches the evidence before it in the light of these principles, that is, that the burden expressed by section 190(b) remains throughout upon the Applicant; that in this proceeding there is the burden of proving a negative, and so one not easily discharged; and that the Tribunal must look to see whether there is a contradiction of the sworn evidence led on behalf of the Applicant and whether acceptance of that evidence alone would be inherently unreasonable and improbable.

Evidence

The evidence for the Applicant consists of statements on oath by the Applicant and his wife that in the relevant years he had no other source of income than those disclosed in his return. The Respondent has based his contentions on the view that the Applicant derived income in other names which were not disclosed. He adduced evidence from an officer of the inspection section of the Australian Taxation Office; members of the staff of relevant banks; and a former employer from whom it was said additional income had been derived.

As we have seen the burden on the Applicant is to prove the negative, that is, that he did not derive the other income contended for by the Respondent. Difficult as that burden may be to discharge, it is to be noted that the Applicant did not call evidence beyond his own denials and those of his wife. For example, evidence of a handwriting expert was not called by the Applicant to disprove similarity in various signatures of the Applicant and his wife on their returns and on deposit slips relating to payment into his known account of cash cheques said to represent the proceeds of non-declared income.

Having heard all the evidence the Tribunal concludes there is a very definite preponderance of probability in favour of the Respondent. The evidence of the Applicant is contradicted by evidence led for the Respondent. It would be inherently unreasonable and improbable to accept the Applicant's sworn evidence by way of denial in respect of the matters at issue in face of the evidence led for the Respondent. The hypothesis advanced by Mr McLoughlin on behalf of the Applicant that the witness employer engaged in some scheme of great complexity to "frame" the Applicant because of malice towards him is not an hypothesis sustained by the evidence.

The Tribunal makes the following comments on aspects of the evidence: Firstly, it is significant that although in the course of proceedings it was established that some of the moneys said to comprise non-disclosed income were paid into the Applicant's known account, no explanation was given why the Applicant did not contest those receipts. This is not a situation such as has occurred in other proceedings where the Applicant has sought to find some explanation for the derivation of the funds shown to have been so paid and contended for that reason that there was no liability to tax. For example: the onus of proof has been held not to be discharged in two cases, one where the taxpayer failed to show that certain moneys paid by him into his wife's bank account arose from winning bets he had made, (Levick v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1949) 9 ATD 103) and in another, where the taxpayer, the proprietoress of a taxi business, failed to show that large sums standing in her name and that of her children in bank accounts were gifts and winnings from gambling - Case B33 (1951) 2 TBRD 140. No such attempt at explanation has been made here.

Secondly, all the names in which the different accounts have been held appear to the Tribunal to have a direct or indirect or derivative link to the name of the Applicant.

Thirdly, the variations in addresses which have appeared in evidence are, in the view of the Tribunal, to be characterized as minor only and with the exception of one case the addresses refer to places occupied or formerly occupied by the Applicant or a friend or associates. Some variations in spelling may be accounted for by the Applicant's attempt to project into English phonetic sounds, the spelling of which was not familiar to him.

Fourthly, the signatures have, on the balance of probabilities, and in the absence of any expert evidence given on behalf of the Applicant, an apparent similarity. The Tribunal forms this view upon its own inspection of the signatures in the face of detailed cross-examination highlighting points of dissimilarity as well as evidence from Mr Thompson who, while not an expert, has some experience in identification of signatures. In particular, assessing the evidence as to signatures, the Tribunal has in mind that the Applicant's own evidence establishes (firstly) that he used two signatures, namely one involving writing cursively and another involving printing, and (secondly) that his handwriting varied from time to time, probably reflecting his limited formal training.

To return to what was said by Fullagar J in Pascoe's case, the Tribunal therefore must ask itself the question whether it is convinced, not beyond reasonable doubt but as a matter of belief, that the Applicant did not earn wages from P company under the name of BA or from G firm for subcontract cleaning work. The answer of the Tribunal is that it is not, on the evidence in this proceeding, so convinced.

Amendment of assessment

It is then necessary for the Tribunal to examine the power of the Respondent to amend the assessment. The 1980 return comes before the Tribunal, not by way of an amended assessment, although the adjustment sheet so describes it, but by way of issue of a notice of assessment. In Batagol v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243, it was held that an assessment within sub-section 170(3) was not made until the Commissioner serves a notice of assessment assessing the taxable income at specified amounts and that accordingly the Commissioner needs no statutory authority to examine prior to the issue of such a notice matters in relation to assessment. The Tribunal considers the principle of that decision should be followed. Accordingly, the Commissioner was entitled to issue the notice of assessment in the 1980 year of income.

An amended assessment issued with respect to the 1981 year of income. In the view of the Tribunal that assessment is justified by the provisions of sub-section 170(2) of the Act. Given the view which it takes of the evidence, the Tribunal considers the taxpayer did not make full and true disclosure on all material facts necessary for his assessment, that there was as a consequence an avoidance of tax and that the Commissioner was empowered by that sub-section as it stood at the date when the amended assessment was issued to make the amendment to prevent the avoidance of tax.

Objections

Turning to the specific paragraphs of the objections lodged on behalf of the Applicant, the Tribunal finds that in relation to the objection concerning the 1980 year of income paragraphs 1 to 5 cannot succeed because of the view which the Tribunal has taken of the evidence. Paragraph 6 to 9 do not fall for consideration because there is not, in that year of income, an amendment of assessment to which section 170 has application.

In relation to the 1981 year of Income, paragraphs 1 to 4 of the notice of objection lodged on behalf of the Applicant do not succeed because of the view taken by the Tribunal of the evidence. Paragraphs 5 to 8 do not succeed in the view of the Tribunal because the Commissioner is entitled in its opinion under sub-section 170(2) to issue the notice of amended assessment.

Conclusion

The Tribunal therefore proposes to affirm the objection decisions under review.
